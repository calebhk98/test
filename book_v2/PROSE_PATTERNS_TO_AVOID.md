# PROSE PATTERNS TO AVOID — Quick Reference
### *The Long Way Home* V2 — Day-to-day writing agent reference

Read this before writing any chapter. These are patterns V1 produced at scale;
V2 must catch them sentence by sentence.

---

## SECTION 1: BANNED CONSTRUCTIONS (zero tolerance)

### 1.1 The correctio / "not X, it's Y"

Any construction that cancels its premise by naming what a thing is NOT before
naming what it IS. All variants are banned. This is the book's single clearest
AI signature: 25-37 violations in V1 despite an explicit ban.

**V1 violations:**
- "I did not have a plan. I had a wound with an idea in it." (ch28)
- "It was not the heart that stopped me. It was the breath." (ch50)
- "It wasn't conversation. It was two men passing a stylus back and forth."
- "It was not a system. It was four women and a wharf."

**What to do instead — 3 corrected examples:**

V1 (banned): "It was not fear. It was something that lived below fear."
V2 (fix): "Something sat below the fear, patient, without a name."

V1 (banned): "It wasn't gratitude he showed me. It was relief."
V2 (fix): "His shoulders dropped. He let out one slow breath. That was all."

V1 (banned): "I did not have a plan. I had a direction."
V2 (fix): "I had one name and enough money for a meal. I started walking."

**The pattern to avoid in all its forms:**
- "It was not X. It was Y."
- "Not X. Y."
- "It wasn't X, exactly."
- "Not because X. Because Y."
- "Less X than Y" as a rhetorical pivot
- "Not just X, but Y"


### 1.2 Narrator stepping outside time

The narrator does not announce significance from the future. No exceptions
outside explicitly flagged encyclopedia/journal interludes.

**V1 violations:**
- "For a long time afterward I got him wrong." (ch04)
- "I have never entirely stopped." (ch07)
- Future-tense predictions embedded in present-tense narration (ch36, 37, 46)

**The permitted version:** "I know now that the meeting cost him more than he
showed." (Reports retrospective knowledge — fine, within the retrospective frame.)

**The banned version:** "I did not know then what that meeting would cost him."
(Performs dramatic irony by declaration. The narrator arrives from the future
to assign weight. This removes the reader's discovery.)

**Fix:** State what Daniel knew or felt at the time. If something will prove
important, build the architecture so the importance is felt without announcing it.


### 1.3 Em dashes

Already banned. Use a normal dash (-), comma, or full stop. No exceptions.
The em dash (—) does not appear anywhere in this manuscript.

## SECTION 2: HARD LIMITS (specific counts per chapter)

### 2.1 Gnomic scene-closing wisdom buttons

A portable aphorism that restates the scene's meaning at the end of a scene
or chapter. Not banned — but limited.

**Limit:** Maximum 1 per chapter. Aim for 0 per 3 chapters (roughly one button
every three chapters across the book).

**V1 examples:**
- "I can't beat it." (ch23)
- "It is the only interesting part." (ch34)
- "Some things you secure. Some things you only confess you could not." (ch49)

These are individually fine sentences. The problem is universal deployment: in
V1, ~55-60% of chapters end this way. By ch20 the reader is waiting for the
button before it arrives. When it arrives on schedule, it confirms rather than
lands.

**Check:** If the last sentence of your scene could be pulled out and used as
a tweet or epigraph with no context, you have written a button. Consider whether
the scene's last concrete image or action is the stronger ending.


### 2.2 One-sentence paragraphs

**Limit:** Maximum 15% of paragraphs per chapter.

V1 ran at 26-27%. At that density, the single-sentence paragraph stops
signaling emphasis and starts signaling paragraph break. The effect the writer
is reaching for — a beat that lands alone, in white space — is only available
when single-sentence paragraphs are genuinely rare.

Practical check: In a 3,000-word chapter with ~70 paragraphs, you have budget
for 10-11 one-sentence paragraphs. If you're writing the 12th, ask whether
this one is doing pivot-work (revelation, decision, death, irreversible silence)
or whether it's a rhythm pause that could be absorbed into the paragraph above
or below.


### 2.3 "the way" / "X the way Y does"

**Limit:** Maximum 3 uses per chapter.

V1: 677 instances (~13 per chapter). The book's defining verbal tic, including
the reusable template "the way you look at a [thing/dog/tool]" (17 uses) and
"looked at me the way" (14 uses). The book even ends on it. When a construction
appears 13 times per chapter, it is a generative habit, not a chosen style.

Use it only when the specific analogy earns its place — when direct language
cannot do what the comparison does.


### 2.4 "looked at me"

**Limit:** Maximum 2 per chapter.

V1: 91 instances in 34 of 53 chapters. This is the stealth replacement for
"he nodded" and "she sighed" — eye contact deployed as scene-connective
punctuation rather than as a charged act. Reserve it for moments when the eye
contact actually means something: when a character is deciding something, or
seeing something for the first time, or refusing to look away.


### 2.5 Chapter openings with template pattern

**Limit:** Maximum 20% of chapters (roughly 10-11 of 53).

V1 had ~23 of 53 chapters open on one of three templates:
- "[Subject] came..." (11 chapters: "The summons came...", "The order came...")
- "There is a [sound/silence/thing/step]..." (4 chapters)
- "The first... / The thing about..." (8 chapters)

These templates are individually fine and often good. As a group pattern
covering 43% of the book's openings, they train the reader to wait for the
pattern to complete before engaging. The strongest chapter openings in V1 arrive
mid-scene or mid-image: "The bag was the size of a man hung head-down." (ch8),
"The yard came with a smell before it came with anything else." (ch10). These
work because they put the reader in a place before explaining what the place means.


### 2.6 The correctio (all variants)

Already covered in Section 1.1. Listed here again as a count reminder:
**ZERO instances per chapter. Per book. No exceptions.**

## SECTION 3: TICS TO WATCH (at most once per 5 chapters)

These phrases are not banned. At V1 densities they become wallpaper.
Each should feel chosen when it appears, not reflexive.

**"the shape of"** — 59 instances in V1, appearing in 36 of 53 chapters.
Good construction ("the shape of what I was asking"), worn smooth by frequency.
Max 1 use per 5 chapters.

**"the whole of"** / **"that was the whole"** — 80 combined instances.
Often used to summarize a scene's meaning at its close ("the whole of the bond
we had, worn to a wire"). This is the "wisdom button" in summary form.
Max 1 use per 5 chapters.

**"which is to say"** — 21 instances. An explanatory self-correction that
usually just restates what was already clear. Ask whether the clarification is
doing work, or whether the sentence before it was already sufficient.
Max 1 use per 10 chapters.

**Macer's ring-turning gesture** — used ~11 times in V1 as his physical tic.
A character's repeated gesture establishes quickly and exhausts just as fast.
Max 3 total in the entire book. After the third use it is habit, not character.

**"Cheap. Mine. Undeniable." refrain** — used multiple times verbatim.
Powerful on first use; loses force with each repetition. Retire after Part III.
If a V2 equivalent refrain emerges, give it a fixed maximum of 2 uses and let
it go.

**The forearm burn-scar** — re-explained in nearly every early chapter in V1.
Explain it once, in detail, in ch01 or ch02. After that, reference only —
"the scar," not "the burn from the forge fire that had given me the mark."
The reader remembers. Trusting them not to is the tell.

**"wait for the water to boil" image** — used to close three consecutive war
chapters (ch28-30) in V1. Never reuse a closing image within five chapters.

**"old hands flat on a surface" closing gesture** — used 4 times in V1.
One more use exhausts the image's force entirely. If it appears in V2, it
appears once, as a deliberate callback, and no more.

**"two feelings, same size, you couldn't get a knife between them"** — used
~4 times in ch46-48 of V1. The doubled-feeling construction is genuinely
distinctive; the verbatim phrase is a crutch. Use the construction freely;
avoid the specific phrasing after its first appearance.

## SECTION 4: WHAT TO DO INSTEAD (positive guidance)

### 4.1 Scene-ending alternatives

**Image cut** — end on a concrete physical fact. No interpretation.
- "The coals settled and dimmed. I did not put more wood on."
- "She set the tablet face-down on the bench. Outside, the yard was already loud."
- "I held the rope for a long time." (The act is the meaning. Don't say what it means.)

**Dialogue cut** — end on a line that says less than it means.
- "'Good night,' he said, in the easy way of a young man to an old one."
  (The casualness is the weight. Don't tag it.)
- After a hard conversation: "She said, 'Go,' and I went."
  (The clipped line ends it. What comes after — Daniel's walk, his thoughts —
  is not needed.)

**Action cut** — end at the start of the next movement, not its completion.
- "I folded the letter and went to find him."
- "I went to keep my appointment with a man who was not my friend."
- Reader knows the destination. Does not need to see the arrival.

**Silence cut** (model: ch42) — end in the gap after something large. The
small physical act that follows a death, a departure, a piece of news that
changes everything.
- "[Heras has just died.] I put out the lamp."
- "[After the thing no one said.] Macer turned the ring on his thumb once.
  I counted the stones in the wall."
- "[After a failure.] I went back to the bench."

**Test:** If your last sentence could be extracted and used as a standalone
aphorism, it is a wisdom button. Ask whether the sentence before it is the
stronger exit.


### 4.2 Showing emotion without announcing it

Three techniques, each with a brief example:

**Technique 1: Behavioral observation.** Show what the character does, not
what they feel. The reader infers.
- BAD: "Celer's sacrifice moved me more than I could say."
- GOOD: "'Thank you,' I said, which was the wrong size, which was always going
  to be the wrong size, and Celer looked at the ground and I looked at the
  ground and neither of us fixed it."

**Technique 2: The object in the hands.** When a character is feeling
something they can't name, give them something physical to do. The doing is
the feeling.
- BAD: "I sat with my grief for a long time after she was gone."
- GOOD: "I sat at the bench. I picked up the bronze stylus. I put it down. I
  picked it up again. I didn't write anything."

**Technique 3: The wrong-sized response.** A character responds to a large
thing with something too small — the wrong word, a practical question, a
change of subject. The gap between the size of the moment and the size of the
response is the emotion.
- BAD: "He was overwhelmed, unable to process what he'd just seen."
- GOOD: "He looked at the thing in the sky for a long time. Then he said, 'There
  were no strings.'"


### 4.3 Varying chapter openings

Five different entry patterns with brief examples:

**Mid-action:** Drop into something already happening. No thesis, no weather,
no setup. The reader orients as they read.
- "The bag was the size of a man hung head-down." (Already in the scene.)
- "I was three steps from the yard when I heard it stop."

**Sensory landing:** One specific sense before any narrative fact.
- "The yard came with a smell before it came with anything else."
- "The hill had a name in Dacian that nobody in our camp could say."

**Dialogue opening:** Begin with a spoken line, before any scene-setting.
- "'Thulean,' Macer said, not looking up from the wax."
  (The reader doesn't know what 'Thulean' is yet; that's fine.)

**Physical object:** Begin with one specific object doing something or being
somewhere specific. No metaphor, no frame.
- "The third step up from the water had worn to a dish from two hundred years
  of men's heels."

**Retrospective entry (use sparingly):** The narrator acknowledges the moment
as a memory without telegraphing its importance.
- "The first thing I got wrong was thinking I'd fallen asleep on the job."
  (Event-first; the frame doesn't assign weight before the scene does.)

## SECTION 5: WHAT V1 GOT RIGHT (preserve these)

Do NOT over-correct these. The following are genuine prose strengths. V2 must
keep them.

**Sensory grounding.** The book's single most consistent strength. Smell, sound,
temperature, materials: lamp soot, wool grease, garum, bronze, wet clay. This
grounds an ancient world without once calling it ancient. Keep every instance.

**Oblique dialogue.** In most scenes, characters do not say what they mean.
They change the subject, answer a different question, say the practical thing
instead of the emotional one. This is right. Preserve it. The scene at the
horse-lines (ch14) — where neither Daniel nor Celer names what passes between
them — is the model.

**Dramatic irony through structure.** Especially ch51-53, where Roman documents
"find" Daniel's legacy without his name attached. This is the correct technique:
build the architecture so the gap exists, don't announce the gap. Preserve and
expand it.

**Daniel's dry, self-deprecating voice.** Never let the prose become earnest
or solemn. The comedy of his situation is always available: a teenager from
Ohio solving Roman metallurgy with half-remembered YouTube videos, getting it
half-right and faking the rest. This voice is what distinguishes the book from
historical fiction. Guard it.

**Deliberate motifs.** The phone (~22x), "the new figures" (~39x), "the little
ones" (~17x), the ladder/rungs (~123x). These are intentional and
well-controlled. Do not thin them while correcting the accidental tics. The
difference between a motif and a tic: a motif tracks the narrative arc; a tic
appears at a near-constant per-chapter rate regardless of content.

**Ch42 and ch50 as models.** Ch42 (Heras's death) renders grief entirely through
action and absence. Nothing is stated. Ch50 (the death scene) handles the
dissolution of consciousness through specific physical sensation, not through
declaration. These are the technical high points of the book. When in doubt
about how to handle a grief scene, read ch42 first.

**Conjunction-led sentences.** "So," "And," "But," at the start of a sentence
is natural and human. V1 used this well. Keep it. Do not strip conjunctions
for a clipped, list-like rhythm.

**Beat-tag clichés — natural use.** V1 eliminated nodding, sighing, and
chuckling entirely (0 instances across 197k words). That was over-correction.
Characters who never perform a small physical beat feel subtly inhuman. V2
should allow 1-2 of these per chapter when the moment genuinely calls for a
beat. The standard: the beat must be specific to this character in this moment.
"He nodded" is filler. "He dipped his head the way he did when he was working
something out" is character. Somewhere between those two is the natural register
that V2 is aiming for.
