# Obvious Tech Gaps — Comprehensive Cross-Referenced Catalog
## Technologies a 2020s US High-Schooler Stranded in 98 AD Rome Would Notice / Push For
### Cross-referenced against the existing audits and book canon

**Scope:** Daniel's life, 98–~155 AD. He is wealthy with a powerful patron (Macer → Hadrian →
endowed institutions). Knowledge ceiling: bright-but-normal 17-year-old 2020s American, algebra
solid, geometry decent, Newtonian intuition, fuzzy science, half-remembered history, a lot of
sci-fi furniture (world rules §3).

**Book status key:**
- IN BOOK = explicitly attempted or delivered in the prose
- ATTEMPTED-FAILED = tried, stated reason for failure
- BIBLE-ONLY = promised in bible/04_tech_schedule.md but absent from prose
- NEVER RAISED = not attempted, not blocked, not mentioned

**Feasibility key (for 98–155 AD with a rich motivated backer):**
- EASY WIN = genuinely achievable with Roman materials + Daniel's knowledge alone
- PLAUSIBLE STRETCH = achievable but requires sustained work, some luck, or a partial
  prerequisite already in hand
- GENUINELY BLOCKED = a hard prerequisite is absent and cannot be short-circuited

## CATEGORY 4: TEXTILES

| Tech | Book Status | Feasibility + True Blocker | Teen-Obviousness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spinning wheel** | BIBLE-ONLY (Phase B: "spinning wheel = ~10x throughput, simple, transformative; social-friction-limited adoption"). **Never appears in prose — one of the top-ranked omissions in tech_plausibility_audit.md.** | EASY WIN. A drive-wheel turning a spindle via a belt — purely wooden, no metal required, the Chinese had it by ~1000 AD and it appeared in India by ~700 AD. Rome's only blocker is "free labor is cheaper." The book should at minimum make this argument on the page the way it made it for the rail (ch23). | MEDIUM — most teens don't know spinning-wheel history; but "faster cloth-making machine" is clearly beneficial |
| **Water-powered fulling mill** | BIBLE-ONLY (Phase B: "repurposed water power (trip-hammers, stamp mills, fulling)"). **Absent from prose.** Rome already had Barbegal water mills; pointing them at fulling (pounding wet wool to felt it) is a direct application. | EASY WIN. Rome has water mills (Barbegal has 16 wheels). Fulling is heavy pounding work currently done by foot (human treading). A water-powered trip-hammer set to pound cloth is simpler than a grain mill and uses existing wheel technology. Adopted in medieval Europe as the "fulling mill." No steel required. | LOW — teens don't know what fulling is |
| **Flying shuttle (for wide cloth)** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. The flying shuttle (Kay, 1733) mechanizes the weft-thread insertion in a loom — a wooden shuttle on a track, struck by a hammer. Doubles weaving width and speed. All-wooden. Requires no metallurgy. The conceptual barrier is the mechanism; Daniel would know cloth can be woven faster but probably doesn't remember this specific invention. | LOW — very low teen-knowledge of loom mechanics |
| **Water frame / mechanized spinning** | NEVER RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED for anything useful. The water frame (Arkwright, 1769) needs precise rollers spinning at controlled differential speeds, wooden frames true enough to hold alignment, and metal spindles. Without some form of precision woodworking, the rollers slip and the thread breaks. Getting the tolerances right is the blocker. Not steel-dependent but precision-dependent. | LOW — teens don't know what a water frame is |
| **Rag paper (instead of papyrus)** | NEVER RAISED in prose. **The single top-ranked omission across all three audits.** Han China had paper by 105 AD. Every ingredient on Daniel's wharf: rags/hemp, water, a screen mould (wire or mesh), the existing screw press. | EASY WIN. Macerate rags in water, pour onto a screen frame, press, dry. The only subtlety is sizing (starch paste to prevent ink bleeding) and getting the pulp consistency right — week-level learning curve. No new materials. The book's printing press runs for decades on papyrus while rag paper sits uninvented. This is the single most indefensible omission. | HIGH — "paper" is the most obvious material; a teen who built a press would immediately ask "why papyrus, why not paper?" |

## CATEGORY 5: POWER & MACHINERY

| Tech | Book Status | Feasibility + True Blocker | Teen-Obviousness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overshot waterwheel (more efficient than undershot)** | NEVER RAISED. Rome had undershot wheels at Barbegal. An overshot wheel uses the weight of water falling over the top — 3-4x more efficient. | EASY WIN. Building an overshot wheel vs. undershot only requires routing the water race above the wheel hub. All wood and iron; no new materials. Roman millwrights could build this. | LOW — teens don't know waterwheel types; efficiency argument requires knowing it |
| **Water-powered trip-hammer / stamp mill** | BIBLE-ONLY (Phase B: promised, never delivered in prose). | EASY WIN. A rotating cam on a waterwheel shaft lifts a hammer and drops it — a purely mechanical, steel-free mechanism. Used for grain stamping, ore crushing, fulling. Barbegal precedent makes the wheel available; the trip-hammer is a 3-piece add-on (cam, beam, head). | LOW |
| **Water-powered sawmill** | NEVER RAISED | EASY WIN. A crank converting waterwheel rotation to reciprocating motion for a saw. All wood and iron. First recorded European sawmill c. 13th century, but it's pure mechanical engineering Daniel could reason to. Huge labor saver for the shipbuilding program (ch36–44) — rough-sawing timber is one of the most labor-intensive parts of boatbuilding. | LOW — teens don't think about how wood gets sawn |
| **Windmill (grain/pumping)** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. Vertical-axis windmills were used in Persia by the 7th century AD; horizontal-axis by 12th century in Europe. A vertical-axis windmill (simpler mechanically — no need to orient to wind) is achievable. The blocker is the sail/vane material and structural load on the center shaft bearing — needs a competent iron bearing, which Rome can make. In wind-rich coastal or arid areas (Roman North Africa, Spain) this is a major grain-milling resource. | LOW |
| **Simple crankshaft / crank-connecting-rod** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. Converting rotary to reciprocating motion (or vice versa) is a fundamental machine element. Rome had cranks on late Roman hand-mills; a crank on a waterwheel shaft driving a piston or saw is a step that needs only good iron work. Not steel-dependent. | LOW |
| **Gearing / gear trains (documented)** | IN BOOK indirectly — Hermes "could cut a gear so clean you could shave with the teeth" (ch23); clock gears are easy. | Gearing as such is not a gap; Rome had the Antikythera mechanism. But applying it to power transmission (waterwheels → machinery) is never explicitly done. | LOW |
| **Steam engine (useful)** | ATTEMPTED-FAILED (ch19, ch52). 40+ years of funded failure. | GENUINELY BLOCKED: no pressure-holding vessel without reproducible high-carbon steel. Correctly handled. | HIGH — every teen knows steam engines |
| **Wheelbarrow** | NEVER RAISED (also listed in Agriculture) | EASY WIN. See Agriculture row. | MEDIUM |

## CATEGORY 6: METALLURGY & FUEL

| Tech | Book Status | Feasibility + True Blocker | Teen-Obviousness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reproducible high-carbon steel** | ATTEMPTED-FAILED (ch15, ch21, ch23, ch44, ch52). The master chokepoint. | GENUINELY BLOCKED: reproducibility requires pyrometry or input-side carbon control. Correctly handled as the book's load-bearing barrier. | MEDIUM |
| **Coke / coal as forge fuel** | BIBLE-ONLY-adjacent (ch15 names charcoal cost as a bottleneck; the coal/coke step is never taken). **Flagged in tech_plausibility_audit.md as "partly contrived."** | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH with an honest complication: raw coal sulfurizes iron (sulfur embrittlement), so coking is required — heating coal in an oxygen-reduced environment to drive off sulfur and volatile compounds, leaving nearly-pure carbon fuel. Coking is not complex (brick kilns work) but it's a real two-step Daniel probably doesn't remember. **However:** the book names a fuel-cost problem at exactly the place coal belongs and Daniel never reaches for it. The complication (coking) is actually a good dramatized failure: try coal → iron turns brittle → learn why → try coke. Rome knew coal outcrops (Britain, Belgium); naphtha seeps were known in the Near East. | MEDIUM — teens know coal fuels industry and is "the old fuel"; applying it to a forge requires knowing about coking, which most don't |
| **Taller blast furnace → cast iron** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH with caveats. A taller furnace with water-powered bellows raises the carbon content of iron enough to produce cast iron (3–4% carbon, lower melting point, pourable but brittle). Cast iron is NOT the steel Daniel needs — it's brittle under shock — but it has massive uses: pipes, cauldrons, cooking vessels, cannon-ball blanks (non-structural). China had cast iron by ~500 BC. The Roman bloomery could be extended upward with water-powered bellows. The blocker: the resulting cast iron is not steel and Daniel might not know why the product is different (cast iron vs. wrought iron vs. steel carbon-content distinction). | LOW — "blast furnace → iron → steel" chain is vaguely known by some teens; cast iron specifically is low |
| **Water-powered bellows for the forge** | NEVER RAISED explicitly (hinted at: water power promised in Phase B) | EASY WIN. Leather bellows driven by a cam on a waterwheel — the trip-hammer's cousin. Doubles or triples forced-air volume into the forge at zero human-labor cost. This directly attacks the steel problem (more air = higher sustained temperature = better chance of hitting the window). Rome had the wheels; it's a leather-and-wood add-on. Medieval European forges used exactly this. | LOW |

## CATEGORY 7: TRANSPORT & NAVIGATION

|---|---|---|---|
| **Magnetic compass (lodestone needle)** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. A magnetized iron needle floating on water or suspended by thread points north. Lodestones (magnetite) were known to Greeks and Romans as attracting iron. A needle rubbed on a lodestone holds magnetization. The Chinese had a south-pointing fish (floating magnetized fish shape) by the 1st century AD. The cognitive leap is recognizing that the needle always points the same direction. For Daniel's ocean program (ch36–44), this is the obvious complement to latitude navigation. | HIGH — every teen knows the compass; "sailors use compasses" is universal knowledge |
| **Sternpost rudder (vs. steering oar)** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. A hinged rudder on the sternpost gives far better steering control than side-mounted steering oars, especially in rough weather. The ocean-going hulls of the book (ch36–44) use Roman oars for steering. A sternpost rudder requires a straight sternpost and an iron pintle-and-gudgeon hinge — iron-age metalwork, no steel needed. First European use ~12th century AD. For the ocean program, this is directly relevant. | LOW — teens don't think about steering mechanisms on ancient ships |
| **Lateen/fore-and-aft sail (vs. square sail only)** | NEVER RAISED | PLAUSIBLE STRETCH. A triangular lateen sail rigged fore-and-aft lets a ship sail much closer to the wind (roughly 60° vs. 90° for square rigs). Critical for Atlantic sailing — the return voyage from America requires sailing into the prevailing westerlies. The book's ocean hulls (ch44) are described but their rigging is unspecified. Lateen sails were known in the Indian Ocean and Mediterranean by the 1st–2nd century AD to some extent; Daniel could push for a systematic program. | LOW |
| **Stirrups** | NEVER RAISED | EASY WIN. A leather loop hung from the saddle for the foot. Trivially constructable. First attested use in India ~2nd century BC; widespread in Central Asia by ~500 AD, arriving in Europe c. 600–700 AD. | **Improved saddle (horned / rigid tree)** | NEVER RAISED | EASY WIN. The rigid saddle tree distributes the rider's weight over the horse's back (vs. the Roman cloth pad or pad saddle), dramatically improving riding endurance and preventing back injury to horses. Predates stirrups; all-leather-and-wood construction. | LOW |
| **Horseshoes (iron nailed)** | NEVER RAISED (also Agriculture) | EASY WIN. Iron + blacksmith + conceptual knowledge. See Agriculture. | LOW |
| **Rail / wagonways** | IN BOOK (ch23) — works perfectly, rejected on slave-economy economics. Best honest beat. | ATTEMPTED (functioned). Economic barrier correctly stated. | MEDIUM |
| **Wheelbarrow** | NEVER RAISED (also elsewhere) | EASY WIN. | MEDIUM |
| **Longitude solution** | IN BOOK (ch36) — correctly identified as needing a precision clock; deferred to post-Daniel. | GENUINELY BLOCKED: needs precision clock → precision metal → steel. Correctly handled. | HIGH |

## CATEGORY 10: ELECTRICITY

| Tech | Book Status | Feasibility + True Blocker | Teen-Obviousness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Voltaic pile (wet-cell battery)** | NEVER RAISED in prose. **Flagged in tech_plausibility_rebuttal.md as an easy win wrongly lumped with "impossible bait" (world rules §4).** Zinc, copper, brine, wire — all in Rome. | EASY WIN. Two dissimilar metals (zinc/copper or iron/copper) alternated with brine-soaked cloth or leather pads produces a weak but real current. The specific "two different metals in salt water makes electricity" principle is something a teen who took high-school chemistry would know as a fact-fragment. The product: a low-voltage battery that can shock someone, electrolyze water, produce light sparks, and decompose simple salts (electroplating). **No practical killer app at this voltage**, but the demonstration value is extraordinary. | HIGH — "lemon battery," "zinc + copper + electrolyte = electricity" is a classic classroom demo |
| **Electromagnet (coil of wire on iron core)** | NEVER RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED for a useful electromagnet. Requires a sufficient current source (a Volta pile strong enough) and insulated wire. Roman wire is uninsulated; wrapping bare copper wire produces a short-circuit. Shellac or rubber insulation doesn't exist in Rome; the closest available would be waxed thread wrapped around wire — workable for low-voltage pile but very tedious and fragile. Blocked primarily by wire insulation. | HIGH — teens know magnets; electromagnetic coils are physics-class content |
| **Electric telegraph (simple on/off pulse)** | NEVER RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED. Requires: (1) insulated wire over long distances — practically impossible without gutta-percha or vulcanized rubber; (2) a sensitive galvanometer or sounder; (3) a reliable current source. The long-distance insulation problem is the true show-stopper. Short-range demonstrations (across a room) are possible but have no useful application. | HIGH — teens know the telegraph; Morse code is cultural knowledge |
| **Dynamo / generator** | NEVER RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED. Requires precision-wound coils, a reliable permanent magnet or electromagnet, and high-speed rotation with close mechanical tolerances. Downstream of the steel + precision machining wall. | HIGH — teens know generators produce electricity |
| **Spark-gap wireless (pulse only, no data)** | NEVER RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED even for a single pulse. Requires a high-voltage induction coil (needs a reliable battery + precision winding), a tuned resonant circuit (needs precision components), and a sensitive detector. None of these are achievable. The claim in the task brief that this is "feasible-stretch" is generous — the induction coil alone needs closely-wound, well-insulated secondary turns numbering in the hundreds. | HIGH — teens know wireless exists; this particular path is very unclear to them |
| **Radar (reflection ranging)** | NOT RAISED | GENUINELY BLOCKED, multiply so. Requires: a stable high-frequency oscillator (GHz range), a directional antenna, a time-resolution of nanoseconds, and vacuum-tube amplification. None of these are even remotely achievable in any pre-industrial context. The laws of physics do not change but the engineering threshold is the entire 20th century. Correctly "impossible bait." | MEDIUM |
| **Static electricity / amber demonstrations** | NEVER RAISED | EASY WIN as a demonstration. The Greeks knew amber (elektron) attracts light objects when rubbed. A systematic exploration — rubbing different materials, collecting sparks with a Leyden-jar analog (metal foil + glass jar + conductor) — is achievable. A Leyden jar (metal-foil-lined glass bottle with a conductor in it) can store a surprising static charge and give a strong shock. **No practical application** but demonstration value, and it seeds the concept. | MEDIUM — "static electricity" is known; Leyden jar specifically is obscure |

## TOP TIER: THE MOST DAMNING OMISSIONS

These are technologies that are simultaneously:
- **(A) High teen-obviousness** — an average modern teen would push for these immediately
- **(B) Easy win or plausible stretch** — achievable with Roman materials and Daniel's knowledge
- **(C) Never raised or Bible-only** — skipped by the book with no stated excuse

Ranked from most to least damning:

---

### T-1: RAG PAPER — THE SINGLE MOST INDEFENSIBLE OMISSION

**Why this is first:** Daniel has a printing press. The tech_plausibility_audit calls paper "the most indefensible omission in the book." It is flagged as the number-one recommendation across ALL three prior audits. Every input is literally on the wharf (rags, water, a screen mould, the existing screw press). Han China had it in 105 AD — contemporaneous with the story. An American teen who set up a printing press would ask "why are we printing on this expensive stiff papyrus?" within a week.

**What the book does instead:** Prints on papyrus for 50+ years and never raises the question.

**The blocker that does not exist:** There is none. The book simply does not ask the question.

---

### T-2: BASIC SOAP — MENTIONED IN THE BIBLE, NEVER IN THE PROSE

**Why this is second:** Phase A of the tech schedule explicitly lists "crude soap (tallow + wood-ash lye)" as an easy-win target. It never appears in the prose. A modern teen who has been told "wash your hands to prevent disease" and who is also dealing with Roman sanitation would immediately try to make soap. This is a primary-school craft project. Both inputs (rendered fat, wood-ash lye) are kitchen waste in Rome. The synergy with Daniel's germ-practice thread (handwashing saves lives) makes its absence conspicuous.

**What the book does instead:** Handwashing is in the book; soap is not. Romans used oil-and-strigil, which is acknowledged — but Daniel never makes soap as an easier alternative or as an export product.

---

### T-3: IMPROVED DISTILLATION / ALCOHOL ANTISEPTIC

**Why this is third:** Also promised in Phase C of the tech schedule but absent from prose. Roman stills existed. The missing piece is a water-cooled condenser (a bent copper tube in cold water — Daniel could make this in an afternoon). The product is high-proof alcohol for wound care. Every teen who has seen an antiseptic bottle knows "alcohol kills germs." The connection from "we need to clean wounds better" to "we need stronger alcohol" to "we need to distill it better" is an obvious three-step chain.

**What the book does instead:** Uses boiled water and posca (sour wine) for wound care, which is good, but never upgrades to distilled spirits despite having all the materials.

---

### T-4: MAGNETIC COMPASS — THE OCEAN PROGRAM'S OBVIOUS TOOL

**Why this is fourth:** Daniel runs a multi-decade ocean exploration program (ch36–46). He knows the compass exists. Lodestones were known in Rome. A needle rubbed on a lodestone, floating on water, points north. "Sailors use compasses" is the most universally known navigational fact. In the context of the ocean program, this omission is glaring — a compass does not solve longitude but it dramatically helps dead-reckoning navigation. The book's ocean hulls (ch44) are navigating without one.

**What the book does instead:** The ocean program focuses on hull design and the longitude clock prize. The compass is simply never mentioned.

---

### T-5: HELIOCENTRISM AS EXPLICIT TEACHING

**Why this is fifth:** Zero cost, zero materials, zero prerequisite. Daniel knows Earth orbits the Sun. He knows Aristarchus proposed it (it's in the Greco-Roman intellectual tradition). He has standing, credibility, and a circle of educated Romans (Heras, Hadrian, the contest community). The rebuttal audit calls it "the single biggest free win the book does not use." A modern teen in a room full of flat-earthers who thought the Sun went around the Earth would say something about this; it would be impossible not to.

**What the book does instead:** Never raises it. The contest spawns stories about telescopes; no one is pointed at the solar system.

---

### T-6: VARIOLATION / INOCULATION — WITH THE ANTONINE PLAGUE ON THE HORIZON

**Why this is sixth:** The Antonine Plague (probably smallpox) hits ~165 AD — approximately 10 years after Daniel's death (~155 AD). Daniel has the conceptual knowledge that "a small amount of disease prevents the severe form." He has a physician line (Heras → Zoticus). He has wealth. In his final decade, he is watching people die of infections he cannot prove his "little ones" theory about. Setting up an empirical variolation program — even a crude one — in the 140s–150s AD would be his most direct medical legacy and would arrive just in time for the Plague. The narrative setup is perfect and untouched.

**What the book does instead:** Germ theory spreads in practice (boiling, washing) but variolation is never attempted.

---

### T-7: SPINNING WHEEL + WATER-POWERED FULLING (THE VANISHED PHASE B)

**Why this is seventh:** The tech schedule explicitly promised these as easy wins in Phase B (99–102 AD). They vanished from the prose without explanation or stated barrier. The spinning wheel (10x throughput, simple, wood only) and water-powered fulling (Barbegal precedent, trip-hammer add-on) are the classic early-industrial-revolution low-hanging fruit. The slave-economy argument that killed the rail carts might apply — but the book never makes that argument for textiles. An unexplained omission of promised content is more damaging than a stated failure.

**What the book does instead:** The only "spinning" in 53 chapters is the aeolipile toy; a loom-weight appears as a paperweight.

---

### T-8: ERATOSTHENES / CIRCUMFERENCE CHAIN

**Why this is eighth:** Two sticks, two shadows, one known road distance. Every US student learns the Eratosthenes story. Daniel knows the result (Earth is round, roughly right circumference) and vaguely knows the method. Rome has the land-survey infrastructure. The payoff chain (circumference → surface area → solar distance via Kepler's third law, seeded even if Daniel can't complete it) is a pure knowledge win. This is the classic "Daniel points, Romans complete" scene that the teen_knowledge_and_change.md audit calls the biggest single missed "pointing" scene. No materials. No patron needed. One afternoon conversation.

**What the book does instead:** Nothing.

---

### HONORABLE MENTIONS (close to Top Tier but slightly lower priority)

- **Stirrups:** Easy win, medium teen-obviousness, but the book has no cavalry-focused narrative strand; less damning as an omission.
- **Wheelbarrow:** So simple it's almost embarrassing; very low drama value.
- **Voltaic pile (wet-cell battery):** Easy win materials-wise; moderate teen-knowledge. The rebuttal audit flags it correctly. Not in prose despite being achievable. Lower priority than T1-T8 because the demonstration value (shock, spark) has no practical application at Roman scale.
- **Seed drill:** Medium plausibility, low teen-knowledge.
- **Coal/coke for forge fuel:** Named as a bottleneck in the book; Daniel never reaches for the obvious modern answer. Partly contrived per the audit.
- **Semaphore / optical telegraph:** Easy win; medium teen-knowledge; relevant to military and ocean-program communications.
- **Citrus on ocean voyages:** Easy win in context of the ocean program; scurvy prevention is widely known.
- **Double-entry bookkeeping:** Natural extension of the numeral win; low drama but zero materials cost.
