# Bad Prose Catalog: Sentence- and Paragraph-Level Failure Modes
### A Reference for Fiction Reviewers and Grading Agents

This catalog documents recurring weaknesses in prose at the sentence and paragraph level, with particular attention to patterns AI language models overproduce. Each entry follows the format: **NAME** — description — bad example — why it fails.

---

## LEVEL 1: SENTENCE RHYTHM

### 1.1 Uniform Sentence Length (Length Monotony)
**Description:** All sentences in a passage run to approximately the same word count, creating a metronomic, soporific rhythm with no dynamic range.
**Bad example:** "The rain fell on the cobblestones. She walked through the market slowly. The vendors called out their prices. Her coat was soaked to the collar. She did not look up from the ground."
**Why it fails:** Natural prose varies sentence length to signal weight and pace. Uniform length drains urgency from tense moments and fails to give any beat special emphasis.

---

### 1.2 The "Two Long, One Short Punchy Fragment" Cadence
**Description:** A pair of long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences followed by a short declarative fragment, used repeatedly as a rhythmic unit throughout a passage or chapter.
**Bad example:** "She had carried the letter in her pocket for three days, turning it over in her fingers each night before sleeping. She had rehearsed what she would say, imagined every possible version of his face when she said it. Now she was here."
**Why it fails:** The pattern is briefly effective; repeated, it becomes a tic. Readers begin to feel the machinery — the punch is anticipated before it lands, and it teaches nothing about the specific weight of each moment.

---

### 1.3 The Tricolon Crutch ("X. Y. Z." Three-Beat Rhythm)
**Description:** Compulsive use of three-item lists, three parallel sentences, or three sequential images as the default unit of dramatic emphasis.
**Bad example:** "He looked at her. She looked away. The candle guttered." / "She was angry. She was tired. She was done."
**Why it fails:** The Rule of Three is a real rhetorical tool — which is exactly why LLMs and weak writers resort to it constantly. When every climax, every description, every emotional beat arrives in threes, the number stops carrying weight. It also imposes artificial closure on moments that should remain open.

---

### 1.4 Anaphora Overuse (Repeated Sentence-Initial Words)
**Description:** Beginning multiple consecutive sentences with the same word or phrase, not for deliberate rhetorical amplification but because the writer has no other way to maintain forward momentum.
**Bad example:** "She thought about the house. She thought about the garden. She thought about the smell of bread. She thought about how none of it was hers anymore."
**Why it fails:** Anaphora is powerful in speeches and poetry, where its formal repetition signals intention. In narrative fiction, overused, it reads as a drafting shortcut — the writer spooling out thoughts instead of composing prose. The repetition calls attention to structure rather than content.

---

### 1.5 Participial-Phrase Openers (The "-ing Leash")
**Description:** Most sentences in a passage begin with a participial phrase attached to the subject, creating a syntactic monotony regardless of how varied the underlying actions are.
**Bad example:** "Turning toward the window, she saw the street below. Pressing her palm against the glass, she felt the cold seep through. Remembering last winter, she closed her eyes."
**Why it fails:** Participial openers imply two actions happening simultaneously, which is sometimes physically impossible ("Leaving the room, he sat down"). Even when logically valid, the pattern softens agency — things happen *while* doing other things rather than as decisive acts. A prose style where every sentence begins this way feels tentative, even evasive.

---

### 1.6 Coordinating-Conjunction Sentence Openers (The "And/But Flood")
**Description:** Heavy, indiscriminate use of "And" or "But" to open sentence after sentence, mistaking this for a casual, literary voice.
**Bad example:** "She opened the door. And the light was different. And he was standing by the far wall. But he didn't turn around. And she didn't speak."
**Why it fails:** Starting sentences with "And" or "But" is a legitimate stylistic choice — but requires sparing use. When it becomes the default connector, it generates false intimacy and a breathless, childlike tone that evacuates the prose of complex causality.

---

### 1.7 The Rhetorical Question as False Depth
**Description:** Ending a paragraph or section with a rhetorical question posed to the reader or character, used as a substitute for genuine emotional complication.
**Bad example:** "She stood at the edge of what had been her life. Was any of it real? Had it ever been?"
**Why it fails:** The rhetorical question signals profundity without delivering it. It asks the reader to supply meaning the writer has not provided. AI prose uses it to suggest interiority without constructing any.

---

## LEVEL 2: STRUCTURAL TICS

### 2.1 The Correctio / Antithesis Pattern ("It Wasn't X. It Was Y.")
**Description:** A sentence that cancels its own premise — naming what something is *not* before naming what it *is* — used as a routine device for building emphasis.
**Bad example:** "It wasn't fear, exactly. It was something older, something she had no name for." / "It wasn't that she hated him. It was that she had forgotten why she'd ever loved him."
**Why it fails:** The form creates an illusion of nuance: the writer appears to be refining their thought in real time. But AI and weak writers use it reflexively, for things that don't require refinement, manufacturing a false sense of psychological precision. The pattern appears so often in LLM prose that it now flags the writer's hand.

---

### 2.2 "Not Because X, But Because Y" (False Motive Reveal)
**Description:** A construction that offers a shallow reading of a character's motivation, dismisses it, and substitutes a slightly deeper reading — as though the prose is doing psychological work.
**Bad example:** "She didn't leave because she was afraid. She left because, for the first time in years, she wasn't."
**Why it fails:** Like 2.1, this simulates insight by discarding one answer and proposing another. Overused, it becomes a verbal trick: the prose *sounds* penetrating without actually penetrating anything. The reader begins to distrust every "not because/but because" as a formula rather than a discovery.

---

### 2.3 The Omniscient Stage Direction ("Somewhere, X Was Happening")
**Description:** A sentence — often paragraph-opening — that zooms out to an unnamed, thematically rhyming event occurring elsewhere, as if to situate the scene in a wider cosmos.
**Bad example:** "Somewhere across the city, a child was crying. Here, in this apartment, she was crying too." / "Somewhere, the war was still going on. In the kitchen, water was boiling."
**Why it fails:** The device gestures at thematic resonance without building it. It is fundamentally a stage direction — telling the reader how to feel — rather than a scene that generates feeling organically. The vagueness of "somewhere" is a red flag: the writer cannot locate the parallel event, so they leave it floating.

---

### 2.4 "And That Was the Thing About X" (Essayistic Intrusion)
**Description:** A sentence that pivots from scene to essay, framing a character or situation as a topic for thematic analysis mid-narrative.
**Bad example:** "And that was the thing about grief: it never arrived on schedule." / "That was the thing about Marcus — he always knew what you needed before you did."
**Why it fails:** The construction imports a TED-talk or self-help tone into fiction. It signals that the writer doesn't trust the scene to communicate its own meaning. AI uses it as a bridging device when transitions are too hard to construct organically.

---

### 2.5 The Portentous Closing Aphorism
**Description:** Ending a paragraph or scene with a single-sentence, present-tense generalization that "seals" the emotional meaning of what just happened.
**Bad example:** "She walked out of the hospital. [para break] Some doors, once closed, never opened again." / "He folded the letter and put it away. [para break] Memory was a house you could never quite leave."
**Why it fails:** The aphorism substitutes for earned resonance. Instead of leaving the reader inside the experience, the writer gestures at the Meaning of the Experience. This is fundamentally a trust problem: the writer does not trust the image or event to carry its own weight.

---

### 2.6 The Smile That Didn't Reach the Eyes (Exhausted Telling-Cliché)
**Description:** The use of well-worn behavioral shorthand — physical gestures that have been narratively coded for so long they carry no information — as if they were fresh observations.
**Bad example:** "He smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes." / "She laughed, but there was no warmth in it." / "His voice was calm, but his hands gave him away."
**Why it fails:** These phrases have been repeated so many times in commercial fiction, screenwriting manuals, and writing-advice forums that they have become empty tokens. They perform the work of characterization without doing any. The reader fills in a generic template rather than seeing a specific person.

---

### 2.7 Filtering Verbs (The Perception Scrim)
**Description:** Interposing a character's awareness — "she felt," "he noticed," "I realized," "she saw that," "I watched as" — between the reader and the experience being rendered.
**Bad example:** "She noticed that the room was darker than usual. She felt a chill settle over her. I realized that something was wrong."
**Why it fails:** Filtering verbs create narrative distance. Rather than placing the reader in the experience, they remind us we are receiving a report from a perceiver. The unfiltered version ("The room was darker than usual. A chill settled over her.") is more immediate and trusts the POV without announcing it. AI prose over-uses filters because it is structurally reporting rather than inhabiting.

---

### 2.8 The Nested Flashback Intrusion
**Description:** Dropping a mini-flashback into the middle of present-action sentences, flagged with "She thought of…" or "He remembered when…," and then snapping back without narrative integration.
**Bad example:** "She reached for the gun. She thought of her father, how he'd taught her to shoot in the field behind their house, the smell of gunpowder in her hair. She pulled the trigger."
**Why it fails:** Isolated this way, the flashback does not deepen the scene — it interrupts it. The device is used as shorthand for "this moment has backstory weight" without doing the work of making backstory and present action genuinely interact.

---

## LEVEL 3: ABSTRACTION AND VAGUENESS

### 3.1 The Gnomic Universal (Meaning-Summary Statement)
**Description:** A present-tense generalized statement that summarizes the thematic meaning of the beat just dramatized, often placed immediately after the emotional climax.
**Bad example:** "He walked away without looking back. [beat] That was how it ended — not with fire, but with someone's back growing smaller." (Fine so far, but then:) "People always left before you were ready. That was the nature of loss."
**Why it fails:** The gnomic universal tells the reader what to conclude rather than letting the scene produce its own conclusion. It is the fictional equivalent of labeling a painting. The reader's job is not to receive meanings but to discover them; the gnomic universal forecloses discovery.

---

### 3.2 Emotion Named Instead of Shown (The Flat Affect Report)
**Description:** Directly naming an emotion — sad, angry, anxious, devastated, overwhelmed — rather than rendering the physical, behavioral, or cognitive signs from which that emotion can be inferred.
**Bad example:** "She was devastated." / "He felt an overwhelming sense of loneliness." / "She was angry, angrier than she'd been in years."
**Why it fails:** Named emotions are inert. "She was devastated" could describe ten thousand different people in ten thousand different circumstances. The specific texture of *this* character's devastation is what makes it real. AI defaults to naming because naming is computationally easier than embodying.

---

### 3.3 "Something Shifted Between Them" (The Vague Relational Change)
**Description:** Signaling a change in interpersonal dynamic with placeholder language rather than a specific action, exchange, or image that enacts the shift.
**Bad example:** "Something shifted between them after that." / "Something in his expression changed, and she knew nothing would be the same." / "She couldn't name what passed between them in that moment."
**Why it fails:** These sentences promise dramatic content they refuse to supply. They are IOUs. The writer senses a shift is needed but cannot locate its texture, so they describe the shift's category rather than its substance.

---

### 3.4 Vague Body-Cued Emotion (Anatomical Shorthand)
**Description:** Assigning emotion to an anatomical location with a vague, non-specific physical metaphor — as though the body's involvement is what makes the emotion concrete, when in fact the metaphor is empty.
**Bad example:** "A knot tightened in his chest." / "She felt something hollow open up behind her sternum." / "He carried the weight of it in his shoulders."
**Why it fails:** These phrases *gesture* at embodiment without achieving it. They are not genuine physiological observations — they are received metaphors. Readers have read them so many times they have become another class of named emotion, only wearing anatomical costume.

---

### 3.5 "The Weight of It All" (Weight/Burden Inflation)
**Description:** Using "weight" or "burden" as a metaphor for unspecified emotional or existential pressure, often without connecting the weight to any specific event or object.
**Bad example:** "She felt the weight of everything pressing down on her." / "He didn't know how much longer he could carry the weight of what he knew." / "The weight of years settled into her bones."
**Why it fails:** Weight is a default metaphor for gravity, consequence, and history — vague enough to apply to anything, specific enough to suggest depth. AI prose reaches for it whenever a scene needs to feel meaningful. Overuse drains it completely.

---

## LEVEL 4: IMAGERY

### 4.1 Stacked Non-Cohering Metaphors
**Description:** Multiple metaphors applied to the same subject in rapid succession, each drawn from a different conceptual domain, so that the overall image is incoherent.
**Bad example:** "The city was a wound that never healed, a symphony of broken promises, a maze without an exit, a fire slowly going out."
**Why it fails:** Each metaphor implies a different ontology: the city as body, as music, as architecture, as combustion. Stacked this way, they cancel each other. The reader cannot form any image because each new metaphor displaces the last. AI generates metaphors freely but rarely tests whether they cohere.

---

### 4.2 The Sensory Tricolon (Obligatory Three-Sense Description)
**Description:** Obligatorily noting three sensory details — usually sight, sound, smell or touch — as a formulaic method of establishing scene, regardless of which senses are actually relevant.
**Bad example:** "The bar was dark and smoky, the sound of jazz drifting from somewhere unseen, the smell of spilled beer sharp beneath it all."
**Why it fails:** Sensory grounding is a real virtue; formula is not. When every scene begins with three senses checked off in sequence, the description feels like a writing-workshop exercise. The senses that matter most to a specific character in a specific moment get lost in the obligation to be thorough.

---

### 4.3 Over-Poetic Abstractions (The Precious Image)
**Description:** Reaching for lush, literary-sounding language at moments that do not warrant it, producing images that call attention to themselves rather than illuminating the subject.
**Bad example:** "She closed the door, and the silence was a cathedral she had built from her own loneliness." / "The coffee cooled, an elegy in a ceramic cup."
**Why it fails:** Precious images flatten emotional register — everything gets the same heightened treatment, so nothing is actually heightened. They also reveal a writer performing "literary prose" rather than being in the scene.

---

### 4.4 The Repurposed Nature Pathetic Fallacy
**Description:** Deploying weather, seasons, or natural phenomena as transparent emotional mirrors without irony, distance, or complication.
**Bad example:** "As she read the letter, the sky outside clouded over, a storm gathering on the horizon." / "He left. The first leaves of autumn fell."
**Why it fails:** Pathetic fallacy can be powerful when handled with awareness — used reflexively, it is lazy symbolism. The equivalence between inner and outer state removes all ambiguity. It also signals a writer who doesn't trust the scene's intrinsic emotional texture.

---

### 4.5 The Dead Metaphor Resurrection
**Description:** Using a dead or near-dead metaphor — one so common it no longer operates as a metaphor — with fresh poetic investment, as though it retains its original force.
**Bad example:** "Time was a thief, stealing her youth one morning at a time." / "Her heart was a compass that always pointed home." / "Life was a journey, and she had been walking alone."
**Why it fails:** Dead metaphors have been processed into idiom. Treating "time is a thief" as if it is a discovery embarrasses the prose. AI frequently resurrects dead metaphors because they are highly represented in training data and "sound like" elevated writing.

---

## LEVEL 5: DIALOGUE

### 5.1 Characters Stating Subtext Plainly
**Description:** Characters articulate the precise emotional or thematic subtext of a scene in direct speech, eliminating the indirection that makes dialogue alive.
**Bad example:** "'You never really trusted me,' she said. 'That's what this has always been about.' / 'I think we're both using this situation to avoid what we're really feeling.'"
**Why it fails:** Real people rarely speak their subtext; that is what makes drama possible. When characters name the subtext in dialogue, the scene collapses into therapy-speak or self-help summary. AI tends toward this because subtext requires managing two levels simultaneously — what is said and what is meant — and stating it plainly solves the problem at the cost of verisimilitude.

---

### 5.2 Every Character Equally Articulate
**Description:** All characters — regardless of age, class, education, emotional state, or culture — speak in the same syntactically complete, rhetorically polished prose.
**Bad example:** Child: "I don't understand why you're making me feel invisible." Farmhand: "Sometimes the things we avoid are the very things that define us."
**Why it fails:** Voice differentiation is one of the hardest and most essential crafts in dialogue. When everyone sounds like the narrator, the fictional world collapses into a single register. AI defaults to its own diction for all characters because constructing genuine idiolects requires something more than pattern-matching.

---

### 5.3 Adverbial and Elaborate Dialogue Tags
**Description:** Using dialogue tags beyond "said" and "asked" to do character work the dialogue itself should carry — or loading "said" with adverbs that tell the reader how to interpret the line.
**Bad example:** "'I suppose I always knew,' she admitted softly." / "'You can't mean that,' he said, his voice barely above a whisper, his hands trembling.'" / "'Fine,' she snapped bitterly."
**Why it fails:** Good dialogue stands without being told how to read it. Adverbial tags are a sign the dialogue has failed: the tone isn't in the words, so the writer tapes a label to the outside. AI uses them because it evaluates the emotional valence of a line and then annotates it — rather than embedding the valence in the diction.

---

### 5.4 The On-the-Nose Thematic Speech
**Description:** A character delivers a speech whose sole function is to articulate the novel's theme explicitly, with no dramatic motive beyond expressing that theme.
**Bad example:** "'The thing about this city,' he said, leaning back, 'is that it asks you to forget who you were. And eventually, you comply. And then you spend the rest of your life wondering if you ever really existed.'"
**Why it fails:** Theme must be arrived at by the reader through experience, not delivered by a character as manifesto. On-the-nose speeches suggest the writer was trying to make sure the reader got the point — a failure of trust and craft simultaneously.

---

### 5.5 Dialogue as Information Delivery (The "As You Know, Bob" Problem)
**Description:** Characters telling each other things they both already know for the reader's benefit, with no dramatic or social purpose internal to the scene.
**Bad example:** "'You know,' Marcus said, 'ever since your father left us at the refugee camp when you were six, you've had trouble trusting men.'"
**Why it fails:** Real speech conveys new information; people do not summarize shared history to each other in ordinary conversation. When dialogue exists only to transmit exposition, the fictional world becomes a delivery mechanism for the author's plotting needs.

---

## LEVEL 6: PARAGRAPH AND SCENE STRUCTURE

### 6.1 Scene Closing That Restates the Takeaway
**Description:** Ending a scene with an explicit statement of what the scene meant, often in the POV character's direct reflection, immediately after the scene has already shown it.
**Bad example:** "[Scene dramatizes two characters failing to communicate.] She watched him drive away. [para break] They had never really spoken to each other. Not once. Not about anything that mattered."
**Why it fails:** If the scene is well-constructed, the reader already knows what happened between these people. Spelling it out is redundant at best, condescending at worst. It is the prose equivalent of explaining a joke.

---

### 6.2 Template Chapter Openings
**Description:** Beginning chapters with the same structural formula — usually: establish time/weather, zoom to character, hint at mood — regardless of what each chapter actually requires.
**Bad example:** "The morning came gray and cold, the kind of morning that made promises it couldn't keep. Elena stood at her window, watching the city wake up below her, thinking about what the day ahead would bring."
**Why it fails:** When chapter openings follow a template, the reader's experience at the start of each chapter is generic, not specific. The chapter opening should do exactly what *this* chapter needs — sometimes that means throwing the reader into the middle of action; sometimes into silence. One-size openings flatten narrative architecture.

---

### 6.3 Over-Explaining the Already-Inferred
**Description:** Following an image, action, or exchange with an explanation of what it meant, as if the prose cannot trust its own show-don't-tell work.
**Bad example:** "He turned away from her. [Beat.] It was his way of ending conversations he didn't want to have — she'd learned that years ago. It was his way of saying: this is over."
**Why it fails:** Every additional sentence of explanation erodes the power of the original image. The reader is an intelligent adult; they inferred the gesture's meaning. Explaining it signals a writer who is anxious about being misunderstood, or an AI that is attempting comprehensiveness rather than art.

---

### 6.4 Telling Then Showing (Redundant Double-Beat)
**Description:** Stating an emotion or dynamic directly, then illustrating it with a scene — the reverse of the preferred sequence, and doubly wasteful when the scene is strong enough to stand alone.
**Bad example:** "She was a woman who controlled everything around her. [Then scene of her rearranging objects, correcting the waiter, straightening her napkin.]"
**Why it fails:** The correct order, when both are needed, is to show first and let the reader conclude. But often the telling is entirely unnecessary because the scene that follows is self-evidently clear. AI errs toward over-explanation because it structures from summary to example, rather than from scene to meaning.

---

### 6.5 The Mid-Chapter Mood Reset (Emotional False-Start)
**Description:** A scene builds emotional intensity, then the prose deflates it with a transitional paragraph of neutral description before attempting to rebuild — essentially erasing the tension rather than sustaining it.
**Bad example:** "[Intense argument between characters.] She slammed the door. [Para break, now describing the weather, the traffic, what she made for dinner.] Two days later, she still hadn't called."
**Why it fails:** Tension management is a structural skill. The neutral buffer feels like a draft artifact — the writer pausing to gather themselves — left in the final text. Sustained scene-level pressure requires that transitions *carry* tension rather than disperse it.

---

### 6.6 The False Chapter Cliffhanger
**Description:** Ending a chapter on an artificially heightened note — usually a short, italicized fragment or a vague threat — when the actual material does not support that level of tension.
**Bad example:** "[Quiet domestic scene.] She set down her coffee. Outside, a car door slammed. [Chapter ends.] *She didn't know yet what that sound meant.*"
**Why it fails:** Manufactured suspense trains readers to distrust chapter endings. The cliffhanger promises more than the narrative delivers. AI applies chapter-ending emphasis without assessing whether the content warrants it.

---

## LEVEL 7: LEXICAL

### 7.1 The AI Register (Elevated Vocabulary Cluster)
**Description:** The prose reaches for a specific cluster of words associated with depth, richness, and complexity that AI models have learned to over-associate with literary quality.
**Common offenders:** delve, tapestry, testament, palpable, symphony, mosaic, intricate, profound, resonate, nuanced, multifaceted, labyrinthine, ethereal, poignant, visceral (used as a generalized intensifier), undeniable, unwavering, beacon, testament, navigate (used metaphorically), in the realm of, at the heart of, stands as a.
**Bad example:** "Her grief was a palpable tapestry of loss, a testament to the intricate web of connections that had once sustained her — now a labyrinthine path through which she must navigate alone."
**Why it fails:** These words have been so reliably associated with quality writing in training data that they emerge as default intensifiers, drained of precise meaning. They signal "this is elevated prose" without doing the work of elevation.

---

### 7.2 Over-Hedging in Narrative Description
**Description:** Qualifying observations in the narration with "seemed," "appeared," "perhaps," "almost," "as if," and similar hedges when the POV does not require epistemic uncertainty.
**Bad example:** "The room seemed larger than she remembered. The walls appeared to close in around her, or perhaps it was just the exhaustion. He almost smiled."
**Why it fails:** Hedging in narration signals a writer (or AI) unwilling to commit to a fictional fact. In close third or first person, "the room was larger" is entirely valid — it is the character's experience. Compulsive hedging creates a ghostly, uncertain fictional world where nothing has weight because nothing is definitively asserted.

---

### 7.3 Filler-Transition Phrases
**Description:** Using transitional phrases that convey no information, carry no rhythm, and exist only to bridge two sentences the writer could not otherwise connect.
**Bad example:** "With that in mind, she turned to face him." / "It was then that she realized the truth." / "As if on cue, the phone rang." / "In the silence that followed, no one spoke."
**Why it fails:** "In the silence that followed, no one spoke" is a near-perfect example — silence is the absence of sound, so "no one spoke" is redundant. These fillers are verbal throat-clearing, evidence of a writer marking time rather than building forward.

---

### 7.4 Adjective-Stacking Without Discrimination
**Description:** Modifying nouns with two or three adjectives in series when one (or zero) would be more precise and forceful.
**Bad example:** "She wore a long, dark, expensive coat." / "He had a low, quiet, steady voice." / "It was a cold, empty, forgotten place."
**Why it fails:** Stacked adjectives create a diffuse impression rather than a sharp one. The brain reads them and averages them into vagueness. A single precise adjective does more work than three approximate ones.

---

### 7.5 The Intensifier Inflation ("So," "Very," "Truly," "Deeply")
**Description:** Using intensifiers to compensate for flat diction rather than finding the diction that doesn't need intensification.
**Bad example:** "She was so very tired of it all." / "He truly didn't know what to say." / "It was deeply unfair." / "She was absolutely devastated."
**Why it fails:** Intensifiers are a sign that the base word is not strong enough — but the solution is a stronger word, not an amplifier. Stacked intensifiers ("so very deeply") compound the problem. AI uses them because it is calibrating emotional register by addition rather than substitution.

---

## CROSS-CUTTING NOTES FOR REVIEWERS

**Clustering effect:** These patterns compound. When a passage opens with a participial phrase, uses a tricolon, closes with a portentous aphorism, and features the phrase "something shifted between them," each individual tic is reinforced by the others. Grade severity by density of co-occurrence, not just presence.

**The illusion of specificity:** Several patterns (vague body-cued emotion, anatomical shorthand, "something shifted") are notable because they *appear* specific — they name a physical location, a sensory category — while actually being vague. Flag these especially carefully because they fool casual readers.

**Competence masquerading as depth:** The correctio pattern (2.1), the "not because/but because" pattern (2.2), and the gnomic universal (3.1) are particularly insidious because they perform nuance without achieving it. They are the prose equivalent of a confident wrong answer.

**AI-specific density:** Patterns most over-represented in AI-generated prose relative to human prose: 2.1, 2.4, 2.5, 3.1, 3.2, 5.1, 5.4, 7.1, and 1.3 (tricolon). When five or more of these appear in a single scene, AI authorship or heavy AI drafting is likely.
