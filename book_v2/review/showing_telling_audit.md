# Show vs. Tell Craft Audit
### Focus: Filtering, Psychic Distance, Emotion-Naming, Redundancy, Abstraction, POV Consistency

---

## 1. FILTERING / PSYCHIC DISTANCE

**Grep counts across all 53 chapters (total lines: ~5,340):**

| Filter verb | Occurrences |
|---|---|
| "I knew" | 166 |
| "I thought" | 49 |
| "I felt" | 48 |
| "I watched" | 28 |
| "I saw" | 23 |
| "I noticed" | 10 |
| "I realized" | 2 |
| **Total** | **~360** |

That is roughly one filtering construction every 15 lines — a meaningful density for first-person retrospective, though not alarming on its own. The distribution matters more: **"I knew"** dominates at 166 hits, which is the subtler, higher-risk filter because it slides from legitimate retrospective knowledge into the habit of announcing comprehension rather than embodying it.

**High-distance moments that should be closer:**

Ch. 9, the Macer meeting — the scene earns its tension but then explains itself:
> "I understood, slowly, with the back of my neck, that the room had been arranged so that the room could hear this."

The physical "back of my neck" is good, but "I understood… that" is the filter. The scene has *already shown* the clerks stop scratching, the numbers-man go quiet. The understanding was given; the filter restates it as something Daniel registers. Drop the filter verb entirely and the image does its own work.

Ch. 9:
> "I understood the sentence was a leash with two ends and I was holding one of them now whether I had asked for it or not."

This is strong writing trapped inside a filter. "I understood the sentence was a leash" — the metaphor is earning its place, but "I understood" puts glass between the reader and the recognition. The leash image on its own would land harder.

Ch. 33, the spy's visit:
> "I was at the back of the press room and saw the shape of him through the door and felt my skin go before I knew why."

"Felt my skin go" is excellent — unfiltered and embodied. But then "before I knew why" is the narrator's retrospective management of the scene, stepping back to frame what the moment felt like instead of trusting the reader to feel it with him.

Ch. 50:
> "I knew even then, the part of me that always knows, that the lightness is not staying up, the lightness is only the lift…"

This is the death chapter and the distance is earned — the narrator has the perspective of dying to frame what he felt. But "the part of me that always knows" is a bit of a verbal flourish that the prose is otherwise too clean for.

**Contrast: well-managed distance.** Ch. 33 opens with a genuinely brilliant piece of filtered observation converted into a concrete image:
> "The first I heard, I did not hear it. I felt the city stop talking."

This is filter language used masterfully. The paradox ("did not hear it / felt") and the synesthetic image turn a high-distance observation into a close physical sensation. It is the model for how the book handles this when it is working.

**Ch. 1 uses "I want to be careful here" and "I want to be honest about" at two key structural moments.** These retrospective interruptions are genuine — they flag the narrator catching himself about to falsify memory — but they also put the narrator between the reader and the Colosseum reveal at a moment that should be purely cinematic. The second one in particular ("I want to be honest about what happened in my head because for a while nothing happened") delays the reveal unnecessarily when the prose that follows is strong enough to carry it.

## 2. EMOTION NAMED VS. DRAMATIZED

**Worst tells:**

Ch. 14:
> "What I felt was tired in a way that sleep did not touch, and a low steady relief that I was ashamed of…"

"Tired in a way that sleep did not touch" is strong — it refuses the cliché of simple exhaustion. But "a low steady relief that I was ashamed of" names both the emotion and the narrator's moral judgment of it simultaneously. The shame is announced rather than dramatized. The paragraph that follows (about Sabinus going home in a different box) earns the shame via image, which means the announcement was unnecessary.

Ch. 14, the grief naming:
> "That night I had burned a corner of cloth at the river and that was grief, the kind that wants to destroy a thing with its hands."

This is a borderline case. "That was grief" names, but "the kind that wants to destroy a thing with its hands" earns it back with specificity. The name is used as a launching pad for a precise image, not as a substitute for one. It is among the book's better emotion-handlings.

**Flat emotion reports across chapters:**

Ch. 24:
> "I think about that more than I should."

Following the child's ladder-drawing scene — one of the book's best moments — this final sentence is pure tell. The scene has already communicated his feeling. The line adds nothing and slightly deflates what the image had built.

Ch. 45:
> "I had wanted to be useful and forgotten. I had got the useful."

This sentence pair does thesis-statement work on a theme the whole book has been dramatizing for chapters. It is honest writing but it is also the narrator refusing to trust the reader to have followed the argument.

**Where emotion is dramatized well:**

Ch. 14, the Celer scene at the horse-lines is the book's strongest emotional rendering:
> "He was a man who would rather be flogged than caught caring out loud."

Daniel never says what he feels about Celer's sacrifice. He renders the dynamic via behavioral observation, names Celer's character rather than his own response, and then:
> "'Thank you,' I said, which was the wrong size, which was always going to be the wrong size…"

The inadequacy of the words is the emotion. This is masterful — zero filter verbs, zero named emotion, fully dramatized grief and gratitude through dialogue failure.

Ch. 50, Tyche's hands:
> "Tyche's thumb is moving on the back of my hand the way you smooth a leaf flat before you write on it."

No emotion named. The simile does all the work. This is the book at its closest and most effective.

Ch. 9, the Tyche introduction:
> "She was small, fourteen maybe, in a plain undyed tunic, her hair cut off short and uneven the way you cut a slave girl's hair so it is one less thing to keep, and she moved the way the slave behind the door had moved, quiet, ready, present before you needed her and gone before you could thank her, except that her eyes were not quiet at all."

The "except" clause lands because everything before it has been concrete. Daniel never says he is disturbed by the slave system; he describes haircuts and eyes. His discomfort is entirely in the gap between "quiet" and "not quiet at all."

## 3. TELL-THEN-SHOW / SHOW-THEN-TELL REDUNDANCY

**Most significant instances:**

Ch. 24, the contest scene:
> "I had drawn that ladder myself, in the dark, in a script no one in that room could read, years before that girl was old enough to hold a stylus."

This is emotionally resonant and true to the scene. Then:
> "I think about that more than I should."

The added sentence tells us the scene mattered after the scene has already communicated it. Cut to "I gave her the prize" and the resonance would be stronger.

Ch. 33, Macer sends Daniel out through the tanning yard:
> "He had warned me. That was the thing I carried down the hill, turning it the way I turned the buckle. He had looked at the smell of me and chosen not to denounce, only to warn, and the difference between those two things was the whole of the bond we had, worn to a wire, and the wire had held. It was not nothing. It only felt like nothing, in the lane, with the door shut."

This paragraph follows a scene that has already dramatized all of it — Macer's speech, the back-door exit, the tanning smell. The reflection is elegantly written but largely restates what the scene showed. "It was not nothing. It only felt like nothing" is the book's correctio habit (catalog 2.1) — refined and self-aware enough to avoid being mere formula, but present.

Ch. 14, the crating scene is the best structural example of telling *before* showing being made to work:
> "I want to be precise about this, because it mattered, and because I did it cold and on purpose…"

This flags the significance of what follows before delivering it. Normally this is a trust failure. Here it works because the signal prepares a reader for deliberate action in a retrospective narrator — it distinguishes this crating from grief-action, which matters for the subsequent scene with Naso. It is an unusual case where tell-first is structurally justified.

**Recurring minor pattern:** Multiple scenes end with Daniel's retrospective framing of what the scene meant. Ch. 24: "That is what a rung looks like, I think, when you are standing on the one below it." Ch. 33: "It was not nothing. It only felt like nothing, in the lane, with the door shut." Ch. 45: "A thing you are sure of has edges. You can set your hand on it. This had no edges." These aphoristic closers (catalog 2.5) are usually well-crafted, but they occur often enough to become structural tics — the reader begins anticipating the paragraph-closing philosophical sentence.

## 4. ABSTRACTION OVER CONCRETION / SUMMARY VS. SCENE

**Balance:** This novel has exceptional scene-to-summary ratio overall. The long chapters (9, 14, 33) are almost all rendered scene. The problem is not too much summary but that the summary passages the book *does* use are often doing thematic heavy lifting that should be carried by scene.

**Notable summary passage, Ch. 14:**
> "I thought about the flux a great deal on that road. I had filed it away going north, a thing I was powerless against, and powerless I still was, but somewhere between the Loaf and the Alps the filing-away curdled into something with a harder edge."

This summarizes the road south (a potential rich scene) into a few sentences in order to deliver Daniel's moral pivot. The pivot is important. But the summary discards the journey entirely. A single rendered scene on that road — one specific man refusing the boiled water, one specific death — would make the pivot feel like it cost something. Instead the philosophical shift is reported rather than dramatized.

**Ch. 45's most consequential summary:**
> "I asked the empty bench whether a man who carries water into a camp so the men do not die of the flux is answerable for what the men he kept alive then do with their hands."

This is the moral core of the novel's most serious chapter, argued in summary form against an empty bench. It is moving precisely because Heras is gone. But the argument itself — the hardest philosophical moment in the book — is given in compressed summary. The reader gets the conclusion of the wrestling, not the wrestling. This is the clearest case in the sample where a rendered scene (Daniel at his own bench, working through the logic with a physical object in his hands, the way he works through everything) would carry more weight than the compressed retrospective account.

**Where abstraction works:** Ch. 50's "I had a song once" passage is the best example of summary-as-close-interiority:
> "I went looking for one, the way you go looking through a chest you have not opened, and found three words of it and a shape where the rest had been…"

The summary here is the emotion — the memory's structure enacts the loss. This is a case where rendering the song in full (if Daniel could) would destroy the effect. The summary is the scene.

## 5. POV DEPTH CONSISTENCY

**Retrospective frame:** The novel is explicitly retrospective — Daniel is old, writing this. That frame licenses far more telling than a present-tense first person. It licenses the philosophical interludes, the "I want to be precise," the "that was Heras" explanatory asides. The frame is established early and maintained well.

**Moments of frame slippage:**

Ch. 14:
> "He did not ask what happened. That was Heras. A lesser friend would have asked, would have wanted the story, would have offered the thing people offer, that it was not my fault, that I had warned them, that the army overruled me, all of it true and all of it useless. Heras knew it was useless."

"All of it true and all of it useless" is retrospective wisdom that rings true. But "a lesser friend would have asked" — Daniel is now performing the thought process of hypothetical other people. The retrospective frame permits knowing what happened; it doesn't quite permit knowing what Heras compared to all other possible friends in this abstract way. It reads like the narrator reaching for a general truth and coming slightly untethered from the scene.

Ch. 33, the spy scene:
> "whether he was the colleges' or the prefect's or Crispus's or some little delator's own, a man who sniffed at doors on his own account hoping to sell what he found to whoever would buy that season, which was the worst kind, because he was hungry and had no master to be reasoned with."

This passage is in close retrospective: Daniel never learned whose the man was. The parenthetical ("the worst kind, because...") is present-Daniel's analysis applied to past-Daniel's fear. It is technically sound for retrospective — he now knows the type, even if not the specific man — but the paragraph slides slightly into omniscient framing of the spy's motivations ("a man who sniffed at doors on his own account"). This is a minor inconsistency that most readers will not feel.

**No cases of genuine POV violation** — Daniel never describes scenes he could not have witnessed or knows things unknowable to him in ways that break the frame. The boundary-case passages above are within the latitude of elderly retrospective narration.

**Best POV management:** Ch. 9, the Tyche introduction, maintains strict limits: Daniel sees her eyes, her haircut, her movement pattern. He does not enter her interiority. The restraint here — sustained across every scene with Tyche — is one of the novel's finest POV achievements.

## 6. VERDICT

**Show-vs-tell is a STRENGTH, not a weakness, in this novel.** The retrospective frame is used with discipline rather than as excuse. The filtering frequency (~360 filter constructions across 53 chapters, roughly 7 per chapter) is higher than a fully rendered present-tense novel would sustain, but the frame justifies it, and the quality of what appears after the filters is consistently high.

The genuine weaknesses are different from the usual AI failure modes. The novel does not name emotions instead of embodying them as its default (catalog 3.2). It does not use "something shifted between them" (catalog 3.3). It does not deploy the portentous closing aphorism (2.5) mechanically. Its show-vs-tell problems are subtler: a reliance on retrospective interpretation after scenes that have already carried their own weight, and a tendency to deliver the book's hardest philosophical moments in summary rather than in rendered scene.

**The retrospective frame earns the telling. The problem is that the frame is sometimes used to deliver analysis of scenes the scenes already completed — not instead of rendering, but in addition to it.**

## 3 MOST USEFUL FIXES

**Fix 1: Delete the scene-closing restatement paragraphs.**
After a strong scene (the child's ladder in ch. 24, the Macer back-door exit in ch. 33, the Celer horse-line conversation in ch. 14), the prose consistently adds one paragraph of retrospective framing that restates what the scene dramatized. Most of these paragraphs should simply be cut. The scene at the horse-lines already earns "the not-saying of it was the realest thing that passed between us" — the following paragraph about getting the size of it wrong is surplus. Where the closing paragraph contains a new idea not present in the scene, it earns its place; where it restates, cut it.

**Fix 2: Replace at least two summary philosophical passages with a single rendered beat.**
The road south in ch. 14 (Daniel's moral pivot about building the wrong thing) and the bench argument in ch. 45 (Daniel's argument about the figures and the captive column) are the book's two most important ethical moments — and both are given in summary. One rendered scene each — a physical object in hand, a specific exchange, a moment of realization caught in action rather than retrospection — would make these pivots feel earned rather than announced.

**Fix 3: Cut "I knew" by half, especially when it introduces an inference the scene already makes visible.**
Of the 166 "I knew" instances, roughly half are retrospective shorthand for inferences the reader has already drawn. "I knew exactly enough [to be afraid]" follows a sentence that has already shown what enough looked like. "I knew the shape of him by then" before reporting a question Daniel answers before it's asked — the reader knows the shape too, by that point. The filter is being used not to convey information but to signal Daniel's awareness of what he understood. Removing the signal forces the scene to carry the weight, which it almost always can.
