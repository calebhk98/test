# Cold Reader Reactions: Chapters 1–3

## 1. What do you think this book is about, in one sentence?

A teenage retail worker named Daniel gets yanked back two thousand years to ancient Rome with nothing but his phone, his math brain, and the wrong instincts, and has to figure out how to survive — and how much to reveal — before his battery runs out.

## 2. What do you expect to happen in the next ten chapters?

The "foreigner who knows bodies and tongues" who gets sent for at the end of Chapter 3 is obviously going to be the key relationship — someone Daniel can actually talk to, or at least communicate with better than mime. I expect the next several chapters to be about Daniel learning Latin (or Greek, or whatever they're speaking) through this person, probably fast enough to feel slightly too convenient but necessary for the story to function.

I expect the map to cause real problems. The man at the table was already doing sums. Someone higher up the chain is going to get a copy of Daniel's world map and want to know who made it and why he knows what he knows. That pulls Daniel further up the food chain than he wants to go — toward people with more power and more to lose.

The phone hits zero percent, probably at a bad moment. Daniel has been measuring everything in battery life; the book is setting up that loss as the real floor-falling-out moment, worse than the cell or the ropes, because it ends his last private link to home.

I think Daniel gets pulled toward Rome proper — toward the city he could see from the hill — either as a resource someone wants to use or as a prisoner someone wants to examine. The Colosseum being whole is the image the book put in front of me to stay, and I don't think that was just for Chapter 1's payoff. We're going there.

I do not expect him to find a way home in ten chapters. The whole setup is too invested in "how do you survive and who do you become" for this to resolve quickly.

## 3. Was there any moment in these three chapters where you considered stopping? Why?

Honestly, no — not completely. But there was one stretch where my attention skipped: the long paragraph in Chapter 2 where Daniel is in the cell writing in Notes. The list of things he half-remembers — pi to six digits, the quadratic formula, "can't remember" for the boiling-point rule — goes on a beat or two past where it earns its length. I understand what it's doing: it's showing the horror of realizing how shallow his knowledge is, the gap between "educated person in 2024" and "actually useful in 98 AD." That's a good idea. But the execution lists a few too many items, and by the time we get to GUNPOWDER with the missing third ingredient (saltpeter — I know it, he doesn't, which is a nice effect), I was aware I was being held in the cell with him rather than riveted there. Maybe two items cut from that list and it lands harder.

Nothing in Chapters 1 or 3 lost me. Chapter 1 in particular I read without looking up.

## 4. What confused you, if anything?

Two things.

First: the language. The people in this settlement are described as speaking fast, full of hard k-sounds, nothing Daniel can parse. Late in Chapter 2, Daniel decides he's in "Rome 98 AD" based on the Colosseum math, and in Chapter 3 the man at the table is described as having a "dead language I'd never even learned, except it wasn't dead yet." That's almost certainly Latin. Fine. But the text never says Latin. I spent a few pages unsure whether I was supposed to infer Latin or whether the book was deliberately withholding the language as part of some later reveal. If it's just Latin, name it. The withholding creates a small but unnecessary friction.

Second: the optio. I had to sit with the word before I accepted it. He's introduced in Chapter 2 as having a "strip of something dark, leather or braid, on his shoulder" that Daniel reads as rank — and Daniel calls him "the optio" from that point forward as if he looked up the word somewhere. He didn't. The phone is in airplane mode and Daniel has been in a cell. I'm willing to accept that Daniel is reconstructing this account after the fact and is using the correct terminology in retrospect, but the text doesn't signal that the narration is retrospective until Daniel uses the phrase "I know now" or similar, and in these three chapters it only does that once (the Chapter 1 note about lying to people who asked about his first morning). The optio thing is a small seam in the point of view that pulled me out for a second.

Neither of these is a reason to stop. They're the kind of thing you flag on a second read.
