## Verdict: CONSISTENT

## Evidence (chronological, ch + quotes)

**Ch12 — First meeting at the Danube bridge (first war, ~99 AD)**
Apollodorus is introduced immediately as proud, direct, and technically precise. He grasps Daniel's hot-air principle in two sentences before Daniel speaks ("Hot air... it rises. Like a cup pushed mouth-down into water wants to rise.") He neither praises nor condescends — he files the balloon as "a clever toy that does one thing, and the one thing is worth doing, which is more than most clever toys." When Daniel pushes back on the bridge not moving, Apollodorus corrects him with a lecture on river pressure that is both competitive and genuine. His now-canonical maxim arrives organically from the relationship, not as a set piece: "A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day. The job is the thing you can do on a bad one." Daniel's internal note — "He was proud the way a wall is plumb, all the way down, no give in it" — establishes the governing metaphor for all subsequent appearances. His pride here is earned, his contempt surgical, and he is never gratuitously cruel. He rejects Daniel's counterweight suggestion precisely because it would fail in his specific conditions — he is right, and both men know it.

**Ch13 — The Loaf, before Sabinus flies (~99 AD)**
Apollodorus comes to inspect the balloon before the army bets on it. His verdict is the same ("A clever toy that does one thing. I told you that at the bridge. The one thing is not this."), and he adds a practical engineer's reading of the fire-risk. He acknowledges the shared situation: "for a moment there was something in it that wasn't rivalry, that was just one man who builds things looking at another man who builds things across a thing that was going to go wrong." His advice is blunt and correct ("Say so to the man who can stop it, if such a man exists in this camp, which I doubt"), but he does not linger. He returns to his bridge — stone and stubbornness, no greased linen — and Daniel feels envy and hatred in the same breath. Voice: identical to ch12. Pride, accuracy, no softening.

**Ch14 — Brief mention (reflection, post-war packing)**
Daniel imagines "Apollodorus would have called it sentiment" when he folds the dead man's flight patterns under the crate lid. Consistent with the established voice — he is used here as an internalized standard, a presence that implies judgment even in absence.

**Ch17 — Brief mention (demonstration prep)**
"A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day, Apollodorus had given me that and I'd paid for it in a man's life." Confirms the ch12 maxim has become part of Daniel's permanent mental furniture. No inconsistency.

**Ch18 — Second war, Drobeta bridge (~105 AD)**
Apollodorus has completed the stone bridge across the Danube — twenty piers. His pride is the same but now has even more to stand on. He receives Daniel's genuine compliment ("It's a good bridge") without false modesty: he simply looks at it and says "It will outlast the province." This is not boasting — he says it "flat, a fact, the cold long view." He acknowledges Daniel's freedom curtly ("They freed you... The emperor collects useful Greeks and useful barbarians the way other men collect bronzes.") He has not changed toward Daniel: the balloon is still "the one thing," his professional respect is still real but never warm. At the siege of Sarmizegetusa, he visits the fouled water-cut professionally ("Neat"), then delivers a moral observation without softening it: "The same hand that keeps your men off the flux fouls theirs. You didn't think it would stay on the one side." His "don't make the face" is simultaneously the cruelest and most honest thing said to Daniel in the war. Voice: fully consistent. Older, harder, more confirmed.

**Ch19 — Brief mention (Macer's speech about the prize contest)**
Macer compares Daniel's contest to Apollodorus's bridge-building glory. Apollodorus appears only as a reference point for imperial recognition — a correct contextual use of his reputation. No voice inconsistency.

**Ch23 — Brief mention (quarry railway demonstration)**
"I wanted this to be a true thing on a true day" — Daniel invokes the ch12 maxim directly and attributes it by memory. Consistent with how the character has been embedded as a professional conscience.

**Ch26 — The Forum dedication (~113 AD)**
This is the most sustained appearance since ch18 and the pivotal scene of escalating pride. Apollodorus has not changed — "in the way the truly proud do not, which is to say the world had gone on arranging itself around him and confirmed him in everything." The Forum is behind him as something he made, worn lightly. His greeting is the same ("Your bag is up there... a clever toy that does one thing") — eleven years on, he says it "without malice and without memory of having said it, because to Apollodorus my balloon had always been the one true sentence and he had no reason to vary it." He challenges Daniel on the clock, and Daniel hands him the failure honestly, and Apollodorus responds not with cruelty but with a kind of cold respect: "Then you've reached the bottom of the well you brought with you... Build the things you can hold in your hands." This is in character — rigorous, not unkind, the truth delivered without decoration.

The crucial Hadrian scene: Apollodorus sees the bearded Spaniard with the draughtsman's roll and his contempt is "total and serene, the contempt of a man who has never once been wrong about water." His dismissal ("He draws gourds. Domes like cut melons...") is delivered "low, not low enough" — we are told he knew it was audible and did not mind. When Daniel warns him not to have said it loud, Apollodorus answers with his governing principle: "I have been saying true things loud enough to be heard for sixty years, and it has built me everything you see." The pride here is not new — it is the same pride from ch12, only now it has been confirmed by sixty years of being right, which makes it both more magnificent and more lethal. He has not become prouder; the world has simply moved the stakes around him without his noticing.

**Ch30 — Brief mention (the succession, Trajan's death)**
Daniel recalls the Forum scene and Apollodorus's voice ("Go and paint his gourds") as the moment he ought to have recognized Hadrian. The pride is remembered accurately and functions as retrospective dramatic irony — the reader knows Apollodorus is already doomed.

**Ch35 — Apollodorus offstage, Daniel's Hadrian meeting (~117 AD)**
Apollodorus is mentioned twice. First, when Daniel finally recognizes Hadrian as the bearded man at the Forum: "I had stood twenty feet from him eight years before, at the dedication of a Forum, and watched Apollodorus spit gently into a well." The image is accurate to ch26. Second, in the coda: "Somewhere in the city Apollodorus was inspecting his drains, white-haired and certain, saying true things at full volume because true things at full volume had built him everything he could see." Daniel understands that he cannot warn him and that Apollodorus would not take warning even if offered — "A man who has been right for sixty years does not take warning from the barbarian with the bag." This is perfectly consistent with every established element of the character: the certainty, the volume, the inability to imagine the well belonging to someone else.

**Ch37 — Apollodorus Falls**
The confrontation scene is the test. Does the pride feel earned or manufactured for the plot? It holds. When Daniel arrives at the yard, Apollodorus greets him with the old teasing ("Come to watch real work"), asks when he last failed at something with his own hands (consistent with ch26's identical question — this is an established habit, not a new beat), and says the right things about walls and wells. When Daniel tells him the bearded man is the emperor, Apollodorus does not flinch: "I know what he is... A man who designs is a man who designs, and his rank is the least true thing about him." He claims to have said the gourd-insult loudly on purpose, as a public act of principle — "What I am for is to be the man who said so." Then the speech that condemns Daniel as a bender of true things: "You fold your true things smaller and smaller until there is nothing left to fold." He gives Daniel the plumb-line as a competitive parting insult. Everything here is legible as the same personality, now facing exactly the consequences his personality was always building toward. The pride has not arrived abruptly — it has been the same pride, named and shown and accumulated across six prior appearances, now arriving at its logical cost. The chapter earns the fall by showing a man who has always known what he is and has never once varied from it.

**Ch43, ch46, ch47 — Post-mortem references**
In ch43, Apollodorus's death is the benchmark for the greatest mind Daniel met ("the best mind I had met in this world since Apollodorus went into the ground"). In ch46, Daniel reflects on the loss of "the only other restless mind" and frames the peer-rivalry honestly: "we had circled each other for years as two predators circle, each knowing the other could kill him and each finding the other too interesting to do it yet." In ch47, the plumb-line maxim ("you draw the picture first and ask the stone to agree") is written into the cipher-book among Daniel's failures — Apollodorus's words preserved as the truest engineering principle Daniel encountered. All posthumous references are consistent and reinforcing.

**Ch51 — Epitaph framing (late in the book)**
The narrator's report ("Apollodorus the bridge-builder called it a clever toy that does one thing... Apollodorus was a sour man who died badly for the sin of speaking his mind to one who outranked him in everything save mind") is an outside-looking-in rendering that accurately compresses both the established voice and the established fall.

## Drift Flags

**None.** Every appearance is consistent with the governing metaphor established in ch12 ("proud the way a wall is plumb, all the way down"). The following points were assessed and found not to constitute drift:

- Ch26's "total and serene" contempt is explicitly the same contempt established in ch12 and ch13, now backed by an additional decade of being confirmed right. The volume of the insult is foreshadowed by the character's established behavior and Daniel's consistent worry about it.
- The ch37 speech ("You fold your true things smaller") is the logical extension of ch26's "A man who softens his true things to suit the listener is building on sand" — the argument has not changed, only the urgency and the cost.
- The question "When did you last fail at something with your own hands?" appears in both ch26 and ch37, which reads as intentional characterization (a tic of the proud craftsman) rather than redundancy. It works as a signature.
- The plumb-line as prop: it appears in ch18 (habit), ch26 (implicit in the context), ch35 (inspecting drains), and ch37 (the gift). Its accumulation is deliberate and earned.
- Apollodorus never warms toward Daniel across the twenty-year span. The relationship remains "not friendship" at every stage, consistently described as peer-rivalry, mutual professional respect without personal affection. This is held exactly.
