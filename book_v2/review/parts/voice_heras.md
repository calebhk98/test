# Voice Audit: Heras

## Verdict: CONSISTENT

Heras is the most consistently voiced secondary character in the book. His register — dry, learned, ironic, resistant to enthusiasm — holds from his first appearance in ch04 to his death in ch42 and beyond (references to him in ch43–53 remain faithful). His arc from puncturing-skeptic to reluctant believer in *method* (never magic) is paced across roughly thirty-five fictional years and earns every degree of softening.

---

## Evidence (chronological, ch + quotes)

**Ch04 — Introduction.** Tone established instantly. He tests Daniel with arithmetic before he speaks socially, watches the long-division blunder without cruelty: "dry as a bone, whatever it was, with a thread of something in it I would spend the next year learning to recognize, somewhere between amusement and respect held at arm's length." His face does "nothing, deliberately nothing." His verdict on the Thule claim is flat skepticism, yet he leans *in* — "A lie that good is worth taking apart." The voice is perfect from line one.

**Ch05 — The germ pitch.** The canonical skeptic scene. He listens to Daniel's invisible-creatures pitch, then dismantles it with humors, miasma, and the gods — the *best thinking on the planet in that year*. Crucially he does not sneer; he takes it as seriously as he takes Hippocrates. His warning about "witch-talk" is protective, not dismissive. He then quietly adopts boiling without conceding the story: "To the end of that winter and probably the end of his life he thought my invisible animals were a superstition." This is exactly right — he credits the *practice* without the theory, which is the correct skeptic's position.

**Ch06 — The first balloon.** "He said it was very pretty. He said it the way you'd tell a child his mud horse was very lifelike. And then he asked me, in the dry voice, whether I planned to burn down the household I was a guest in." Voice fully in register.

**Ch07 — The tethered model flies.** Heras watches the untethered sack rise and then *works to recover* his irony: "He pulled his eyes down at last and put the dry irony back on like a coat." The moment of genuine wonder is handled well — he does not capitulate, he reboots. His verdict: "You have made a thing that flies away from you. Congratulations. It is the most useless miracle I have ever seen." Then, immediately, the quiet concession that it is a thing no one can argue with. This is the first shift — he acknowledges the *undeniability* of the flying while preserving his skepticism about its usefulness. The pacing is careful and earned.

**Ch07 — Persuading the owner.** "I have spoken to him about you… I told him what I watched go up over the midden this evening with no string on it, and that I would swear to it, a Greek physician of Pergamon, before any man he named." He acts; he does not praise. Voice consistent.

**Ch09 — Meeting Macer.** Heras operates in a background role, the dry expert witness. His exchange with Macer ("He did not mention it because it had not happened. I sold you the sum, Macer.") shows the same boardroom irony, applied to managing a patron.

**Ch10 — The printing press.** He holds the crooked sheet up to the light and is silent "for longer than was like him," then: "Now make it not ugly, and you will have done something, and not before." Precise. He grants the principle before the execution, which is consistent with his pattern of conceding what works while holding the bar high.

**Ch14 — Return from the Dacian war.** Reunion scene. He does not ask what happened: "A lesser friend would have asked…Heras knew it was useless. He was a physician." He offers the bath and Epicurus in lieu of comfort, names the water work before Daniel raises it. The line "He looked at me with those dry clever eyes that had punctured every grand thing I had ever brought him and had, every time, quietly kept the part that worked" is the book's clearest articulation of his function — and it is exactly right, showing the shift from adversarial skeptic to method-carrier without making him sentimental.

**Ch15 — The knife scene.** He notices Daniel has "stopped listening" (mid-surgery) and simply states it, then goes back to the strop. No lecture. One perfect line. The collaboration on the filter is active and unromantic — "Heras pretended the second one was a Greek physician's old wisdom about cooked water and bad air, which made it respectable, which made the wharf men do it, which was the whole game." He is now a co-conspirator in practical medicine, carrying Daniel's method under his own professional cover. The shift is believable because it is *strategic*, not emotional.

**Ch16 — The figures and the press.** Two excellent Heras moments. On the figures: "Nobody adopts a wonder, he said. They adopt a thing that saves them an afternoon." On the contest idea: "You want to make men homesick for a country that doesn't exist yet… That's either the wisest thing you've said to me or the saddest, and I genuinely cannot tell which." Both are perfectly in character — the first is the empiricist's wisdom, the second is the admiring-but-unresolved stance he takes to ideas he cannot dismiss.

**Ch18 — Pamphilus freed.** He feeds Daniel the formula words he can't remember, then takes the collar halves away silently. No speech. The restraint is completely consistent.

**Ch19 — The contest.** "You want to pay people to lie. / To imagine. / In Rome those are close cousins, and the family is not respectable." He supplies the Quinquatrus festival framing for the prize — practical cleverness dressed as dry aside.

**Ch20 — Tyche freed.** He goes "very still by the door" during Tyche's speech, then: "She's not wrong, you know. You solved a problem and bought yourself a harder one. Congratulations. It's what you always do." In Greek — still the sly registral note. Voice perfect.

**Ch21 — The niter work.** He helps with the powder research while disapproving: "the careful way he did things he disapproved of, which was to make sure I did them less stupidly than I would have alone." His challenge is surgical: "You're a man who built a wall and now spends his evenings learning where the gate is." He knows Daniel is lying to himself and names it without melodrama.

**Ch22 — Marcia.** He sends Daniel to her with one of his best one-liners: "Her name is Marcia. Don't be charming at her. She's had charming men in her warehouse her whole life and she counts the spoons after." At the wedding he looks "insufferably satisfied with himself." Consistent tone; the slight warmth is earned across two decades.

**Ch23 — The water-clock.** "It is very beautiful… and I would not plan a funeral by it." Echoes his exact register from the balloon — calling Daniel's failures beautiful is an established verbal habit by this point, which shows continuity.

**Ch24 — High ground scene.** The novel's fullest skeptic speech, twenty years in. He grants the figures, the water, the wounds. He challenges the rest: "The tube that bursts… the drawings of carts that carry the grain of Egypt with no men… You light a torch and you say it's for the ones not born yet. I'm a man who has watched a great many people die, Daniel, and I have learned to be suspicious of any gift addressed to people who can't yet say no to it." Then: "I cannot tell the difference… between a man doing a duty no one asked him to do and a man who has decided he knows better than the whole world that made him." This is the conscience function at full power — it is *not* warm, it is genuinely challenging. He has believed in the method for years and still refuses to believe in the man without question. That is exactly right.

**Ch32 — Hadrian analysis.** Heras is the political analyst now, the city Greek who hears things. His portrait of Hadrian as "the most dangerous master a clever man ever serves is the one clever enough to know exactly how clever the servant is being" is perfectly in character — it is the skeptic's mode applied to a new threat.

**Ch34 — The treasury approach.** Heras acts as go-between for the Eudemus contact, but frames it precisely: "I am not glad to do this… if it goes wrong you will not have heard the word fiscus in this garden." Then: "It is good you came. I am too old to be left out of the dangerous part of your life. It is the only interesting part." Affection admitted, but through irony and self-mockery.

**Ch37 — Apollodorus's fall.** He brings the news, refuses to soften it, tells Daniel to go and knows he'll go anyway: "There is not… and you know there is not, and you are going to go and try the thing that is not there to do, because you cannot help it… Come back, because I am too old to teach another barbarian his manners." The tone is the same as ch04 — exasperated fondness worn as dry wit.

**Ch38 — Daniel's failing eyesight.** He examines the eye with clinical precision: "And you cannot make it. / Then it is a thing your people had and you do not… which is most of what your people had, as far as I have ever been able to tell." Gentle delivery of the cruelest fact. Then, in the crucial advice scene, he drops irony for the only time the text marks explicitly — "Then he did the thing he did rarely, which was to drop the irony entirely, and it always landed harder than the irony" — and speaks plainly about building for after death, quoting the lamp-in-six-hundred-years image. The dropping of irony is presented as unusual, which preserves the register by noting its absence as the exception.

**Ch41 — The teaching bench.** He comes down to the wharf at seventy-nine, walks with sticks, watches Tyche's three students work in silence. He does not say anything clever; "he had stopped, mostly, saving the cleverness for when his breath was up to it." Then the speech about fire carried by people who cannot see the far end — the most openly warm thing he has ever said, and it is delivered carefully ("he had decided lately") as a recent philosophical conclusion, not a conversion. He laughs at Chloe's correct answer. His last fully dramatized scene ends with the dry laugh intact.

**Ch42 — Death chapter.** He sends for Daniel "the way he sent for the wine merchant, by a boy with a list." His final instructions include insisting Daniel not bring a physician "on the grounds that he was one." He dies talking about the waste of learning to see and then losing the eyes — the Hippocrates he can no longer read. His very last remark (to Zoticus, relayed after the fact) is a joke at the gods' expense: "on the whole he preferred the gods, as company, even not believing in them." Death in character. No deathbed conversion, no tearful summing-up.

**Ch42 — Post-death, the empty bench.** Daniel drinks the boiled water at the bench and has the retort shaped and ready with nowhere to go. The scene is one of the most affecting in the book precisely *because* it doesn't sentimentalize. The method — the water — is the vessel for the loss. That is entirely Heras's logic applied to his own death, which suggests voice consistency has been so thorough it shapes how his absence is written.

## Drift flags

**None of substance.** Minor notes only:

- **Ch24, late scene:** Heras's speech about "the tube that bursts" and gifts "addressed to people who can't yet say no" is slightly longer than his usual mode — it verges on the author needing a voice for conscience. However, it does not break his register; the ideas are ones he has built to over thirty chapters, and the *manner* (mild, not polemical, looking at the river not at Daniel) holds his dryness intact. The scene is the one place where his function as "book's skeptic/conscience" is most visible as a function, but the writing keeps it earned.

- **Ch38, the irony-drop scene:** Flagged in the text itself as exceptional ("the thing he did rarely"). The speech about building for after death is close to inspirational, which is not his mode. It is saved by (a) the framing as unusual, (b) the ending on "Now go home, it is late, and I am too old to comfort a barbarian past the second cup," which re-applies the habitual dismissive warmth. Borderline, but not a drift.

- **Ch22 (wedding):** "Insufferably satisfied with himself" risks cosiness. It is a very brief touch and is held in the narrator's description rather than given as direct speech or thought, so it reads as Daniel's reading of Heras rather than Heras going soft. Acceptable.

**No chapter or line requires a hard drift flag.**

## Arc assessment

The shift from puncturing-skeptic to method-believer is gradual and structurally sound. The stages are:

1. **Ch04–05:** Pure skeptic. Dismantles germ theory, adopts boiling *silently*.
2. **Ch06–07:** Acknowledges undeniable evidence (balloon flies), reboots irony — does not concede.
3. **Ch10–15:** Active collaborator on method. Carries Daniel's practices under professional cover. Intellectual sparring continues on new work.
4. **Ch16–24:** Trusted friend and political reader. Still challenges (the high-ground scene is the sharpest challenge in the whole arc, placed a decade past the "conversion" to keep him honest).
5. **Ch32–38:** Elder statesman of the method. The late scenes about building for after death and the value of a skeptic in the room are the culmination — he articulates what he *has been doing* rather than doing it fresh.
6. **Ch41–42:** Completion. He watches the method walk into the future without him, laughs at a child's right answer, dies with a philosopher's joke. No softening of the intellectual core.

He never becomes Daniel's cheerleader. He grants the figures, the water, the wounds, and keeps the rest under review until the end. That is the correct arc for this character and the novel executes it without a false note.
