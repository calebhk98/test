## SECTION 1: THE ROMAN DISEASE PROFILE IN ~113 AD

### What Romans Actually Carry

A Roman sailor departing Gades (Cadiz) in 113 AD carries a disease package that is
meaningfully narrower than what a Spanish sailor carried from Palos in 1492. The
two most catastrophic killers of the Columbian Exchange — smallpox and measles —
are almost certainly not yet endemic in the Roman world. This is the pivotal fact
for the novel.

**Smallpox (Variola)**

The Antonine Plague of 165–180 AD is the event historians have traditionally
associated with smallpox's emergence in the Roman Empire. The current scholarly
consensus — supported by recent ancient DNA work — favors measles as the more
likely culprit for that epidemic. Whether it was measles or smallpox, the point
for 113 AD is the same: the epidemic hasn't happened yet, and the disease ecology
it represents had not yet crystallized in the Roman Mediterranean. Rome has been
urbanized for centuries, which means *some* viral load circulates at all times, but
viruses require dense, sustained, interconnected populations to stabilize as endemic
childhood diseases. The Roman Empire of 113 AD is urbanized, but not in the
continuous, tightly networked way Northern Europe plus Atlantic maritime trade
routes would be by 1492. Smallpox likely not present in the Roman expedition's
crew as an endemic disease.

**The honest uncertainty:** It is conceivable that a smallpox-like orthopoxvirus
circulated at low levels in some regions of the Empire — the Near East, Egypt —
without being documented as a distinct disease. The safe narrative position is:
smallpox absent from the ship's crew in 113 AD, with a small acknowledged margin
of uncertainty. What's not uncertain: no Roman writer before the Antonine Plague
describes anything that reads unambiguously as smallpox. The absence of
documentation in a heavily documented medical tradition is meaningful.

**Measles (Morbillivirus)**

The first unambiguous clinical description of measles as a distinct disease appears
in the 9th-century AD writings of the Arab physician al-Razi (Rhazes). This doesn't
mean it didn't exist — it means it wasn't recognized as a standalone disease, which
suggests it wasn't causing the kind of mass-mortality events that would force
documentation. If it was present in Rome at all in 113 AD, it was at low prevalence,
not established as the regular childhood epidemic it became later. Like smallpox:
probably absent from the expedition as an endemic, well-established disease. Same
honest uncertainty applies.

**What Romans DO carry in 113 AD:**

- **Tuberculosis (Phthisis):** Definitively endemic and well-documented. Caused by
  *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, transmitted by respiratory droplets. A Roman sailor
  may be an asymptomatic carrier. In naive populations with no prior exposure and
  no developed immune response, TB's attack rate rises sharply. Active TB in a naive
  population: estimated 10–20% active disease rate vs. the ~5–10% in a partially
  immune population. Slow killer — chronic, wasting, over years — but a killer.

- **Malaria (Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax):** Highly endemic in Rome, especially
  around the Tiber marshes and Ostia. Roman sailors from Ostia or the Campanian coast
  carry *Plasmodium* in their blood. Mosquito-vectored, so it doesn't spread person-
  to-person — but the mosquitoes it needs (*Anopheles* species) already exist in the
  tropical Caribbean. Once an infected sailor is bitten and the parasite cycles through
  local *Anopheles*, malaria becomes endemic in the contact zone within a single
  transmission season. This is geography-dependent: devastating in tropical lowlands,
  limited in highland or temperate regions.

- **Typhoid and other Salmonella strains:** Endemic in Roman urban water supplies.
  Fecal-oral transmission. Roman camps using river water downstream of latrines
  create persistent reservoirs. Devastating in populations with no prior exposure,
  particularly when sanitation collapses under stress.

- **Dysentery (bacterial and amoebic):** Ubiquitous in any premodern military
  expedition. Amoebic dysentery (*Entamoeba histolytica*) can be carried
  asymptomatically. The first time it encounters a population with no prior exposure
  and inadequate sanitation, it can cause 15–25% mortality in outbreak conditions.

- **Influenza:** Probably present in recognizable seasonal form by 113 AD, though
  not documented as a distinct disease. Roman medical texts describe seasonal
  respiratory fevers that match influenza's clinical picture. A new influenza strain
  in a naive population: serious but not catastrophic at the population level (unlike
  the 1918 pandemic, which involved unusual immunological dynamics). Mortality in a
  naive population: 2–8% for a typical strain.

- **Hepatitis A:** Fecal-oral, endemic in Roman cities. Novel to any population that
  has never been exposed. Rarely fatal in adults with good nutrition, but a
  significant contributor to morbidity and weakness that makes other infections worse.

- **Various bacterial respiratory diseases (not influenza):** Streptococcal
  pneumonia, pertussis (whooping cough), possibly diphtheria. These require
  sustained close contact to transmit efficiently but can cause significant mortality
  in naive populations, especially children.

### The Critical Comparison: 113 AD vs. 1492

| Pathogen | Present in Roman expedition (113 AD) | Present in Spanish expedition (1492) |
|----------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Smallpox | Almost certainly absent | Endemic; major European childhood disease |
| Measles | Probably absent or very low prevalence | Endemic since at least 6th–9th century AD |
| Bubonic plague | Absent (arrives in Europe 541 AD) | Survivors of post-Black Death Europe |
| Typhus | Absent (no European record until 1450s) | Possibly present |
| Tuberculosis | Present | Present |
| Malaria | Present | Present |
| Typhoid/Salmonella | Present | Present |
| Dysentery | Present | Present |
| Influenza | Probably present | Present |
| Syphilis | Not yet brought back | Not yet brought back |

**Conclusion:** Roman contact in 113 AD is significantly less lethal than 1492
European contact, but not benign. The two great killers of 1492 — smallpox and
measles — are absent. The secondary killers — TB, malaria, typhoid, dysentery —
are present. Native populations in contact with Romans in 113 AD face serious
epidemic disease, possibly 25–45% mortality over the first generation of sustained
contact, depending on the disease and geography. Not 80–95% in a generation like
1492. More like the slower, uneven die-off that might have characterized early
Bronze Age contacts between previously isolated populations.

**What changes after 165 AD:** If and when the Antonine Plague event occurs and
its pathogen (measles, or a measles-like virus) becomes endemic in Rome and
subsequent contact expeditions carry it west, the second-wave contact becomes
dramatically more lethal. Daniel knows this. He is not just thinking about the
113 AD expedition; he is thinking about what the 160s expedition will carry.

## SECTION 2: NATIVE AMERICAN IMMUNITY PROFILE IN ~113 AD

### What Indigenous Americans Have and Lack

**Pre-contact Americas had their own endemic diseases.** This is often misunderstood.
The Americas were not a disease-free paradise. Indigenous populations had:

- **Tuberculosis:** DNA evidence confirms TB was present in pre-Columbian Americas,
  likely a distinct strain (*M. tuberculosis* complex) with deep roots in the western
  hemisphere. Some prior exposure exists — but the Roman TB strain would still be
  partially novel, and stress, crowding, and malnutrition sharply increase active
  disease rates.

- **Treponematoses including syphilis:** Recent ancient DNA evidence (confirmed by
  multiple 2024–2025 studies) places the origin of syphilis (*Treponema pallidum*
  pallidum) firmly in the Americas, with evidence dating to 5,500+ years ago. In
  113 AD, treponemal diseases including syphilis are endemic at some level in the
  Caribbean and Mesoamerica. The reverse-flow risk to Rome is real.

- **Chagas disease:** Caused by *Trypanosoma cruzi*, endemic in tropical Americas.
  Vector-borne (triatomine bugs); not directly person-to-person transmissible.
  Limited transmission to sailors making brief contact.

- **Various intestinal parasites:** Hookworm, roundworm, giardia — present in
  pre-Columbian Americas. Not immediately fatal but significant burden on a
  population already stressed by epidemic contact.

**What they lack:**

- The specific Old World viral diseases (smallpox, measles) — confirmed absent by
  ancient DNA and epidemiology; these require the specific human-livestock interface
  of Old World animal domestication to emerge.
- Influenza — the specific Old World strains carried by Romans are novel.
- Old World bacterial strains of dysentery and typhoid — partially novel,
  even if functionally similar pathogens exist locally.

### Are They MORE or LESS Vulnerable Than 1492 Populations?

The honest answer: roughly similar baseline vulnerability, with the critical
difference being what's *brought to them*, not their own immune status.

The "1,400 years of isolation" argument — that by 1492 indigenous populations had
been isolated from Old World disease for an extra 1,400 years compared to a 113 AD
contact — doesn't straightforwardly translate to greater 1492 vulnerability. Immune
memory is individual and generational, not civilizational in that sense. What matters
is whether specific pathogens have been present, not the duration of absence.

A 113 AD Caribbean islander's immune system is as naive to smallpox and measles as
a 1492 Caribbean islander's would be — because neither population has ever been
exposed. The timeline of isolation doesn't change that naivety; only the arrival
of the pathogen does.

What makes 113 AD contact *less* devastating is simply that the Roman sailors
don't carry smallpox. The population vulnerability is similar; the pathogen package
is smaller.

### What Mesoamerica Looks Like in 113 AD

The Aztec Empire does not exist. Tenochtitlan is not founded until 1325 AD.

In 113 AD, Mesoamerica is in the middle of the Classic period. What exists:

**Teotihuacan** (~50,000–125,000 people; the largest city in the Americas in 113 AD):
This is the dominant urban center, located in the central Mexican highlands, about
1,300 km from the Yucatan coast. A Roman expedition using the Columbus trade-wind
route does not encounter Teotihuacan on first contact — it's well inland. But
Teotihuacan's trade networks extend to the Gulf Coast and the Yucatan peninsula.
Goods from Teotihuacan appear archaeologically across Mesoamerica. This matters:
disease introduced at the Caribbean coast will, through trade networks, eventually
reach Teotihuacan. The connection is slow (months to years for trade goods to
move); but it is not zero.

**The Maya lowlands:** Classic Maya city-states are in their peak period in 113 AD.
Tikal, Calakmul, Copan, Palenque — not all of these are at full development yet,
but the lowland Maya civilization is active and extensive. The Yucatan peninsula
is Maya territory. A Roman expedition making landfall anywhere in the Caribbean
or Gulf of Mexico is likely to encounter Maya traders or coastal settlements
before it encounters any inland polity.

**The Caribbean (first landfall zone):**
The Columbus route — southwest from near the Canaries at ~28°N latitude to catch
the northeast trade winds, crossing in 5–6 weeks — makes landfall in the Bahamas
or the Greater Antilles (Cuba, Hispaniola, Puerto Rico). In 113 AD, these islands
are occupied by **Saladoid** or early **Ostionoid** peoples — the ancestors of the
Taíno who Columbus encountered in 1492. Smaller populations than 1492, with similar
material culture: fishing-focused, agricultural (manioc, sweet potato, maize),
ceramic-producing, politically organized at the village-to-chiefdom level.

Not city-states. Not empires. But not uninhabited.

**The First Contact Geography:**

Roman ships following Daniel's Columbus route (southwest to ~28°N, trade winds
west across the Atlantic, 5–6 weeks) most probably make first landfall at:
- The Bahama chain, or
- The northern coast of Hispaniola, or
- The eastern coast of Cuba

The people they meet first: proto-Taíno or Taíno-precursor Ostionoid communities.
Population of these islands in 113 AD: estimates range widely, but the pre-Columbian
Caribbean population was likely 200,000–500,000 across the Greater Antilles. Lower
density than 1492 because the islands hadn't yet reached agricultural carrying capacity.
These are not small hunter-gatherer bands — but they are also not armies.

## SECTION 3: MILITARY DYNAMICS — WHAT FIRST CONTACT ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

### The Expedition's Capabilities

By 112–115 AD, Daniel has given the expedition the following assets:

**Hot air balloon observers (2 rigs, standard doctrine since ~108 AD):**
An observer at 200 feet in a stable balloon basket has unobstructed line-of-sight
roughly 17–18 miles to the horizon on flat terrain. In the hilly Caribbean coast,
effective observation range is 6–10 miles. This observer sees canoe concentrations,
village layouts, forest movement, and settlement patterns before any Roman sets
foot on the beach. Crucially, he cannot be reached by any pre-contact weapon. The
maximum range of a thrown spear is ~30 meters. A good stone-tipped arrow, well-shot,
might reach 200 meters horizontally. A balloon at 200 feet (60 meters) altitude
at 150–200 meters horizontal distance is outside the effective kill zone of any
indigenous weapon of this period. The balloon is effectively invulnerable.

The psychological impact of this on any indigenous observer on the beach: incalculable.
It is not just a military advantage. It is a cosmological rupture. Men floating in
the sky.

**Early gunpowder cannons:**
By 112–115 AD, Daniel's cannon program has been running for several years since the
cannon-that-bursts demonstration (~108 AD). The technical state: bronze or cast-iron
tubes, probably 2–4 inch bore, loaded with black powder and solid stone or iron shot.
Accurate at close range (under 100 meters), unreliable at longer distances. Rate of
fire: perhaps 1 round per 3–5 minutes with trained crew. But accuracy is beside the
point in a first-contact scenario. The noise of a cannon discharge — a sound with no
natural equivalent in the Americas — combined with smoke, flash, and the complete
destruction of whatever the ball strikes, has an impact that no military advantage
figure captures. The cannon's primary value in first contact is not its kill radius.
It is the 30-second absolute silence that follows the first shot, before anyone on
either side does anything.

**Roman military discipline:**
The expedition is not a haphazard colonial venture. It is run with legionary-grade
discipline. Formation drilling, standing signals doctrine, established chain of
command. Each decision to engage or hold is a command decision, not a mob.

**Printed tactical maps and communication:**
The expedition carries printed charts of the expected landfall zone (inferred from
Daniel's world map), standardized signal flags, and written orders. Information
can be duplicated and distributed — every ship's officer has the same chart, the
same signals, the same standing orders for first-contact situations. This is not
how ancient expeditions worked. This is how modern ones do.

### What Indigenous Defenders Have

**Superior local knowledge:** They know the reefs, the currents, the paths through
the interior, the fresh water sources, the seasonal patterns. In a guerrilla war
or a running conflict, this is decisive. In an initial contact situation — before
any Roman has made an error that turns a crowd into a war party — it is less relevant.

**Numbers:** Village populations of 200–800 people. Multiple villages that could
coordinate: potentially 1,000–3,000 warriors in a region, given time to summon them.
This is a meaningful number against 200–400 Roman soldiers in a landing party.

**No horses, no iron:** Copper and stone weapons. Spears, bows, slings. Against
unarmored opponents at close range, these are lethal. Against Roman legionaries
in formation with shields and armor, substantially less so.

**No concept of what they're seeing:** The balloon is not in any existing
cosmological framework. The cannon is not in any existing technological framework.
The three-masted Roman vessel — larger than anything they've seen on water —
is not in any existing scale of reference. The first reaction to the expedition
is almost certainly not aggression; it is paralysis, then curiosity, then one of
two things: a gift exchange or a misread signal leading to violence.

### The First Encounter Scenario

The expedition's goal (per Daniel's framing to the sponsors) is: scouting,
geographic confirmation, and trade samples. Not immediate conquest. Not colony
establishment on the first voyage.

**Most likely first contact sequence:**

1. Roman ships anchor offshore. Balloon goes up — the watching coastal people see
   a floating fire-basket for the first time. Many withdraw into the trees.
   Some stay and watch. A few approach in canoes to look at the ships.

2. Romans lower a boat and approach the canoes with trade goods (mirrors, glass
   beads — Daniel will have thought of this — cloth). The expedition has a
   linguist or two who speak Punic, Greek, Aramaic, and nothing useful. There is
   no shared language. Communication is gesture, demonstration, exchange of objects.

3. The exchange either: (a) goes well, generating mutual wariness and some objects
   changing hands, or (b) breaks down on a misread gesture — a Roman reaching for
   a weapon to show it, a local man interpreting a sudden movement as attack.

4. If (b): a Roman soldier fires a crossbow or discharges a hand-cannon in the
   ensuing confusion. The canoes scatter. The watching crowd on the beach disperses
   into the forest. The Romans land on an empty beach. The expedition has been
   seen; the news spreads inland faster than any Roman can follow it.

5. Either way, within 48 hours: more people arrive to look, including presumably
   figures of authority. The trading/contact cycle begins again.

**What happens if a local group attacks in force?**

This is the scenario Daniel cannot prevent by negotiating from Rome. A village
that decides the strangers must be driven off launches an attack — canoes surrounding
a Roman ship, or a beach ambush of a landing party.

The balloon observer sees this before the Romans on the ground do. Signal goes
down: large canoe formation, 200 meters, coming from the west. The Romans form
a defensive square on the beach. The approaching force — let's say 80–100 warriors
in a dozen large canoes — is met with the following: a cannon discharge into the
water 10 meters in front of the lead canoe (Daniel's specific instruction: fire
in front of, not into, the boats the first time). The explosion of water. The noise.
The smoke. Every canoe stops dead in the water. Some men dive overboard. The
formation breaks up. There is no second wave.

The Romans do not pursue. They hold position.

This is a demonstration, not a battle. The local community processes what happened,
concludes these strangers cannot be fought with conventional means, and the
relationship pivots to cautious negotiation. Probably.

**The failure mode:** One of the expedition's junior officers — a centurion with
twenty years of Dacian/Parthian campaigning — misreads "hold position" as "pursue
advantage" and orders a landing party forward into the treeline when the canoes
retreat. In the treeline, the Romans do not have balloon cover, do not have
formation advantage, and encounter people who know the forest. The Romans take
casualties. The centurion is court-martialed upon return. This becomes, in the
book's telling, the first signal that the expedition's discipline is the thing
Daniel can control and that human nature is the thing he cannot.

**The honest answer about conquest difficulty:**

The expedition is not trying to conquer anyone on the first voyage. With balloon
observers and cannons, a determined conquest attempt on a Caribbean island against
spear-and-bow defenders would be militarily feasible in a way that was genuinely
unprecedented — no pre-gunpowder indigenous force can effectively resist an organized
unit that can see everything from 200 feet and discharge explosive force from
outside their effective range.

But the expedition is 200–400 men, months from resupply. Their strategic constraint
is not military capability; it is logistical exposure. They cannot hold territory.
They can demonstrate capability, establish contact, take geographic and biological
samples, and leave. Whether what follows that first demonstration is trade or
conquest depends entirely on what political orders arrive on the second expedition.
Daniel knows this. He knows he is designing the first act; he has limited control
over the second.

## SECTION 4: THE COMMUNICATION BREAKDOWN → COLONIAL INDEPENDENCE ARC

### The Math of Distance

Rome to the Caribbean, via Daniel's route: approximately 6,500–7,000 km by sea.
At Roman cargo ship speeds (roughly 100–150 km/day in favorable conditions, much
less in unfavorable), this is a one-way voyage of 45–70 days under ideal conditions.
Call it 5–8 weeks out, 6–10 weeks back (the return route, sailing north to the
westerlies at ~40–45°N, is longer and less predictable). Round trip minimum: 3–4
months of sailing plus any time spent at the destination.

A realistic expedition cycle: departure in spring, arrival in early summer, 2–4
months at destination, departure in late summer or early fall, return in winter or
the following spring. Total round trip: 9–14 months.

A colony established in the New World that wants to send a message to Rome waits
9–14 months for a reply. For any practical governance decision — a military
threat, a resource allocation, a judicial case — the colony has to act first and
report back, because waiting for Roman guidance means waiting a year.

This is not a communication problem. It is a sovereignty problem.

### Daniel Knows Exactly What This Means

He has 10th-grade American history. He knows:
- The British Parliament passed the Stamp Act in 1765.
- The Continental Congress declared independence in 1776.
- Eleven years. Distance plus communication lag plus local interest divergence
  equals independence.

He will never say this aloud. Not to Hadrian, not to Antoninus Pius, not to any
expedition sponsor. The sentence "your western colony will eventually seek independence"
is the sentence that gets the entire ocean program shut down before it starts.

What he does say, carefully and in writing (specifically in his encyclopedia, in a
passage that is framed as "theoretical governance considerations for distant
provinces"):

*"The effective governance radius of any empire is determined not by the emperor's
will but by the speed at which his instructions arrive. Where instructions cannot
arrive before the crisis has resolved itself, the local governor must act as
sovereign in practice, whatever his legal status. History shows that men who must
act as sovereigns come, in a generation, to think as sovereigns. The wise emperor
plants institutions and laws that remain good without his personal intervention;
he does not plant commanders who await his word before they breathe."*

This is the most dangerous passage Daniel writes. It is framed as political
philosophy. It is actually a prediction.

### How Daniel Designs the Expedition to Shape What Follows

**What he tries to build in:**

1. **Charter language emphasizing local autonomy:** Daniel drafts the expedition
   charter (through intermediaries — he is not formally a government official) to
   include explicit language about the expedition commander having "full proconsular
   authority" in matters that "cannot await the guidance of Rome." This is
   conventional language for distant campaigns. But Daniel knows that precedent,
   over decades, becomes constitution.

2. **The contest model transplanted:** He lobbies for the colony's founding
   documents to include a "prize competition" — a standing prize for any colonist
   who develops a new technique for local agriculture, navigation, or building.
   This is not just about innovation. It is about building a local culture that
   solves its own problems rather than waiting for Rome to send solutions.

3. **Printed legal forms:** He ensures the expedition carries printed copies of
   Roman contract law, census forms, and property registration procedures — not
   to integrate the colony into Roman bureaucracy, but because populations that have
   their own legal infrastructure become harder to govern from a distance.

4. **The physician line:** He sends one of Heras's trained physicians on the
   first expedition (or arranges for one to be on the second). A community that
   has internal medicine capacity does not need Rome to survive an epidemic.

**What he knows he cannot control:**

- Who Rome sends as expedition commander. Military competence is not the same as
  political wisdom. The man Rome picks to lead a naval expedition is almost
  certainly a career military officer who thinks in terms of conquest and tribute,
  not trade and co-development.

- What the colony looks like after the second, third, fourth wave. The first
  expedition is exploration. The second is settlement. The third is exploitation.
  Daniel can shape the first. The second and third are driven by Roman political
  incentives he cannot rewrite.

- His own mortality. He will be 70+ when meaningful colonial independence becomes
  a political question. He will probably not be alive.

### The Independence Timeline

If a colony is established around 115–120 AD:

**Years 1–20 (115–135 AD):** The colony functions as a distant Roman outpost.
Communication lag means the governor acts autonomously on most things. Rome
gets reports; Rome issues general orders; the governor fills in the gaps. This
is normal for any frontier province.

**Years 20–40 (135–155 AD):** A generation of colonists grows up in the New World.
Their relationship to Rome is mediated entirely through the governor's household
and the occasional supply ship. They have never seen Rome. They speak Latin with
an accent. Their wives may be local women (formal Roman marriage law applies only
to citizens; the actual practice on distant frontiers is messier). They have
economic interests that are different from Rome's — they want low tribute, local
trade rights, control of the cacao and rubber supply.

**Years 40–70 (155–185 AD):** The Antonine Plague hits Rome. The supply ships
come less frequently. The governor makes decisions that Rome, occupied with plague
and the Marcomanni and a succession crisis, doesn't respond to for two years.
The colony passes laws about land use that Rome never formally approves. It taxes
itself to fund its own defense. A local assembly, initially advisory, begins making
decisions that Rome can't practically countermand because it takes a year to get
a message there. This is not a revolution. It is just what happens when
communications break down during a metropole crisis.

**Years 70–100 (185–215 AD):** The colony has its own institutions, its own
interests, its own culture. It still calls itself Roman. It still uses Latin.
It still sends tribute — sometimes. When Rome sends a new governor, the local
assembly "advises" him on local custom. The governor, a practical man, goes along
with it. Formal sovereignty is Roman. Practical sovereignty is the assembly's.
Independence is not declared; it is simply the operating reality.

**What Rome thinks it's getting:** A province. Tributary. A source of rubber,
cacao, maize, tobacco, geographic knowledge, and naval prestige.

**What it actually gets:** An eventually autonomous polity 7,000 km away that
it cannot govern in practice, that it cannot reliably tax, and that it cannot
bring to heel without an expenditure of naval and military force that would exceed
the value of any tribute ever received. The colony becomes independent not by
revolution but by the arithmetic of distance and communication lag, playing out
over three or four generations.

Daniel knows this. He writes it in a dead language in a lead box. He doesn't
say it to anyone.

## SECTION 5: RESOURCE ECONOMICS

### What the First Voyage Returns With

**The first voyage is not a commercial expedition.** It is proof of concept.

It returns with:
- Geographic confirmation: the world map is real. The western continent exists
  where Daniel said it would. This is the primary commercial return — not goods,
  but credibility. Daniel's map is proven. His credibility reaches an apex. Every
  claim he has ever made that people doubted is now retrospectively validated.
- Soil and rock samples.
- Plant specimens (probably not viable for replanting without careful handling,
  but identifiable).
- Possibly a small quantity of cacao beans, if the expedition reached Mesoamerican
  trade routes. Possibly maize, if they made contact with agricultural communities.
- Possibly a local person, taken willingly or otherwise (see Section 6).
- Logs: current patterns, wind patterns, depth soundings, island positions.
  These are immediately valuable for the second expedition's navigation.

**What it does not return with:** Rubber (processing is needed), tobacco (drying
and preparation methods not understood), large quantities of anything.

### What Exists Where in 113 AD

Via the Columbus route, the expedition lands in the Bahamas or Greater Antilles:

**Maize (corn):** Present in the Caribbean and throughout Mesoamerica. Caribbean
islanders grow maize in 113 AD, though it is not their primary staple (manioc is).
A Roman expedition can pick up maize cobs and kernels from any agricultural
settlement they visit. Germination: possible. Maize is hearty and can be grown
from stored kernels. First wave return: small quantity of seeds.

**Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas):** Native to the Americas, grown in the Caribbean
in 113 AD. Easier to transport than maize because the tubers survive longer storage.
First wave return: viable tubers or cuttings.

**Tobacco:** Present in the Caribbean. The local populations are using it in 113 AD —
ceremonially and habitually. A Roman expedition seeing people inhale smoke from
burning leaves would notice this. Whether they understand what it is or bring
meaningful quantities back on the first voyage: uncertain. More likely returned
on the second expedition.

**Cacao:** Primary cultivation in Mesoamerica. Not yet in the Greater Antilles in
significant quantities in 113 AD. The expedition, unless it sails to the Gulf of
Mexico coast, may not encounter cacao on the first voyage. Second or third
expedition territory.

**Rubber (Hevea brasiliensis):** Native to the Amazon basin — not the Caribbean.
Not accessible via the Columbus route on an initial contact voyage. However: the
Gulf Coast of Mesoamerica (which the expedition might reach on an extended first
voyage, or on the second) does have contact with the rubber-using cultures of
Mesoamerica. The Olmec used rubber for balls, waterproofing, and adhesives going
back 3,500 years. Classic Maya still process rubber in 113 AD. The expedition
encounters rubber either on the Yucatan coast or via trade goods from interior
Mesoamerica. If they bring back a block of processed rubber on the second voyage,
Daniel immediately knows what it is.

**What Daniel does with rubber:**

Daniel knows from standard education:
- Rubber is waterproof
- It can be formed while warm and holds shape when cool
- Vulcanization (adding sulfur to stabilize it) was discovered by Goodyear in
  1839 — he doesn't know the chemistry, just the name
- Pre-vulcanization natural rubber softens in heat and stiffens in cold

What he can do without vulcanization:
- Waterproof boots and cloaks (enormous military and civilian value)
- Create gaskets and seals for the steam pumps (this is operationally significant —
  every steam pump he has built leaks; rubber seals fix this)
- Waterproof the balloon envelopes (extending their service life dramatically)
- Create watertight containers for grain storage on ships

The gasket application alone — applied to the steam pumps that are already running
in Roman mines — is worth the cost of the expedition several times over in terms of
pump efficiency and lifespan. Daniel knows this the moment he sees a rubber sample.
He will not be able to explain to anyone why he is so excited about a lump of black
gum.

**Potatoes:** Not in the Caribbean or Gulf Coast. Andean origin. Not accessible
via the Columbus route. Third or fourth expedition territory, if the Romans push
south from the Caribbean to the South American coast.

**Tomatoes:** Mesoamerican/Andean origin. Possibly accessible via the Gulf Coast
on later expeditions.

**What the expeditions actually return with:**

The constraint is not one crop per voyage; it is identification, preservation, and
successful transplant. A Roman expedition can identify and bring back multiple botanical
samples and seeds simultaneously, provided the sailors understand what they're looking at,
can preserve it through the voyage, and can establish it in Roman soil.

- **First voyage:** Maize seeds, sweet potato tubers/cuttings, possibly tobacco samples.
  These are the most obvious domesticated crops encountered on the Caribbean coast.
  Sweet potato especially survives long sea voyages better than fresh fruit. The crew
  brings back whatever they recognize as useful food and whatever local inhabitants
  suggest is worth carrying. Multiple species per voyage is not a constraint.

- **Second expedition returns with:** Established maize and sweet potato planting stock
  (from successful trials), cacao beans or cacao plants (if Gulf Coast contact achieved),
  rubber samples (identified from Mesoamerican trade goods or Gulf Coast collections),
  and whatever other botanical material the sailors can identify and preserve.

- **Third and subsequent expeditions:** Focus on establishing supply routes for rubber,
  expanding geographic knowledge toward continental coasts, and first contact with
  Mesoamerican civilization at a city-state level. The bottleneck shifts from what
  one voyage can carry to what Rome can establish as viable agriculture and what
  supply routes can be maintained.

### The 5-Year Voyage Cycle and Roman Financial Culture

Round-trip expedition: approximately 9–14 months.
But the second expedition cannot depart until the first returns and the information
is processed, ships repaired, crews replaced, political authority secured. Realistically:
2–3 years between expeditions.

A Roman investor committing capital to the first expedition is looking at:
- Year 0: investment
- Year 1–2: first expedition returns (if successful)
- Year 2–3: second expedition departs
- Year 4–5: second expedition returns with commercial quantities of trade goods

So a 5-year return horizon for the first meaningful cargo. Roman commercial practice
in 113 AD does not yet have a good framework for 5-year speculative investment horizons —
maritime contracts typically cover a single voyage, not a multi-voyage program.

Daniel has already introduced double-entry bookkeeping by this point. He now
needs to introduce the concept of a venture partnership that survives multiple
voyages — a *societas* specifically structured for an expedition that runs 5–10
years. This is not a radical legal innovation; the Roman societas already handles
multi-year arrangements. But it requires:

1. Printed prospectus documents laying out expected voyage sequence, anticipated
   return goods, and estimated timeline. (Daniel can produce these with his press.)
2. A clear accounting of costs vs. expected return. (His double-entry format
   handles this directly.)
3. Political backing that prevents the venture from being appropriated once it
   becomes valuable. (This requires imperial patronage — the same kind of
   protection that a mine concession needs.)

The actual commercial model: the first expedition generates no direct financial
return, only proof of concept. The second generates small returns (crops, samples).
The third generates the first meaningful commercial cargo (cacao, rubber, maize for
the imperial granaries). By the fifth expedition, a working supply route is
established. Investors who put in at year 0 are seeing returns at year 6–8. This
is long-term capital in a culture of short-term maritime trading. It requires
institutional backing — probably imperial — rather than private commercial investment.

Daniel pitches this to Hadrian, not to Ostia merchants. He frames it as prestige
infrastructure, like the aqueducts — the empire builds it because the empire
benefits, not because any individual investor profits. Whether Hadrian's successor
Antoninus Pius continues that funding is the political cliff the program hangs over
(and partially falls off — see V2_HISTORICAL_IMPACT.md, ch46 note).

## SECTION 6: DANIEL'S MORAL POSITION

### What He Knows and When He Knows It

Daniel's knowledge set in 113 AD, when he's facilitating the first expedition:

**What he knows:**
- The Americas are inhabited. He has a world map in his head that shows two
  large continents. He knows from standard education that the pre-Columbian
  Americas contained tens of millions of people.
- Contact will cause epidemic disease in the contacted populations. He knows the
  mechanism — he has spent 15 years spreading germ theory through the Roman
  medical community. He knows his own expedition's crew carries TB, malaria, and
  GI pathogens.
- Military contact between a technologically superior force and a technologically
  inferior population ends in conquest. He has watched Rome conquer Dacia and
  campaign into Parthia. He has no illusions about what Roman generals do when
  they find land and people.
- Communication breakdown plus distance equals eventual colonial independence.
  He knows the British-to-American arc.
- He knows the Antonine Plague is coming (~165 AD). If it is measles, and if
  measles becomes endemic in Rome, then later expedition waves will carry it
  west. The disease package gets worse over time, not better.

**What he doesn't know:**
- Exactly how lethal the 113 AD disease package is to Caribbean populations with
  no prior exposure. He knows the *mechanism* but not the precise *magnitude* in
  this specific pathogen configuration.
- Whether the first expedition's crew members are currently infectious. He cannot
  test for TB carrier status. He cannot know who's carrying *Plasmodium*.
- What political decisions Rome will make about the contact zone once the first
  expedition returns successfully. He is not the emperor. He is not even a senator.
- Whether any of his interventions (quarantine protocols, the physician on the
  expedition) will meaningfully reduce transmission.

### The Lie

In 98 AD, on his first day of usefulness to Heras, Daniel describes the western
continents as "probably uninhabited" or "likely empty of civilization." He says
this because it is the only version of the truth that gets the ocean program
started. An inhabited western continent in the Roman political imagination is a
war prize. An uninhabited western continent is exploration. He needs it to be
exploration, at least long enough to build the ships and the navigation capability.

By 113 AD, he has been living with this lie for fifteen years.

He maintains it — never directly retracting, always using hedged language.
"The distances suggest any population would be sparse." "The geography of the
western ocean implies limited landmass." "Uninhabited Thule is the model." The
lie has a protective function at every stage: it prevents the ocean program from
being weaponized before the first voyage. It prevents a military expedition
from being sent instead of an exploratory one. It keeps the first voyage focused
on scouting rather than conquest.

### Daniel's Cover Story for Inhabited Land

When the first expedition returns with evidence of inhabitants — a local person,
artifacts, clear proof of established settlements — the framing "uninhabited"
collapses. Daniel does not recant explicitly. Instead, he shifts to a new
narrative that preserves his position of "I did not know":

*"My people came from the far north — we never ventured south enough along those
coasts to know what was there. I told you what I knew. What lies further south was
beyond our reach."*

This is technically consistent with his Thule framing throughout the book: his homeland
is northern, his knowledge was local. The lands encountered by the expedition lie further
south than Thulean exploration extended. He knew of the western ocean; he did not know of
these specific inhabited coasts. The claim is unfalsifiable — there is no one alive who
can contradict what Thulean explorers did or did not do. "My people never ventured that
far south" is defensible even as the lie grows.

It allows him to maintain the "I did not know" position publicly while being aware that
he knew perfectly well. The distinction between "I did not know this specific coast was
inhabited" and "I knew the western lands were inhabited but lied about it to get the
program started" is narrow, but it holds in public discourse. Daniel knows he is parsing
the difference between ignorance and deception in his favor. He also knows it is the best
he can do without admitting the original lie and destroying the entire framework that
made the ocean program possible.

He tells himself:
- He is not lying about the disease (he doesn't know exactly what will happen).
- He is not the one deciding to go — Rome is deciding to go; he is only providing
  the knowledge.
- The contact will happen eventually whether or not he does anything; he is
  accelerating the timeline, not creating the contact. (He knows this is weak.
  An earlier contact with a less lethal Roman disease package — before smallpox
  becomes endemic — may be marginally better than a later contact with a worse one.)
- If he refuses to facilitate the ocean program, Rome spends its ambition on
  military conquest of the East, which has its own body count.

He also knows what he is doing. He is making a utilitarian calculation that
privileges Roman civilization's long-term survival over the lives and cultures
of people he has never met. He doesn't say this. He doesn't write it in Latin.
He writes it in English, in the encyclopedia, in language that future readers
will have to decode, in a section headed "On the Western Peoples":

*"I knew. I had always known. I told myself the knowing was uncertain enough that
the telling would have been irresponsible — that to say 'there are people there
and our diseases will likely kill a substantial fraction of them' before anyone
had seen them was speculation dressed as fact. I believed this at the time. I
believe it less now. The truth is simpler: I wanted the ocean program to succeed.
The program required Rome to see the west as an opportunity, not a problem. An
inhabited west was a problem. So I made it uninhabited until I could no longer
make it so. This is the thing I cannot fix. I plant the encyclopedia to fix
the rest. This one I cannot fix."*

This is the most honest thing Daniel writes in the entire novel. It is written in
a language no one will read for generations.

### The Dramatic Moment: The Returned Person

The first expedition returns with a local person. This is almost certain —
every Age of Exploration first voyage returned with someone, willing or not,
as proof, as curiosity, as interpreter-in-training, as exhibit.

Daniel's reaction to this person, when the ship docks and the crowd gathers to
look, is the scene the novel has been building toward since page one.

This person does not speak Latin, Greek, Punic, or any language anyone in Rome
knows. They are probably frightened, probably disoriented, probably having survived
a sea voyage of 6–10 weeks in conditions they had no framework to understand.
They are standing on a dock in Rome, the largest city in the western world, surrounded
by people who look at them like they look at the exotic animals from Africa in the
Colosseum.

Daniel sees this and understands: he has done this. Not the crew member who decided
to bring them. Not the expedition commander. Daniel built the ship, designed the route,
convinced the sponsors. This person is standing on this dock because Daniel spent
fifteen years making it possible.

He does several things:

**Immediately:** He instructs that the person be housed in his compound, not in
the imperial collection or the public exhibitions. He arranges food that is
recognizable (the expedition has brought back food samples; he picks the closest
thing to what this person would have eaten at home). He assigns Tyche — who by
this point has 20 years of experience navigating cultural dislocation — to be
their primary contact. Not a guard. A person who understands what it is to live
in a world that is not yours.

**Over the following weeks:** He begins the work of constructing a communication
bridge. He is not a linguist; he has no formal methodology. He has a 21st-century
person's intuitive understanding that language learning requires patience,
context, and not treating the learner like a trained animal. He gets this mostly
wrong at first. He gets it more right over time.

**What he does not do:** He does not prevent the imperial court from requesting
access to this person. He cannot. He negotiates conditions — not an exhibition,
a private audience; not indefinitely, a specific timeframe. He negotiates from
a position of enormous political capital (the expedition proved his map; he is
at peak influence) but he is still negotiating against an emperor's curiosity.

**What this moment costs him:** It forces him to stop using the comfortable
framing that "they" are abstract populations in a future history he's altering.
This is a person with a name he cannot pronounce yet. The abstract becomes
specific. He will spend the rest of his life with the weight of that specificity.

## SECTION 7: WHAT CHANGES IN THE BOOK'S WORLD

### Daniel's Credibility After the Crossing

Before the crossing: Daniel's world map is the single most audacious claim he has
ever made. He showed it to Hadrian around 118–121 AD as a gambit to redirect Roman
ambition. For twenty years, it has been the thing that most precisely distinguishes
the people who trust him from the people who think he's a clever fraud. Crispus
has spent years calling it a fantasy. The educated Roman's default position is:
the ocean may be finite, but land in the west is speculation.

After the crossing: it wasn't speculation. The map was right. The trade winds
were where he said they would be. The crossing took the number of weeks he said
it would. Land was where he said land would be.

The retrospective validation effect is total. Everything Daniel has ever claimed —
the germ theory, the economic projections, the tech forecasts — is now viewed
through the lens of "this man said there was land in the west and he was right."
He goes from influential to prophetic in Roman public understanding. This is
dangerous in its own way: prophets get treated differently than advisors.
People stop arguing with them.

Daniel dislikes this intensely. The point was never to be believed on faith;
it was to be right about specific things that could be verified. He has been
verified in the most spectacular possible way, and the effect is the wrong one.

**Crispus's final erasure:** The successful crossing is the event that ends
Crispus's credibility. He has built his career on the claim that Daniel is a
fraud, a barbarian, a manipulator of emperors. He is on record — repeatedly,
publicly, in writing — saying the western land claims are lies. When the ships
return with maize and sweet potato and a living person from a land Rome had
never seen, Crispus does not recant. He pivots. He claims he always believed
in the western lands but questioned Daniel's methods. This is visible as what
it is. A public figure he has targeted before — probably a younger senator who
has found Daniel's patronage useful — cites Crispus's written denials in a
public address. Crispus says nothing after that. Not dead; erased.

### How It Affects Roman Imperial Ambition

The "hold the borders" strategy that Hadrian championed — consolidate, fortify,
manage what Rome has — is permanently complicated by the successful crossing.

There is now more world. There is always more world. The borders that Hadrian
carefully calibrated were the borders of the known world. The known world has
just approximately doubled in land area.

This does not immediately translate to conquest. The first crossing establishes
contact, not control. But it changes the political imagination: Rome now knows
that beyond the western ocean there are lands, peoples, and resources. The
argument for consolidation was always implicitly about limits — "this is what
there is; let's govern it well." The argument is harder to make when "this is
what there is" turns out to be half of what there is.

Daniel has accidentally created the argument for imperial expansion that he most
wanted to prevent. He wanted to redirect Roman ambition toward oceanic exploration
as a *substitute* for military conquest. He has succeeded so well that oceanic
exploration becomes the *prelude* to conquest.

He saw this coming. He saw it before the first expedition departed. He could not
stop it without stopping the expedition itself.

### How It Affects the Book's Ending

Daniel dies (~155 AD, age ~74) knowing:
- A colony exists or is being established
- He does not know if it has survived its first epidemic season
- He does not know what Rome has decided to do politically with the new world
- He does not know if the local people he briefly met and briefly cared for
  are alive or dead or enslaved
- He does not know if his encyclopedia — written in a dead language, buried in
  a lead box — will ever be read by someone who can use it
- He does not know if the Antonine Plague will break the quarantine protocols
  he spent thirty years building
- He knows a colony exists, because he built the ship, and he was on the dock
  when it left

The "all tracks lead to Rome" line — which Daniel delivers or inherits in some
form, reframing the old roads-to-Rome cliché — lands here. All roads lead to
Rome; all tracks eventually lead away from it. The railroad he argued for and
couldn't build; the ships he built that now sail away; the people who will read
his encyclopedia and use his arithmetic and think thoughts he couldn't think.
Everything he built is a track leading somewhere Rome can't control. This is
his legacy: not what Rome became, but what left Rome.

He wanted to save an empire. He planted things that will eventually escape it.
Whether that is failure or success is the question he cannot answer before he dies.

The correct emotional note at death: not triumphant, not despairing. The specific
ambivalence of a man who built something larger than he intended and cannot see
where it lands. He knows something is in motion. He does not know what it will
become. This is enough.

## SECTION 8: CHAPTER ASSIGNMENTS

*Keyed to the 53-chapter structure. These are beats — the writing agent has
latitude on how to dramatize them.*

**Ch36–38 (~118–121 AD): Hadrian, the Ocean Gambit**
The ocean program pitch happens here. Daniel presents the world map to Hadrian
and frames the western lands as uninhabited and reachable. The "uninhabited" lie
is planted clearly enough for readers to notice, without Daniel commenting on it.
Hadrian sanctions a bounded program: survey ships, hull testing, navigation
prizes. The commission is secured here, not in a single scene but across these
chapters.

**Ch38–39 (~121–125 AD): First Serious Expedition Planning**
The Atlantic probe voyages (three of them before the crossing, per V2_HISTORICAL_IMPACT.md)
begin here. These are not the crossing; they are range-finding. Each extends
further west. The balloon rigs are tested in open-ocean conditions. The hull
design is refined. Daniel argues with the naval architects about hull depth and
provisioning capacity. He insists on citrus. He argues about it in terms the
Roman naval mind understands ("sailors who lose their teeth cannot work the lines")
without explaining vitamin C. The specific tension: military commanders want armed
escort ships; Daniel wants provisioning ships. Finite imperial budget; competing
interests. He loses one argument (two warships are included), wins another
(the provisioning ratio he specifies).

**Ch43–44 (~130–135 AD): The Decision to Commission the Crossing**
Hadrian's health is failing; Antoninus Pius will succeed. Daniel knows the ocean
program is about to lose its imperial champion. The commission for the actual
crossing is secured here — under Hadrian, before his death — specifically because
Daniel pushes for it knowing that Antoninus Pius will not continue it. This is
a political race against a dying man's schedule. Hadrian signs the commission
from Baiae, where he is dying. Daniel is present. This is one of the novel's
key images: Daniel asking a dying emperor to authorize something he will never
see the result of, for reasons he cannot fully state.

**Ch45 (~135–138 AD): Departure**
The expedition departs from Gades (Cadiz) with Daniel on the dock. He watches
the three ships until they are too small to see. He is ~54 years old. His wife
Marcia is with him or she is not (this is a continuity question for the character
thread). The balloon rigs are visible on the rigging of the lead ship. He knows
where the ship is going. He knows what it will find. He says nothing to the
crowd around him that reveals any of this. One sentence of internal narration,
close third or first person, that contains the weight of everything he knows
and cannot say. Then he turns and walks back to the road.

The "all tracks lead to Rome" line belongs here, or nearby. The roads all run to
Rome; Daniel is watching something leave Rome on a track that runs the other way.

**Ch47 (~140–143 AD): First Return News**
The expedition returns (or a partial expedition — it is dramatically acceptable
for one ship to be lost). The news travels from Gades to Rome by optical semaphore
faster than any horse. Daniel hears it before the ship docks. He goes to Ostia
or to Gades; the scene happens at the water, not in a senate chamber.

What the expedition returns with: maize, sweet potato, rubber samples, geographic
logs, and a local person. The crowd at the dock. The local person standing on
the dock, looking at Rome. Daniel's reaction: see Section 6.

The proved-map effect: this is when Crispus's credibility collapses.

**Ch48 (~143–147 AD): The Inhabited Land Truth Becomes Undeniable**
In the weeks after the return, as the local person becomes a presence in Daniel's
compound, as Roman scholars examine the plants and rubber and artifacts, the
"uninhabited" framing cannot hold. Scholars who have read the records want to
know: if there's a person, where are the others? Daniel's careful hedging begins
to unravel. He does not recant publicly — he shifts language again: "sparse
populations, not organized civilization." This is still a lie, but a smaller one.

The chapter's real subject is Daniel's private reckoning: he is writing the
encyclopedia passage quoted in Section 6 here. The dramatic action is the
composition of that passage. He sits in his workshop, the person from the
western islands in the next room, and writes in English about what he has done
and cannot undo.

**Ch49–50 (~147–152 AD): Resource Arrivals**
The second and third expeditions return. Cacao reaches Rome — the first samples,
demonstrated to Antoninus Pius (or his court). Rubber gaskets are applied to
steam pumps; Daniel makes this case to the mining engineers and watchs the pump
efficiency numbers change. Maize is growing in three imperial farms. Sweet potato
has been distributed to four provinces. The economic argument for the program is
now verifiable; even Antoninus Pius's cautious administration can see the numbers.

The parallel track: Daniel is aging. He uses these chapters to show the
physical narrowing: he can no longer make the voyage to Gades himself. He watches
the expedition departures from reports, not from the dock. The people he trained
are running the program now. This is partly grief, partly what he intended.

**Ch51–52 (~152–155 AD): Daniel's Private Reckoning**
These chapters carry the full weight of what he's done. Not a speech; not a
monologue. Action and absence. Tyche (if alive) is the only person he can
speak to with anything approaching honesty, in English, in shorthand,
about what the western program has actually set in motion.

He reviews what he knows: a colony may be established or establishing. A generation
of Romans have been born who grow up knowing there is a western world. The ocean
program is running on institutional momentum now — it does not need him. The
epidemic protocols are running on institutional momentum. The printing press is
running on institutional momentum. He has made himself unnecessary, which was always
the goal, and the specific loneliness of that is exactly what he expected.

He does not make peace with the western program. He does not resolve it. He
writes about it in English and seals the lead box. He makes sure Tyche knows
where it is buried.

**Ch53 (Epilogue, ~190 AD):** 
This is already specified in the divergence ledger: a later pilot, generations on,
stands off the inhabited shore, the sea-clock running below decks. Daniel is
a "worn unread name at the foot of the oldest copied navigation table." The
shore is inhabited. There are fires. There are drawn-up boats. There are people
at the waterline.

The novel ends on the beginning of the thing Daniel set in motion: not the
conquest, not the colony, not the independent polity. The approach. The moment
before anything is decided. Unresolved, on purpose.

## KEY UNCERTAINTIES — BE HONEST ABOUT THESE

**Disease magnitude:** The 25–45% mortality estimate for a naive population
encountering the Roman 113 AD disease package is an inference from TB and malaria
epidemiology in historically analogous contact situations. It is not a number from
a controlled study. The actual figure could be lower (if the contacted population
had some cross-immunity from their own endemic pathogens) or higher (if multiple
simultaneous infections create cascading mortality). The range is honest; a
specific number is not.

**Smallpox status in 113 AD Rome:** The best current scholarship favors "not yet
endemic." This is not certain. A sufficiently pessimistic reading of the Antonine
Plague scholarship could place smallpox in Rome as early as the 100s AD. The novel
takes the most defensible position (not yet endemic) while flagging the uncertainty
in Daniel's own voice.

**First contact location:** The Columbus route makes the Bahamas or Greater Antilles
the most probable first landfall, but variations in the specific route (how far
south the ships ride the trade winds, whether they overshoot) could put them as
far north as Bermuda or as far south as the Lesser Antilles. The first-contact
population changes significantly depending on this. The novel can afford to be
specific about the route ("southwest to the latitude of the Canaries, then west")
without specifying the exact landfall; leave the specific island unnamed, which
is also historically accurate to how expeditions logged first contact.

**What the local person's experience is:** This is the area of greatest imaginative
responsibility. The novel treats this person as a person — specific, frightened,
capable, with their own understanding of what has happened to them — not as a
symbol or a moral prop. Their name is not given an easy Latin or Greek equivalent.
Their reaction to Rome is not wonder; it is probably something closer to the
specific numbness of someone who has survived something incomprehensible and is
waiting to find out if it kills them.

**Colonial independence timeline:** The 70–100 year estimate for meaningful
practical independence is modeled loosely on the British-American arc and is a
narrative heuristic, not a prediction. Real colonial independence dynamics vary
enormously based on the economic structure of the colony, the severity of
metropolitan crises during the key formative period, and whether the colony has
internal governance capacity. The estimate is reasonable for a colony that has
institutional foundations (which Daniel designs for) and a metropolitan power
facing compounding crises (which Rome will, starting with the Antonine Plague).
