# Master Outline — *The Long Way Home*

Seven parts plus a coda and epilogue. 53 chapters, approximately 3,000 words each.
~155,000 words target. First-person past tense, narrated by an older Daniel.

**The book's argument:** An ordinary teenager — not a prodigy, not an engineer — armed
only with what a standard 2020s education gives you, could change history. The bottleneck
is not genius. It is courage, credibility, language, money, and doing things in the right
order.

---

## Part I: Arrival and Survival (98 AD, ch01–11)

**What the part must accomplish:** Strand Daniel, make survival the only goal, prove the
world is real and lethal, and get him from mute prisoner to "useful curiosity." End: he
commits to the balloon because it is cheap, his alone, and undeniable.

**The arc:** Spring 98 AD. Daniel wakes in a harvested field outside Rome with a dying
phone. Two weeks of dwindling phone use — he wastes the start panicking, writes rushed
partial notes, then it goes dark for good. Arrest, the rope, the cell. Heras is called
to examine him. The numerals demonstration earns him "useful curiosity" status. Latin
begins (gestures to words). He is placed in Macer's household under loose guard.

**Required beats this part:**

*Lead pipes (earliest possible):* Daniel sees lead pipes in a Roman kitchen or cistern
and makes the connection. He stops using lead cooking vessels in his own space immediately.
He advises Heras and Macer against defrutum (grape must reduced in lead vessels). He
tries and fails to change anything at scale — the city's lead infrastructure is too large
and too expensive to retrofit. His own household becomes a small island. This honest
defeat is stated once and not revisited as a complaint.

*Soap (Phase A, first year):* Daniel notices there is no soap in a Roman household. He
makes it: rendered tallow plus wood-ash lye, boiled together, poured to set. It is harsh
and skin-burning. He iterates ratios over weeks. He gives the recipe to Heras. Whether
Heras uses it depends on social dynamics; the making of it is on the page.

*Eratosthenes demonstration:* Two sticks, two shadows, a sunny day. Daniel performs this
for Heras — Earth's circumference from shadow-angle difference at two locations, or at
one location with a known distance to a reference point he invents. Zero materials needed
beyond the sticks and sun. This is the demonstration that establishes "pointing works"
before any tech breakthrough. He will write it in the first encyclopedia draft.

*Gambling culture discovered:* Daniel loses coins at Roman dice within his first weeks.
He catalogs the observation. A few months later he finds a child playing terni lapilli
and has a moment of pure temporal vertigo — the game scratched into Roman pavements for
centuries, exactly as he knows it. He says nothing. The sportsbook idea clicks into place
around the same time: not a plan, just a mental note filed alongside "lead pipes" and
"no soap." Romans bet on everything. He is the only person in Rome who understands
expected value.

*Double-entry bookkeeping seeded:* By the time Daniel is embedded in Macer's household
(end of Part I, early Part II), he has introduced double-entry bookkeeping to Macer's
accounts. One junior accountant traces the columns and sees the audit advantage. Macer
himself may not fully capitulate yet; the system is present in practice.

*Balloon development begins (unmanned):* Daniel commits to the balloon as his first real
project. Cheap materials (linen, tallow/resin sealant), his concept entirely, no
precedent in Rome. Failed experiments with paper, then linen, then fire management.
The unmanned balloon is the thing that will make him un-ignorable.

*Food crisis:* Daniel's first meal in Rome is shown through what he does and doesn't eat,
not through interior complaint. Garum. No tomatoes, no potatoes, no anything he
recognizes as normal food except bread, olive oil, eggs. He adapts without making speeches
about it.

*Food arc — Part I:* The garum evolution is the part's quiet through-line: disgust on
first encounter (the smell, the pervasiveness), avoidance, then sometime in ch05 territory
the recognition that clicks — it is fish sauce, it is umami, it is the same thing that
makes a ramen broth work. He stops complaining and starts using it. The evolution is shown
across chapters 03–05 in small moments, never announced. Also in this part: his first
private food experiment, an omelette made for himself alone — eggs, whatever herb is to
hand, a pan and oil — not for the household, not as a demonstration, a small act of being
himself. Someone in the household sees it and says something like "that is how barbarians
eat." Both statements are true.

**Part I ends:** He has committed to the balloon project. He has a patron, a language,
and a small collection of practical wins that demonstrate value without requiring anyone
to trust him on faith.

## Part II: The Man Who Flew (99–102 AD, ch12–22)

**What the part must accomplish:** The unmanned balloon makes him un-ignorable. Macer's
patronage gives protection and a cage. The workshop spreads the first technologies. The
army wants flight. The First Dacian War shows Daniel the price of his gifts. End: he
returns from the Danube changed, asking how far to go.

**The arc:** 100 AD — the unmanned demonstration balloon rises in public. Sensation.
Macer invests; the workshop is founded. Tyche is assigned to Daniel. The military contact
begins through Celer. The First Dacian War (101–102 AD) is where the kite reconnaissance
system gets its first real test.

**Required beats this part:**

*Stirrups:* During army contact with Celer — the first time Daniel is in proximity to
Roman cavalry — he sees a cavalryman mount and immediately registers the absence of
stirrups. He does not need to research this; he knows from a thousand Westerns and
medieval movies that foot-loops make mounted combat viable at speed. He sketches a
leather loop design and gives it to Celer. Celer does the math himself. The army may
be slow to adopt it, but the design enters the record here, in Part II. By the end of
Part III, "the new foot-loops" are referenced as spreading standard practice. By ~108 AD
they are universal in the cavalry arm.

*Crossbow spanning lever:* When Daniel first gets serious access to Roman military
equipment through Celer, he sees the arcuballista (Roman crossbow) and understands what
it's missing. He doesn't know the term "goat's-foot lever" but he knows the concept:
mechanical advantage applied to the string. A simple L-shaped hook on a lever that
catches the string and multiplies force. He sketches it. Hermes builds a prototype.
The pitch to Celer leads with the training-time argument, not the power argument:
"How many of your replacements arrive knowing how to shoot a bow?" Legions constantly
replace casualties; longbow skill requires childhood training; crossbow skill requires
weeks. Celer does the math. This is one of the cleaner instances of Daniel's cognitive
edge — he leads with what the other person actually cares about.

*Kite reconnaissance system:* By late 100 AD, Daniel has a stable delta kite system with
a tethered observer platform and signal-cord relay. A man at 150 feet of altitude can see
a mile in every direction across forested terrain. This is not fragile — he has been
iterating for two years by the time of the First Dacian War. The system is operational
before the Danube crossing.

*First Dacian War — history measurably changed:* In real history, Trajan walked into at
least two successful Dacian ambushes that cost him heavily and forced strategic pauses. In
this book, both ambushes are detected from kite altitude before the column enters the
killing ground. The engagements still occur — the Dacians are not idiots — but the Romans
are not surprised. Over the campaign this translates to roughly 900 fewer Roman dead, and
to Trajan's unbroken confidence in the aerial system. This is the book's first measurable
divergence from real history. It becomes doctrine: Trajan asks two questions about the
balloon system (Can it carry fire? Can an enemy shoot it down?) and assigns a permanent
unit of balloon operators to the Praetorian camp before the year is out.

*Sportsbook begins:* Daniel runs a small sportsbook operation through an intermediary.
He does not do this openly. The mathematics of expected value — setting odds so that
the total implied probability across all outcomes exceeds 100% — is rendered on the page
without spelling it out. The other bookmakers near the Circus don't understand why they
keep losing to him. This is his first independent income, before the workshop revenue
solidifies. It also teaches him Roman social dynamics faster than anything else: who has
money, what they care about, how rumors spread, which officials are reachable.

*First card game introduced:* At a workshop gathering — mixed social strata at the same
table — Daniel introduces Go Fish or War. The social reaction: confusion, then delight,
then an argument about rules in two languages. He realizes what he has. The card design
carries three simultaneous information systems on every numbered card: pip count (no
literacy required), Roman numeral (what Romans already recognize), Hindu-Arabic numeral
(what Daniel is trying to spread). One object, three audiences.

*The manned balloon disaster:* The vow after Sabinus's death is stated on the page as:
*"I won't put untrained people into an untested design."* This is a rational engineer's
safety protocol, not a traumatized teenager's superstition. The distinction is explicit.
The vow covers untrained operators in untested designs — it does not cover kite rigs with
nobody aboard, and it does not cover systematically tested manned platforms once the
testing is done. Unmanned and kite-class glider work resumes within one to two years.
Manned flight returns as a live question within that same window.

*Food arc — Part II:* Now that the workshop is founded and Daniel has a kitchen and
income, food experimentation becomes possible. Pasta is introduced here — durum flour and
eggs, rested dough, rolled thin, cut into strips, boiled in salted water and served with
oil and garum and herbs. Not laganum baked in a dish; the boiled-then-sauced thing Rome
does not do yet. He makes it, serves it at workshop gatherings, watches people eat it.
Some find the texture wrong. Nobody makes a speech about it. The workshop gatherings
establish food as social lubricant in this part: people come partly because the food is
better than comparable Roman events at his level. Egg-forward dishes (the frittata
principle — eggs, leeks, crumbled cheese, heat) appear at those gatherings. Around ch07
territory, Daniel finds Gnaeus — a tavern-keeper near the workshop — and makes a quiet
verbal arrangement: Daniel supplies certain recipes in exchange for a standing meal and a
small cut of anything Gnaeus puts on his menu that came from Daniel's kitchen. It is not
a business. Gnaeus thinks of Daniel as "the barbarian who has interesting ideas about food
and is good for business." That is the correct relationship.

*Prize innovation — Part I/II boundary (ch05/ch07 area):* The first informal prize post
appears in background during ch05 as Daniel is establishing workshop operations and
hitting early production problems. It is small — a glass clarity spec, posted through
a guild contact Macer introduces him to. Three craftsmen attempt it. One wins: a
glassmaker from Brundisium using a decolorant technique his father developed for
decorative vessels, which the man had no idea was relevant to optical glass. Daniel
paid two hundred sestertii and received a technique he did not know existed, solving a
problem he had been approaching from a different direction for over a year. The
model clarifies itself: he is not buying invented solutions. He is buying disclosure of
knowledge that already exists in Roman craft tradition but is invisible to him. He
writes down everything the Brundisium man tells him about his father's method. He does
not explain this to anyone.

**Part II ends:** Daniel returns from the Danube changed. He has seen what his gifts do
in the hands of generals. The kite system worked. Men are alive who would be dead. Other
men are dead because his engineering made the siege efficient. He doesn't know how to
hold both of those facts at once yet. He is twenty-one.

## Part III: How Far to Go (103–110 AD, ch23–33)

**What the part must accomplish:** The hard push. Steel, the press, the contest, freeing
Tyche, citizenship, Marcia. Run face-first into the chokepoints. End: a powerful, lonely
high point; the hubris-or-duty question named aloud for the first time.

**The arc:** Daniel back in Rome, rising. The workshop expands. Steel work begins with
Hermes (the chokepoint bites immediately — temperature control without pyrometry, carbon
atmosphere guesswork). Telescope frustration (Roman glass striae). The printing press
progresses from block prints to movable type. The story/writing contest launches. Germ
framing spreading through the physician line. Trajan grants Daniel citizenship (~104–105
AD). Marcia enters (~107–108). Tyche freed and apprenticed; English teaching begins as
"homeland script."

**Required beats this part:**

*Manned balloon archers doctrine by 106 AD:* By the Second Dacian War (105–106 AD),
Daniel has trained six balloon operators with 50+ hours of tethered flight time,
double-basket construction, explicit written safety protocols, and voluntary consent.
The military case is undeniable and Trajan has watched the First Dacian War. A hot-air
balloon with archers 200 feet up is practically unkillable with ground weapons and can
rain arrows — or in this book, bolts from pre-spanned crossbows — down on any formation.
(Crossbows are specified over bows for basket use by 108 AD: a pre-spanned crossbow in
a swaying confined basket requires only aim and trigger; a regular bow requires full arm
extension and a standing draw.) By 106 AD manned balloon archers are military doctrine.
No Roman general would accept "we stopped because one person died in a prototype" — they
do not apply that logic to catapults or siege engineers. Daniel makes the deliberate
calculation that military advantage saves more lives than the program risks, sets the
conditions, and makes the call. This is more honest than a ban that somehow survives
forty years of a man who iterates on everything else.

*Rag paper:* Macerate cloth rags, screen, dry. Han China had it in 105 AD. Daniel knows
the concept from a history class. He makes it in Part III. The first batches are rough
and expensive, but the material cost is a fraction of papyrus. By 110 AD, rag paper is
in use at the press.

*Compass prototype (~103–105 AD):* A magnetized needle floating in water or suspended
from thread, oriented by lodestone. Daniel has known the principle since before he
arrived. Getting the materials right — magnetizing an iron needle reliably, building a
consistent housing — takes time and iteration. By ~108 AD the compass is in reliable
prototype. He demonstrates it to Heras and to a naval contact. This enables the Atlantic
coastal route opened by compass (one trade partner makes a winter crossing that previously
required hugging the coast; by 110 AD this trade route is documented).

*Volta pile:* Zinc in Roman brass, copper, brine. Daniel knows this from a chemistry class.
Simple cells stacked in series. By ~108–110 AD he demonstrates the pile to Heras: shock
(press both terminals, take the jolt), electrolyze water into hydrogen and oxygen, run
current through a wire in a magnetic field and watch the compass needle deflect.
The galvanometer principle is demonstrated. No practical application found yet — Daniel
knows the thermocouple chain comes later. But the demonstration is on the page by 110 AD.

*The press and the writing contest (ch~19 area, 106 AD):* The contest launches on Daniel's
return from the Second Dacian War. He is flush with imperial favor, money, and standing.
He is also morally unsettled from Sarmizegetusa. The contest is, in part, a recalibration.
The stated occasion: a prize for the best tales of machines that do the work of a hundred
men, vehicles faster than horses, devices that reveal the invisible. The first contest
receives forty to sixty entries. Daniel reads everything himself. He discards most of
them immediately. He finds three or four that genuinely surprise him. One frightens him
slightly: a soldier's story about a machine that kills at a distance without the soldier
having to see the face of the man it kills. He prizes it anyway. He does not comment on
it aloud.

*Uncomfortable first entries:* Soldiers write about war with accurate brutality. Slaves
write about freedom with an anger that exposes Daniel's compromised position — he freed
Tyche, but he operates within and benefits from the system. One slave's story is
essentially about him, told with contempt. He prizes it for fiction. He doesn't tell
anyone why.

*Heliocentrism stated aloud:* Once Daniel has a standing relationship with educated Romans
in the educated circle (Heras, the contest community), he states aloud that Earth orbits
the Sun. He frames it as "the old Samian was right." The social consequences — mockery,
mild scandal, one person genuinely interested — register on the page. This is not a
lecture; it is one sentence dropped into a conversation that changes the room's temperature.

*Tyche's freedom (~104 AD):* Daniel pays Macer for Tyche's freedom. Cost: roughly
75,000–80,000 HS including a starting settlement. Macer finds this baffling and slightly
insulting. Tyche is not grateful in a sentimental direction. She has her own agenda,
always has had. She begins learning English as "the homeland script."

*First formal casino:* Small, attached to workshop space. The legal question handled
through Macer (who either laughs at Daniel for worrying or tells him exactly who to pay).
Multiple card tables, house dealers, printed rule sheets posted on the wall, betting
tokens that exchange for coin. The backgammon rules he knows (from tabula's Roman cousin)
and the updated latrunculi rules spread in the background — he doesn't push them; people
just find them sharper.

*Epidemic test (germ theory):* A localized outbreak — dysentery or typhoid — in a dense
urban district. Daniel's practices (boiling, washing, isolation of the sick) are applied
in one quarter and not in an adjacent one. The death toll difference is measurable and a
skeptic notices. The impiety charge gets raised: if the invisible creatures cause disease
and handwashing stops them, then preventing the sickness is also preventing the gods'
will. Daniel does not resolve this argument. He lets Heras respond through the physician
line. At least one named secondary character dies. The debate normalizes the empirical
framing in educated circles without winning it.

*Divergence checklist on the page by 110 AD:*
- Stirrups in use by at least one cavalry unit ("the new foot-loops" referenced as
  spreading standard practice)
- Soap sold or distributed from Daniel's workshop (even niche adoption)
- Rag paper in use at the press
- Double-entry bookkeeping in Macer's accounts; at least one trade partner using
  Hindu-Arabic numerals without Daniel present to explain them
- A specific military dispatch Daniel reads notes the contrast between cohorts using
  "the barbarian's water discipline" and those that don't — specific unit numbers
- Magnetic compass in reliable prototype, demonstrated to Heras or a naval contact
- Printing press has produced at least 500 copies of one text circulating beyond Rome
- One trade route opened by compass enabling open-sea navigation
- Volta pile demonstrated as shock-and-electrolyze demonstration

*Food arc — Part III:* The verbal arrangement with Gnaeus solidifies into a standing
partnership. A few dishes from Daniel's repertoire — the pasta preparation, the egg-forward
dishes, a couple of sauce preparations — become permanent items on Gnaeus's rotating menu.
Daniel gets a small cut. He does not manage the place. Gnaeus resists Daniel's push toward
doing fewer things very well; they compromise; the result is a thermopolium that is
slightly better than average, not a culinary revolution, which is the correct outcome.
Sauce work is the part's other food note: no tomatoes yet, so Daniel works with what
exists. He makes a fig-and-vinegar reduction — figs or plums reduced with vinegar, honey,
cumin — because he wants ketchup and cannot have it. He uses it on roasted pork and with
cheese. He knows it is not what he wanted. He uses it anyway. One or two sentences, no
pathos. By the end of this part, garum is a standard tool in his cooking. He no longer
thinks about the smell.

*Prize innovation — Part III:* The specs become semi-public in this part — appearing in
the press's broadsheet alongside commercial notices. Multiple open simultaneously.
First appearance of Glabrio (ch19 or ch21): a bronzesmith who shows up in prize records
Daniel reviews — attempting the steam cylinder tolerance prize, the alloy composition
prize — getting close each time, never quite winning. Daniel knows his name. They have
never spoken directly. Glabrio's repeated near-misses are a small running acknowledgment
that the model distributes risk by transferring some of it to people who cannot fully
afford it. Also in this part: the ten-year lens-grinding spec, posted around ch15 during
the steel and press work. The prize attracts the workshop community as a recurring
background fact. It will not be won for nine years. Also in Part III (ch25–27 range):
Crispus figures out the aggregate. Not the individual prizes — those are just commercial
transactions — but the pattern: Daniel is posting specs that collectively describe a
technological program, paying for solutions without directing approaches, watching what
Roman craft knowledge produces when given an economic target. Crispus's realization is
fear plus reluctant admiration. He does not say it aloud except perhaps to Vibenius.
The texture of his antagonism shifts: he attacks the model's cultural legitimacy
(prizes destabilize craft guilds' traditional authority structures) rather than continuing
the blunter fraud accusations. This makes him harder to answer. He is partly right.

**Part III ends:** Peak building. Daniel has citizenship, a wife, a workshop, a press, a
contest, a freed apprentice who is teaching herself English. He is twenty-eight and the
most interesting foreigner in Rome. The hubris-or-duty question is named aloud for the
first time, probably by Heras. It does not get answered.

## Part IV: Too Valuable (110–117 AD, ch34–38)

**What the part must accomplish:** Peak influence equals peak danger. The three faces of
danger (Scaeva, Crispus, Vibenius) close in. A near-fatal accusation. The Parthian War
pulls him east. Trajan dies and blindsides him. End: the ground drops out.

**The arc:** 112–113 AD — Trajan's Forum and Column dedicated. The Parthian War begins
(113). Daniel at peak influence and peak danger. Scaeva tries to use him as a factional
tool. Crispus escalates his fraud/impiety accusations. Vibenius holds the religion card.
A near-fatal crisis that usefulness or a well-bought omen barely survives. Daniel works
army logistics and medicine during the Parthian campaign (dysentery is still the army's
biggest killer; his boiling protocols get tested in the field). The world-map ocean gambit
begins forming — the Parthian overreach shows him what happens to an empire that runs
further east than its supply lines. 117 AD: Trajan dies of a stroke at Selinus in Cilicia.
Daniel is blindsided.

**Required beats this part:**

*Balloon archers in the Parthian War:* By 113 AD, manned balloon crossbowmen are standard
formation attachments (two rigs per legion on campaign). Balloon archers wreck Parthian
cavalry tactics in set-piece engagements. The Parthians' main tactical asset — cataphract
and horse-archer formations relying on choosing when and where to engage — depends on the
ability to concentrate suddenly. A balloon observer identifies cavalry concentrations before
they can close. Roman tactical losses in set-piece engagements are substantially lower than
real history. The siege of Ctesiphon falls approximately two weeks faster through aerial
observation of the city's layout and defender positions. The Kitos War revolts (115–117 AD)
are unchanged in cause — they are driven by Jewish diaspora rage and Roman provincial
misgovernance, problems that balloons and arithmetic cannot address. Communication
improvements (printed maps, standardized orders duplicated in dozens of copies) shorten
the suppression response by weeks. The strategic outcome is unchanged: Trajan still
retreats. Mesopotamia is still ungovernable from Rome at 1,500-mile supply lines.

*Gunpowder leak incident:* The book's stated number-one danger is dramatized once. A
craftsman who worked on the niter beds is approached by someone in a Parthian diplomat's
household, or a letter arrives at the workshop asking specific questions about saltpeter
composition with a provenance that traces eastward. Daniel hears about it and acts. He
contains it — but the containment was not automatic. The specific mechanism matters:
the language barrier, the social network, the printed codex versus the oral secret. The
scene makes clear that the leak risk requires active management, not just worry.

*Crispus — public credibility collapse begins:* One witnessed event where a prediction
or claim of Crispus's (about Daniel's work, about the gods' verdict, about some technical
matter) is visibly refuted by physical evidence. A public figure — someone whose opinion
matters — says aloud that Crispus was wrong. Daniel hears about it secondhand or witnesses
it from a distance. The scene is economical: half a chapter or less. The point is not his
humiliation but his erasure — he stops mattering, on the page, in a single moment the
reader witnesses.

*Vibenius — debt called once:* Before Vibenius's death (which should be at least partially
on-page in Part IV), Daniel calls the debt from ch27 once. The favor should be something
that cannot be refused but that costs Vibenius something real: access to a specific record,
a word to a specific magistrate, a passage in his will benefiting the workshop collegium.
Vibenius pays it — not with grace, but with the quiet acknowledgment of a man honoring a
debt he cannot pretend does not exist. Then he dies, with dignity, on his own terms. The
ambiguity of the original liver reading (was it a lie or a true reading that happened to be
convenient?) is preserved but now it has been transacted rather than dissolved by death.

*Atlantic crossing planning becomes serious by ~108–110 AD:* The compass is working by
103–105 AD. Trade wind knowledge is immediate — Daniel has had it in his head since
arrival. By ~108–110 AD, with the compass proven and money accumulating, serious
expedition planning begins. Daniel knows the Columbus route: southwest to approximately
28°N latitude, catch the northeast trade winds, five to six weeks, return by sailing
north to where the westerlies blow east. He does not need a marine chronometer for the
basic crossing — Columbus did it without one in 1492. Roman cargo ships of 300–500 tons
are already better than the Nina and the Pinta. The planning is underway in Part IV;
the ship departs in Part V.

*Trajan dies (117 AD):* Daniel is blindsided. He did not know the precise date, only the
approximate window. Whatever political network he has built is built around Trajan and
Trajan's circle. When Trajan dies at Selinus in Cilicia, Daniel's position — not his
life, but his leverage and his patron-web — evaporates.

*Food arc — Part IV:* The first New World crops arrive in this part's second half, once
the Atlantic expedition returns (~115–120 AD) and the agricultural lag of one to two
growing seasons passes. Sweet potato first: he roasts one whole in a fire within the
first week of receiving tubers. He eats it plain. It is sweet and filling and it needs
nothing added to it. He serves them at the workshop. They are an immediate hit because
they are unlike anything Rome eats and genuinely good. Corn follows: he makes rough
polenta from the first maize — cornmeal cooked slowly in water with salt, served with
olive oil and hard cheese — and serves it to the workshop. Dense and unusual. He knows
what it will become. Then the tomato moment. He grows them from seed, loses the first
season mostly, gets a real harvest the second. He makes a basic tomato sauce — tomatoes
crushed and reduced with oil and garlic, a bit of garum for depth — and puts it on pasta.
The pasta he has been making for years. The sauce he has been unable to make for years.
He eats it. He puts down the bowl. He says nothing. Three sentences. No commentary. If
anyone else is present, they notice he has gone quiet. Nobody asks why. Within weeks,
ketchup: tomatoes, vinegar, onion, spices, reduced thick, given to Gnaeus without
explanation. Gnaeus puts it on the menu. Note: Daniel pushed for the Atlantic crossing
partly because he knew what was growing there. He never said this aloud.

*Prize innovation — Part III/IV boundary (ch24–29 range):* The unexpected farrier's
pyrometry table. A horseshoe-maker, not a smith Daniel would have thought to approach,
submits a heat-color correlation table for iron — developed over years of judging iron
readiness for horseshoe shaping. Someone told him the workshop paid for things like that.
The table is cruder than Daniel's own data and more useful than Daniel's own data, because
the farrier calibrated it against thousands of actual horseshoes instead of a handful of
controlled experiments. Daniel sits with this. The prize model does not just accelerate
his program; it maps knowledge that already exists in Roman craft tradition — empirical
knowledge accumulated by a thousand farriers and glassmen and bronzesmiths — knowledge
that has never been written down or shared across trade boundaries because there was no
mechanism to make it visible. The prize created the mechanism. He does not say this aloud.
Also in Part IV (ch32–38 range): the failure mode. One prize winner sells the winning
technique to someone Daniel did not intend to have it — most likely a glass-grinding
method reaching a military optician working against Daniel's press interests, or an alloy
formula reaching a Parthian trade intermediary through a chain Daniel cannot trace. He
calculates whether the prize program is still net positive. It is. He does not change the
structure. One brief scene or half-scene; not a major thread.

**Part IV ends:** The ground drops out. Hadrian's succession is contested (widely believed
to be engineered by Plotina and Attianus). Daniel is on the wrong side of a transition.
He is thirty-six and will have to rebuild everything.

## Part V: Starting Over (117–125 AD, ch39–46)

**What the part must accomplish:** Succession terror — Hadrian, the Four Consulars.
The patron-web shredded. Rebuild middle-aged from scratch. The ocean-map gambit deployed.
Apollodorus falls. End: Daniel finds footing in Hadrian's consolidating order.

**The arc:** 117–118 AD — Hadrian proclaimed at Antioch (the deathbed adoption widely
doubted). 118 AD: the Four Consulars affair (Palma, Celsus, Nigrinus, Lusius Quietus
killed without trial) poisons the Hadrian/Senate relationship for years. Daniel's
patron-web is shredded; Macer's position shaken; protectors gone or compromised. He
must rebuild every relationship from scratch with an emperor who has different priorities
and who is, in his own way, more interesting than Trajan — a traveler, an administrator,
a man genuinely concerned with imperial cohesion. Daniel eventually finds this useful.

**Required beats this part:**

*Scaeva — debt-collection attempt and exit:* After the Four Consulars, Scaeva makes one
attempt to collect the "point the powder for me" IOU — claiming the powder knowledge,
using Daniel's name for political cover, or extracting a specific technical favor. Daniel
has anticipated this. The scene shows him deflecting Scaeva's attempt without violence,
using the legal and institutional protections he now has: the press, Hadrian's growing
attention, the endowed collegium that is not his personal property to bargain with. Scaeva
leaves with less than he came for. The scene demonstrates that Daniel is no longer the man
Scaeva lent weight to — he is now the institution, and the institution is not extractable.
Shortly after, Scaeva exits: one line confirming his death (plague, old age, or political
accident), not a scene.

*Scribes' guild backlash:* Around 122–124 AD, an organized scribes' guild petitions a
praetor asking that "the barbarian's machine" be taxed per copy produced, the revenue to
compensate scribes displaced. This is the correct response to labor displacement and Daniel
has anticipated it. The petition fails — the commercial class that benefits from cheap
contracts is larger and wealthier than the scribal class — but it happens, and Daniel
witnesses it. He does not feel good about winning that argument.

## Part VI: The Ladder (125–138 AD, ch47–50)

**What the part must accomplish:** Institution-building so the work outlives him. The
encyclopedia and the English cipher. Heras dies. Small signs the trajectory has bent.
The hubris-or-duty question at its sharpest under imperial brutality. End: Hadrian dies;
Daniel is old; the work is institutional.

**The arc:** 125–138 AD. The press, the contest, and the workshop are being endowed
as permanent institutions that do not require Daniel's personal authorization to function.
The encyclopedia is written in earnest — English first, then partial Latin for the parts
he wants found and understood by the next generation; other parts remain in English
intentionally, for the generation after that. Tyche and a small circle of apprentices
are learning English. Heras dies — rendered through action and absence, not stated. The
Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 AD) is suppressed approximately fourteen to eighteen months
faster than real history through aerial observation stripping Bar Kokhba's mountain-pass
advantage. Roman casualties are roughly 30–35% lower. The Jewish diaspora still occurs.
Judaea is still renamed Syria Palaestina. The tragedy is not averted; it is more efficient.
Daniel knows this and hates it.

**Required beats this part:**

*Thermocouple and galvanometer:* The Volta pile from Part III has been developing into a
measurement chain. Iron-copper thermocouple plus compass-needle galvanometer — calibrate
against known melting points to get temperature measurement. By Part VI this chain is
underway. Daniel is attempting to source platinum from Hispanian mines (platinum was found
in Spanish alluvial gold deposits from antiquity; he knows this from a geology class vague
memory). The thermocouple is not yet finished in his lifetime; the chain is established.

*Newcomen-class atmospheric pump trialed:* The first atmospheric steam pump operates
in a silver mine in the Carthago Nova region of Hispania around 118 AD — bronze cylinder,
leather piston, steam condensed by water injection, perhaps 200 gallons per hour, crude
and dangerous and barely efficient. By 128 AD, eight such pumps operate across the empire.
By Part VI the steam pump is established technology. Its primary narrative value: the
leather gaskets leak. When rubber samples return from the Atlantic expeditions, Daniel
immediately knows what he has — the gasket application alone, on pumps already running
in Roman mines, justifies the cost of the expedition several times over.

*Encyclopedia written and secured:* The encyclopedia is Daniel's transmission mechanism,
not a shortcut. It is written across Parts V–VII and secured in multiple copies by the
end of Part VI: three copies in distributed libraries, one buried lead-cased copy whose
location Tyche knows. The English sections contain what Daniel cannot say safely in
Latin: the communication-breakdown analysis of colonial independence, the moral reckoning
on the western contact, the railroad argument in full technical detail, the variolation
protocol. The Latin sections contain what he wants found and acted on: the astronomical
proofs, the germ framing, the double-entry accounting system, the numerical primer. The
cipher is a deliberate design: the English sections are for a generation that decodes
them, not the generation that finds them.

*Contest self-sustaining:* By the Part V–VI transition, the contest is the *Collegium
Certaminis Fabularum* — a chartered institution with a permanent secretariat, a rolling
seven-person judge panel, an annual anthology published under a standing contract that
does not require Daniel's personal authorization, a capital fund managed independently,
and library endowment agreements with libraries across the empire. The anthology sales
percentage covers the prize pool. Daniel's subsidy has gone to zero. He reads the results
in the press like everyone else.

## Part VII / Coda: The Long Way Home (138–155 AD, ch51–53)

**What the part must accomplish:** Daniel old, declining, securing the ladder. The last
deaths (Macer, Marcia, others). Tyche and the apprentices carry it. Daniel dies far from
home, most dreams unbuilt, but the bottom rungs moved. End: a post-death Roman document.

**The arc:** Daniel in his final decades. He can no longer make the voyage to Gades
himself. He watches expedition departures from reports, not from docks. The people he
trained are running the programs now. He writes, secures the encyclopedia, arranges
the legal structures for the endowments. Marcia dies. Macer dies. Tyche is the last
person he can speak to in English with anything approaching honesty.

**Required beats this part:**

*Second organized Atlantic expedition departs (~149 AD):* Daniel watches it leave.
He is seventy-something and the last one standing on the dock. He knows the ship will
find land. He knows the land is inhabited. He knows the lie he told on his first day
in Rome — "uninhabited western continents" — will be corrected by the ship's return,
after he is gone. The "all tracks lead to Rome" line belongs here, or nearby. The roads
all run to Rome; Daniel is watching something leave Rome on a track that runs the other

*"All tracks lead to Rome":* This line — the reframing of the roads-to-Rome cliché —
appears in Part VII if not earlier. Spoken by Daniel, by Felix, or by a later voice in
the contest community. The railroad he argued for and couldn't build. The ships that sail
away. The people who will read his encyclopedia. Everything he built is a track leading
somewhere Rome can't fully control. This is his legacy: not what Rome became, but what
left Rome.

*Variolation groundwork:* Through the physician line (Heras → Zoticus), Daniel sets up
a crude, ethically fraught program of deliberate low-dose exposure experiments. He knows
the Antonine Plague is coming around 165 AD. He will be dead by then. He cannot stop it.
He can change what Rome does when it arrives. The variolation program is groundwork —
not a vaccine program, not a medical triumph, just the institutional precondition for
quarantine doctrine and the empirical investigation of immunity. He writes the protocol
in the encyclopedia. He trains Zoticus. Whether it will hold is the question he cannot
answer.

*Encyclopedia secured (three copies, one buried):* Three copies distributed to major
libraries. One buried in a lead-cased box, the location known to Tyche. The English
sections contain the things Daniel cannot say safely in Latin. He writes the passage
about the western contact in English, in the workshop, and seals it.

*All three antagonist exits completed before Part VII closes:* Crispus erased (completed
in Part V). Scaeva deflected and dead (completed in Part V). Vibenius's debt called and
death witnessed (completed in Part IV). By Part VII, all three are settled history.

*Tomato, one sentence:* After return news confirms the Atlantic crops are arriving —
after the tomato is in Roman hands — Daniel tastes one. One sentence. No speech.

*The "Prize for Demonstrated Truth" endowment in the will:* A capital fund held in trust,
deliberately not activated in Daniel's lifetime. The will specifies: "Hold this until
there are enough claimants to argue." The prize fund is for demonstrated empirical
contribution — too small a population of researchers to matter now, but the fund will
wait. A prize on a long fuse.

*Daniel dies (~155 AD, age ~74):* Far from Ohio. Knowing: a colony exists or is being
established. Not knowing: if it has survived its first epidemic season; what Rome has
decided to do with the new world; if the local people he briefly met are alive or dead
or enslaved; if the encyclopedia in the lead box will ever be read. He knows the crossing
happened because he built the ship and was on the dock when it left. He does not make
peace with the western program. He does not resolve it. He writes it in English and seals
the box. He makes sure Tyche knows where it is buried.

The phone is buried with him.

The correct emotional note at death: not triumphant, not despairing. The specific
ambivalence of a man who built something larger than he intended and cannot see where
it lands. He knows something is in motion. He does not know what it will become.

**Post-death Roman documents:** Ch53 (or within Part VII) presents post-death documents
discovering Daniel's work: the steam-cart toy in a senator's letter; the phone described
as an amulet; Crispus's coined phrase outliving him. Dramatic irony — they see the
objects, not the idea. The contest is still running. The press is still printing.

## Epilogue: Someone Climbs

A jump forward — "a generation, then a distant whisper." Not dated precisely. Enough
time for Procula's escapement solution to have propagated through a second generation
of horologists and navigators. Enough time for the contest to have generated inventors
who learned from other inventors, not from Daniel.

The press still runs. The contest is still held. A young reader — the kind of person
who would have found the encyclopedia's English sections and spent years decoding them —
solves something with what they find.

**Required beats this epilogue:**

*Atlantic landfall confirmed inhabited:* A single ship, generations up the ladder Daniel
laid, has crossed the western ocean and stands off an unknown western coast. The
navigation suite: gimbaled sea-clock (descended from Procula's escapement, longitude
now solved), compass, latitude measurement, printed charts. The shore is inhabited —
a tended fire, drawn-up boats, figures at the waterline, more smokes up the hills.
Daniel's "uninhabited" lie is corrected by the physical fact. No landing, no conquest
depicted. The book ends on the approach: the moment before anything is decided.

*New World crops arriving:* Corn, sweet potato, cacao already circulating back through
Rome. The economic argument for the ocean program has been made and is running. Roman
political ambition is shifting from "hold the borders" to "there is more world."

*Contest and press running without Daniel:* The contest entrants have been teaching each
other for decades. A British miner designed a cam-driven ore-crushing machine without
any knowledge of Daniel's workshop, simply by watching contest entries for fifteen years
and iterating on what he saw. The chain is running on its own momentum.

*"All tracks lead to Rome" (if not spoken earlier):* The phrase lands here in its final
form, from whichever voice carries it — Daniel's, Felix's, or a later generation's in
the contest community. All roads lead to Rome; all tracks lead away from it.

*Daniel forgotten:* He survives as a worn, half-legible name at the foot of the oldest
copied navigation table. The pilot at the rail does not know who put the numbers in the
table. The book ends there. Unresolved, on purpose.

**The contest flywheel:** Nine loops traceable from Part III through the epilogue. Do not
narrate the loops — render them in action, dialogue, and observed consequences. Never have
Daniel explain the flywheel to anyone. Show the press's revenue line, not a speech about

**The steel wall:** Reduced but not gone. Thermocouple gives temperature measurement;
the residual wall is carbon-atmosphere control in the crucible melt. Show it narrowing
over decades, never fully solved in Daniel's lifetime. The wall is there in every Part from
III onward — acknowledged once per part at most, in a single line, never dwelt on.

**Endings:** Max one gnomic / wisdom-button chapter ending in three chapters; aim for zero.
End on image, action, dialogue, or silence. Model: Heras's death chapter (empty bench =
grief). The phone's death in Part I. The dock at Gades. These are the book's emotional
registers — earned through restraint, not announcement.
