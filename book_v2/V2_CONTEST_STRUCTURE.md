# V2 Writing Contest Reference — *The Long Way Home*

**Read this before writing any chapter that references the contest.** This document
synthesizes all authoritative contest information for the V2 writing agent. It replaces
scattered references in V2_STORY_PLOT_NOTES.md (Section 7) and chapter_list.md.

When in conflict with V1 chapter text, this document wins.

---

## 1. WHAT THE CONTEST IS

### Origin
The contest launches in ch19 (106 AD), on Daniel's return from the Second Dacian War.
He is flush with imperial favor, money, and standing he did not have before. He is also
morally unsettled from Sarmizegetusa: his gifts have served slaughter efficiently. The
contest is, in part, a recalibration — proof that the press and his cultural position
can serve something other than siege logistics.

The stated occasion is simple: a prize for the best tales of *machines that do the work
of a hundred men, vehicles faster than horses, devices that reveal the invisible.* The
launch framing is modest. Daniel does not announce a cultural institution. He announces
a prize with money attached and a submission deadline.

### Stated Purpose (what Daniel says publicly)
A prize contest to encourage stories about machines, wonders, and natural philosophy —
fiction and speculation welcome, all classes eligible to enter.

### Actual Purpose (what Daniel understands privately)
Multiple, layered, and not all acknowledged even to himself at launch:

1. **Seeding a civilization that dreams in technology before the technology exists.**
   The Jules Verne principle: fiction normalizes possibility. If Romans grow up reading
   contest stories about flying machines and invisible creatures that cause disease, the
   next generation of craftsmen and physicians asks different questions.

2. **Building a self-funding cultural institution** that makes his press, his libraries,
   and his paper more valuable — the 9-loop flywheel (see Section 3).

3. **Political protection through cultural centrality.** Being Rome's Hollywood is a
   different kind of armor than money or patronage. You cannot easily silence the man
   who publishes what Rome reads.

4. **Seeding his own knowledge base** into Roman culture through his own story entries:
   plots he remembers from high school, framed as oral traditions from Thule. (See
   Section 7.)

5. **Subverting class capture.** The dual-prize structure (see Section 5) is deliberate:
   it prevents the educated class from owning the contest entirely, which would eventually
   kill it as a populist institution.

Daniel does not articulate all five of these at launch. He is 25 years old and building
intuitively. The full understanding develops over the first decade of the contest's
operation.

## 2. OPERATIONAL STRUCTURE

### Frequency
Annual or biannual. Annual in early years; the schedule adjusts when submission volume
grows too large. A biannual contest is more manageable once entries number in the
hundreds. Do not set the schedule in stone in early chapters — let it evolve on the page.

### Round 1: Culling by Paid Judges

**Target:** 1,000 entries reduced to 20 finalists.

**Mechanics:** Daniel hires educated readers — literate freedmen, low-status scribes,
teachers without wealthy patrons — to read and score submissions. They are paid per batch
reviewed. This is deliberate: Daniel is funding literacy as labor. The judges are not
volunteers; they are workers. A slave who reads well can be hired. A freedwoman who reads
well can be hired. The contest's economic infrastructure runs on the people it ostensibly
serves.

**Early-contest scaling problem:** In the first contest (ch19), entries arrive in dozens,
not hundreds. The 1,000-entry target is years away. In the early years, Daniel reads
everything himself. This has consequences for what he finds and how he reacts (see
Section 6, Part III beats).

**Judge panel composition (mature contest, Part V onward):**
- 5-7 standing judges, renewed on a rolling basis so no single panel member builds
  permanent gatekeeping power
- Deliberately cross-class: at least one judge with a freedman or slave background
- At least one woman in the panel by Part VI (after the woman-wins beat in ch41)
- Daniel is not on the panel after Part III — too politically exposed; his
  involvement becomes administrative rather than evaluative

**Scoring criteria given to judges:**
- Does the story hold together? (Internal consistency)
- Does it contain something that surprised the judge? (Novelty requirement)
- Would someone read it aloud in a tavern and hold the room? (Populist test)

The third criterion is what allows crude early entries to survive the round. It also
seeds the public-readings format of Round 2.

### Round 2: Public Readings and Voting

**Venues:** Taverns, the Forum, the theater. Public readings of all 20 finalists.
In early years this is 20 readings scattered across a week. In mature years it becomes
a civic event, scheduled, with crowds.

**Voting mechanics — two options; both may be used simultaneously:**
- **Token voting:** Audience members receive a clay token at entry; they deposit it
  in the basket for the story they prefer at the end. Daniel borrows this from gladiatorial
  crowd dynamics — Romans understand voting with objects.
- **Raised hands / cheers:** The cruder, faster, more energetic version. Works better
  in tavern settings where tokens would be chaotic.

The reading format means oral performance quality affects the public vote — a well-read
story benefits from a skilled reader even if the writing is rough. Daniel is aware of
this variable. He does not fix it. It is part of what makes the public prize genuinely
popular: live performance matters.

### The Two Prizes

**The People's Prize** — determined by public vote across the Round 2 readings.
**The Judges' Prize** — determined by the standing panel's evaluation of finalists.

**Critical structural feature:** These prizes frequently go to DIFFERENT stories.
This is not a failure condition. It is the contest's most valuable design feature.

The tension between the two prizes is itself a story the press covers annually. Readers
form opinions about which prize matters more. Factions develop. Felix, who submits
repeatedly across Parts III-VI, probably wins the People's Prize before the Judges'
Prize (or vice versa), and the gap between his two prizes is a minor running thread.

A working-class story in rough Latin — told from a dockworker's perspective, maybe by
someone from that class — can win the People's Prize while the Judges' Prize goes to
an elegant philosophical piece about the nature of machines. Daniel loves this outcome
quietly. He engineered it. It prevents the educated class from capturing the contest.

The press covers both prizes with equal ceremony. A story that wins only the People's
Prize is not reported as a lesser victory. This editorial choice is deliberate.

### Prize Money

- **Grand prize (either category):** 100-500 sesterces. Adjust within this range by
  era — early contest prizes are on the lower end; mature contest prizes reflect the
  contest's increased prestige and Daniel's deeper funding.
- **Category prizes (regional runners-up, subcategory winners):** 10-50 sesterces.
- **Finalist stipend:** Each of the 20 finalists receives a small amount — 5-10 sesterces
  — just for reaching Round 2. This matters to the contest's class accessibility. A
  slave who reaches the finals with their owner's permission receives this; Daniel has
  thought through the legal mechanics of how a slave receives prize money and built in
  a mechanism for it.

Self-funding mechanism (Phase D/E, Part V onward): A percentage of anthology sales — the
annual printed collection of prize-winning and notable stories — funds the following year's
prize pool. Once this exceeds Daniel's annual subsidy, the contest is financially
self-sustaining. This crossover point should be placed around ch38-39 (Part V-VI
transition, 120s AD).

## 3. THE 9-LOOP FLYWHEEL

Each loop must be traceable in the prose across the book's parts. This section describes
how each loop manifests in specific scenes and chapters. Writers should not narrate the
loops — they should render them in action, dialogue, and observed consequences.

### Loop 1: More People Writing
**What it is:** Low barrier to entry ("just write a story, win money") draws participants
from every social class with minimal literacy. The prize is enough to motivate a working
man's week of effort.

**How it manifests:**
- ch19: First entries arrive. Soldiers write about war. Slaves write about freedom. Both
  categories make Daniel uncomfortable for different reasons (see Section 6). The
  uncomfortable categories are not disqualified.
- Part IV (chs 25-31): Entry volume has grown substantially. Daniel no longer reads
  everything himself; the paid-judge system exists.
- Part VI (chs 39-46): Entries arrive from outside Rome — from provincial towns, from
  military postings, from merchants who carry the contest announcement with their trade
  goods. The printed submission rules travel as far as the press's distribution network.

### Loop 2: More People Reading
**What it is:** More stories means more readers. Each contest cycle produces a new
anthology. Libraries stock the anthologies. Literate people read the stories; some of
them teach others to read because they want to read the stories.

**How it manifests:**
- The anthology is the contest's primary print product. It is Daniel's press's
  most reliable seller.
- ch41 (Part VI): Felix teaches a dockworker to read specifically so the man can enter
  the contest (or read Felix's entry). This is never presented as a lesson; it is a
  transaction between two men who want something from each other.
- Part VI: Literacy rates in specific districts near Daniel's libraries measurably tick
  up. This is a one-line observation in a document Daniel reads, not a speech.

### Loop 3: Tech Inspiration (Jules Verne Effect)
**What it is:** Contest stories about flying machines, invisible disease-creatures, ships
to the western horizon, and devices that think are fiction today and engineering prompts
tomorrow. A generation raised on these stories asks different questions when they reach
a forge or a physician's table.

**How it manifests:**
- Daniel seeds this loop deliberately from the first contest: he submits or commissions
  stories about technology he is actively working on (see Section 7).
- The accidental parallel beat (see Section 8) is Loop 3 producing unexpected results:
  someone writes a story describing technology Daniel is building without knowing he is
  building it. The loop is real. The ideas are spreading independently.
- Part VI, ch41 and ch44: A craftsman from Gaul improves on a pulp recipe Daniel had
  introduced — not by reading Daniel's notes, but by reading contest stories about
  paper-making and experimenting on his own. The fiction became an instruction.
- Epilogue, ch52: The chain of inventors learned from other inventors, not from Daniel.
  "A Gallic smith who found a better process Daniel had not suggested; a British miner
  who designed a cam-driven ore-crushing machine by watching contest entries for fifteen
  years." This is Loop 3 at full speed, a generation after Daniel's death.

### Loop 4: Makes Daniel's Books More Valuable
**What it is:** The press that publishes contest collections becomes culturally central.
Every library wants the annual anthology. Daniel's press is the publisher of the cultural
record. Owning a Daniel press anthology signals that a library is serious.

**How it manifests:**
- By Part IV, the anthology is mentioned alongside Pliny and technical works as a library
  staple. No single scene required — it is background.
- The press's anthology revenue is what makes the contest self-funding. One economic
  chain: story entered → story printed → anthology sold → prize funded → more stories
  entered. This should be visible in Daniel's financial accounting by Part V.

### Loop 5: Makes Daniel Culturally Influential
**What it is:** He is not just an inventor. He is Rome's Hollywood. Every Roman who reads
knows his name as the founder of the contest. This is political protection that cannot be
fully purchased.

**How it manifests:**
- The contest's prestige protects Daniel during periods of political vulnerability (Part
  V, the Four Consulars aftermath). Attacking the man who funds what Romans love to read
  is bad politics.
- ch35 (Part V): Hadrian is aware of the contest before Daniel's name is formally raised
  in their first real encounter. The contest precedes Daniel's reputation as an inventor
  in some circles.
- ch45: When Daniel confronts the Bar Kokhba moral weight, the contest is one of the
  things he can point to that is not in service of power's cruelty. It is not a clean
  answer. But it is a real one.

### Loop 6: Builds the Library System
**What it is:** Contest archives require libraries. Libraries require readers. Daniel
endows contest libraries specifically — each must stock the full backlist of prize winners.
The contest is the reason for the library; the library is the reason to read the contest.

**How it manifests:**
- The library requirement is written into the contest's institutional charter (Part V-VI,
  when the contest is formally endowed). Any city or collegium that wants to be a
  recognized contest venue must maintain a collection of prize-winning anthologies.
- ch39 (Part VI): The institutional endowment creates this requirement formally. Before
  that, the library connection is informal but growing.

### Loop 7: Makes the Printing Press More Useful
**What it is:** More content means more printing. More printing means lower per-unit
cost through press efficiency. Lower cost means more accessible books. More accessible
books mean more readers. More readers mean more contest entries, which is more content.
The press is a platform, not just a machine.

**How it manifests:**
- This loop is most visible in Daniel's financial records and in Marcia's management of
  the press operation. It does not need a dedicated scene; it is the background economic
  logic of the press's growth from ch19 onward.
- By Part V, the contest anthology is the press's largest single print run each year.
  The infrastructure built for the anthology run is what enables other large print jobs.

### Loop 8: Makes Daniel's Paper More Useful
**What it is:** Every story is written on paper. Every copy is printed on paper. Every
library needs paper for correspondence. The contest creates demand that justifies
Daniel's paper mills.

**How it manifests:**
- The paper mills were introduced in Part III (ch16 onward, rag paper in production).
  The contest, introduced in ch19, is almost immediately the paper's largest internal
  customer.
- By Part IV, Daniel's paper is being purchased by people outside his enterprise
  specifically for writing contest entries. The paper and the contest are, in the market,
  a single product ecosystem.

### Loop 9: Daniel Seeds His Own Ideas
**What it is:** He enters stories he remembers from high school — Shakespeare plots,
Jules Verne adventures, mythology, any story a 2020s student would know — framed as
oral traditions from Thule. These are not vanity projects. They are tech-inspiration
vectors and cultural deposits. Over decades, some become Roman classics.

**How it manifests in the story — see Section 7 for full detail.**

## 4. CONTEST CATEGORIES

The categories develop over time. Do not present all categories as existing from launch.
The contest begins with one general category and differentiates as entry volume grows
and as specific types of story prove popular.

### At Launch (ch19, 106 AD): One Category
**Machines and Wonders** — stories about devices, vehicles, natural philosophy, and
things that could be but are not yet. Fantasy, speculation, and myth-adjacent are
explicitly welcome. Daniel does not want to scare off storytellers by demanding
technical accuracy.

### By Part IV (110-117 AD): Three Categories
**Fiction / Adventure** — fantasy, myth-adjacent, hero journeys, stories of invented
lands. This category is the largest by volume and produces the most People's Prize
winners.

**Natural Philosophy / Speculation** — "what if" stories about technology, medicine,
the heavens, and the nature of matter. This is the category Daniel cares most about
as a seeding mechanism. Stories about flying machines, ships to the western ocean,
invisible creatures, devices that think. The Judges' Prize disproportionately goes
here.

**History and Legend** — stories reworking Roman founding myths, legendary battles,
and ancestral tales. This category explodes in popularity; Rome loves its founding
stories told fresh. It also becomes the category where Daniel's Shakespeare-derived
plots land most naturally (see Section 7).

### By Part VI (125-138 AD): Four Categories, with the Anonymous Track
**Anonymous Submissions** — a track for entries with no author name attached, managed
by a sealed-envelope system administered by the judge panel. This exists specifically
for slaves and lower-class entrants who face social consequences if their name appears
on a piece of writing their owner or patron didn't authorize.

Daniel introduces the anonymous track after the first contest cycle in which he notices
entries arriving with clearly fictitious Roman names — slaves writing under borrowed
identities because the contest has no protection for their real ones. He builds the
protection in. Whether a slave can legally receive prize money is handled through the
sealed-envelope system: the prize goes into a designated collegium fund that the
winner can draw on in ways that do not require their owner's permission.

The anonymous track is controversial. A faction of judges argues that an anonymous
story cannot be properly evaluated because the author's character cannot be assessed.
Daniel's response, through the press: the story either holds together or it doesn't.
The author's character is not a judging criterion.

## 5. THE CLASS TENSION DYNAMIC

### The People's Prize vs. The Judges' Prize
This dynamic is the contest's most important ongoing narrative. It is not incidental.
It is built into the structure.

**What happens:** In most years, the two prizes go to different stories. The People's
Prize tends to go to stories that work in oral performance — strong forward momentum,
clear stakes, physical action, characters who feel like people the audience knows. The
Judges' Prize tends to go to stories that reward re-reading — more complex structure,
unusual ideas, philosophical weight.

Neither type is inherently class-marked, but in practice:
- The People's Prize often goes to a story from a lower-status entrant, told in a more
  direct register, about characters who are not senators or soldiers.
- The Judges' Prize often (but not always) goes to a more formally educated writer.

Daniel's favorite outcome is a year in which a Pamphilus-register story — rough Latin,
dockworker protagonist, no elegance — wins the People's Prize, while the Judges' Prize
goes to a philosophical piece about the nature of machines. Both outcomes are reported
by the press with equal ceremony. The press does not rank the prizes.

**What this prevents:** A contest with only one prize would eventually be captured by
whichever class dominates the judging panel. Two prizes with separate determination
mechanisms mean that even if the judges become an educated guild, the public vote
gives a separate result. The contest belongs to everyone because both prizes are real.

**The annual press narrative:** Each year, the press covers the two prizes as a story.
*Did they agree or disagree this year?* becomes a question Romans who follow the contest
ask each cycle. When they agree — the same story wins both — it is treated as remarkable,
an event. When they disagree, the press covers the disagreement as culture news, not as
a failure.

**For the writer:** The People's Prize / Judges' Prize disagreement is a recurring beat
available for any chapter in Part IV onward. Use it when you need texture about the
contest without needing a major plot event. It can be one paragraph — a press report
Daniel reads, or a conversation between contest regulars, or Felix's opinion on which
prize matters more.

## 6. REQUIRED BEATS PER PART

These are the narrative beats that must appear somewhere in the indicated chapter ranges.
"Must" means the book is incomplete without them. Chapters listed are the most natural
placements; adjacent chapters are acceptable.

### Part III (ch15-ch24, 103-110 AD) — The Contest Launches

**Beat A: First contest, rough entries (ch19)**
The first contest receives perhaps 40-60 entries. Daniel reads all of them in one
sitting. He discards 35-40 immediately — they are barely stories, more like complaints
with a frame around them. He finds 3-4 that genuinely surprise him, combinations of
ideas or voices he didn't anticipate. And one that frightens him slightly: a soldier's
story about a machine that kills at a distance without the soldier having to see the
face of the man it kills. The story is crude. The idea is not. Daniel prizes it anyway.
He does not comment on it aloud.

**Beat B: Uncomfortable entry categories (ch19)**
Among the first entries:
- Soldiers write about war with accurate brutality that Daniel finds disturbing because
  it is *accurate* — he made those machines more efficient.
- Slaves write about freedom with an anger that exposes Daniel's compromised position —
  he freed Tyche and Pamphilus, but he still operates within and benefits from the
  system. One slave's story is essentially about him, without naming him, told with
  contempt. He prizes it for fiction (the Judges' Prize second year). He doesn't tell
  anyone why.

Both categories receive prizes. This is deliberate. Daniel does not exclude
uncomfortable entries; excluding them would reduce the contest to a flattery engine.

**Beat C: Felix introduced (ch19)**
Felix appears as a recurring entrant from the first or second contest cycle. His early
entries are not good. They are earnest and imaginative in the wrong ways — he reaches
for effects he cannot yet execute. But the voice is there. Daniel notices the voice.
Felix does not win anything for at least four contest cycles. He keeps entering.

**Beat D: Press covers the contest as culture news (ch19-24)**
The press's coverage of the contest results is itself a product. Which story won?
Did the People's Prize and Judges' Prize agree? What did the crowd shout? By ch21-22,
the contest results are being reprinted in copies distributed outside Rome. The contest
has a readership beyond the participants.

### Part IV (ch25-ch31, 110-117 AD) — The Contest Matures

**Beat E: A lower-status entrant wins (ch25-31, exact chapter writer's choice)**
A story entered by someone of lower social standing — possibly a freedperson, possibly
a provincial, possibly someone writing in a rougher register — wins a prize. The more
interesting version: they enter with minimal information about their status, win the
Judges' Prize or the People's Prize, and the judges discover afterward that the winner
is not from the educated elite they expected. Real tension emerges. Some judges protest.
At least one questions whether someone from this background could have written something
of this quality (implicitly assuming lower-status entrants cannot produce sophisticated
work). The press covers the controversy. Daniel makes the award public and does not
editorialize. He lets the press do the editorializing. The precedent is set. The next
lower-status winner faces less friction, and the contest's credibility as a genuinely
open venue is reinforced.

The story should be relevant to the contest's tech-inspiration purpose — a story about
medicine, or about an invisible thing that moves through water, or about a ship that
navigates by the stars. The subject matter should be strong enough that it cannot be
dismissed as a novelty win. The interesting rupture is class, not gender.

**Beat F: Protégé goes wrong (ch38, Part V, see note)**
One person in the contest community misuses something Daniel built. Most naturally placed
in Part V (ch38), but seeded here with a character who becomes significant later. The
full beat belongs in Section 6 Part V.

### Part V (ch32-ch38, 117-125 AD) — The Contest Survives Political Exposure

**Beat G: Daniel withdraws from direct judging (ch32-35)**
After the Four Consulars (ch33), Daniel is politically exposed. His direct participation
in judging is now a liability: anything he prizes publicly can be used against him. He
formalizes the standing committee and removes himself from the evaluation process. He
remains the contest's founder and funder. He does not pick the winners. This is a real
loss — he misses the reading. But the institution survives because it does not depend
on him to function.

**Beat H: The contest endowment formalized (ch38-39)**
Somewhere in the Part V-VI transition, the contest is endowed as a permanent institution:
a capital fund generating its own revenue, the standing committee formally chartered, the
multiple prize categories codified, the anonymous track established, the library
requirement written in. The prize money is funded by anthology sales percentage. Daniel's
subsidy is no longer necessary.

This is not a celebration scene. It is a piece of institutional paperwork Daniel signs,
probably on an ordinary afternoon, that is one of the most consequential things he
ever does. The scene, if written, should be anti-climactic on the surface and heavy
with implication.

**Beat I: Protégé goes wrong (ch38, most natural placement)**
One person in the contest community — a winner, a judge, or a frequent entrant — misuses
what Daniel built. The cleanest option for the contest specifically: a contest winner uses
the printing press (which they now have access to as a published author) to publish slander
against a rival family, demonstrating that the press amplifies lies as efficiently as
knowledge. Daniel finds out. He cannot undo the damage. The discovery and aftermath are
brief. The point is that the contest community is not inherently good — it shares the
tools Daniel built, and tools serve whoever holds them.

### Part VI (ch39-ch46, 125-138 AD) — The Contest as Institution

**Beat J: Felix wins (ch41 or nearby)**
Felix wins a prize — most naturally the People's Prize first, which feels right given
his voice. What he writes should echo something from Daniel's tech program: the contest
and the workshop in dialogue. His story might involve a flying machine, or a map of the
western ocean, or a device that sends a signal faster than a horse. He does not know
Daniel has been working on the signal device. The contest and the workshop have been in
the same orbit for decades; this convergence is earned, not contrived.

Felix's win is a quiet scene. Daniel is in the crowd when the public reading happens.
Felix does not know Daniel is there. Daniel does not make himself known during the
reading. He leaves before it ends.

**Beat K: The accidental parallel (ch41, tied to Felix beat or separate)**
Someone — not Felix, or separately from Felix — writes a story that accidentally
describes a technology Daniel is currently developing without knowing it. This is the
accidental parallel beat (see Section 8 for full treatment). It belongs in Part VI,
ch41 or nearby.

**Beat L: Judge of lower-status origin on the panel (ch41 onward)**
Following the class-tension beat (Beat E, Part IV), eventually someone of lower-status
background — a freedperson, a provincial, someone outside the traditional educated elite —
joins the standing judge panel. This is not announced as a landmark. It happens because the
person is qualified. The cultural friction about this appointment is less than it was
for the first lower-status winner, but it is not gone. The structure is working as
designed: the judges now include perspectives that the educated elite alone would not.

## 7. DANIEL'S OWN STORY SUBMISSIONS

### What He Enters
Daniel remembers stories from high school — Shakespeare plots, Jules Verne adventures,
Greek myths as a 21st-century teenager knows them, fairy tales, any story he can
reconstruct from memory. He also draws on American pop culture: story structures he
absorbed from movies and books without ever consciously registering them.

He submits these as *oral traditions from Thule* — stories his people tell, passed down
across generations. This framing is not quite a lie; some of the stories he submits
genuinely are oral traditions from his homeland, carried in cultural memory through
centuries of retelling. He is merely the only one who knows which homeland the
stories came from.

### Specific Story Types and Examples

**Shakespeare plots as Roman tragedies/comedies:**
The most natural fit. Rome has a rich theatrical tradition; tragic and comic plots
are a recognized form. Daniel's version of *Romeo and Juliet* enters the contest in
the History and Legend category, framed as a Thulean legend of two families and
a doomed union. It wins the Judges' Prize in the year it enters. He enters it under
the pseudonym *Mercator Thulensis* (the Thulean Merchant). Scholars later dispute
whether it is an authentic Thulean tradition or an elaborate forgery. Both camps are
partly right.

**A Trip to the Moon / Verne-style adventures:**
He enters a story about a man who builds a vessel that flies to the moon and finds it
inhabited, in the Natural Philosophy category. He steals the plot from Verne (the
cannon launch) and from Lucian's *True History*, which he vaguely remembers from a
history class. The story is medically incoherent (no vacuum, no pressure suit) but
he does not fix this because he does not want to fix it — he wants people imagining
the moon, not worrying about whether a man can breathe up there. It wins the People's
Prize in the second year of the Natural Philosophy category.

**Disease and invisible creatures:**
He enters a story about a physician who discovers that illness travels through water —
that the invisible things in bad water can be stopped by boiling. The story is fiction.
The protagonist is a skilled physician trained in practical observation. The plot involves
an epidemic in a city and the physician's struggle to convince the magistrates to act —
a conflict driven by institutional resistance and the magistrates' disbelief, not by the
protagonist's gender. The story predates the city epidemic scene by several chapters;
when the epidemic happens, some readers remember the story and its central argument about
transmission and boiling.

**The Western Ocean story:**
He enters a story about a sailor who crosses the western ocean and finds a land of
people who have never heard of Rome. The story ends before there is a conquest; it
ends on the sailor standing at the edge of a forest, hearing a language he doesn't
know. He enters this story early (Part III or IV, before the actual Atlantic crossing
is staged). It wins nothing its first year. It wins the Judges' Prize three contest
cycles later, in a different entry year, after he revises it.

### How He Frames Them
Most entries go in under the *Mercator Thulensis* pseudonym. A few he enters openly,
listed as *Daniel bar Iudaeus* (his adoptive legal name) presenting "a traditional
tale from Thule." The open entries are more cautious in their content — he does not
want his name directly on the moon story, for instance, because the astronomy
implications would draw Crispus-style attack. The pseudonym protects the more
ambitious stories.

Over time, the Mercator Thulensis entries are recognized as a distinct voice. By Part
VI, scholars argue about whether Mercator Thulensis is one person or a tradition.
Daniel watches this argument without intervening.

### What Becomes of These Stories
By the time Daniel is old, some of the stories he entered have become Roman classics —
assigned reading in schools, quoted in other authors' work, referenced as "the old
Thulean tales." He lives to see schoolchildren memorizing plots he carried in his head
from Ohio. This is one of his private, complicated satisfactions. He does not tell
Marcia or Tyche which stories he wrote. He does not tell anyone. He considers this
appropriate. The stories are out there now. They belong to whoever reads them.

The Romeo-and-Juliet story, specifically, is mentioned in ch51 (the post-death document
chapter) as a famous tragedy, attributed to "the Thulean oral tradition as recorded by
the foundational contest." No living person knows it was Daniel's.

## 8. THE ACCIDENTAL PARALLEL BEAT

### What It Is
Someone enters a story describing a technology Daniel is currently developing — without
knowing he is developing it. They reached the same imaginative place independently,
through the contest culture's accumulation of tech-adjacent stories over years. This
validates the seeding strategy in the most unsettling possible way: the ideas are
already spreading without Daniel's direct input.

### When It Happens
Chapter 41 (Part VI, 125-138 AD). The contest has been running for roughly 20 years
by this point. Loop 3 has had time to accumulate. The entrant who writes this story
has been reading contest anthologies for years.

### What the Story Describes
The accidental parallel should involve technology Daniel is actively working on at the
time of the story — not something already public and deployed (stirrups, the press,
the compass) but something still in his workshop as an active project. The best candidate
is the optical semaphore network or the atmospheric steam pump. A story about a system
of fire-signal towers that can carry a coded message across an empire in one day, or a
story about a machine that lifts water using the power of steam — either fits the
timeline of Daniel's actual work in Part VI (the semaphore is in development around
128-135 AD per ch44; the steam pump has working trials from ~118 AD onward).

The story does not get the details right. It gets the *idea* right. The entrant imagined
a signal network because they read ten years of contest stories about sending messages
faster than horses. They are not Daniel's protégé. They are not someone he knows. They
are a provisioner's clerk who writes in his evenings.

### How Daniel Reacts
He reads the story during round 1 culling (he still reads finalists, even if he no
longer judges). He goes quiet. He sits with the story for a while. He does not discuss
it with anyone. He adds it to the finalists list. He writes a brief judge's note on the
submission: *Prizes it. Reason: the idea is already loose.*

The writer agent should not over-explain this moment. The quietness is the point.
Daniel's reaction is private and slightly frightening. The contest works the way he
designed it to work. That is frightening precisely because he designed it.

## 9. HOW THE CONTEST BECOMES SELF-SUSTAINING

### The Process (Parts V-VI, chs 38-46)

The contest becomes self-sustaining in three overlapping ways:

**Financially self-sustaining** (ch38-39 transition): The anthology's annual sales
percentage covers the prize pool. The capital endowment covers administrative costs.
Daniel's subsidy goes to zero around 120-125 AD. Marcia confirms this number at some
point, probably in passing during a business discussion that is not about the contest
specifically.

**Operationally self-sustaining** (ch33-39): After Daniel withdraws from judging
in Part V, the standing committee runs the evaluation process without him. The
committee has its own procedures, its own reputation, its own internal politics.
By Part VI, Daniel's role is formal rather than operational — he is the founder, he
attends the final ceremony, but the machine runs without his hands on it.

**Institutionally self-sustaining** (ch39): The formal endowment creates a legal
entity — a collegium with a charter, a capital fund, a governance structure — that
cannot be dissolved by Daniel's death or by any single patron's withdrawal. The
contest does not depend on any living person. It depends on the charter and the
revenue.

### What Form It Takes by Part VI

By ch39-41, the contest is the *Collegium Certaminis Fabularum* (or a Latinized name
close to this — the exact name is a writer's decision, but it should have the formal
weight of a chartered institution). It has:
- A permanent secretariat (3-4 paid staff, freedmen or free workers)
- A rolling seven-person judge panel with term limits
- An annual anthology published by Daniel's press under a standing contract that
  does not require his personal authorization for each print run
- A capital fund managed by a financial trustee distinct from Daniel
- Library endowment agreements with 12-15 major libraries across the empire
- A formal submission process with regional collection points in major cities

By ch46 (Hadrian's death), the contest has survived one emperor transition already
(Trajan to Hadrian) and is positioned to survive the next (Hadrian to Antoninus Pius).
When Daniel dies in ch50, the contest does not need a scene of continuation — the
epilogue (ch52) simply references it as running, as it has been running, as it will
keep running.

## 10. WHAT NOT TO DO

These are the most common ways a writing agent will handle the contest incorrectly.
Each is listed with the failure mode and what to do instead.

### Do Not: Make the Contest Immediately Successful
The first contest is rough. Entries are crude. The crowd at the first public reading is
small. The press coverage is brief. Daniel reads everything himself. The contest does not
feel like a cultural institution yet. It feels like an experiment by an eccentric
foreigner with money.

Success accumulates over years. By Part IV (110-117 AD), it is well established. By
Part V (117-125 AD), it survives political turbulence because it is already too embedded
in Roman reading culture to kill. That trajectory requires the rough beginning.

### Do Not: Have Daniel Control the Outcomes
Daniel designed the structure. He does not control the results. The stories that win
are not the stories he would have chosen. The year the slave-freedom story wins the
People's Prize is not a story Daniel picked; it is a story the crowd picked. He has
to live with that. It is both the contest working correctly and a mirror he doesn't
always want to look in.

Once the standing committee takes over judging (Part V), he doesn't even know who
the finalists are until the reading. He reads the results in the press like everyone
else.

### Do Not: Make the Class Tension Comfortable
The People's Prize / Judges' Prize dynamic produces real friction, not picturesque
diversity. There are years in which wealthy patrons withdraw support from the contest
because a slave's story won. There are years in which the judge panel argues for days
before agreeing on a winner. There are years in which the People's Prize goes to a
story that Daniel privately thinks is bad, and he cannot say this because he removed
himself from the process.

The contest is not Daniel's comfortable triumph over Roman elitism. It is a designed
conflict that he set in motion and then stepped back from.

### Do Not: Announce the Flywheel Loops
Do not have Daniel explain the nine loops to anyone. Do not have him describe the
contest as an "economic flywheel." Do not have Marcia or Tyche summarize what the
contest has done for the press or the paper mills. The loops are visible in what
happens, not in what characters say. A reader who sees the contest's effects across
Parts III-VI should be able to infer the loops. If you find yourself writing a speech
about how the contest benefits the press, cut the speech and show the press's revenue
line instead.

### Do Not: Make Daniel's Story Submissions Easy to Identify
No character should recognize a Mercator Thulensis story as Daniel's in his lifetime.
No one should say "this plot sounds like something from your homeland, Daniel." The
pseudonym works because no one looks closely. The stories are presented as oral
traditions; oral traditions don't have identifiable authors. The only moment the
connection should be available to the reader is in Daniel's private narration — and
even there, he should not over-explain it. He submitted the story. It is out there.
He does not dwell.

### Do Not: Let Felix Win Easily or Early
Felix's win is earned by years of entering and not winning. If he wins in Part III,
the win costs nothing dramatically. If he wins in Part VI, after appearing across
four parts as the man who keeps trying, the win costs something — it is a long time
coming, and Daniel knows how long Felix has been entering, and neither of them says
that aloud during the reading.

### Do Not: Skip the Class-Tension Moment
The lower-status winner's award is not a progressive triumph. It is uncomfortable.
Some of the judges are genuinely surprised and express doubt about whether someone from
that background could have produced work of this quality. Some of the audience at the
public reading does not want to cheer for the story once they know the winner's actual
status. Daniel does not resolve this by making a speech. He makes the award and lets
the press cover the tension. The tension is part of the record. Years later, it will
look smaller in retrospect than it was in the moment. In the moment, it is real. The
class rupture the dual-prize structure was designed to create has happened.

### Do Not: Have the Contest Run Without Problems
The protégé-goes-wrong beat (Section 6, Beat I) is required specifically because the
contest is not a clean good thing. The press the contest helped build can print slander.
The literary community the contest created can be weaponized. Daniel built something
powerful and then deliberately relinquished control of it. Powerful things that no one
controls go wrong in the hands of people with bad intentions. This is not a failure of
Daniel's design. It is the expected consequence of building something that outlasts
its creator.
