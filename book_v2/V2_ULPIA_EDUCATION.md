## SECTION 1: The Premise and Why It Matters

Daniel has been performing a careful, limited version of himself for everyone else for his
entire life in Rome. Heras guessed at the depth of his knowledge and accepted not-knowing
as the price of the friendship. Macer never cared about origins, only results. Tyche knows
about the English cipher and has learned it as a keeper's tool — but she knows the *shape*
of what Daniel carries, not the full substance. Marcia knows he is unusual in ways she has
chosen not to examine directly. None of them know the complete thing: where he is from,
what the world he came from was like, how different the future is from everything they
imagine.

Ulpia is different. She is his daughter. She grows up hearing English from birth. She grows
up hearing stories — not parables or moral examples, but actual stories from his world:
plots of novels he half-remembers, science fiction premises he can reconstruct from memory,
history that hasn't happened yet. By the time she is old enough to ask "Father, where did
you learn that?", the answer is already half-obvious to her. She has been accumulating
evidence for years before he says it directly.

This is also Daniel's one act of pure generosity with knowledge. The cipher's two-at-a-time
pacing, the tech program's careful sequencing, the encyclopedia's hooked marks distinguishing
known from guessed — all of it is measured, all of it is for later. With Ulpia, he gives
everything. Not because he thinks she can use it or that it's safe to tell her. Because she
is his daughter and the loneliness is real and she is the only person in the world he can
be himself with.

This is not a subplot. It is the most intimate relationship in the book.

The arc does not produce a plot event. Ulpia does not change Rome using what she knows. She
keeps the secret, holds the encyclopedia, and eventually becomes his interlocutor and
collaborator. The arc's payoff is quieter than that: a father and daughter, in a garden or
a study, talking in a private language about the shape of the universe. Most of what they
say to each other never makes it to the page.

## SECTION 2: Phase Timeline — What He Teaches Her and When

### Age 0–5 (~118–123 AD): The Language

English from birth, simultaneously with Latin. This is not a project Daniel plans — it is
what happens when a man who thinks in English raises a child. He talks to her in English
without deciding to. He reads to her in English because the stories in his head are in
English.

**What he reads to her:**
He has no books. He reconstructs from memory: nursery rhymes he can only partly recall,
the plots of fairy tales he can skeleton out (the glass slipper, the spinning wheel, the
wolf at the door — the bones are there even when the flesh is gone), picture book concepts
even though he has no pictures. He knows the shape of *Charlotte's Web* well enough to
tell it. He knows *The Phantom Tollbooth* well enough to explain the Doldrums, the Whether
Man, the city of Dictionopolis. He tells her these things as bedtime stories, over meals,
while walking.

**Numbers:** She learns to count in English before Latin because Daniel counts in English.
When he's doing arithmetic in his head she hears the English. She learns to associate the
English words with the concepts before she learns the Latin ones. Later this becomes an
advantage: she can switch between the two numeral systems intuitively, the way bilingual
children switch languages without effort.

**Songs:** He sings to her. He knows a lot of songs — or knows the shape of them. The
problem is the holes. He knows the opening verse of a dozen songs and then the words blur.
He knows the chorus but not the bridge. He knows the melody but cannot find the second
verse. He fills in what he can, hums through what he cannot, and she doesn't know the
difference because she has nothing to compare it to. But he knows. Singing her to sleep
with the second verse missing from a song he hasn't thought of in twenty years is a quiet
private grief that is entirely his. She sleeps. He sits with it.

**What she learns:** English as a natural language, not a studied one. By age five she
switches between Latin and English the way she switches between speaking to her nurse and
speaking to her father. She doesn't know that this is unusual. She doesn't know that English
is not simply what her father's family spoke. She knows it is private — they do not speak
English in front of servants or guests — but she thinks of this as a family custom, not a
secret.

### Age 5–9 (~123–127 AD): The Playground and Early Lessons

**The playground:**

Daniel builds something in the garden that Romans don't build: a dedicated play structure.
He builds it because he knows what a swing feels like. He builds it because he wanted her
childhood to have something.

What he can build, and what he cannot:

- **Swing (YES):** Romans had *oscillum* — swings with a religious dimension, but also
  recreational, hung from trees and frames. He builds a proper wooden swing with rope
  and a seat. This is the most Roman-adjacent thing in the structure. A neighbor or
  visitor who asks about it can be told it's an *oscillum* frame. This is not quite a lie.

- **Climbing frame (YES):** A wooden frame with horizontal and vertical members,
  handholds carved in, a rope ladder. This is just carpentry. No Roman equivalent exists,
  but there's nothing technically challenging about it. He builds it in an afternoon with
  Pamphilus's help. Pamphilus does not ask why. He builds what he's told to build.

- **Sandbox (POSSIBLE):** Sand is available. He makes a shallow enclosed area with sand
  in it. Romans don't do this as a dedicated play space, but there's nothing difficult
  about it. He does it because he remembers sandboxes.

- **Slide (NO):** Functional inclines exist. A play slide does not. He doesn't build one —
  not worth the construction complexity, and his memory of playground slides is vaguer
  than his memory of swings.

- **Monkey bars (NO):** Invented in the 1920s. He probably doesn't think of them. If he
  does, he can't reconstruct the design well enough to build them safely.

The play structure is noticed by neighbors and by workshop visitors who come to the house.
It is found slightly odd — not offensive, not alarming, just unusual. A child of equestrian
or better family is expected to play games, not on a wooden structure in the garden. Daniel
explains it briefly if asked ("children play on it"), does not explain the reasoning, and
the topic drops. The structure is not a scene. It is a one-paragraph beat in a chapter
where a visitor or neighbor notices it. Then it is simply there.

**What this says about Daniel:** He is recreating something from his own childhood for her
without being able to explain why it matters. He cannot say "this is what playgrounds look
like where I come from." He builds it instead.

**Early education:**

She is being educated by household tutors in the standard way — Latin letters, basic
figures, appropriate behavior for a girl of her class. Daniel supplements this in their
private sessions.

- **Reading and writing in English:** She can already read and write in Latin; English
  follows the same literacy, applied to a different script. She is faster at this than
  Daniel expects. She asks why the letters look different. He tells her it is an older
  script from his family's homeland. This is as close to true as anything he tells her
  at this stage.

- **Arithmetic extensions:** She knows addition and basic multiplication from her Latin
  education. Daniel teaches her fractions and what long division is for. He explains it
  as "finding out how many times one number fits inside another" — this makes more
  intuitive sense to her than the algorithm, so he starts there and works backward.

- **Geography:** He draws maps for her. The maps he draws are different from Roman maps —
  they have familiar outlines of the Mediterranean, but they also show outlines of other
  landmasses: Africa extending farther south than anyone has confirmed, large shapes to
  the west that Roman maps don't have. He tells her these are lands he read about when he
  was young — "there are people there," he says, pointing west, "and Rome's ships will
  find them eventually." She asks if he's been there. He says no. This is true.

- **Basic astronomy:** The earth is round — she already knows this from her standard
  education. The earth goes around the sun — he tells her this matter-of-factly. "The
  sun doesn't move. We go around it." She asks why they don't feel it. He explains the
  way a ship's passenger doesn't feel the ship moving when the sea is calm. She accepts
  this. She does not repeat it to her tutors.

- **Biology basics:** He tells her about bodies — how they work, what makes people sick,
  why washing matters. He knows germ theory at the level of "things too small to see can
  make you sick" and he tells her this as a fact, not a theory. She grows up knowing it
  as a fact, which means she grows up doing the practices without needing the argument.

- **Stories:** This is the most consistent part of their sessions. He tells her Shakespeare
  plots — simplified, without the blank verse, but with the shape intact. She has strong
  opinions. She thinks Hamlet took too long and is not interested in his reasons. She
  thinks Romeo and Juliet were both being stupid and she says so, waiting for Daniel to
  disagree. He mostly doesn't disagree, which she finds satisfying. She thinks Titania was
  manipulated and that Oberon was wrong and that the play presents this as comedy when it
  shouldn't. Daniel is not sure she's wrong about this.

  He tells her Jules Verne plots — the submarine, the balloon, the journey to the
  interior of the earth. She is fascinated by the submarine and asks how it is propelled.
  He explains the principle (sealed hull, ballast tanks, screw propulsion, mechanical
  engine). She says: "So it needs fire?" He says yes, essentially. She thinks about this
  and asks how the fire gets air. He explains the sealed-tank approach. She asks if he
  could build one. He says he doesn't have the materials. She asks if he could figure out
  the materials. He says that's a different question.

  He tells her science fiction premises — the ones where people go to other planets, or
  build machines that think, or travel through time. He doesn't describe these as fiction
  at first; he describes them as things that happen in stories from his world. Later, when
  she is old enough to distinguish categories, she asks him which of these are real. He
  tells her: "People have been to the moon. Machines that think are something people were
  working on when I left." She asks: "Were they good machines?" He says: "Some of them were
  useful. Nobody knew yet if they were good."

### Age 9–14 (~127–132 AD): The Serious Schooling

By now she reads English fluently — faster than Daniel reads Latin, which is a running
private joke between them. He writes materials for her: textbook-style notes in his cipher,
covering things her Roman tutors don't know.

She is also, by this point, the only person outside the cipher circle who reads English.
She doesn't know she is reading a cipher. She thinks she is reading in her family's private
script. She will understand what she is reading when she is told the full truth.

**Mathematics:**

- *Algebra:* He can do this. He teaches it as "finding the unknown," which is the Islamic
  framing — the word algebra itself comes from a mathematician not yet born, but the
  operations exist in a form Daniel can reconstruct. She takes to it immediately. She has
  a habit of reducing problems to their simplest form before doing anything else, which
  is good algebraic instinct and also characteristic of her in every other domain.

- *Geometry:* Rome already knows substantial geometry (Euclid is in circulation). Daniel
  extends it into applications she hasn't seen: he shows her how to calculate the area of
  an irregular shape by breaking it into triangles, how to estimate the height of a building
  from shadow angles, how to think about solid geometry. She is particularly interested in
  the practical applications — how do you actually cut stone to make an arch? He explains
  the keystone principle. She asks how Apollodorus did it for the bridge at Drobeta. He
  tells her what he knows, which is actually quite a bit.

- *Calculus (the idea of it):* He cannot fully reconstruct calculus. He was in precalc
  when he left. What he can give her is the concept: rates of change (how fast something
  is changing at a specific moment), the idea that you can find the area under a curve by
  slicing it into thin pieces and adding them up, the notion of instantaneous velocity.
  He describes these as "ways of handling things that are changing while you look at them."
  She finds this interesting but can see the gap where the formal machinery would be. She
  notes the gap. He acknowledges it. She writes "UNFINISHED — Father does not know the
  full method" in the margin of her notes, in English. He sees this and does not correct it.

**Physics:**

He teaches her Newton's laws as principles, demonstrated with objects around the room.
- Things in motion stay in motion unless something stops them.
- Force equals mass times acceleration (he demonstrates this with a heavy jar and a
  light jar and the same push).
- For every push there is an equal push in the opposite direction (he demonstrates this
  with a raft in a basin of water: push the raft forward, the water pushes back).

He tells her about gravity as a force that exists between all things with mass, not just
between heavy things and the earth. She asks if the moon has gravity. He says yes, and
that it pulls on the earth's water, which is why the tides move. She has noticed the tides.
She says: "So the moon is pulling the sea." He says: "Yes." She says: "Then why isn't the
sea on top of us?" He explains that the earth's pull is stronger and the moon's pull only
tugs at the surface. She thinks about this for several days and comes back with three more
questions he is not fully equipped to answer. He answers what he can and marks the rest.

**Chemistry:**

He knows the periodic table imperfectly. He can reconstruct hydrogen and helium (the
first row), lithium through neon (the second row), and then the common elements — carbon,
nitrogen, oxygen, sodium, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold,
mercury, lead. Past that, his memory becomes unreliable and he knows it. He tells her:

"There is a table of all the basic substances things are made of. Each one has a number
and a weight. I can tell you the first twenty or so with confidence. After that I'm
guessing."

He tells her atoms exist, that everything is made of them, that they are too small to see
but their behavior can be predicted. He cannot give her modern chemistry — he can't remember
organic chemistry well enough to be useful — but he can give her the framework: everything
is atoms, atoms combine in fixed ratios, some combinations release energy and some require
it. She finds this interesting as a framework and immediately asks what the small things
that make people sick are made of. He tells her they are also made of atoms, that they are
small living things with their own structure, that they reproduce. She asks how they know
to make more of themselves. He says that is one of the questions his world was still
working on when he left. She writes: "UNFINISHED — biology of reproduction at the small
level — unresolved even in Father's time."

**History — his history:**

This is the most careful part of the teaching. He tells her real future history, but he
doses it with attention to what will be useful and what will be paralyzing.

- He tells her what happens to Rome. Not as prophecy — as history he knows, which he
  acknowledges may have changed because of him. He tells her about the Antonine Plague
  coming (not in her lifetime; in the next generation, roughly). He tells her about the
  pressures Rome will face — the Germanic migrations, the overextension, the succession
  crises. He tells her this not to frighten her but because she will be a keeper of his
  encyclopedia and she needs to understand that the encyclopedia is a race against a clock
  she cannot see.

- He tells her about the medieval period, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment — not in
  detail, but in shape. "After Rome falls, there will be a long time when fewer people
  read and write and the knowledge is held by monasteries. Then people will find it again
  and there will be a period of rediscovery. Then there will be a period when people start
  building systematically on what they know, and the rate of discovery gets faster and faster.
  That is the world I came from." She asks: "Did people in your world know this was coming?"
  He says: "They knew the history. They didn't know if the pattern would repeat."

- He tells her about democracy as a system — not the Athenian version she knows from
  education, but the modern form: elected representatives, written constitutions, the idea
  that government derives its legitimacy from the governed. He tells her about the American
  Revolution — what it was about, what it produced. He tells her why he thinks it matters.
  She asks: "Was it better?" He says: "For a lot of people, yes. For some people it took
  a long time to be included." She understands this immediately as a description of a
  familiar problem. She does not need it explained.

**Ethics:**

He does not lecture. He has conversations. He tells her, over years, about things that were
treated as normal in Rome that he cannot treat as normal: slavery, what women are allowed
to own and decide, the assumption that birth determines worth. He is careful not to make
her feel that her world is uniquely evil — he tells her about the ways his world continued
to do harm, the ways the future he came from had its own failures. But he is honest:

"There are things here I can't make myself believe are right. Your mother knows this about
me without my saying it. You're old enough to know it directly."

She takes this seriously. She does not perform enlightenment about it or become a crusader.
She is a Roman woman of her class in most of her public behavior. But she thinks about what
he says. He can see her thinking.

**Technology concepts:**

He explains principles:
- *Internal combustion:* Controlled explosions pushing a piston, which turns a shaft.
  He demonstrates the concept with a sealed container and steam (a small version of the
  atmospheric pump). She understands the principle immediately.
- *Flight:* The shape of a wing and why it produces lift (he gives her the pressure
  difference principle — faster air over the curved top, slower air under the flat bottom).
  She asks if he's been in a flying machine. He says yes, several times. She asks what
  it was like. He says: "Boring, mostly. The inside looked like a room. You couldn't see
  the speed."
- *Radio:* Information carried on waves in the air — not visible, but the waves are
  real; they can be generated and detected. He cannot build a radio. He can explain that
  it works and roughly how.
- *Cameras:* Light on a surface that reacts to it — a chemical reaction fixed in place.
  He explains the pinhole-camera principle (she has seen one; Roman opticians use them).
  The photographic chemistry he cannot reconstruct in useful detail, but she understands
  the concept.

She accepts all of this as things that are technically possible. She does not ask him to
build them. She understands that he is describing a world she will not see.

### Age 14–16 (~132–134 AD): The Truth

This is the scene the whole arc has been building toward. See Section 3 for the full
structural note on the scene itself.

The teaching continues through and after the truth-telling. The disclosure changes its
register: she is no longer a student receiving what her father decides to share. She is
a person who knows what she is working with. The sessions become more like editing
sessions than lessons.

She begins keeping her own notes in English. Not just receiving what he writes for her,
but writing down questions, marking gaps, noting where his knowledge becomes uncertain.
She has his habit of the hooked mark (she invents it independently, or inherits it; he
cannot tell). She uses it to flag things he has told her that he has also flagged as
uncertain.

### Age 16–20 (~134–138 AD): Peer

By the time she is sixteen, their sessions look different. She brings him questions he
cannot answer. She identifies gaps in the encyclopedia's structure that he hadn't noticed.
She pushes him on things he has stated confidently that she suspects are imprecise.

"You told me the periodic table has over a hundred elements. You can give me about thirty.
What happened to the other seventy?"

"I don't remember them. I knew them once. They're gone."

"Should I note that the table is incomplete in what you've written?"

"Yes. Note it."

She notes it. She adds her own note: "Father believes approximately one hundred basic
substances exist; has given us approximately thirty; believes the remainder are
retrievable from first principles in a better-equipped laboratory. This should not be
taken as a complete enumeration."

This is her voice. Not his. She has internalized the method — acknowledge what you know,
mark what you don't, don't let the gaps be mistaken for completeness — and is applying
it to his own work.

Daniel finds this almost unbearably useful and also slightly humbling. She is more
systematic than he is about the boundaries of his own knowledge. He was trained in the
habit of marking uncertainty; she applies it more rigorously than he does.

---

### Age 20–35+ (~138–153 AD): Keeper

After Hadrian dies (~138 AD) and the ocean program loses its champion, Daniel begins the
explicit work of securing the encyclopedia against his own death. Ulpia is the primary
keeper of the English-language version. She edits it. She adds to it. She knows what he
knows and what he does not know, and she keeps the distinction with more precision than
he would have kept it himself.

Their conversations in this phase are the most equal they have ever been. She has read
everything he has written. She has asked him to reconstruct, from memory, things he
thought were gone, and recovered more than he expected. She has also found things in his
encyclopedia that she can update from her own observation — medical practices she has seen
Zoticus apply that have diverged from what Daniel originally described; engineering
developments in the workshop that have advanced past his initial framing.

Some of their best conversations are in English, about things he cannot say in Latin
because Latin does not have the words. Concepts in mathematics that he can only express
using English notation. Principles from physics that require a vocabulary Rome has not
developed. The private language is not just personal now — it is technically necessary.
Some of what he knows can only be said in the words he learned it in.

In his last years, when his eyes fail and his hands are less reliable, she reads his notes
back to him. She reads the encyclopedia. She reads his own handwriting to him when he
cannot make it out. He corrects errors in her reading. She corrects errors in his original
text. They are, by this point, collaborating on a document neither of them could have
produced alone.

## SECTION 3: The Truth-Telling Scene

**Structural placement:** ch43 or ch44 territory (~132–136 AD, Ulpia ~14–16)

**Setting:** His private study, or the garden in the evening when the household is quieter.
In English. This is important: the full truth gets said in their private language, not in
Latin. Latin is the language of Rome, of public life, of everything he performs. English
is for this.

**Tone:** The scene is NOT sentimental and NOT dramatic. He has been building toward it
for years and he is still not sure how to say it. He says the facts. She asks questions.
The questions are good. He answers them.

**What he tells her:**

He tells her the year he was born — approximately 1993 AD (he has to translate this; he
uses the Julian calendar to work back and tells her "roughly nineteen hundred years from
now, by the calendar Romans will eventually adopt; I can show you the calculation"). He
tells her what the world was like — briefly, because she already has pieces of this from
years of stories. He tells her how he arrived: he doesn't know. He tells her that he has
told no one the full truth. He specifically names who knows partial truths:

"Heras guessed at the depth of what I know. Tyche has learned English and knows the
cipher is real. Your mother knows I am not what I seem. None of them know this. You are
the only person I have told the complete thing."

He tells her she does not have to believe him.

He tells her: "You can check. Everything I have told you about what is coming — the
things about the western lands, the things about what Rome will eventually become — I
have been right about the parts that have already happened in your lifetime. The rest
will happen or it won't. You can watch."

**Her response:**

She does not weep. She does not exclaim. She processes.

Then she asks specific questions:

*How does he know he didn't imagine it?* He shows her things — English notes he made in
the first weeks, his own handwriting in a script she can read, notes so specific and
internally consistent over the first days that confabulation seems less likely than the
alternative. He describes his phone — a small black device that held more information
than the largest library in Rome, that he exhausted in a matter of weeks, that is now
buried with him when he dies. He describes it with enough specificity — the exact things
he searched for, the sequences of what he found, the way the battery depleted — that she
cannot easily construct an alternative explanation.

*What happened to the people he knew?* He tells her about his family: parents, a sister,
the neighborhood he grew up in, the school he attended, the specific small irrelevant
things he remembers — a friend's name, a teacher who was kind, a dog he walked twice a
week. These are not strategic. They are just true, and telling them to her is the closest
he comes in the scene to something that isn't information.

*Do you want to go back?* He gives her an honest answer. He says: "I wanted to for a
long time. Now — I have a life here. You are here. I don't know anymore what it would
mean to want to go back. I don't know if it would be possible even if I knew how. What
I know is that I am not going back." This is not a triumphant resolution. It is the honest
state of a question he has stopped asking.

*Why are you telling me this now?* He says: "Because you are old enough to hold it, and
because I am not going to live forever, and because what I know should not die with me in
a form no one can use."

**After the scene:**

The education shifts register. She is no longer a student receiving what he decides to
share. She begins asking questions he has been avoiding — not because she is cruel but
because she is thorough and the gaps she can see are real.

"You've never written down the full sequence for soap-making at scale. Why not?"

"I thought Heras had it."

"Heras is dead. Zoticus has a version. Is Zoticus's version correct?"

He tests it against his memory. He tells her what is right and what is approximate.
She writes it down. This is the new mode.

## SECTION 4: What She Does and Doesn't Tell Others

**She tells no one the full truth.** This is not a decision she agonizes over. It is a
decision she makes once and holds without revisiting. The full truth is not useful to
anyone who couldn't already use it, and the people who could use it — the cipher keepers,
the people building the ladder — already have what they need from the encyclopedia. The
story of where Daniel came from would become a distraction, a legend, something to
argue about. She doesn't want it to become a legend. She wants the work to survive.

**She tells Marcia** that Daniel taught her things he doesn't discuss with everyone, that
their private sessions covered more than Latin letters and household management. Marcia
already suspected this. She does not push. There is an unspoken acknowledgment between
them that this is Daniel's corner of the household and Marcia's interest in it is limited
to its not causing trouble.

**She tells Tyche very little.** Their relationship is one of mutual respect and mutual
wariness. They both know they are the two women who will carry Daniel's work forward.
They are not rivals, but they are not close. Tyche knows English; Ulpia knows the full
truth of Daniel's origins. Neither woman tells the other what she knows. This is a
deliberate, unspoken arrangement that neither of them acknowledges.

**The private language in public:** She and Daniel sometimes use English in public settings
— a word, a phrase, a reference that means something only they know. This is not coded
conversation about the encyclopedia or about the future. It is just: *they have a private
language and sometimes they use it*. It is the most visible sign of their relationship and
it is visible as nothing more than a father and daughter with an unusual bond. Anyone who
hears it assumes it is Daniel's "homeland dialect," a Thulean peculiarity inherited by
his daughter. Nobody thinks much about it.

**One specific example that can appear on the page:** She addresses a question to him at
a workshop gathering, in English, about something technical. He answers in English. Everyone
present assumes they are using a private dialect and carries on with the conversation.
What they have actually done is hold a technical consultation about something in the
encyclopedia, in front of a room full of people, with no one the wiser. This is one of
the very few moments in Daniel's life in Rome where he is not performing.

## SECTION 5: The Playground as Character Signal

**Placement:** Part VI, early — shortly before Ulpia's first significant appearance in
the narrative. She is 3–5 years old (~121–123 AD), Part V territory, ch35–36 area.

**The beat:**

A workshop visitor or neighbor comes to the house on business. On the way through the garden
or past the garden wall, they see the structure — the swing, the frame, the rope ladder. It
is not enormous. It is not alarming. It is simply unusual: wooden, deliberate, not a game
set up in the moment but something built to be there.

The visitor asks what it is. Daniel says: "My daughter plays on it." The visitor looks at
it again — at the seat on the swing, the carved handholds — and asks nothing further,
or asks "where did you get the design?" Daniel says something brief and noncommittal. The
conversation moves on.

He built it because he knows what a swing feels like. He built it because he wanted her
childhood to have something. He did not build it for Rome. He built it for her.

**One paragraph. One observation. One small exchange. Then it is just there.**

Do not expand this. Do not make it a scene about Daniel's grief for his own childhood.

## SECTION 7: What NOT to Do

**Do not make Ulpia preternaturally wise.** She is smart, relentless, and direct. She is
also a child until she isn't, and then a young Roman woman with Roman assumptions she has
not entirely shed. She will defer to authority in social situations even while privately
disagreeing. She will accept things that Daniel has marked uncertain without being able
to resolve them. She will ask good questions but not always the right ones. Her specific
voice from the character file: "the relentless questioner — states things flat, the way
she says a sum; pushes at a problem certain there is a true figure." That is her gift.
It is not omniscience.

**Do not have the truth-telling scene become a weeping reunion.** It is two people having
a factual conversation about an impossible thing. He tells her facts. She asks questions.
The questions are good. He answers honestly. She does not perform amazement. She processes
and then works. If there is any emotion in the scene, it belongs to Daniel, briefly, not
to her — and even then it should be implied, not stated.

**Do not have Ulpia go around correcting Romans with future knowledge.** She keeps the
secret. She uses what she knows to take care of the encyclopedia and to manage Daniel's
work. She does not tell anyone that Rome will fall or that the Antonine Plague is coming
or that democracy will be invented. She is not a prophet. She is a keeper.

**Do not make Daniel's lessons explicit school scenes with him lecturing.** They are
conversations. She asks questions he doesn't expect. He answers what he can and marks
what he can't. She pushes on the marks. He explains why they're there. The teaching is
driven by her curiosity, not by his lesson plan.

**Do not make the playground a major set piece.** One paragraph. One note from a visitor
or neighbor. Then the swing is just there, in the garden, being used.

**Do not have Daniel know everything.** He was in high school when he left. He knows
algebra but not advanced mathematics. He knows Newton's laws but not general relativity.
He knows the first two rows of the periodic table with confidence and becomes increasingly
uncertain after that. He knows the plots of books he's read but not the exact text of
most of them. She will eventually know some things better than he does, because she has
read his notes more carefully and more recently than he has, and because she is more
systematic about marking the limits of what she knows.

**Do not let the Naming Note cause confusion.** Tyche's freed name is "Ulpia Tyche" — a
nomen she refuses and is never called on the page. Ulpia Severa is Daniel's daughter. They
are different people. The friction belongs to Tyche's nomen only. Ulpia Severa's name is
not a problem for her.

## A NOTE ON VOICE IN ULPIA SCENES

She speaks more than Tyche and less than Marcia. She does not have Tyche's habit of
stating the damning fact and stopping. She does not have Marcia's habit of building a
long structural argument with every side laid out. She asks the question that needs to
be asked, waits for the answer, and asks the next question. She is, in Daniel's words
to no one, the only person he's ever talked to who makes him feel like the conversation
is going somewhere.

The private language between them is a register shift for Daniel. When he speaks English
with her, he sounds slightly more like himself than he does in Latin — looser, less
performed, more likely to reach for a modern reference and actually be understood. She
catches the references. She does not always understand them, but she notes them and asks
later. She keeps a list, in English, of words and phrases Daniel has used that she cannot
find in anything he has written for her. She intends to ask him about all of them. She
does not always get to.

The last thing he says to her in the narrative is technical. That is correct.
