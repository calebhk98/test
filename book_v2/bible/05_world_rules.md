# World Rules — the hard constraints (no exceptions without coordinator sign-off)

These are the physics of the book. Break one and the whole premise leaks.

## 1. No magic, no second miracle
The ONE impossible thing is the arrival itself, and it is **never explained**.
No mechanism, no portal, no voice, no system, no quest-giver. Daniel does not get
a second supernatural assist, ever. Everything after the first morning obeys the
real world.

## 2. The phone
- A modern smartphone, ~98% charge on arrival. No signal, no GPS, no wifi,
  forever (treat as a permanent dead zone; he stops expecting otherwise within
  days).
- It works as a self-contained device: clock, calculator, camera, notes,
  whatever was already downloaded (some music, some photos, maybe an offline
  map app that's useless without data, a couple of saved articles by luck).
- **Battery: it dies within DAYS, not weeks.** CANON (per ch01-03): his panicked
  first day - calls, the dialer, camera, the maps app, and above all the cellular
  radio screaming at full power hunting for a signal that isn't there - torched
  most of the charge (98% on arrival down to ~11% by the morning of day two,
  despite switching to airplane mode and dimming the screen overnight). So the
  phone dies for good around day three to four, not in two weeks. This makes his
  notes an even more rushed, panicked fragment, which suits the theme. After it
  dies, EVERYTHING is human memory, fallible. No charging it, no solar trick, no
  Roman battery. The dead phone becomes a glass relic he keeps - a talisman, a
  danger if seen.
- Do not let the phone be a cheat. It is a clock and a calculator for two weeks
  and a grief object after.

## 3. Daniel's knowledge ceiling
- Only what a bright-but-normal 2020s 17-year-old plausibly carries: arithmetic
  and algebra solidly, geometry decently, the IDEA of calculus (limits, areas
  under curves) but not the rigor, Newtonian intuition, a fuzzy picture of atoms/
  cells/germs/evolution/the solar system, half-remembered history, a lot of
  sci-fi furniture.
- He is allowed - required - to be WRONG the way such a teen is wrong:
  misremembering which century something happened, thinking China had gunpowder
  by 100 AD (it didn't), thinking copper springs would work (they won't),
  garbling a formula under pressure. See `research/tech_feasibility.md` for the
  truth he should keep bumping into.
- He cannot derive what he never knew. No conveniently total recall of a
  textbook. When he doesn't know, he guesses, fails, or works it out slowly with
  Roman craftsmen, and sometimes simply can't.

## 4. Technology obeys material reality
- Every invention costs money, sweat, failed prototypes, and social maneuvering.
  Nothing "just works." Follow the feasibility tiers in
  `research/tech_feasibility.md` and the schedule in `04_tech_schedule.md`.
- The three signature struggles (write him losing here): the **manned balloon**
  (crashes/burns before it flies clean), the **mechanical clock** (the escapement
  never clicks in his memory; the promised clock never keeps time), and **useful
  optics** (Roman glass gives a blurry, color-fringed 2-3x toy). The atom-seeing
  microscope is a deliberate LIE he tells and it stays a lie.
- "Impossible bait" (steam engine, electricity, engines, aircraft, floating
  trains, computers): he NAMES these to seed imagination and is honest with
  himself that he will never see them built. They are never built in his life.

## 5. Language acquisition (no instant fluency)
- Latin under survival pressure: gestures and a handful of words in weeks,
  functional broken Latin in a few months, conversational within about a year,
  fluent-with-an-accent over years. He never loses the accent; he makes errors
  that cost him. Greek he picks up more slowly, via Heras and the educated world.
- Early chapters: render the language barrier honestly (misunderstandings, the
  humiliation of being unable to speak, drawing instead of saying). Do not let
  him eavesdrop on plot-critical Latin he couldn't yet understand.
- Over the long book his English narration slowly takes on Latin cadence and
  Roman assumptions; late on he reaches for the English word and misses. Show the
  drift; never announce it.

## 6. The germ framing
- Daniel cannot say "bacteria." He reframes disease as **tiny invisible creatures
  the gods send (or that sneak in) to climb inside you and attack from within** -
  a story that motivates boiling water, washing wounds, and cleaning, without a
  blasphemy or quackery charge. It maps onto nothing in Roman medicine (miasma,
  humors, divine wrath), so it is fragile, mockable, and dangerous. It half-works,
  gets absorbed in distorted form, and a bad plague year may seem to prove or
  disprove him by luck. He has no statistics and never sees the mortality curve.

## 7. The anti-slavery method
- Daniel never wins this with arguments or speeches. He fights it structurally:
  running his workshop on paid freedmen and freeing those he can, demonstrating
  that free skilled labor out-produces chained labor, building rail carts that
  reduce the need for slave porters - and being defeated, often, by the brutal
  fact that slaves are cheaper, so people don't adopt the alternative. Keep the
  argument in events. Keep Daniel's revulsion shown, not preached.

## 8. History bends but does not break on a whim
- Real events (Trajan's wars and death, Hadrian's accession, etc.) happen roughly
  on schedule early; Daniel's influence accumulates and begins to deflect things
  later. Any divergence from real history must be deliberate, logged in the
  timeline, and earned. Daniel does not know the future precisely (he's a teen);
  he misremembers dates and is sometimes blindsided (e.g., he may not know when
  Trajan dies).

## 9. Mortality and stakes are real
- People die of infected cuts, in childbirth, in war, of plague, of old age. The
  era is lethal. Daniel's fear of a fatal cut is rational. Named characters can
  and should die. Daniel himself ages and dies in the book; the encyclopedia and
  the people he taught are what survive him.
