# World Rules V2 — the hard constraints

These are the physics of the book. Break one and the whole premise leaks.
Rules added or changed from V1 are marked [V2 CHANGE] or [V2 NEW].

---

## 1. No magic, no second miracle

The ONE impossible thing is the arrival itself, and it is NEVER explained. No
mechanism, no portal, no voice, no system, no quest-giver. Daniel does not get a
second supernatural assist, ever. Everything after the first morning obeys the
real world. The phone is a clock and a calculator for two days, then a grief
object. It is never recharged. There is no solar trick, no Roman battery.

---

## 2. The phone

- A modern smartphone, ~98% charge on arrival. No signal, no GPS, no wifi, forever.
- It works as a self-contained device: clock, calculator, camera, notes, whatever
  was already downloaded (some music, some photos, maybe an offline map app useless
  without data, a couple of saved articles by luck).
- **Battery: it dies within DAYS, not weeks.** Canon (per ch01–03): panicked first
  day of calls, the dialer, the camera, the maps app, and the cellular radio
  screaming at full power hunting for a signal torched most of the charge (98% on
  arrival to ~11% by morning of day two, despite airplane mode and dimmed screen
  overnight). The phone dies for good around day three to four. Notes are a rushed,
  panicked fragment; this suits the theme. EVERYTHING after is human memory,
  fallible.
- The dead phone becomes a glass relic he keeps — talisman, danger if seen. Do not
  let it be a cheat. Do not let him retrieve facts from it after day four.

---

## 3. Daniel's knowledge ceiling

Only what a bright-but-normal 2020s 17-year-old plausibly carries:
- Arithmetic and algebra solidly; geometry decently; the IDEA of calculus (limits,
  areas under curves) but not the rigor.
- Newtonian intuition: F = ma, gravity, action/reaction.
- Fuzzy picture of atoms/cells/germs/evolution/the solar system.
- Half-remembered history — he misremembers dates. He thinks China had gunpowder
  by 100 AD (it didn't). He thinks copper springs would work (they won't). He
  garbles formulas under pressure.
- Knows as fact-fragments: Earth orbits the Sun; the compass works; different metals
  in salt water make electricity (lemon-battery demo); alcohol sterilizes wounds;
  lead is poisonous (Roman pipes = famous); paper can be made from rags; you can
  make soap from fat and ash; double-entry bookkeeping tracks both sides; vaccines
  work by giving a small amount to prevent the severe form; stirrups help cavalry;
  sailors need citrus to prevent scurvy.
- A lot of sci-fi furniture.

He cannot derive what he never knew. No total recall of a textbook. When he does
not know, he guesses, fails, or works it out slowly with Roman craftsmen — and
sometimes simply cannot.

He is required to be WRONG the way such a teen is wrong: misremembering which
century something happened; confident mistakes that cost him.

---

## 4. Technology obeys material reality

Every invention costs money, sweat, failed prototypes, and social maneuvering.
Nothing "just works." Follow the feasibility tiers in `04_tech_schedule.md`.

**[V2 CHANGE] "Rome lacks X" is NOT a permanent wall.** A funded, motivated,
knowledgeable teen manufactures the missing prerequisite and creates demand for it.
"We lack precision" is a business plan, not a verdict. "We have no electricity" is
an excuse to build a Volta pile, not a permanent stop sign. The author agent must
show Daniel attempting to manufacture prerequisites before declaring a wall.

**[V2 NEW] Distinguish static walls from dynamic walls:**
- A STATIC wall is: the prerequisite does not exist AND cannot be manufactured in
  Daniel's lifetime with available materials and his knowledge. Very few things
  qualify. (Examples: rubber/gutta-percha insulation for telegraph wire; high-
  frequency vacuum-tube oscillators for radio.)
- A DYNAMIC wall is: the prerequisite does not currently exist BUT Daniel can
  manufacture it with a bootstrapping chain. These are NOT walls; they are
  multi-phase projects. (Examples: precision temperature measurement via Volta pile
  → galvanometer → thermocouple; stationary atmospheric steam via foundry bronze
  → boring bar → condensation injection; precision length via vernier calipers →
  surface plate → go/no-go gauges.)

A writing agent that writes "Daniel sighed; Rome had no X" without first showing
Daniel attempt to manufacture X is violating the book's central argument.

**The four signature struggles (write him losing here):**
- The manned balloon — crashes/burns before it flies clean; costs blood. The vow
  covers manned fire-flight ONLY; unmanned kite/glider work resumes in 5–7 years.
- The mechanical clock — the escapement never clicks in his memory; the promised
  clock never keeps time; he builds water clocks instead.
- Useful optics — Roman glass gives a blurry, color-fringed 2–3x toy; hype
  outruns the glass.
- The atom-seeing microscope — a DELIBERATE LIE he tells; it stays a lie.

**[V2 CHANGE] Steam is no longer "impossible bait" in full.** A Newcomen-class
stationary atmospheric pump engine (near-atmospheric pressure, rough-cast bronze
cylinder, leather-packed piston, condensation-injection) is achievable in ~15–25
years of funded work. It is dangerous, ~1% efficient, and leaks. But it pumps water
from a mine. Mobile/locomotive/high-pressure steam (Trevithick/Watt class) remains
correctly blocked on the metallurgy wall.

**[V2 CHANGE] Electricity is no longer "impossible bait" in full.** A Volta pile
(zinc, copper, brine/vinegar — all in Rome) produces real current. A compass-needle
galvanometer detects it. An iron-copper thermocouple produces a measurable voltage
at forge temperatures. These are achievable with 5–20 years of funded work by a man
who knows the fact-fragments. The dynamo, the motor, and the electric telegraph
remain correctly blocked.

## 5. Language acquisition (no instant fluency)

Latin under survival pressure: gestures and a handful of words in weeks, functional
broken Latin in a few months, conversational within about a year, fluent-with-an-
accent over years. He never loses the accent; he makes errors that cost him. Greek
he picks up more slowly, via Heras and the educated world.

Early chapters: render the language barrier honestly. Do not let him eavesdrop on
plot-critical Latin he couldn't yet understand. Over the long book his English
narration slowly takes on Latin cadence and Roman assumptions; late on he reaches
for the English word and misses. Show the drift; never announce it.

---

## 6. The germ framing

Daniel cannot say "bacteria." He reframes disease as "tiny invisible creatures the
gods send (or that sneak in) to climb inside you and attack from within" — a story
that motivates boiling water, washing wounds, and cleaning, without a blasphemy or
quackery charge. It maps onto nothing in Roman medicine (miasma, humors, divine
wrath). It half-works, gets absorbed in distorted form, and a bad plague year may
seem to prove or disprove him by luck. He has no statistics and never sees the
mortality curve.

---

## 7. The anti-slavery method

Daniel never wins this with arguments or speeches. He fights it structurally:
running his workshop on paid freedmen and freeing those he can, demonstrating that
free skilled labor out-produces chained labor, building rail carts that reduce the
need for slave porters — and being defeated, often, by the brutal fact that slaves
are cheaper, so people don't adopt the alternative. Keep the argument in events.
Keep Daniel's revulsion shown, not preached.

## 8. History bends — and must be shown bending by 110 AD

[V2 CHANGE — this rule is now stronger and more specific]

Real events (Trajan's wars and death, Hadrian's accession, etc.) happen roughly on
schedule early. Daniel's influence accumulates and begins to deflect things. Any
divergence from real history must be deliberate, logged in the timeline, and earned.

**[V2 NEW] The book MUST show measurable historical divergence by 110 AD**, not
just "texture changes" (capped numerals, hygiene). V1's macro-timeline through
117 AD was essentially real history. This undermines the book's premise.

By 105–110 AD the prose must contain at least two of these (or equivalent):
- The Roman army adopts stirrups ~30 years early.
- Doubled literacy rate in one city (measurable, not asserted).
- A different outcome in one siege or engagement because of Daniel's specific
  input (field sanitation, a signaling device, a design change to a siege engine).
- A trade route opening earlier than historical record because of the compass or
  a navigational improvement.
- An epidemic curve that bends differently because handwashing spread through a
  garrison.

These are small but real. The book does not need world-historical upheaval; it
needs evidence on the page that things are already different.

Daniel does not know the future precisely — he is a teen; he misremembers dates and
is sometimes blindsided (he may not know when Trajan dies).

---

## 9. Mortality and stakes are real

People die of infected cuts, in childbirth, in war, of plague, of old age. The era
is lethal. Daniel's fear of a fatal cut is rational. Named characters can and should
die. Daniel himself ages and dies in the book; the encyclopedia and the people he
taught are what survive him.

---

## 10. Daniel must notice what any teen would notice [V2 NEW]

A 2020s American teen stranded in 98 AD Rome would IMMEDIATELY notice and act on:
- **Lead everywhere** — pipes, cooking pots, wine sweetener (defrutum). The most
  famous schoolbook fact about Rome. If Daniel does not notice this, readers who
  know it will stop trusting him. He must notice it by the end of Part I and act
  on his own household at minimum.
- **No soap** — the standard handwashing thread (Rule 6) requires the next step:
  making soap. Both inputs are in Rome (tallow, wood-ash lye). If Daniel teaches
  "wash your hands" but never makes soap, the logic has a visible gap.
- **No paper** — a teen who builds a printing press immediately asks "why are we
  printing on this expensive stiff papyrus?" Rag paper must appear in Phase B.
- **Fire risk** — Rome's wooden insulae are firetrap tenements. Daniel knows about
  the Great Fire of Rome. If he causes or witnesses a significant fire and never
  proposes a fire-watch brigade, he looks oblivious.
- **Citrus on long voyages** — Daniel runs an ocean program. He knows "sailors need
  citrus or their teeth fall out." He must at minimum pack citrus on trial voyages
  and state why.
- **Stirrups** — a leather loop hung from a saddle for the foot. Daniel spends
  time with the Roman army. He must notice horses are ridden without stirrups.

These are not optional flavor. A writing agent that skips them makes Daniel look
like a caricature who only remembers the things the author finds interesting.

---

## 11. "All tracks lead to Rome" — catchphrase required [V2 NEW]

The book's reframing of "all roads lead to Rome" was named in the original bible
and was NEVER WRITTEN in V1. It must appear in V2 — either as Daniel's line when
talking about the potential for rail, or as something Felix or a later generation
says about the rail-and-steam dream. Do not let the book end without it.

---

## 12. Tyche's "Ulpia" nomen — one on-page friction scene required [V2 NEW]

The bible says she refuses "Ulpia" and is only ever "Tyche" on the page; the
imposed nomen is lasting friction. V1 carried this as narrative description only —
no one ever calls her "Ulpia" to her face with the camera watching. V2 requires at
minimum one short scene where someone uses her full freed name ("Ulpia Tyche" or
simply "Ulpia"), she responds with silence or one syllable, and Daniel notices what
just happened. This scene does not need to be long; it needs to happen once.
