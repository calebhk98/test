# Chapter 52: A Generation Later

The gears came in a cedar box with a sliding lid, and the lid stuck, and Procula stood over it in the cold of the press-room with her thumbnail under the lip and worked it loose the way you work a stubborn shutter, a little at each end, until the wood gave and the smell came up out of it. Old grease gone to wax. Iron that had been wept on by the river air for longer than she had been alive and had taken a brown skin she could rub away with her spit and a rag to find the gray underneath, still good, still cut clean. She lifted out the straw, gone the color of a wasp's nest, and laid the gears on the bench in the order they came, which was no order, a jumble a dead man's hands had made and forty years had pickled.

She was twenty, that spring, or near it. Her mother had been bought into the storeroom as a girl and freed in the great widow's will and had married a freedman of the press, and so Procula had been born free into the smell of lamp-black and had never spent a day out of earshot of the screw coming down. She kept the day-book for the casting end of the fund now, the way her mother had kept the storeroom book and the woman before her mother, a line of women who had each taught two others to read the marks and then taught them the other thing, the secret thing, the homeland script that ran the wrong way along the page and made a sound in your head like no Latin ever made. She had been six when she learned the marks, eleven when old Vibia set the cipher in front of her and watched her find the first word and made her swear the oath every keeper swore, to set it down whole and to teach not less than two.

So she could read what was scratched on the box. Most could not. The lid had a line cut into the underside in the foreign letters, faint, a careful hand gone shaky at the end of it, and she had read it the first day the box was given over to the casting shed and had read it a hundred times since, standing where she stood now with the gears spread out cold in front of her.

BEGIN WHERE I FAILED.

---

The fund paid her father's cousin Saturius to fail. That was the joke the wharf made of it, and it was not wrong, only it was said as a sneer and meant, by the dead founder and the deed that bound them all, as the highest praise. A man came under the fund on a living wage and a hand or two and coal and metal for a term of years, and what they kept of him for the judging was the record of his trying, every attempt set in the book with the date and the way it broke, so the next man need not break it the same way twice. Saturius had spent eleven years on the self-feeding pot, the engine of fire and water the founder's own contest had set the children dreaming of before any of them were born. He had got it to spin a little wheel and scald his arm and never once to pull a cart, and he would die at it, cheerful, because the steel that could hold the fire's whole strength was not there yet and would not be in his lifetime, and a man could lay a rung he would never stand on. Felix had said the same before him. The book had Felix's hand in it near the front, in the marks and the cipher both, the failures of a man dead these thirty years, and Saturius's after, and now Procula's own.

She was not on the pot. She had asked for the clock.

---

It was not a single morning. It was the better part of two years, and most of it was wrong. The founder had left the clock the way he left everything, honest and incomplete and marked where it lied. She read his pages on it the winter the box came down. He wrote that a hanging weight will fall and turn a wheel and that this is easy, and that the trouble is all in the not-falling, in the catching, in some little part that must let the wheel go a step and then hold it and then let it go a step, even and even and even, no faster when the weight is full than when it is nearly down. He drew teeth that caught on a swinging bar and a swinging bar that caught on teeth, and at the foot of nearly every drawing he had put the hooked stroke, the mark the keepers all knew, the founder is guessing here, do not trust this, climb past it. On one page he had written under the drawing, in the failing hand: it wants to be even and it will not be even, the falling is greedy, find the thing that makes it patient. And then the hooked stroke after, twice, because he did not trust that he had even asked the question rightly.

She built his drawings first. All of them. That was the rung he had laid and she meant to stand on every plank of it before she cut her own. She had Saturius's apprentice cut the teeth and filed the swinging bar herself and hung it on a pivot of hardened pin in two cups of bronze, and set the little weight to fall, and the wheel ran away under the bar the way the founder said it would, greedy, all the weight spending itself in a breath, the bar knocked aside, the teeth a blur, the whole of it stopped against the frame with a buzz and a smell of hot metal. With the bar heavier the wheel ran away slower and still ran away. With it lighter the wheel would not turn the bar at all and sat dead. She wrote each one in the book with the date and the way it failed, because that was the oath under the other oath, and some nights she read the founder's hooked strokes and her own beside them and could not have said whose were whose.

---

What she understood, she understood with a piece of cloth in her hand and not in her head.

She had been turning the swinging bar over. It caught the wheel and was kicked off it and swung free and came back and caught again, and the catching was the whole problem, where the greed got in, the wheel shoving the bar away too hard so the next catch came too soon. She sat with it a long time. The press was going on the far side of the wall, the long even shudder of the screw, fifty to a page, a thing that did not hurry no matter how much paper was stacked to feed it, and she had heard it all her life and did not hear it now. She was thinking about a swing. Not the bar. A child's swing, the kind they hung in the colonnade for the little ones, a board on two ropes, and how a child on it goes out and comes back in the same time whether you push her hard or soft, the far swings only wider, never quicker, the rope deciding the time.

The rope decided the time. Not the push.

She put the bar down and picked it up and felt the way it wanted to swing of itself, slow, in its own good time, if only the wheel would stop bullying it. The founder had it backwards, or she had read him backwards, and it did not matter which, only that the wheel must not drive the swing. The swing must rule the wheel. The wheel must come asking leave to move and be let go a step only when the bar in its own slow swing came round to open the gate, and take from the wheel only the least push, enough to keep its swinging up against the drag of the air and no more, the weight's whole strength held back behind a gate that opened a crack at a time on the swing's own count.

She had no word for it. There was no word for it, in Latin or in the cipher, because the thing had never stood up in the world for anyone to name. She made the pivot truer. She made the bar longer and hung two small weights on it she could slide along its length, in to swing quicker and out to swing slow, and cut the gate-teeth fresh and shallow so the wheel could push the bar only the littlest amount and could not shove past it. She filed and hung and let the weight fall and watched the wheel buzz away to nothing, and slid the bobs, and filed the teeth shallower, and hung it again, eleven times, twelve, the lamp guttering, her hands black to the wrist and one knuckle bleeding where the file had jumped.

On the thirteenth hanging the bar swung out and the wheel let down one tooth and the bar swung back and the wheel held, and the bar swung out and the wheel let down one tooth, and held, and the sound it made was one she would have to teach the wharf a word for. A small dry knock, and a wait, and a small dry knock, the same knock and the same wait, the weight falling all the while behind the gate and being made to fall patiently, a step and a wait and a step, even and even and even while she stood and did not breathe and counted it against the press through the wall, which went down and up four times to the bar's seven, and stayed at four to seven.

She let it run. She did not touch it. She watched the weight go down the whole of its drop, slow, slow, the wheel turning under the patient bar, and when the weight came to the floor and the knocking stopped she found she had been standing so long her feet had gone, and that her face was wet, and that she had not made a sound the whole time for fear of frightening it, which was foolish, it could only keep its one even step against the greed of the falling world.

---

It was not a clock yet. A clock wants more than the patient step. It wants the step counted and shown, a wheel of hours, a face, a hand, a way to wind the weight back up without the whole of it running away while you wind, a year of fitting after the thing first knocks. But the hard rung was the step, and the step was climbed.

She fitted it through the spring. She geared a slow wheel off the fast one to drag a single pointer round a wooden dial marked in the divisions of the day, the day split in two and the halves in twelves the way the sundials split them, uneven by the season the way Roman hours had always been uneven, because she had no other hours to give it and the city kept none. She set one weight to last a half a day on its drop and a second to take over while the first was wound, and stood the whole of it in a cedar case taller than herself, against the press-room wall, leveled with wedges and a bowl of water until the bar swung true.

And then she did the thing the deed did not require and no one asked of her. She took down the fund's book, the one with Felix's hand near the front and the founder's pages on the clock copied into it in the cipher, and turned to the place where he had written BEGIN WHERE I FAILED and under it the hooked stroke twice, and on the next leaf, which he had left white with only a note that the page was for the one who came after, she set down in the homeland script, small, the way old Vibia had taught her, what the swinging bar had taught her with the cloth in her hand. That the wheel must not drive the swing. That the swing must rule the wheel and take only the least push to keep itself up. How to slide the bobs, in to quicken and out to slow. Under it her own hand, and the date, and that the step held against the press at four to seven and did not drift across a whole day's drop. She did not put the hooked stroke after any of it. She read it three times to be sure it was true before she let it stand without the mark, because the mark was a debt to the ones not born.

---

They held the contest on the Quinquatrus, the way it had been held every spring since before the founder died, in Minerva's name, the priests paid out of the old endowment to bless what they had once been ready to burn. Procula went up to hear the year's tales read out, the boy with the chariot that ran on a tamed bolt of the sky's own lightning, the girl with the boat that needed no rowers and no wind, the same dreams the printed sheets had been spreading into the children's games for three generations now, a long way ahead of any metal. She had brought no tale. She had brought the case on a cart, two men sweating under it, set it at the edge of the festival ground and let it run, and the wharf stood around it the way the Subura crowd had once stood around a bag of greased cloth that rose and burned.

It knocked. A step and a wait and a step, dry and even, the pointer crawling down its first division of the morning so slow you could not catch it moving and could not, after a while, deny that it had moved. An old man asked her, half angry, what it was for, and she told him it told the hours, and he said the sundial told the hours and cost nothing and did not knock, and she said the sundial could not tell them in the dark or under cloud or at the bottom of the sea, and he laughed at the sea and went away. A boy stood at it the whole afternoon with his lips parted, and when the first weight ran low and she reached in to shift the drive to the second, he asked could he watch how, and she showed him, because that was the oath, not less than two.

She did not know the man who had laid the bottom of it. She knew the name they all knew, the Thulean, the founder, the man who made fire fly and came down out of the ice with the marks and the press and the books, a name on a deed and a hand in a book and a line cut shaky under a cedar lid. She had read his honest failures and stood on them and climbed the one rung he had marked the most carefully because he could not climb it himself, and she had never once thought to wonder what country a man comes from who has a word in his script for a kind of frozen rain that falls on no street in Rome. It was the founder's tongue, and the founder was from the cold north, and the cold north had a word for it. There was no further to wonder, and the truth of him went on lying quiet under the press-room stone and under the dirt of a grave she had never seen.

---

That night she could not sleep for the knocking, which she could still hear though the case stood three walls off, or thought she could, the way you hear a thing your body has learned. She got up in the dark and went to it and stood with her hand flat on the warm cedar, listening to the patient step tell the dark its even count, and thought, not in words, about the next thing.

For it drifted, this, only slow, slower than any clepsydra the founder ever geared, and slow was not still. It gained on the cold mornings and lost in the wet, the way her own pulse ran with the weather, and a clock that gained and lost was no use to the thing the founder had wanted it for and written about in the cipher among his failures, the great hulls he drew going west on the blank water, the wanting to know how far west you had gone by the hour the sun stood high over you set against the hour it stood high over the home you had left. A man could keep that only with a clock that did not gain or lose for a month together while the deck heaved and the salt got in. There was a prize for it still, the founder's prize, never won, the coin of it grown fat with the waiting. He had marked the page everywhere with the hooked stroke and written that he did not know how, only that whoever learned it would learn it from a thing that swung even in a moving ship, which he had never seen and could not draw, only name and leave white.

She took her hand off the warm wood. The step went on without her, even and even, a rung made solid that a man had only been able to point at in the dark. Somewhere above it, in the white part of the page, the next one waited to be cut. She would not reach it, most likely. The hulls would not be built in her life, nor the swinging thing that does not care that the world heaves, nor the half of what the children rolled across the floor in painted clay. But she had two girls to teach now, and a thing to write down small and true under the founder's, for whichever of them was quick enough to stand on it and reach the one above.

She went back through the dark to her bed by the feel of the wall, the way the founder had gone down his last stair, and behind her in its cedar case the patient step kept the count of the climbing night, and far over her head, where the dial did not reach and her hands could not yet go, the next rung waited, white, and the one above that, the long ladder of them going up out of the lamplight into the dark she would spend her life beginning to climb.
