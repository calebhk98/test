# Chapter 19: The Contest

Rome in that winter was drunk on a scale I had no frame for.

The gold I'd counted in a tent on a mountain had come down the road ahead of me and turned into spectacle, games announced that went on for a span of days I didn't believe when somebody first said the number, money given away to the citizens, bread and oil and meat. The wine shops had their amphorae stacked in the street, and a man could not walk to the wharf without somebody pressing a cup on him, because the dead king's gold was paying for it and you'd be a fool to refuse free wine bought with somebody else's war. I drank it in a crowd that loved the emperor and loved that the war had paid, and I thought about the slope, and I drank another.

So when I tell you that this was the winter I finally did the thing I'd been drafting by lamplight for two years, understand that part of why I did it then was that I needed, badly, to make something that wasn't that. I had spent a summer keeping men alive with water and an autumn killing a city with water, the same hand, and I wanted to use the hand on something that just made people imagine. That's not a noble reason to found an institution. It's the true one.

---

The thing I wanted was simple to say and a nightmare to arrange. A prize. Real money, every year, on a fixed day, for the best tale of a machine that never was. A cart that ran with no horse and no slave to push it. A glass that showed the little ones I'd spent five years trying to talk Romans into believing in. A ship that crossed the sea with no wind and no men chained to the oars. An engine that did the work of a hundred men.

I had a head full of machines I could describe and not build, and I'd understood somewhere on that road that the machines weren't the gift. I was never going to live to see a horseless cart. The gift was getting a few thousand people to want one, to think it ordinary to want one, so the wanting would still be there in the heads of people not yet born when the steel finally came good.

I tried to explain this to Heras and got about three sentences in before he held up a hand.

"You want to pay people," he said, "to lie."

"To imagine."

"In Rome," Heras said, "those are close cousins, and the family is not respectable." He was grayer than when I'd met him, and I was selfishly glad of one face that hadn't been on a mountain. "A man may write of gods doing impossible things, because the gods did them. A man who writes of a cart doing impossible things has written a fable, and a fable is for children and Greeks." He paused. "I am a Greek. I am allowed to say it."

He was right, which was his entire function in my life. A foreigner, the man some salon-going people were still being told was a fraud and a possible blasphemer, could not simply announce that he was paying silver for the best made-up story about a machine. That's a foreign oddity buying attention and lying about machines, which is approximately the sentence I could hear Crispus composing before I'd written a word of the rules.

The approach came from the city itself. Rich men endowed things, games and feasts and a fountain with their name on it, *so-and-so paid for this, remember so-and-so,* because in a city with a short memory, your name carved on a thing was how you went on existing. The prize wasn't the problem. The prize was normal. The problem was whose name was on it.

So it couldn't be mine.

---

I went to Macer.

This will not surprise anyone who has been paying attention. He was my patron now in the proper Roman sense, which meant I attended his door in the gray morning with his other clients, an indignity he enjoyed visibly and I endured, because the emperor's freedman queuing in Macer's atrium was a thing Macer could show people, and showing people things was half of what wealth was for. I made the pitch in his study, the room that still smelled of the leather he sold the legions, with him turning the heavy gold ring on his finger the way he did when he was deciding whether a thing would make money or make him a fool, which to Macer were the only two outcomes worth considering.

"You want me," he said, "to put my name on a prize for stories."

"I want your name on a benefaction," I said. "The Macer prize. Given by Titus Flavius Macer, every year, for the best Latin telling of a marvel."

"What marvel."

"A machine. A device. A thing of craft that does something men can't do." Macer did not care about a civilization dreaming forward. Macer cared about leather contracts and his own immortality and being seen to be a man of taste, which he was not, and knew he was not, and minded. "Apollodorus builds bridges across rivers nobody could bridge, and Caesar gives him glory for it. You can't build a bridge. But you can be the man whose name is on the contest where Rome dreams up the bridges nobody's built yet. You'll have your name on the new thing."

"You want my name," he said, "because nobody'll come to a prize given by a barbarian freedman who'll be telling them to wash their hands at it."

"Yes. And the silver's mine. I pay, you're seen to pay. It costs you nothing but lending the name and letting it be read out under your roof once a year."

Macer turned the ring. He saw a low-cost way to be a literary patron without having to read anything, plus the pleasure of the emperor's clever Thulean funding glory for the name Flavius Macer, and that those two things were sufficient.

"What do I have to do," he said.

"Host the reading. Sit at the front. Look like a man of taste."

"I can't look like a man of taste," Macer said. "I look like a man who sells the army boots."

"Then look like a man who sells the army its boots and also funds poetry. It's a better look. Half the senate would kill for it and can't afford the boots."

He laughed at that, the short ugly bark he had, and I knew I had him, because the one flattery that always worked on Macer was the one that was also a little insulting and also true.

"Write me the rules," he said. "Short. And keep your name off the front of it. You're the judge if you want, the Thulean who knows the foreign marvels, that's fine, that's a flavor, people like a tame barbarian to weigh the entries. But the prize is mine."

"It's yours," I said, and meant it, and that was the price and I paid it gladly. Almost everything I built in Rome, I built by handing the credit to a Roman. I stopped minding it years before this. You learn, as the foreigner, that your name on a thing is a weight the thing has to carry, and most things fly better if you take the weight off.

---

We set the day at the *Quinquatrus*, the festival of Minerva in the spring, which Heras suggested and which was perfect, because Minerva is the goddess of craftsmen and the schools, and a prize for marvels of craft given on her festival had the gods covered, which mattered more than I can say with Vibenius still out there somewhere weighing me. You did not, in Rome, launch a strange new thing about impossible machines without nailing it firmly to a god first.

The silver was a hundred denarii to the winner, a third of a legionary's yearly pay, enough that a poor man with a quick imagination and somebody to write his words down would walk a long way to try for it. I could have made it more, the Dacian gold had everybody throwing money around, but I didn't want senators' sons entering for sport. I wanted the schoolmaster, the surveyor, the freedman clerk, the kid in the Subura who'd never owned anything. A hundred denarii to them was the moon. To a senator it was a tip. I aimed it the way you aim anything, by deciding who I wanted to bend down and pick it up.

The entries came in on whatever people had, the part I loved most once it started. Most people in Rome can't write, and most who can can't afford clean papyrus, so the marvels came on wax tablets and potsherds and the backs of old accounts and once on a strip of board that had been part of a wine crate, the grain showing through the soot-ink. A man brought his on a borrowed tablet and stood over me while I read it because he had to take it back. I kept a clerk just to copy entries off tablets people needed returned, and the clerk was overwhelmed inside a week, and I understood that I had not built a small thing.

---

The first marvels.

I have kept some of them. I'll put a few down exactly, because somebody finding this should know what the inside of an ordinary Roman head looked like when you gave it permission to dream.

A man who drove a cart for a builder wrote about a chariot pulled by trained eagles, in little harnesses. There was the problem of how you steered them, which he solved: a man sat behind the driver holding pieces of meat on long poles, one to each side, so you guided the team by where you dangled lunch. He'd done a sum, in Roman numerals, of how many eagles a loaded cart would need, and the sum was wrong, but he'd done it. At the end he'd written that the trouble was getting the eagles to come down once they were up, that he hadn't solved that part, and that maybe a wiser man could. I read that and laughed out loud and then sat with the *maybe a wiser man could* for a while, because that little sentence was the entire thing I was farming for.

A baker's wife sent in a bronze man who ground grain. Life-sized, wound up somehow, she was vague on the winding, and he turned the quern all day and never tired and never had to be fed. She'd thought hardest about the part that was hers, that the bronze man would never steal flour, never get sick, never need watching, and could grind through the night so the bread was ready at dawn. There was nothing in it about how the bronze man worked, because the how wasn't her dream. The not-having-to-own-the-grinder was the dream. I gave her tablet to Tyche, who read it twice and said nothing and gave it back, and I think about that one more than is comfortable.

There was a boy, maybe twelve, who'd had a grammarian's slave write his out in exchange for errands, and his marvel was a glass that let you see the moon as a country, with mountains, because he'd reasoned, the way a sharp kid reasons, that if it threw back light the way a hill throws back light then it had hillsides, and a thing with hillsides is a country. I had a single-lens magnifier in a drawer that would have done nothing for the moon and a head full of a telescope I couldn't build because Roman glass was full of bubbles, and this child had walked in and asked for the one marvel I knew was actually coming, someday, for somebody who wasn't either of us. I asked how he'd thought of the mountains. He looked at me like I was slow and said the moon wasn't smooth, anybody could see it wasn't smooth, what did I think the dark parts were. I told him it was a good marvel, which it was.

And there was the ship.

---

The ship's tale came in on the back of a grain-factor's discarded day-sheet, in a hand that was careful and a little cramped, the hand of somebody who'd learned letters late and was proud of them. It was signed with one name, the way slaves and the very poor sign, and the name was Felix.

Felix's marvel was a ship that needed no wind and no oars and no rowers. So far, the same dream as a dozen others, the no-rowers dream, which I'd noticed came from people who had reason to think about rowers and never from people who'd never been one. But Felix didn't wave his hand at how the ship moved. He wrote that everyone knew steam, the breath that comes off a boiling pot and lifts the lid and rattles it, anyone who'd watched a pot knew the lid wanted to fly. And if the breath off a pot could rattle a heavy lid, then a very great pot, fire under it always, its breath caught and aimed, could surely turn a wheel, and a wheel turned in the water would drive a ship, and the ship would carry its own fire and its own pot and its own sea, and go where it liked with no wind, against the wind, in a dead calm, forever, as long as there was fire.

I read it twice, then sat very still with the wharf noise coming through the door, and read it a third time, because I had shown Heras, years before, a toy I'd had a coppersmith make, Hero of Alexandria's spinning ball, the *aeolipile,* the hollow ball that whirls when you boil water under it and the steam jets out, and I'd said, *imagine this strong enough to pull a hundred carts,* and Heras had been completely unmoved, because it was a toy, it did one thing, it spun. And here was a man called Felix who'd never seen my toy, who'd watched a pot lid rattle in some kitchen and reasoned his way, on the back of a grain-sheet, to the engine I knew was the door to everything.

He'd gotten the outline of it. Not the working, the outline. And he'd hit the real problem without naming it, because he wrote, near the end, that no pot he knew could be made strong enough and large enough and not burst, that you'd need a metal nobody had, a metal that didn't yet exist.

The kid wanted the moon-glass I knew was coming. Felix wanted the steel I knew was the wall, the thing Hermes and I cracked our heads on in the forge behind the cattle market batch after ruined batch, gorgeous one firing and crystalline garbage the next. They were both standing at the same locked door I was standing at, the difference being that they thought it was a story and I knew it wasn't.

I gave Felix the prize.

---

Before that day arrived, something else arrived with it.

Through the same guild contacts I used for the prize postings, the workshop had a standing offer on a bronze cylinder spec: a component I needed for the steam program, interior surface ground to within a tolerance I could measure with the go/no-go gauges Hermes had helped me build. The spec had been open for seven months, which was about what I'd expected for a target that required patience and precision together.

A bronzesmith named Glabrio had come closest.

His cylinder arrived in a sacking cloth, set down on the counting table by a guild messenger who clearly had no idea what was inside. I ran the gauges myself. Glabrio's interior was smooth, genuinely smooth, the kind of smooth that comes from a craftsman who knows what he's doing and spent real time on it. But the final pass had taken off a hair's breadth too much. The bore was fractionally oversize, enough that the valve fitting would have to compensate, enough that under pressure the seal would eventually weep. The gauge told me so without argument.

I set the cylinder down and looked at the records Marcia kept of the prize attempts. Glabrio had submitted twice before on different specs, getting close each time. The same pattern: excellent work, the finest adjustments done last, and the final pass a fraction too eager. A man who had spent his career working bronze by eye and feel, whose instincts pushed him to fit the surface just a little more, just one more stroke of the file, because that was how you made a thing right when you couldn't measure it and had to trust your hands. His hands were trained for a tolerance they couldn't see.

I wrote his name in the prize ledger under the column for near-misses and entered nothing under the winner's column, because there was no winner. The spec stayed open. The column stayed blank.

Glabrio had not won. He did not know how close he had come, or perhaps he did, and that was its own difficulty to carry.

---

It caused exactly the trouble I should have expected and somehow hadn't.

Felix turned out to be a slave, the property of a man who imported African grain and kept a counting-house of literate slaves, where Felix had learned his careful late letters doing his master's sums. So the prize money, by every law in Rome, belonged to a grain importer who had not written a word about a ship and did not, when I went to see him, have the first idea what a steam-driven hull might be.

I'd built a prize to bend down to the man at the bottom and found the law would not let the man at the bottom pick it up.

I am not going to pretend I solved this cleanly. I bought the result with maneuvering and Macer's name and some of the silver going where I'd rather it hadn't. The grain importer was happy to be flattered as the master of a slave clever enough to win the Macer prize, and happy to take his owner's cut. In the end Felix got to keep, with his master's gracious permission, a portion toward his own *peculium,* toward, someday, if the master stayed gracious, buying himself. I'd turned a hundred denarii for the best marvel into a down payment on a man's own freedom, filtered through a master's vanity and a master's cut, and it was a win, and it was also the bronze grinder run backward, a man who'd dreamed the way out of being owned and got, for it, a faster road to buying the rest of himself from the man who owned him.

I read his tale out at Macer's, on the Quinquatrus, with Macer at the front looking like a man who sells the army its boots and also funds poetry, the room fuller than the salon had ever been. People laughed in the good places, and went quiet at the metal that didn't yet exist, and a man near the back said, not unkindly, "Well, and when the gods give us that metal," and got a laugh, and I thought, *a wiser man could,* and that Felix was at that moment in a counting-house adding up another man's grain, and I said none of it.

---

Crispus came.

Of course he came, oiled and elegant and with his scroll open on the table beside him, three fingers of Falernian and a face arranged into something close to approval. He had learned the last time that fraud and geography and the graver word were a terrain I could fight on, so he did not go back to it. He had something better. He had come as a supporter.

He waited until the reading was done and the room had settled into its talk, and he called for the room's attention in the way he'd always been able to do, a kind of gravitational pull, and he praised it.

Lavishly. The eagles were delightful, genuinely, and the bronze grinder was a lovely piece of Roman ingenuity, and the boy's moon, well, one was reminded of the best tradition of the old philosophical imagination. And then Felix's ship, which had moved him, he said, more than he expected, and he said this with a warmth so complete that the room started to relax, and I felt the thing coming.

What a pity, he said, settling into it, that the prize had gone to a slave's tale. He said it gently, genuinely, the friend of literature speaking carefully. He was not, he wanted to be clear, casting any aspersion on the tale itself, which was charming in its way. But he wondered aloud, with great courtesy toward our host, whether Rome's first annual prize for literary excellence ought to have set its crown on a counting-house slave's fumbling at the craft, rather than on the work of men trained in the tradition. There were forms, he said. There was what the Greeks had built and what Rome had made of it, and that inheritance, on the festival of Minerva herself, deserved custodians, not, he paused to find the word, not well-meaning accidents. He gestured toward me: he had every admiration for the Thulean's enthusiasm. The spirit was exactly right. He only asked whether the letter might be strengthened, whether the contest, if it was to last and to do credit to Titus Flavius Macer's name and to the great tradition it meant to serve, might benefit from judges who understood what that tradition required. His own circle, he said, would be glad to help.

He sat down to a scattered, uncertain murmur, and I saw what he'd done. He had not attacked the contest. He had volunteered to own it.

He was offering to take from me, with exquisite good manners and in front of witnesses, the one thing he could not have taken by argument, which was the thing I'd spent two years building. The prize would become a salon prize, the dream would narrow down to the dream a man needed Cicero to write for him, and in ten years there would be no baker's wife, no moon-boy, no slave with his careful cramped hand and his pot that needed a better metal. There would be Crispus's students, Crispus's version of marvel, and Crispus's version of the goddess.

I stood up.

I thanked him, and I meant the thanks, because it was the clearest thing he'd done to me in four years, and clear enemies are easier than the other kind. I said that I'd heard his concern, and that it was a serious concern, one that any man who loved the tradition should feel. And then I said that I'd had occasion to study that tradition a little, and that when I went to Homer, which I had done, in the Greek, I found that the wanderer Odysseus built his own bed, that Hephaestus made himself women of gold to do the work of apprentices, and that Daedalus put wax wings on a boy who flew. The goddess of craft, in the oldest stories we had from the oldest men we had, had always made her home in the workshop of the man who tried the thing that had never been tried. The eagle-cart driver and the baker's wife and Felix with his pot and his unobtainable metal were working exactly in her house. If that was too low a house for the tradition, I would not presume to renovate the goddess's dwelling on behalf of a tradition that had put her there first.

The room laughed, harder than his had been, and Macer laughed his ugly bark from the front, and Crispus smiled the smile that did not move above the lips and said something gracious about the Thulean's command of the literature, and we were done.

But walking home I knew what that smile meant. He had come to get the prize and had not gotten it. He would be back, not with the same offer, with something else. Men like Crispus have very long patience and they never waste the same approach twice.

---

Then we printed them.

A prize read out once at a rich man's dinner and then forgotten is a dinner, not an institution. The dream had to spread and outlast me.

So Eros, my letterer, carved the winners onto pearwood. Not all of them, the press was crude and gray and we wasted a sheet in four. But the ship and the eagles and the bronze grinder and the boy's moon, inked and pressed fifty crooked impressions at a time, sold for an as or two to schoolmasters and whoever wanted them, because a marvel that won the Macer prize was a thing people wanted to own, to read to a class of boys, to pin up in a tavern. The press that had started its life making times-tables for a Subura schoolmaster now made marvels, and they went out on cheap gray sheets, and got copied by hand by people who couldn't buy even the cheap sheet, and started turning up, Tyche told me, in the mouths of children playing at the wharf, who'd decided among themselves that the bronze grinder was real and lived somewhere and could be visited.

A generation playing at machines that didn't exist. That was the crop. I knew when I planted it that the field was a hundred years long and I'd be in the ground for most of it, and I planted it anyway, on a borrowed name, on Minerva's festival, with a slave's tale of a ship in the front row. There are worse ways to spend stolen gold's leftover good weather.

---

Felix sent word, near the end of that first year, through the grain importer's steward. He'd been allowed paper for his next, and he wanted me to know he was already working on it, that with proper paper and time, he thought he could fix the pot. The metal that didn't yet exist. He thought he could fix it.

I sat in the tally-room with the steward's tablet in my hand, the wharf going on outside, Tyche at the other table running the day's figures in the new marks, Pamphilus down on the dock bellowing at a crane crew with the collar long gone off his neck, and I read the slave's message that he thought he could fix the pot, the one thing in all of Rome I knew for certain could not be fixed, not by him, not by me, not by Hermes and every ruined batch behind the cattle market.

I took up the stylus and wrote back, in my plain accented Latin, that I would very much like to read it, that he should take all the time he needed with the pot, and that the metal would come. Then I smoothed the wax flat with my thumb where I'd pressed too hard, and gave it back, and went out onto the dock to see what Pamphilus was shouting about.
