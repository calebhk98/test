# Chapter 42: Heras

He sent for me the way he sent for the wine merchant, by a boy with a list.

Marcia had the list from the boy and read it to me at the storeroom door, flat, the way she read a manifest, because she had known Heras almost as long as I had and would not have wept in front of his messenger to save her life. He wanted, the boy said, the account of what was owed on the press-society's physic chest, which he had stocked for years out of his own purse and meant now to be paid back into the foundation so it did not lapse when he did. He wanted to know whether Tyche had the right figure for it. He wanted me to come up the Caelian in the morning and not before, because he slept badly and the morning was the only part of the day his head was his own. And he wanted, the boy added, plainly reciting, for me not to bring a physician, on the grounds that he was one.

So I knew the arrangement of it before I went, which was a mercy he had made on purpose, being a man who had spent fifty years deciding how to tell people things they did not want to hear.

---

He was eighty that year, or near it; he had never been sure of his own birth and had made a joke of it that I had heard a hundred times, that a slave is born on the day he is bought and he had been bought twice, so he was either old or very old and would let me choose. He was in the small room off the colonnade where he read, propped against the wall on cushions Marcia had sent up over the years and he had never thrown out, and the lamp was unlit because it was full morning and the light came in clean off the white of the wall opposite. His hands were folded on the blanket. They were the same hands. That was what caught me at the door and that I did not let catch me, the cleanest hands I had ever seen in this world or the other one, gone to spotted paper over the bones, folded on a blanket, and still, somehow, a doctor's, the way a tool keeps the weight of the work after the hand that held it is gone.

"You came up the steps too fast," he said, before I had said anything. "I can hear you breathing. You are fifty-two and you came up an old man's stairs like a boy late for a lesson. Sit down before you make more work for whoever has me next."

I sat down. Marcia had sent up bread and the soft white cheese he could still eat and a jug of the boiled water, our water, the wharf water through sand and charcoal and the long boil, which he had drunk for thirty years while telling me the boiling was a Greek discipline and the creatures were a barbarian's nightmare. He did not touch the cheese. He had me pour him a cup of the water and he held it and did not drink that either, just held it, for the warmth of the cup I think, his hands being cold.

"The chest," he said. "Tell me Tyche has the figure."

I told him she had the figure.

"To the as?"

"To the as."

"Good." He shut his eyes a moment, satisfied the way he was satisfied by a clean prognosis, a calculation known and squared. "Then that is done, and you can stop pretending you came up the hill for an account. Eat the cheese, it will only go to the slaves and they have their own."

I ate the cheese so as to have something to do with my hands.

---

I had sat by a great many dying men by then. I had knelt in churned dirt on a frontier holding a fistful of burning cloth, and I had sat five nights in a supply tent with a cup of water and a wound I had cleaned and packed and dressed perfectly and watched the perfect dressing fail, and I had stood in a yard and taken a plumb-line from a man who would not bend and would be dead in exile by the next season because of it. I knew what it was to sit by a death I had built, or failed to stop, or let happen by holding my tongue. I knew the particular taste of that, keeping the arithmetic running because your hands will not be still, adding the saved against the one lost and getting an answer that slams the scale and means nothing.

This was not that. There was no arithmetic in it. He was eighty and his heart was tired and his lungs filled a little at night, and there was nothing I had built that had reached him and nothing I had failed to build that could have turned it, because no one has ever built what turns it, not in his world and not in mine, where it is only pushed a few years down a hill and arrives anyway. I sat there with my hands full of nothing to fix. I had spent thirty-five years being a man with a tool for everything, and I sat by the one death where the only correct response was to be a man in a chair, and I had forgotten how, or had never known.

"You are looking at me," he said, eyes still shut, "the way you look at a machine that has stopped and you do not yet know why."

"No."

"Yes. I have watched you look at things for thirty-five years. You are doing the face." He opened his eyes. They were the same too, gone a little milky at the rims now, the lens stiffening the way he had told me mine was stiffening, both of us reading at arm's length down the same road, him further along it. "There is nothing in your kit for this, Daniel. I am telling you so you can put the kit down. It is the one complaint I have never been able to do anything for in fifty years either, and I have had longer to make my peace with the failure than you have, so let me have the dignity of it and do not sit there inventing a filter for old age."

I told him I was not inventing anything.

"You are inventing it behind your eyes. I can see the wheel going round." He shifted on the cushions, a small careful negotiation with his own ribs. "Pour yourself the water. You are going to want something in your hand for this and it had better not be wine, you have a hill to go back down."

---

He talked, that morning and the two mornings after, in the way he had always talked, which was around a subject and then suddenly through the middle of it. He did not talk about dying except as a clinical matter, the way he might have discussed a patient who happened to be in the room, and once he caught himself doing exactly that, narrating the slowing of his own pulse to me with two fingers on his own wrist, and stopped, and said it was a hard habit to break, watching, even when the body on the couch was the one you had been issued.

He wanted the medicine settled. That was the work of those mornings, under the talk. He had carried it for thirty years, the boiling and the washing and the clean cloth, all of it framed for Rome as Greek discipline and Hippocratic rigor and never once as creatures, because he had told me at the start that the creatures were witch-talk that would get me killed and had been proven right by the fact that I was not dead. He had taught it to a string of younger physicians and freedman surgeons and one army medicus who had taken it east, and he was clear-eyed to the point of cruelty about what would happen to it now.

"They will keep the parts that work and lose the reason," he said. "They have already begun. There is a man on the Esquiline, a good cutter, hands like a butcher and a butcher's luck which is better than skill, and he boils his instruments and his water and washes his hands raw and tells his patients it is to please Asclepius, that the god loves a clean altar. He does not believe your creatures. He saw that the men he cut lived more often when he did the cleaning, and he kept the cleaning and threw away everything else, and he has put a god under it because a god is what his patients understand." He turned the cup. "That is what becomes of a true thing in the world. It goes loose. It survives by forgetting why it is true. In a hundred years there will be men boiling water and washing wounds across half the empire and not one of them will know it was a barbarian's nightmare about little animals, and they will be right to do it and wrong about the reason, and the men they save will not care which."

I said it was better than nothing.

"It is much better than nothing. It is most of everything." He looked at me. "It is more than the flying thing ever did, and more than the bursting tube, and you should think about that on your way down the hill. The device of yours that will save the most lives is the dullest one you ever made and the one no one will ever put your name on, and I have been telling you that for thirty years and you have been not listening for thirty years, and I would like, before I am too tired to enjoy it, to be told that I was right."

"You were right."

"Say it as though it costs you something."

"You were right, you Greek bastard, the water was the whole thing, the rest was noise." It came out rough and he laughed at it, a short sharp sound, high in the chest and open, shorter than it used to be, and it cost him and he did not care that it cost him.

---

The doctoring itself, the actual sitting-by and the hand on the wrist and the knowing what a body is going to do before it does it, that he could not hand to anyone and he knew it, and the losing of that was the only part of those mornings that I think genuinely grieved him. He had no quarrel with the dying itself. What grieved him was the eye going out of the world. He had a freedman of his own, a quiet Cappadocian named Zoticus who had been his man and his apprentice for fifteen years and was sound and careful and would carry on the practice. But Zoticus was a craftsman and Heras had been something else, a mind that had read a man's death in his color across a courtyard and had also read mine across a wax tablet in a cell when I was a frightened boy doing long division to prove I was real, and that, the seeing, did not pass down a line the way the boiling did. It went out with the man. He knew it. He had watched it go out of better physicians than himself, the teacher dying and leaving the method and taking the eye, and he had made his peace with being on the near side of it and now he was on the far.

"Zoticus will keep them alive," he said. "He will keep the chest stocked and the figures square and your foundation will pay him and he will be a good physician for forty years." He set the cup down on the table beside him, precisely, the way he set down something he was finished with. "And there will be no one left in Rome who can look at you across a room and tell you that you are guessing."

I did not say anything.

"You know what I am talking about."

"I know what you are talking about."

"I am not being modest. I am the last one. Macer could tell you that a practice would not make money, but Macer believes you about everything else because the flying convinced him forty years ago and an old man does not revise. The wife sees you clearer than I do, and she will tell you when you are wrong about a contract or a man, and she will not tell you when you are wrong about the world, because she does not argue the world, she takes it as it is and works it, that is her genius and it is not mine." He looked at the wall, at the clean morning light on it. "I argued the world. I argued you. I have spent thirty-five years telling you that you cannot see the small creatures and you have no proof of them and your map is a guess and your ladder is a prayer with a workman's apron on, and you went on building, and a good half of what you built was sound and the other half I could never be sure of and neither could you, and the only reason the unsound half did not run away with you entirely is that there was a man in the room who would say, out loud, in front of you, that you were guessing." He turned his head back to me. His eyes moved over my face the way they moved over a page, looking for the word that did not sit right. "When I am gone - " he started, and stopped, and tried again from a different angle. "There is a sentence in your head right now, the one you have been turning over since you sat down. I know the sentence. I have heard it twenty times. And what I cannot - " He stopped again. He looked at his hands on the blanket. "A mark on a page cannot ask you a question," he said, at last. "That is all. A mark on a page cannot ask."

It was Tyche's phrase, or she had made it into a mark on a page, but he had handed it to me at the start before either of us had a word for it. The hooked stroke. The device I had built into the books precisely so that a reader four hundred years on would know which rungs were oak and which were oak drawn in the air. I had thought, when we made that mark, that it was a clever solution to the problem of being honest in a book. Sitting in that white room I understood that it was a clay copy of a living man, that I had spent my whole life being kept honest by a Greek who would not be impressed, and that I had tried to fold the Greek into a stroke of cipher because some animal part of me had known he was running out, and that a mark on a page cannot ask you a question, and cannot stop you, and cannot laugh in your face when you deserve it, which is the only correction that ever actually worked on me.

"You will have to do it yourself," he said. "Be your own skeptic. You will be bad at it. Everyone is bad at it. That is why we keep each other." He shut his eyes. "I am tired now. Come tomorrow. Bring the figure on the chest in your own hand and not Tyche's, I want to see whether your sevens have stopped looking like ones."

---

He died on the third morning, before I came up the hill, so I was not in the room, which I have decided to be glad of and have mostly succeeded.

Zoticus told me how it went, plainly, because Heras had trained him to tell a death plainly. He had drunk a little of the boiled water in the night and slept and his breathing had gone shallow and long-spaced toward dawn and then between one space and the next had simply not taken the next, and Zoticus, who had been sitting with him, had waited the way you wait, counting, and the count had run out. There had been no word. Heras had said his last clever thing the evening before, to Zoticus, who reported it to me with a straight face because the man had no idea it was funny. Heras had asked for the lamp moved closer and Zoticus had moved it and Heras had looked at the Hippocrates he could no longer read at any distance and said that it was a great waste, to spend a life learning to see and then have the eyes go before the man, and that if there were gods they had a low sense of humor and if there were none then it was only physics and physics had no sense of humor at all, which was worse, and that on the whole he preferred the gods, as company, even not believing in them, and would Zoticus please blow out the lamp, there was nothing in the book he had not read.

That was the last of it. No god invoked, except as a joke at a god's expense. I would not have had it otherwise and he knew I would not, which is why, I think, he made sure there was a witness to write it down for me.

---

Marcia handled the rites. There was a guild of physicians he had paid into for fifty years and they did it correctly, the Greek and the Roman both, and the pyre was on the appointed ground outside the gate and I stood where a long-standing friend stands, which is close but not closest, because the guild had the closest places, and that was correct too. There is a rule in this place that I learned from Heras as much as from anyone, which is that you do not say the loud true thing out loud, you do the rite and let the smoke carry it and keep the loud truth in a box under a board, and he would have had no patience at all for a barbarian narrating his own grief at a graveside like a hired mourner with a contract.

What I will write is the part that did not happen at the pyre and that I did not see coming.

It was three or four days after. I was at the wharf, at the bench, in the slack hour, and Eros had trimmed the lamps and the press was under its cloth, and I had a page of the upper book in front of me, the chemistry I had left half-dark, and I had written a sentence about the joining of the small parts of things that was more confident than I had any right to be, and I read it back, and I reached for the cup of boiled water at my elbow to wet my throat, and the cup was there, the wharf water, sand and charcoal and the long boil, and I drank it.

And there was a thing I always did, when I drank that water at that bench in the slack hour, which was that there was no one at the other end of the bench to say it to, but I had spent thirty years drinking that water within reach of a man who would, on a good evening, say something cutting about the Greekness of the discipline, and I had drunk it ten thousand times without thinking and would have said, without thinking, some old worn answer, the way you say a thing to a man you have sat beside for half your life. I drank the water and the answer came up in me, shaped and ready, the worn old retort about whose discipline it really was, and there was the whole length of the empty bench, and the answer had nowhere to go, and it sat in my mouth, and I did not say it, because you do not say a thing aloud in an empty press-room like a man who has lost his wits.

So I swallowed it with the water. And then I picked up the stylus, and I went back to the too-confident sentence about the joining of small things, and I read it again, and I made myself ask, out loud, in the empty room, in Greek, because Greek was the language we had argued in, whether I knew that or only felt it. My voice sounded wrong in the empty room, an old man talking to himself in a dead language. I felt it. I marked the sentence with the hooked stroke that meant the Thulean is guessing here, and I read the next sentence, and I asked the room again, and the room did not answer, and I marked that one too, and I went on down the page asking a question into a silence that used to answer back, and marking my own guesses, badly, the way everyone is bad at it, because that is why we keep each other, and there was no one to keep, and the water in the cup went cold while I worked, and I drank it cold rather than get up for more.
