# Chapter 9: Macer

They walked me across the city with the bag still folded under my arm.

The two broad men kept exactly one pace behind me and a half-pace out to either side, so I walked at the point of a little triangle that was pretending not to be a little triangle. Heras walked at my shoulder and said nothing, which from Heras was a whole speech. The steward went ahead, and I followed the back of his neck up out of the poor lanes and along a street that climbed, where the fronts got quieter and the doors got heavier and the gutters stopped stinking quite so personally, and somewhere in there the festival noise fell off behind us.

The door was oak, studded, set back under a little roof. A slave opened it before the steward touched it, which meant a slave had been standing behind it with his ear to the wood, waiting all morning for the sound of us. In this house people stood behind doors so that other people would never have to wait at them.

Inside it was cool and dim and smelled of beeswax and lamp soot and, under that, leather. A lot of leather. The smell of the place a saddle comes from. A floor of small black and white stones laid in a pattern, a square of hard noon light falling from an opening in the roof into a shallow basin of water in the middle of the room, and then the steward stopped and half turned and put his eyes on me with a meaning I could read in any language. Stand here. Do not speak first.

The room was full of business. I had pictured, on the walk, some kind of throne thing, a man waiting to look at me. There was no waiting. Along one side two clerks sat on stools with tablets on their knees, styluses going, a man in a traveling cloak reciting numbers that another man wrote down, a slave crossing the bright square of floor with a tray and crossing back without it, and all of it moving around one chair the way water moves around a stone.

The man in the chair was not looking at me. He was looking at the tablet the slave had brought him, holding it out at arm's length, squinting a little, the posture of a man whose eyes have started to go and will die before he admits it. He was big through the shoulders and going soft over it, gray cropped close, a face that had been out in weather for a lot of years and had the broken veins to show it, a jaw like the end of a beam. He wore a plain tunic, good wool, no stripe on it, because an equestrian wears a narrow stripe and this one could not be bothered, or wanted you to notice that he could not be bothered. A ring on his hand, gold, heavy, and a smear of dried something on the side of his thumb that I think was ink and might have been blood from a butchered animal, and either way he had not washed it off.

He read the tablet. He did not hurry. He let me stand in the middle of his floor with my burned hands and my ash and my folded ruined balloon while the clerks scratched, and I understood that this was the first thing he was going to teach me, before a word: that his time was worth money and mine was worth whatever he decided to pay for it, and that the gap between those two facts was the entire room.

Then he handed the tablet back without looking at the slave who took it, and he looked at me.

A flat look. Professional. The look the steward had given the bag in the lot, only older at it, and wider, taking in the whole sum at once. He looked at the cloth under my arm. He looked at my boots, the last unmistakable thing on me, machine-stitched, soled in a black stuff no cobbler in the world could make, and his eyes stopped on them - they always stopped on them. Then he looked at my face. Then he said something to the steward, sideways, not bothering to lower his voice because I was furniture, and the only word I caught clean was a number, and it was a big one.

The steward answered low. The man in the chair grunted.

"You," he said, and now it was to me, and his Latin came slow and loud and a little too clearly, with very careful spacing, for a child or a dog or a foreigner he suspected of being stupid. "You are the one who burned down a quarter of the Subura this morning."

I knew enough to know what he was doing and not enough to do anything clever back. "No one burned," I said. My Latin came out badly, as it always did under pressure, the cases sliding, the v turning to w in my mouth before I could stop it. "One tunic. One roof. No man."

"One roof." He turned a hand over. The ring caught the light. "Do you know what a roof costs, in the Subura, when a foreigner sets fire to it in front of four hundred witnesses and a priest?"

"No."

"No." He seemed to like that. Something moved at the corner of his mouth, a man tasting a sum he was going to enjoy. "Neither do they. That is the beauty of it. By tomorrow the roof will have been a whole house, and the day after that it will have been three, and a child in it, and the foreigner will have called the fire down with a word. You have made me famous before noon. I have had two men at my door already this morning wanting to know what I keep in my workshop, and one of them was a senator's freedman, which is worse than a senator." He shifted his weight in the chair. The wood complained. "I bought you for less than I pay for a season's mules. Did you know that?"

"No," I said again, because it was true.

He looked down, adjusted the wax tablet in his hand, set it aside on the small table next to his chair. When he reached for it he picked up instead a wooden disc, heavy, the kind a craftsman uses to check a wheel's roundness - it had just been sitting there, part of the table's clutter - and he turned it in his hands, slowly, a man working through numbers. He was not done.

"A scholar from the edge of the world," he said, turning the disc slowly, not looking at me. "Draws maps. Knows the new figures. Heras here told my steward there was a madman in a country jail who could divide numbers in his head and had drawn the whole world on a tablet, and that for the price of a mule I could find out whether he was a marvel or a lunatic, and either way it would be cheap." He glanced at Heras, and there was a whole long history in the glance, money owed in both directions. "He did not mention you would cost me a roof."

Heras spoke for the first time, dry, careful, in the register of a man who has done this before. "He did not mention it because it had not happened. I sold you the sum. The sum was: a strange foreigner, cheap, possibly worth nothing, possibly worth a great deal, and a year to find out which. The year is up." He let that sit, and I thought: they all do this, the whole city talks in pauses. "You found out which this morning. In front of four hundred witnesses and a priest."

The clerks had stopped scratching. The man with the numbers had gone quiet. I understood, slowly, with the back of my neck, that the room had been arranged so that the room could hear this.

The man in the chair set the wooden disc back on the table. He looked at me for a while.

"Sit him down," he said, and a stool appeared under me, pushed by a girl I had not seen come in, and I sat because my knees were glad of it. "Not you," he said to Heras. "You I trust about as far as I can throw a Greek, which is exactly as far as you have been useful and no further, and you have been useful, so stand there and be useful." Heras inclined his head a fraction, a man accepting a compliment that had a knife folded inside it.

The steward stepped forward then, just a half-step, and said, in a voice intended to be formal: "Titus Flavius Macer. Equestrian. Principal contractor, leather and timber, to the Second and Fourth legions in Dacia, among others." He glanced at me to make sure I was attending. I was attending. "You are in his house."

Macer watched me take the name in. His expression said nothing about that at all.

"Here is where we are, Thulean." The first time he used it. He liked it; I could hear that he liked it, a word he could say with his whole mean mouth. "This morning you stopped being a thing I forgot I owned. That is bad for you. When you were a madman who drew maps, you cost me a mule and nobody knew your name, and that was the safest you will ever be in your life, and it is over. Now you are a thing that throws fire at the sky in front of a crowd and a priest reads it. A senator's man was at my door." He let me feel the weight of that, watching to see if I knew enough to be afraid, and I knew exactly enough. "Do you know what happens to a cheap oddity that a senator wants and an equestrian owns?"

I did not.

"Nothing good," he said, "for the oddity." And he gave one short bark of genuine amusement, aimed at me, and it was the first thing in the room that had felt like a real thing a person did rather than a move on a board, and I hated that some animal part of me, after a year of being a sum, was glad to be laughed at like a person.

Then he stopped and got to the point, and the point was a knife dressed as a gift.

"So." He leaned forward, forearms on his knees, and the chair eased. "You are going to stop costing me roofs and start making me money, and I am going to keep you alive while you do it, which this morning became a thing that requires keeping. You follow?"

"I follow some," I said.

"Good. Then follow this."

He picked up the disc again, turning it in one hand, and held up the other - the ring hand - and began folding down fingers, one at a time, the way a contractor counts a tally, his eyes moving between me and a water-stain on the far wall that he seemed to find useful for thinking.

"You want cloth. You will have cloth, by the bolt, the close-woven, as much as you ruin, and you ruin a great deal, Heras tells me, you are a careless animal with fire." The first finger went down, and he tilted the disc in his other hand, inspecting its edge, running his thumbnail along it. "You want hands. Two slaves to start, mine, to fetch and stitch and not to run, and if you teach them anything that costs me money I will sell the cleverer of them, so teach them what you like." A second finger, and he said it the same flat way he had said the cloth and the hands, a man pricing labor. The floor of the room tilted very slightly under me and I kept my face still because Heras was watching me keep my face still.

Macer glanced up, looked at me directly. "Still with me."

"Yes," I said.

He went back to the wall. "You want a place. There is a yard I hold off the timber wharf, a real yard, with a wall and a gate I can lock and a man on the gate, where you can burn what you like and the burning is mine and the wall is between you and the next senator's freedman." Third finger. He set the disc flat on his knee for a moment, then picked it up again. "You want metal, wood, a forge, you put it on a tablet and the tablet comes to him" - the steward - "and he decides if you are lying about the price, and you will be, everyone lies about the price, I built a fortune on it." Fourth finger.

He stopped there and turned the disc over, looked at the other face, tapped its center once with a knuckle.

"And you want money." He set the disc down on the arm of his chair and looked at me. "A man works better with a little of his own in a jar where he can see it. I am not a fool and I am not generous, those are the same sentence. There will be a fund. A peculium." He said the word slowly so I would have it, watching me get it. "It is not yours. Understand that before you love it. It is a thing I let you use as if it were yours, and a clever master lets a clever slave keep a little jar because a man guarding his own little jar guards the master's whole house and never notices he is doing it. I am telling you this to your face." He leaned back. The disc slid off the chair arm and he caught it without looking, one-handed, and set it on the table. "You will take it anyway, because the other thing on the table is a senator and a priest and a crowd that this morning said the word witch out loud, and you have, Thulean, no other door in this whole city but mine."

It was the longest thing he had said and the truest, and he had said it plainly, measuring out the hole's dimensions so I would stop thrashing.

I sat there with the bag in my lap and did the only thing I had, which was think.

A year of being a sum had taught me one thing about a sum, which is that it has two sides. He had spent the whole morning telling me what I cost him, the mule, the roof, the senator, the careful awful list, and the hardware-store part of me that knew what a thing was worth by who was angry about it had started keeping its own tally on the other side of the line. Men at his door before noon. A senator's freedman. He had not had to walk me here under guard like a parcel for something he could throw away. You do not put a wall around a mule.

So I played the one card. I tried to play it small.

"The wall," I said. My Latin came slow and I let it; slow made me sound careful instead of cornered. "The wall is good. The hands are good." I looked at the folded gray cloth in my lap, because looking at a man while you do this is a mistake even I knew. "The thing this morning. It went up. You saw. Everyone saw. It came down because I cannot keep the heat in it. I know why. I have known for a year." I made myself stop there, on the edge, in a pause I had learned from them. "No one else in Rome knows why it came down. No one else in Rome can make it go up at all."

The room was very quiet.

Macer looked at me, and his eyes had changed. Sharper. A man who has just heard the mule talk and is recalculating the mule.

"There it is," he said softly, almost to himself, and then to the steward, in the sideways money-voice, not bothering that I could hear: "He knows what he is. That is the dangerous kind." Back to me, louder, and the bluntness came back into his face but there was something working under it now, a kind of pleasure, the pleasure of a man who has bought a horse cheap and watched it clear a wall. "So that is your little knife. The only one who knows how. You think I have not bought a hundred men who were the only ones who knew how? The only one who knows how dies of a fever and takes the how with him, Thulean, that is what the only one who knows how does." He let that land, and it landed, cold, in my still-blistered hands. "Which is exactly why I am going to give you the hands. So that one fever does not cost me my balloon."

He used the word like he owned it, which he did. It went through me anyway, balloon, the first time anyone but me had named the thing, in his mean mouth, as a possession.

"You will teach the hands," he said. "Some of it. Enough that a fever does not ruin me, not so much that I do not need you, and you will guess wrong about which is which and lose either way, and that, boy, is the actual deal, the one under the cloth and the wall and the little jar. You keep me needing you. I keep you alive. The day you teach too little, your balloon dies with you and I have lost a roof for nothing, and I do not forgive a roof." He sat back. The chair took him. "I find I like you. Do not let that comfort you. I have liked mules."

I did not understand all the words. I understood the logic, and the logic was a cage so well made that it looked from the inside like a workshop.

"Free," I said. I should not have. It came out of the part of me that was still eighteen and stupid and a year homesick. "If it makes you money. If I teach the hands and the balloon flies and I make you money. Then. Free."

The room did something. The steward's stylus stopped. Heras, beside me, went very still in a way I felt more than saw, a man watching another man step toward an edge.

Macer did not get angry. That was the worst of it. An angry man you can read. He looked at me with something almost like fondness, the fondness you feel for a thing that has done exactly what you expected it to do, on cue, like a dog that begs.

"Free," he said, and tasted it, and let it go. "Listen to him. One morning a marvel and already he wants the cap." He looked at Heras. "You taught him the word?" Heras said nothing, which was an answer. Macer turned the heavy ring on his finger with his thumb, the only restless thing about him. "Here is a true thing, Thulean. It is not mine to give. Freedom that is worth anything in this city is a citizen's freedom, and a citizen is made by men a great deal higher than me, men who were at this morning's festival in better seats than yours, men who own me the way I own you, near enough." The thumb went around the ring. "I can keep you. I can feed you and wall you and put a man on your gate and let you keep your little jar. I cannot make you a Roman. No more than you can fly that thing higher than a roof." He looked at me. "We are both reaching past our hands this morning, you and I. The difference is I know exactly how far mine reach. Learn that and you might live a while."

He looked at me a moment longer to be sure it had gone in. It had.

"Now get him out of my floor," he said to the steward, already reaching for the next tablet, the room closing over the place where I had been, filling the gap the same as it had filled the gap where the priest had stood that morning. "And feed him. He looks like a thing I bought cheap."

That was the meeting.

---

The girl led me out.

She had been at the edge of the room the whole time, the one who had pushed the stool under me, and I had not looked at her because you learn fast in that house not to look at the furniture, which was the lesson and I had already half learned it and that was the part that made my stomach turn. She was small, fourteen maybe, in a plain undyed tunic, her hair cut off short and uneven the way you cut a slave girl's hair so it is one less thing to keep, and she moved the way the slave behind the door had moved, quiet, ready, present before you needed her and gone before you could thank her, except that her eyes were not quiet at all. She had watched the whole thing. She had stood there fetching stools and taken in every move on the board, the mule, the roof, the wall, the little jar, free, and filed all of it behind a face that gave nothing back, and when she caught me seeing her see it, she dropped her eyes the way you drop something hot - fast, automatic, a year of practice in it.

"Tyche," the steward said, the way you name a tool. "Yours. Fetch and carry. She does not run." He said it to me, but he was looking at her when he said the last part, and she was looking at the floor, and I understood the sentence was a leash with two ends and I was holding one of them now whether I had asked for it or not.

She brought me water in the corridor, in a plain cup, and bread, because the master had said feed him and in this house when the master said a thing it became true at once in the body of a girl. My hands were shaking, I noticed, now that the room was behind me, and she watched them shake the way she had watched everything, and said nothing, two pieces of this man's property standing in a cool corridor that smelled of leather, and I drank, watered wine, faintly sour, the best thing I had tasted in a year, and I hated that too.

"Thank you," I said. In Latin. It came out wrong, the case wrong, the way it always did.

She looked at me then, full on, one quick measuring look, the same look the master had given me and the steward had given the bag and the priest had given the smoldering cloth, the look this whole city gave a thing to find out what it was worth, except she was fourteen and a slave and had no money to back it with, so it was just the looking, naked, the only thing she owned. Whatever she decided about me she kept. She took the empty cup back out of my shaking hands before I dropped it, turned it once to check the rim, and tucked it against her side.

"The yard is by the wharf," she said. Her Latin was quick and low and clean, a city child's, and she did not slow it down for me the way the master had, which I took, stupidly, as the only respect I had been paid all day. "Long walk. You should eat the bread while you walk. He won't give you time to eat it there."

Then she went ahead of me toward the studded oak door with a slave behind it, carrying the cup that was not hers in a house that was not hers, and I followed her out into the noon light with the bread in one hand and my burned balloon folded under the other arm, into the loud bright city.
