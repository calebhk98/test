# Chapter 43: Family

There is something that happens in a house in winter, in the dark before the lamps are trimmed, when the meal is on and the rain is on the tiles and everyone is somewhere in it making noise. I have no Latin word for it and no English one either, which tells you something about how much I had thought about it before this world handed it to me at the age of fifty-three.

Our house was the upper floor of the wharf building Caepio had left Marcia, which she had refused to leave for anything grander on the grounds that the warehouse was below her feet and a woman should sleep over her money. So the smell came up through the boards, pepper and rope-tar and the dry resinous smell of the hides, and over it the kitchen, and in the cold months the brazier and the wet-wool reek of everyone's cloaks steaming on the pegs by the stair. It was loud. There was no hour of the day when it was not loud. After thirty years I had stopped hearing most of it, and then some evening it would come back to me whole, and I would stand on the stair with my hand on the rail unable to account for the fact that any of it was mine.

---

We ate together more than was strictly Roman. Marcia had decided this early, by which I mean she had decided it and informed me of it. A proper house dined apart, the master reclining, the women and children fed earlier, the slaves after; we did not have the room and Marcia did not have the patience, and so we ate at a long scrubbed table that had come up from the warehouse and still had a chandler's tally cut into one end, all of us on benches, like a crew.

That winter the table was Marcia at the head, because it was her table and her warehouse and there was never any question; me at the foot, where I could get up to the lamps; Lucanus on one side when he came, which was less and less; Ulpia on the other, with her wax tablet beside her plate, which Marcia permitted at the table and nowhere else, having lost that particular war and not the others. Tyche came up most evenings, being family in everything but the law. The girl Chloe brought the food and then ate at the corner once Marcia had told her twice to sit down, a Bithynian raised in a strict house being unpersuadable in fewer than two tellings that she was allowed to.

Marcia said the blessing, such as it was. There was a household Lar in a painted niche by the door, a cheerful crude little figure with a drinking-horn, and she gave it its due in about the time it takes to clear your throat, because the gods, in Marcia's theology, were a creditor you paid promptly and did not flatter. Then she said what was in front of us and what it had cost, by habit, because she could not look at a bowl of lentils without pricing the lentils.

"Lentils," she said, that evening. "And the last of the salt pork, which Chloe will not get into her head is the last of it however many times I say. And bread. The bread is dear this month and will be dearer, so eat it as though you respect it."

"To the Lar," I said, "and to the price of bread."

"Don't be clever at the table," she said. "You're bad at it in front of the children."

---

Lucanus came that night, which was why there was salt pork.

He was two-and-twenty and he came in out of the rain in a toga he had paid to have cleaned, which he wore now even on a wet night because a man who means to stand in a basilica gets used to the weight of it early. He had Marcia's square build and her unhurried way of filling a doorway, and my long hands, which were the only thing of mine he had taken that he could not give back. He was clean-shaven and close-cropped, and the emperor had grown a beard, and Lucanus had not, and I understood why and did not say so, because a freeborn citizen reading law in Rome does not need a Greek beard making him look like he reads anything but law.

He kissed his mother and put two fingers to the Lar without looking at it and sat, and the toga had to be arranged before he could eat, which took a while, and Ulpia watched him do it with the patience of someone waiting for a mechanism to finish its cycle.

"You've folded it wrong," she said.

"I have not folded it wrong."

"The fall's wrong. You look like a man carrying a sheep."

"Ulpia," said Marcia.

He was reading with an advocate named Caesius now, with a practice in the Basilica Julia, taking notes and learning the forms, which magistrate hated to be kept waiting and which loved it. He had a memory for the human weather of a courtroom that I would have given a great deal for at his age, who could read whether a city was inland or coastal but not whether a soft-jawed magistrate had eaten. He told us about a property suit, two brothers and a wall and a drain, and made his mother laugh twice, which was the household's true coin, and I sat at the foot of the table and ate the salt pork and was as happy as a man gets.

Because there was a moment, there was always a moment, when the law-talk turned to the thing under it. Caesius was freeborn of freeborn, three generations clean. Lucanus was freeborn too, full citizen, born in lawful marriage, which had cost me a great deal to give him. But his father was a freedman, which was on no document anyone would show him and on every document about him forever. There is a height above which a freedman's son does not climb, and it is not a high height, and Lucanus had found it already, with the soft hands of a man only just begun to feel for the ceiling of the room he is in.

"Caesius says I could stand at the lower bar in three years," he said. "Small suits. Freedmen's business, mostly. Estates with no one to fight over them." He said it lightly. He had learned lightness from his mother and irony from me and used both as armor, and I knew the armor because I had forged it. "Caesius says a man with a head for figures and a clean Latin can make a good living and never be asked his grandfather's name, so long as he never wants to be asked it."

"That's a living," said Marcia.

"It's a good living."

"It is," she said, and meant it, because to Marcia a thing that fed you and could not be taken from you was very near the top of the world's goods, and she was almost never wrong, and I sat there wanting to put my son's hand on something higher and having nothing in this world to put it on that would not burn him.

What I had to give him was downstairs. The press, the figures, the contest, the long cold lunatic project of the books, a workshop full of people who would have died for the strangeness of it. And he did not want it. He had told me so at sixteen, gently, and meant it more at two-and-twenty, and he was right not to want it. The work had my name on it where my name was a stain. He wanted to walk into a building and have no one able to say what he was. I had spent thirty-six years being a thing no one could name, and I was not going to be the one to tell him that the clean room he wanted was also a small one.

So I asked him about the drain the two brothers were fighting over, and made him explain the easement law to me twice because the second time he stopped performing and started thinking, and his face when he was thinking was Marcia's, and I had that.

---

After, when Lucanus had gone back out into the rain to his rented room nearer the courts, because a man on the respectability track did not sleep over a warehouse, the table thinned to the women and the work.

This was the other half of my evenings and the better-kept secret of them. Ulpia would clear her plate to the side, not away, never away when there was a tablet involved, and pull the lamp close, and we would do the work I was no longer fit to do alone. I could not read my own small figures by lamplight anymore; I needed the light close and the page far and could not have both. Ulpia read them to me, and had for two years, since she was small enough to kneel up on the bench to see over the page, fast and flat, catching my errors before I made them, which only two people in the world had ever done and the other was Tyche.

That night it was the African coast, the survey that came up the river twice a year, leagues and soundings and the lie of the headlands, set into the new figures to one scale by, in the actual fact of the lamplit evening, a sixteen-year-old girl reading numbers to a half-blind man.

"You've the league wrong here," she said. "Or he has. Six hundred where the run before it is two hundred and the coast doesn't bend. Dropped a mark, six for two, the same stroke twice." She had it before I did. She always had it before I did now. "Correct it or note it?"

"Note it. Never correct another man's count, you query it. You don't know what he saw."

"He saw two and wrote six," she said, "but yes." And she noted it, in the small careful hand that was already better than mine had ever been, and somewhere a season's sail down a coast she would never see a headland got its true distance back because a girl in Rome would not let a stroke lie.

She was very good. Better than Lucanus, better than the second clerk, better than I had been at her age and getting better at a rate that frightened me on her behalf. She had the cipher two years; she read the books; she could take Newton's second law in my failing English and tell me where I had hedged it and where I had cheated, more Tyche's pupil than mine in that.

And there was nowhere for any of it to go. A freedman's daughter could not keep a counting-house under her own name, could not plead, could not hold a magistracy that did not exist for women in the first place, could not do one public thing with the best mind I had met in this world since Apollodorus went into the ground. She could do it all under a borrowed name, her mother's, a husband's, as Marcia did, as the whole enterprise already did. She had decided this was enough, at fourteen, with a coldness that was entirely her mother's. Marcia had warned me to teach her no further and Marcia had been right and I had gone on anyway, and there we were at the lamp, the two of us, having both gotten what we wanted and not what was underneath it.

---

The marriage talk had started.

It had started in Marcia's usual register: she had begun to mention names, pricing them the way she priced lentils, who had a son and what the son would come to and what a girl of ours could be made to be worth to such a house. Ulpia was sixteen. A girl of sixteen unbespoken was a girl whose father was dead, broke, or strange, and I was the third, and the strangeness had bought her a few extra years that were running out.

"The Ennii have a son," Marcia said, that same night, after Ulpia had gone to bed and Tyche had gone down to her own room and it was the two of us and the dying brazier. "Grain factors. Decent house. The boy's dull but he's kind, which lasts longer."

"She'd eat him alive."

"She'd eat most of them alive. That's not a reason. That's an argument for finding her a big enough plate." Marcia worked a knot out of her shoulder with her thumb. "I married a dull man the first time and buried him and he left me a warehouse. I married a strange one the second time and he gives me headaches and a son who won't take the business and a daughter too good for the world she's stuck in, and I'd call the strange one the better bargain, but I had the warehouse first. She has no warehouse, Daniel. She has a head no one will pay for and a name that's half yours."

"She has the cipher. She has the books."

"She has a secret she can never use in the daylight, and a husband she hasn't met yet who will own her in the law whatever we write in private, and you and I will be dead, and Lucanus off being clean, and there will be a man who decides whether she keeps a tablet at her own table." She let that sit. She was the least cruel person I knew and the most exact, and the two together could take the wind out of you. "I'm not saying give her up. I'm saying I'm shopping for the man who'll let her be what she is, and that man costs more than the dull kind one, and we should know it going in."

I said I didn't know how to be told this.

"You're not being told it well. Heras was the one who could say a hard thing soft, and he's gone, so you and I are stuck saying them hard." She got up and banked the brazier and stood with her back to me a moment, and then said, not turned around, "Find her a man who can stand a clever wife. I'll do the rest. I always do the rest." Which was as close as she came that year to saying it grieved her, and was more than I could have managed, and I had a country full of people who said such things plainly and could tell none of them to her.

---

The roots came up, when they came up, sideways. Heras used to say the truth comes that way.

It was a few nights on, a hard cold after the rain, the brazier being mean with its heat, and I was doing the dull patient work of the cipher with Ulpia, copying a clean rung of the lower ladder, the four workings, into the fair copy that would go into the lead and the pitch. It is slow work and the hand wanders and the mind wanders with it, and I was humming.

I do that. I only know I am doing it when someone tells me, because in the world I came from a man could hum and the air around him was full of the same songs and no one heard him. I was humming a song I had hummed for thirty-six years, that I no longer had whole, that came up in pieces with the pull of a tune and the wrong sounds in the gaps, and I patched the gaps as everyone does with a half-remembered song, except that everyone else can go and find the song.

Ulpia stopped writing.

"What is that?"

"What's what."

"That. You were making it. With the breath." She set the stylus down. She had her mother's flat patience and my refusal to leave a thing alone, which is a bad combination to be questioned by. "You do it when you copy. The same one. It goes up and then it falls and there's a part in the middle you never reach, you go around it. What is it?"

And I opened my mouth to lie, because I always lied, the road-lie, the far north, the songs they sing in the long dark of Thule, worn smooth from use. And I did not tell it, I am not sure why, except that it was cold and the girl had just spent two hours setting down knowledge I would die owning the only true source of, and I was tired in the particular way that makes a man honest at the wrong moment.

"It's a song from where I'm from," I said. "I don't remember it."

"You're singing it."

"I'm singing what's left. There were words. I had them, going on forty years with no one to sing them to and no way to fetch them back, and they've worn off like a face wears off a coin. You can see there was something, but you can't say what it said." I hummed the up-and-the-fall, and went around the hole in the middle, and stopped. "There's a word right there. I know there was a word. I'd know it if I heard it." I held my hands flat on the table a moment. "I'll never hear it."

It was not like the sleet-word. The sleet-word had been somewhere, real, in a country with enough cold falling that it needed a name. If someone had taken me there and played the song I'd have had it back; the word was not gone, it was merely elsewhere, out of reach. But this gap in the music was a different kind of missing. Forty years of no one to sing it to had worn it past retrieving. The word had been mine alone, in a world that was gone, and I had let it drain away a syllable at a time without knowing it was draining, and now the song had a hole in the center where the thing had been, and the hole was permanent, and not elsewhere, just gone, the way a man's face is gone when the man is gone, not waiting anywhere to be found.

She watched me. She did not believe in Thule, none of them did, too well-mannered to say so and too sharp not to know, and I watched her decide not to ask the question she was too smart not to have, which was: a song from *where*. Where there are no caravans and no ships and no other man who knows the tune.

"Teach me the part you have," she said instead.

So I did. I hummed it and she hummed it back, flat and exact, better than I had it, and we went up and we fell and we went around the hole in the middle, the two of us, in a cold room over a warehouse, and she hummed the gap faithfully around the nothing, and I had to get up and see to the lamp because my eyes were doing something that a man of fifty-three is permitted to do over a poor lamp, and also because there is a particular grief in being heard accurately, in someone carrying forward the true shape of what you have already lost, and I needed a moment with my back to the room.

She never asked again. She filed it where she had filed the cipher's too-tidy word for sleet the year before, and made of it whatever a brilliant girl makes of a father with a song he cannot finish and a word for weather Rome does not have. She did not press. She had decided, somewhere I could not see, that there was a locked room in me and that she would not pick the lock.

---

Macer came to dinner at the turn of the year, which was a production. He was eighty-six, and being eighty-six was the only thing left that genuinely interested him; he had to be carried up the stair in a chair by two of his men, complaining the whole way that the warehouse stank, which it did. He called me Thulean still, as he had for thirty-five years and boy before that, and liked Ulpia in the way a hard old man likes a hard young thing, and told her, with the cruelty old men are forgiven, that it was a pity she was a girl because she'd have made him a quartermaster.

"It's a pity for your quartermaster," Ulpia said. "Not for me."

Macer laughed until he had to be thumped on the back. "Where did you get this one, Thulean. Not from you. You never said a sharp thing in your life you didn't apologize for after."

"Her mother," I said, and he agreed. He set his cup down and pulled at the cuff of his sleeve, the slow fidget of a man who had spent his whole life managing rooms and now had only this small warm one. Marcia brought out the cup I had saved for him — the dark, thick drink I had spent a season learning to make right, ground from the roasted beans that had come back from the second expedition wrapped in oilcloth and labeled in a script I did not know but recognized on sight. He looked at it the way he looked at anything new, which was to say without approval.

"What is it."

"Something sweet. From the expedition's haul. Drink it before it cools."

He drank. He set the cup down with the deliberate precision of a man who does not want his hands to give him away. He did not ask what it was called. After a moment he said, "Again."

I told him the name — or the name I half-remembered from a school lesson forty years gone, which was not right, I knew it was not right, but it was what I had and the word that was right was in a language no one in this room would speak for another thousand years, so I said the wrong name and left it there.

He fell asleep before the bread was done. He woke once, found me across the loud bright room, said, "You did all right, boy," in a voice I had not heard him use, and went back to sleep, and in the morning denied it.

---

There is the thing in the dark before the lamps, that I started with and could not name.

I will not name it now either. But I will say what it was made of, on a cold clear night when the rain had quit and the warehouse breathed its pepper and tar up through the floor. A tablet face-down where Ulpia had left it with a queried mark she would not let lie, and Lucanus's wet footprints by the door already dried to nothing, and Marcia somewhere below counting the day's loads against tomorrow's, and a song in my chest with a hole in the middle of it that the whole house could now hum and none of them could fill.

I went around the house at the end the way I went around the song. The lamps. The brazier banked. The door barred, the Lar with its little horn catching the last of the light. Up the stair to the room where Marcia was already asleep over a tally she'd dropped on the floor, and I picked it up because the figures would smear if it lay open, and I could not read the figures and it did not matter, I knew the hand, and I set it on the chest where she'd find it. Then I lay down in the warm dark of a house that was mine in a country that was not, and listened to the rain start again on the tiles of a roof two thousand years before I was born, over the people I loved and could not tell where I had come from, and I did not finish the song, because there was no finishing it, and the loneliness eased, the way it had always eased in this house and never entirely gone, and slept.
