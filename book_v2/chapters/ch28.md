# Chapter 28: East

The order came in two halves, and the second half was the one with the hook in it.

The first half was a tablet from the imperial freedman's office, the dry house-Latin I had learned to read past the polished surface of. Marcus Ulpius Danihel, libertus, was to attend the eastern expeditio in the service of the army's health, water and camps and the flux that Caesar held in particular dread, and would present himself at Brundisium before the close of the sailing season. My name on it in the new figures for the tally of what I was to be issued, which someone in that office had taken to using because it added faster, so that I was being sent to a war partly by a hand I had taught to count.

The second half came in person, two days later, in a freedman of Scaeva's at my gate with a basket of late apricots and a message that had no apricots in it at all. The senator was glad, said the man, smiling, that Caesar saw fit to send so useful a person where useful persons were so badly wanted. The senator hoped I would write to him of what I saw, the supply, the country, the temper of the legions, small things, a friend's curiosity. The senator had, the man said, taken the liberty of commending me to certain officers of his acquaintance who would be in the east, so that I should not arrive among strangers.

So that was the leash, handed to me with fruit. The emperor sent me to keep his soldiers' bowels in their bellies. Scaeva sent me to be his eye, and had arranged, before I had agreed to anything, that the men I worked under would already half-belong to him. I gave the freedman a cup of wine and thanked the senator for his care and ate two of the apricots after the man had gone, standing in the yard, because they were good and because there was nothing else in the morning I had any power over.

Marcia did the figures of it before I did. She always did. That night she sat with the lamp and Caepio's old ledger habit on her, and she worked out how long the eastern campaign might run by the simple measure of how much grain the office had told me to expect to oversee, which was not a number you laid in for a season. "This is a war that means to stay where it goes," she said. She did not say what that meant for how long I would be gone. She set the stylus down. "Tyche keeps the figures. I keep the rest. The contest will go on under the priests' hand whether you kneel for it or not, and the press will print, and Lucanus will grow, and you will come back to a thing that did not need you, which is the only kind worth building." It was the closest she came to telling me to live.

I left her the seal and the will and the floorboard, which she already had. I left Tyche the cipher, which was hers more than mine now. I held the boy in the grey before dawn while he was still too asleep to know it, the small weight of him, the milk-and-wool smell of him, and then I put him back in the basket and got on the cart, because a man who is owned by the emperor's house does not get to stay for the waking.

---

I had gone north to a war once and thought I had seen the machine. I had seen a finger of it.

The road south to Brundisium and then the sea and then the long crawl of the coast toward Syria showed me the arm, and then the body, and I never did see all of it, which is the truth about it I have never been able to put plainly to anyone who was not there. You cannot stand far enough back. Rome going to war in the east was something you were inside of, like weather, with no front edge to it and no outside to watch it from.

At Brundisium the harbor was so full of bottoms that the late ships rode out in the roads waiting their turn at the moles, and the grain lay on the quays in hills under their leather and the men slept on the hills to keep the porters honest. I stood among barrels stacked taller than a house, my barrels, the filter batteries I had drawn the plan of years ago in a yard by the Tiber, and watched a clerk tick them off a list against a tablet, and the tablet was one of a stack, and the stack was one of a room, and somewhere there was a man whose whole task in this life was rooms of tablets that were rooms of barrels that were the drinking of an army that had not yet reached the place it would drink. The reach of it. I had thought I understood numbers. I understood a number. I did not understand the arithmetic where you keep finding, under the number, another number, all of them pulling east.

We coasted Greece and put in for water and went on. The harbors of Asia were strung along the shore like beads on a wire and every one of them had Rome's grain in it and Rome's pay-chests under guard and Rome's sick lying in the shade of the warehouses waiting to be carried home or buried, and the wire ran on east past where I had any names for the places, and the whole length of it was tightening toward the same point, the way a net does when it is drawn. Antioch, when we reached it, was a great roaring city that had become something it was not built to be, an army's mouth, everything coming in and being chewed and sent on, and over all of it the dust of men and animals moving in one direction, which was the direction the maps in my head ran out of detail.

I lay awake my first nights in that dust and did the arithmetic I could not stop doing, the bushels and the miles and the mules and the men, and the answer my arithmetic kept giving was a kind of vertigo. There was no end built into any of it. The grain came east to feed the men who went east to take the land that would, when it was taken, need grain sent east to it, and the line of supply that fed the conquest would have to lengthen to feed the conquest's fruit, and lengthen again, and there was no place in the design where a Roman had drawn a wall and written here we will be content. They were pouring the whole weight of the world they had into a country to take the country next to it, so that they might pour the weight into that one, and I could not find the bottom of the want.

---

Celer found me. He always found me. He came into the camp outside Antioch where I was arguing with an engineer about the fall of a drain, an older man now, the grey gone right through the cropped hair, the broken-set nose, the same dust-colored tunic as though it were the one I had met him in, and he stood and watched me lose the argument for a while before he said, "You've not got any prettier."

"You've not got any kinder."

"No money in it." He looked at the trench, at the engineer, at the camp running off in its straight Roman lines to the haze. "They told me a freedman of Caesar's was coming to teach us to drink water. I said I knew him. I said he'd kill more of us with boiling than the Parthians would with arrows, just to make us obey him." He let that sit, the old dry way. "Walk with me. I want to show you something and I don't want to do it in front of your drain."

He was a prefect now, of an auxiliary cohort, and what he wanted to show me was the cohort's stretch of the supply, which was to say a road, and the road was the point. We walked out along it past the picket and it ran east straight as a plumb line over country that had been somebody's fields before this year, and on it, both ways, the traffic never stopped. Mule trains nose to tail. Ox carts groaning under timber for engines and bridges. Remounts. Wine. A century marching out with their stakes on their shoulders to build the next camp on the next clean water I or someone like me had not yet sited. Empty carts coming back for more. And the sick, coming back, always coming back, in the empty carts, a man's foot hanging off the tail, gray-faced, the flux taking them as it had always taken them, more of them than any battle would.

"How far does it go," I said. I meant the road. I think I meant the road.

"Armenia's ours now," Celer said, as a man tells you the price of bread. "King knelt to Caesar. They took his crown off him and made it a province between one new moon and the next, and the lads who did it are already grumbling about going on to where it's worse." He stopped and looked along the line of it east, where the dust took the road and you could no longer tell road from sky. "I've fought on the Rhine and the Danube and I'm here. My whole life's been the edge of it moving out from under me. You build the camp, you win the ground, and the next morning the camp's not the edge anymore, it's the rear, and the edge is a day's march further on and you're packing up to chase it." He spat into the dust. "There's lads in my cohort whose fathers I served with. We're further out than their fathers ever stood, and the talk in the tents is all of how much further we'll go, the rivers there are, the cities, the gold. Nobody asks where it stops. You ask a Roman where it stops and he looks at you like you've asked where the sky stops."

"And where does it stop," I said.

He turned and gave me the look he gave a recruit who had said something either very stupid or worth hearing, he had not yet decided which. "When we run out of world," he said, "or out of us. One of the two always comes first. I've a wager with myself which." He started back toward the camp. "Mind your drain. The lads die of the water out here same as everywhere, and I'd as soon you kept mine alive long enough to die somewhere useful."

I walked back behind him with that in me like the splinter Crispus had put in, the realest thing said in the worst place. Out of world or out of us. I kept my soldiers' guts in them and they marched east on the strength of it to be spent further along the line, and that was my trade now, the same trade it had been at the siege, the knot I had stopped pretending I could cut. I made them able to go on. Going on was the disease.

---

The work itself was the work. There is no glory in it and I will not invent any. I sited camps where the ground would carry the filth away from the water instead of into it, which is the whole of the art and most of the time it is enough. I set the barrel batteries and made the centurions answer for the boiling, century by century, a tedium of standing over fires telling grown men who had killed other men to wait for the bubbles, and being hated for it, and saving them. Heras was not with me, being old now and kept in Rome, but his methods were, the clean cloth and the cooled boiled water on the wound, said in my mouth as Greek wisdom because that is how it went down. I lost men anyway. You always lose men. You lose fewer, and the fewer go on east to find the place that kills them, and you tell yourself the arithmetic and the arithmetic does not love you back.

The empty basket went up when the legates wanted to see over a ridge, the tethered bag with no man in it, the cloth strips for signals, useful only in still air as it had ever been, and there is a country south and east of Antioch where the air is still enough in the morning that it earned its keep more often than it had on the Danube. They asked me, twice, both times an officer new to me and once an officer of Scaeva's commending, whether a man could be sent up in it to see the further country, the rivers, the marching order of an enemy that had not yet shown itself. I said no flat and total, a freedman's no that this time was solid because the emperor's letter had given me the camps and the water and the bag both, and the no came with the bag. I did not tell them why. The why was a soldier in Pannonia with a scar that made him look amused, and a sound, and cloth coming down. I had buried the why under a lid years ago and nailed it shut, and I let them think me only stubborn.

It was on one of those still mornings, watching the empty basket hang at the end of its line over country that ran flat and brown and enormous toward a horizon the army was going to cross, that the other thought came to me. A wound with an idea in it. I told no one.

I was looking east because that was where the bag pointed and where everything pointed. And I thought, the kind of thought you immediately wish you had not had, that all of this, the road and the grain-hills and the mule trains and Celer's lads grumbling toward the next river and the carts of the gray-faced sick, all of it was a hunger that had to go somewhere, and that it had chosen to go where there were people on the ground to be ground up, because that was the only direction the maps in their heads ran. And I had drawn them other maps once. On a wax tablet, in a cell, before I could speak to them, I had drawn the world and put two badly-drawn continents across a blank western sea, lands with no king to kneel and no city to starve and no cohort grumbling that the further river was worse, empty on the page, a year of open water away, reachable by no ship Rome had yet built.

The hunger could be pointed west. The thought arrived whole and obscene and I felt it the way you feel the lip of a height. A want with no bottom, turned at a sea instead of a country, would grind up no one but the men who chose to sail it. It would need ships, which meant timber and shipwrights and the patience of years, which meant it could not happen in a week, which meant no Sabinus would fall out of it before I could stop him. It would empty Rome's appetite into the one place I knew was empty, because I was the one who had drawn it empty.

And I recoiled from it standing there with the line in my hand. Because I knew what it was. The same move that had cut Sarmizegetusa's water and burst the bronze tube and put a boy in a basket over a hill, the move of a man who has noticed that he can turn the wheel and has not yet found a reason good enough to keep his hands off it. And because I did not know if there was anything in that western sea to land on within a year's sail or three, my drawing being a copy off a dead phone and not a measured survey, so that to point the hunger west might be to point it at open water and drown it, which some nights I thought would be the kindest thing I could do for the people on the eastern ground and other nights thought was the worst lie I would ever tell.

I hauled the empty basket down and folded the bag and said nothing to the officer who asked again what a man might have seen from up there, and I went back to the drains.

---

Celer and I ate together the night before his cohort moved up, in his tent, posca and hard bread and a bit of the bad local wine he'd got hold of, and we did not talk of where it stopped again because we had already said the only true thing there was to say about it. We talked of the Danube, the cold, a mule that had bitten an optio, men we both knew who were dead now of one ordinary thing or another, the long boring catalogue two men keep who have been frightened in the same places. He asked after the boy and I told him the boy was fat and furious and Marcia ran the whole enterprise better than I ever had, and he said that was no surprise, that he'd met her once and she'd priced his loyalty to my face and got it about right. Naso's name came up, the ruined hand, the ankle; Celer had kept him on with the baggage, alive, mending mules in the rear, and I was more glad of that than I let him see.

When I got up to go he said, not looking at me, working a strap on his kit while he wanted his hands busy for a hard thing, "You'll mind yourself out here. You're a senator's man now, they tell me. That's worse than being nobody's, in a war. Nobody's man just dies. A great man's man gets used first and dies after." He pulled the strap tight. "Build them their water. Keep your no about the bag. And when this is over, if it's ever over, go home to the fat furious boy and stop saving the army. The army doesn't deserve you and I should know, I'm the army."

"You're a sentimental old bastard."

"I'm a tired one." He looked up then, the broken nose, the grey, the dry mouth that did not smile but you knew. "Go on. I've a war to lose sleep over."

I went out into the camp with the cooking fires going down all along the straight lines of it and the picket calling and beyond the picket the road still moving in the dark, the carts you could hear but not see, east, always east. Somewhere ahead of all of it the country flattened out and ran on toward rivers I did not know the names of and the army would learn them one at a time and add them to the list of things it had taken and could not let go. I stood at the edge of the firelight a while. Then I went to my tent.
