# Chapter 18: The Siege

The price of Pamphilus was four thousand sesterces, which Macer named to me across his own atrium with the gold ring going on his thumb, and which was, he wanted me to understand, robbery in my favor.

"He's a strong back going gray," Macer said. "Ten years of carry left in him if his knees hold. I'd get more at the block off a man who didn't know his joints. You I'll bleed slow because you'll come back for the girl and I want you to remember I was kind." He had a steward bring the tablet, the bill of sale, and he had me read it through, the careful law-words, *Pamphilus, servus,* a collared porter of the household of Titus Flavius Macer, sold for the named sum to Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified house, and there it was on the wax, a man bought and a man buying, both of us his once, and I counted out the coin from my own peculium that was mine now in law and not in sufferance, heavy bitten silver, and Macer's clerk weighed it and bit two of the aurei and wrote the receipt, and Pamphilus belonged to me.

That was the morning. The freeing I did myself in the yard, badly, because I had never done it and there is a way to do it and I did not know the way.

You are supposed to go to a magistrate. The full thing, a rod laid on the slave's head, a citizen made in a morning by a man with the power to do it. I did not have eight days and a praetor's patience, I had a war to march to, so I did the household thing, the cheap door, which makes a free man but a lesser-free man, only the start of citizenship. I sat Pamphilus down on the bench by the well and said the words wrong twice and got Heras, who was there because Heras was always there when a thing mattered, to feed them to me, and I declared before him and before Tyche and a wharf-clerk I'd dragged over for a witness that this man was free.

The collar was the trouble.

It was riveted. It had been riveted for the eleven years Pamphilus had worn it and the iron had thinned where it rode his collarbone and gone green-black in the creases of his neck, and you do not unlock a thing like that, you cut it, and I had not thought about that part, the way I had not thought about a great many parts, because in my head freeing a man was a sentence said and a tablet signed and the man stood up different. The man did not stand up different. He sat on the bench with his hands on his knees while Hermes set a cold chisel against the rivet at the back of his neck and looked at me to ask was I sure, because a slip there opens the big vessel and a freed man bleeds out on his first free morning, and Pamphilus held still, stiller than I have ever seen a living man hold, and Hermes struck the chisel twice and the rivet sheared and the collar came away in two halves and left a band of paler skin, soft, never weathered, like the print of a thing lifted off a sun-bleached table.

Pamphilus put his hand up to his own neck and felt the bare skin there.

"You free," he said, after a while, to nobody in particular, and took his hand down.

He had said *you* when he meant *I.* Eleven years of Latin learned in a house where somebody else was the subject.

I had imagined, God help me, that he would weep, or grip my arm, or do some version of the thing that happens in the story when the chains come off. What I wanted was the clean feeling, the one clean thing, after Sabinus and the boring water that keeps men healthy enough to be killed and all the rest of the ledger I was carrying. I wanted Pamphilus to make me feel like I had done a good thing.

What he did was ask whether he was still to cut the cloth.

"You don't have to cut anything," I said. "You're free. You can go."

"Go where," Pamphilus said.

He was asking. Not bitter - asking. He was a man past forty with a band of pale skin on his neck and no family that had survived being sold twice and no trade but carrying and lifting and cutting bolt-cloth, and the question was real, *go where,* and I did not have an answer that was not the answer the world gives every freed man, which is: nowhere, you go nowhere, you stay near the man who freed you because he is your patron now and you owe him the duties a freedman owes and outside his door is a city that eats men with no door, and the chain you can't see is the better fitted of the two. I had freed him into being my client. I had moved him one rung and called it the sky.

"You stay," I said. "On wages. Real ones, into your own hand, your own peculium that's yours now, not sufferance. You cut the cloth because I'll pay you to cut the cloth, and when you don't want to cut it you tell me and we'll find what else, but Pamphilus, you eat at this yard and you sleep dry and nobody" - and here my Latin failed me and I said it too plain - "nobody owns you. That's the whole of it. It's smaller than I wanted it to be."

He looked at the two halves of the collar lying in the dirt by the well. Tyche had gone very still by the shed, watching, because she had asked *can you free us,* us before her, and she was watching what free turned out to be, and I could not look at her.

"Small." Pamphilus rubbed the pale band with two fingers. "But mine."

Then he picked up the shears and went to the cloth, because that was the morning's work and he was a man who did the morning's work, and I stood there holding a freedom that had cost four thousand sesterces and felt like a man who'd paid full price for half a thing.

Heras took the collar halves away. He said he wanted them for something. He never said what, and I never asked, and I think he simply did not want them in the yard where I'd keep looking at them.

***

I marched north in the spring with my name on the orders.

That is the difference I cannot make small enough, between the first war and this one. The first time I went up the Via Flaminia I went as a thing on loan, Macer's property lent to Celer's command, a slave with a clever toy, and I rode in the baggage and boiled my own water and watched the machine of the army move and was moved by it like a parcel. This time I went as Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified house, with a warrant that requisitioned timber and lime and digging-men in my name, and a mule of my own, and Pamphilus and two hired wharf-men I paid wages, and a sheaf of the new figures the quartermasters were now ordered to suffer, and I went because the emperor's man does not get asked whether he is going.

The army was bigger. I keep wanting to say the war was bigger and that is the historian's word and not the true one. The war was the same hills. The army was bigger, doubled, more than doubled, legions pulled off the Rhine and out of the East, a flood of men and mules and the smell of them going north all spring, the road never empty, the dust never settling, and I understood walking in it that the client-king peace I'd watched them make four years before, the relief and the anticlimax, Decebalus keeping his head and his hills, had been a thing Trajan meant to come back and finish, that it had been an intermission, that the whole vast machine had been quietly oiling itself for the second act the entire time I'd been in Rome teaching shopkeepers to count. Macer had known. Macer had been stockpiling leather for tents the whole while. The flux talks, he'd said, and the flux had talked, and here was the answer to it, going up the road in a column you could not see the end of.

And there was the bridge.

I'd seen Apollodorus build the first bridges, the wood on the cofferdams, the river fought back a span at a time, and I'd thought that was the great thing. The great thing was at Drobeta. He'd put a bridge of stone across the Danube. Twenty piers of dressed stone standing in the river that had drowned his cofferdam-men in the first war, the timber arches riding up off them, the thing running bank to bank wide enough for the army to cross it marching, and the legions tramping over it in their thousands as if crossing a river that size were a thing a man simply did on a Tuesday.

I found him on the Dacian bank, plumb in hand, watching a pier the way a man watches a child he doesn't trust near water.

"You came back," he said, not turning. "They freed you. I heard." He said *freed* the way you'd say a man had been promoted into a worse job, which was about right. "Marcus Ulpius now. The emperor collects useful Greeks and useful barbarians the way other men collect bronzes."

"It's a good bridge," I said, because it was, and because I wanted to see what he'd do with a true thing said plainly.

He turned then. He'd gone grayer and the sun had worked his face harder and his hands were the same scarred thick instruments. He looked at the bridge, his bridge, and there was no false modesty in him and no boasting either, just a man looking at a sum that had come out.

"It will outlast the province," he said. "They'll take the country and lose it in two lifetimes, three, and the bridge will still stand when there's no Rome on either bank to use it." He said it flat, a fact, the cold long view of a man who built in stone for a state that thought in seasons. Then he looked at me. "Your flying toy. Still does the one thing."

"Still does the one thing."

"Good. Do the one thing." He turned back to his pier. "Don't put a man under it. I heard about that too." And that was Apollodorus, who did not forgive a botched demonstration and did not pretend the dead man hadn't been my fault, and who I respected more than men who'd have been kinder, and I left him to his bridge and went up into the hills to do the one thing.

***

What I did in that war, set down plainly, so that whoever finds this knows what the work was:

I sited camps. That is the dullest sentence I will ever write and it kept more men alive than anything I have built. I walked ground ahead of the engineers and I said put the latrines here, downstream, downslope, lime them, dig them deep, and the water-point here, upstream, above the horse-lines, above the fouling, and I made them mean it, because the emperor's warrant meant I could make a centurion move a latrine, and a centurion does not forget being made to move a latrine by a barbarian in work boots, and I paid for that in small ways all summer. I set the filters at the water-points, the sand and the charcoal and the gravel, big ones now, barrel-batteries, and I made the water boiled by century and not by whim. Where the men did it the flux thinned and where the men were lazy the flux did what the flux does, and I watched both happen in the same army in the same month and had no way to count it, only the latrine-pits, fuller in one camp than another, and the carts of the sick, lighter where I'd been listened to.

I sent up the tethered eye. Just the eye, the bag on its hundred feet of rope, no man, a code of colored cloth and a soldier with good distance-sight in a basket no higher than a man could survive falling from, and it saw what it saw on the still mornings and was useless the rest of the time, and Celer's successors used it and cursed it and used it again.

Celer was there. He'd come up with the second war the way he'd gone down from the first, clipped and dust-colored and gallows-humored, the broken-set nose, and when he saw the warrant with my name on it he read it twice and looked at me and said, "They made you real," and there was something under the dryness I won't name because he'd have hated me naming it. We never said Sabinus. He checked, the first day, that the bag I'd brought went up empty, ran his eye over the basket and the rope, and when he found no man's harness in it he let out a breath and said, "Good. The boring stuff, then." So we did the boring stuff, the water and the eye, and he stayed alive all that war, which I am setting down because I spent the whole of it braced for the other thing, and the other thing did not come, not in Dacia.

And I did the figures. The grain and the fodder and the days of march and the men to feed, columns of it, the new marks racing ahead of the old freedmen and their abacuses, and a quartermaster who'd fought me in the spring came to me by autumn with a sheaf of his own done in the marks and a column that wouldn't balance, *find my error,* not *teach me,* a man too proud to learn and too pressed not to, and I found his error, a transposed pair, the kind the marks catch because you can't quietly thicken a stroke the way you can a tally, and he took the sheaf back without thanks and used the marks the rest of the war. Need spread the figures, not gratitude. The grudging man's version of it, but it spread.

Pamphilus had come north with me, carrying and lifting and cutting whatever needed cutting, three months of it. He did not complain about the road. He did not complain about the cold. One evening in camp, after I'd moved a latrine trench for the fourth time and a junior centurion had spent ten minutes explaining to me in close detail what I could do with my warrant, Pamphilus looked over from where he was sorting the rope-coils and said, "That man. Loud." He thought about it for a moment. "You want I move the ropes so he trips." He had not said it as a joke. It was a practical offer, delivered without affect, and his Latin was worse than ever from three months of soldier's street-talk, and it was the kindest thing anyone said to me that week.

"No," I said. "Don't."

"Right," Pamphilus said, and went back to the rope.

***

Sarmizegetusa sat up in the mountains in the cold thin air with the walls of murus dacicus that the engineers admired and hated, stone faces packed with rubble and tied with squared timber so the rams bit and the wall ate the bite, and behind it the Dacians and their king and their gold and their gods, and around it the army dug in, the lines of circumvallation going up, the ballistae hauled into the heights piece by cursing piece. A siege is patience and engineering and starving a place, and I had not understood that the way I understood it that autumn, watching men dig lines around other men to kill them slowly.

The water came into the place from springs, channeled the way water is always channeled, in pipes and covered runs along the contours of the hills, ceramic and lead and hollowed stone, the same craft I'd spent four years admiring in Rome, the aqueducts I'd gawked at, the Roman genius for moving water that I had loved without reservation. And an engineer of Apollodorus's, a young hard Greek with a survey-staff, found the lines on the slope below the walls, traced them, and brought the question up the chain, and the question came to me, because I was the emperor's water-man and the warrant said so.

Could it be cut.

I read the slope. I knew, looking at where the springs had to be and how the runs would have to fall to feed a fort that high, roughly where the channels ran, and I said so. I said cut here, and here, where the contour forces the run, and where you can't reach the buried part, foul it - put the camp's filth above their intake, do to their water what I spend my life stopping us from doing to ours - and I heard myself say it in my plain accented Latin to a man with a survey-staff, the exact inversion of every word I'd said to the emperor in the cold room, and the young Greek wrote it on his tablet and it was good engineering and it was the cleverest thing I did in that war and it broke a city.

It did not break it cleanly. Nothing works cleanly. They had cisterns, they had rain, they had the discipline of a people who had held that hill against Rome for a generation, and the cut water did not bring the walls down in a day. It worked as the flux works. Slowly, and on the weakest first, and out of sight. I did not see them die of it. That is the thing I keep coming back to: I did the water and never saw the water do its work, the same as I never saw the boiled water save a man, no number either way, only the result that came down the mountain in the end, that the place broke, that the fight went out of it, that a city full of people ran dry on a high cold hill because a man from a country that didn't exist had read the slope right.

There was an inch where I could have been slow, vague, wrong on purpose - the inch I'd used for two years to drag my feet on the powder, *not yet, maybe not ever.* I did not use it, because I was the emperor's man and the warrant had my name and a freedman of the deified house who is slow and vague at a siege is a freedman who has discovered what the state does with tools that lie to it. I could have refused and I would have died for it, or worse. I read the slope. I said cut here. I am not going to dress up the fact that I chose to live as a moral act.

Apollodorus came to see the cut once, professionally, and he stood looking at the fouled run with his plumb-line still in his hand out of habit and said, "Neat," and nothing else for a while, and then, because he was the only man in that army who knew what the water work was supposed to be, who'd watched me at the bridge keeping legions from the flux, he said, "It's the same knowledge." Not a question. Not comfort. He said it the way he'd said the bridge would outlast Rome, a fact about the world that he had no intention of softening. "The same hand that keeps your men off the flux fouls theirs. You didn't think it would stay on the one side."

"No," I said.

"Then don't make the face," he said, and walked off down the slope, and I have wondered since whether that was the cruelest thing anyone said to me in that war or the kindest, and I have never decided.

***

The place fell. The fight went out of it and the lines went in and the city that had never been taken was taken, and the king, Decebalus, who I never saw, who was a name to me and a reason for all of it, did not let himself be led down that mountain in chains. He cut his own throat. A cavalry scout found him doing it, or just done, the story changed in the telling the way every soldier's story changes, but the throat was the constant of it, the king who would not be a placard, and they cut off his head and his right hand to carry south as proof, because the state needs proof, the state does cold sums and a head is a sum that balances.

And then the gold.

I had heard there was gold. Everyone had heard there was gold, Dacia's gold, the king's treasure, stories all summer of the river dammed, the hoard buried, slaves killed to keep the secret. What I know is what came down the mountain in the autumn, cart after cart under guard, more guard than I'd ever seen put on anything, and the men knew what was in the carts the way men always know, and the mood of the army changed around it, a greed coming off the columns like heat, because every man in that machine knew that a haul like that meant a donative, coin in the hand, and meant games, and meant that the war had paid, that the dying in the valleys had been an investment and the investment had come good, and you could feel the whole vast thing turn from a war into a triumph while it was still standing on the conquered ground.

I did the figures on some of it. They put the emperor's water-man and his quick marks onto the reckoning of part of the haul, and I sat in a tent counting Dacian gold by weight into the columns of the new figures I'd carried up that road to keep men from dying of bad water. The two things sat in my hand at once, the column of grain that had kept a cohort off the flux and the column of looted gold that the same cohort had died and killed to take, the same marks, the same hand, and I balanced the column, because it balanced, because the marks always balance, that is the whole virtue of them.

Dacia was a province now. The country with the bridge across the great river was Rome's, the gold was Rome's, the king's head was going to Rome, and Rome was going to spend the gold on the longest games anyone could remember. I would hear about those games from the city when I got back down, the days and days of them, the killing made into a holiday, paid for out of the carts I'd helped count.

***

I came down off that mountain a different man than the one who'd gone up, and I do not entirely have the measure of the difference even now, writing it. I had freed one man in the spring and found that freeing was smaller than I'd dreamed. I had spent the summer keeping men alive with water and the autumn killing a city with water, the same knowledge, the one hand. And somewhere in the middle of it, doing the cut, a thought came that I have never been able to put back in its box. The first war had ended in a client-king peace, an intermission. This one ended in annexation and a dead king and the gold, finished, the way I half-remembered from a history class two thousand years off that it had ended. Had it ended that way. Was I remembering it or watching it. Had any of it gone otherwise than it would have without a barbarian by the timber wharf who read a slope, and I had no way to know, no number, no second world to lay beside this one and check the column.

We crossed back over Apollodorus's stone bridge in the cold, the army and the carts and the head and the gold and me, twenty piers standing in the river that had drowned the men who built them. I stopped at the rail a moment in the press of the column and looked down at the gray water going under, fast, indifferent, the water I knew better than anything in the world, and I put my hand flat on the cold stone. Then a mule behind me balked and the column shoved and I went on across to the Roman bank, where my warrant was still good and my name was still the emperor's, and there was a winter of figures waiting, and a city full of gold, and a girl at the wharf I had promised, next, to buy.
