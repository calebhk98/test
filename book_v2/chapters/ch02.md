# Chapter 2: The Cell

Nobody shot me, which is the bar I'd set for the morning and I cleared it.

The one in front kept his arm out toward the others and came at me sideways, slow, the way you'd come at a dog you weren't sure about. I kept my hands up. I kept them so far up my shoulders ached. He said something short and I said nothing because I had nothing, and he said it again louder, like volume was the problem, like I was a tourist and he could find the English under there if he shouted, and I shook my head, which I think is close to universal, and his eyes went flat and careful in a way I did not like.

He reached out and took a fistful of my T-shirt at the shoulder. Not to hurt. To feel it. He rubbed the cotton between his thumb and two fingers and his face did a thing, a small stalling, and he looked back at one of the others and said a word and the other one came up and felt it too, and now there were two grown men with spears standing close enough that I could smell them, woodsmoke and sweat and something sour and animal under that, fingering the hem of my sleeve and talking over my head in fast low voices about my shirt. The shirt is from a three-pack. It cost about six dollars. I wanted to tell them that and there was no version of me that could.

They tied my hands.

Rope, not cuffs. I'd half braced for the cold bite of metal because that's what my brain had filed under "arrest," and instead it was rough fiber, scratchy, smelling of hay, wound twice around my wrists in front of me and knotted hard by the second man while the leader watched my face the whole time. The rope hurt more than cuffs would have. It pinched in a narrow line and there was no give in it. I remember thinking, stupidly, with the clean idiot clarity of a person in shock, that I knew a better knot. I'd learned a trucker's hitch off a video to tie a kayak to the Corolla. I knew a knot these men didn't and it was the single most useless fact in the entire valley.

Then they walked me down.

I have tried a lot of times since to put the town in order and I can't, because I didn't go through it in order, I went through it the way you go through a wave, all of it at once and over your head. The path turned to a rutted lane and the lane filled up with people the way water fills a low spot. The smell came first and never left: smoke and dung, the dung of more animals than I could name, and under it a thick rotten low note I'd later learn was the tannery and a sharp one I'd learn was the fish sauce they put on everything, and woodsmoke again, always woodsmoke, and bodies. Heat off the walls. The walls were close and the upper floors leaned in and there were faces in the dark doorways and faces above in the windows and they came out to look at me, all of them, a kid with his hands tied being walked through by armed men, and that was a normal enough thing that nobody was alarmed by it, they were just curious, and what they were curious about was me.

A woman pointed at my feet and said something to another woman and they both looked at my boots. A boy ran alongside us for a while at a careful distance, staring, until a man cuffed him out of the way. Somebody laughed. Somebody else, an old man sitting on a step shelling something into his lap, looked at me for a long flat second and then deliberately looked away and spat, and that one I felt in my stomach, because there was a judgment in it I couldn't read and couldn't argue with. I was the strange thing. I was the thing that was wrong in the picture. Every single person on that lane knew how the world worked and I was the only one who didn't, and they could all tell.

I kept my eyes down after the old man, mostly. The ground was its own education. Mud, straw, a dropped fish head, a dead thing flattened that had been a rat, a gutter running down the center of the lane that I do not want to describe and that I stepped over without anyone telling me to because some things you just know. My boots, my good steel-toes, were already the color of the lane to the ankle.

The building they put me in was stone at the base and brick above, and the cell was a room off a yard, low, with one window high up and barred, real bars, iron, and a door of heavy planks with iron bands. So they had iron. They had iron for the door and rope for me, and I spent a while sitting on the dirt floor working out what that meant and the answer was that I didn't rate iron, I rated rope, and that was either good news or it meant they hadn't decided what I was yet, and I went back and forth on which.

They left my hands tied. The leader stood in the doorway after the others had gone and looked at me for a while, and I looked back, and I tried, I really tried, I said "Daniel." I tapped my own chest with my bound hands as best I could and I said it slow. "Dan-iel." He watched my mouth. Then he said something, four or five words, none of which I had, and he waited, and I had to do the head shake again, and whatever patience had been in his face packed up and left. He pulled the door shut. A bar dropped on the outside, a heavy wooden clunk that I felt in the floor.

I sat in the half-dark and shook for a while. I'm not going to pretend I didn't.

When it passed, or when it leveled off into a background hum that I could think over, I did the thing I do, the only thing, I started counting. The window was maybe a foot square. The room was maybe eight feet by ten, pacing it heel to toe with my hands tied, which is a stupid-looking way to measure a cell. The light coming down the high window had moved a hand's width along the far wall in the time I'd been in here. I could time the day off that wall if I had to. I didn't have to.

I had a clock.

I'd been so careful not to think about it. The whole walk down I'd had it pressed against my thigh through the denim and I'd been aware of it the entire time the way you're aware of a sunburn, and now I was alone behind a barred door and I got it out of my pocket, which is harder than it sounds with your wrists lashed together, a whole contorted business of fishing two-handed.

It woke up. Of course it woke up. Ninety-eight percent.

I almost called my mom again. My thumb was already moving and I caught it, physically stopped my own hand, because I'd watched the call die twice already and I knew it would die a third time and I knew something else now that I hadn't really let myself know up on the hill, which was that the battery was the whole game. The battery was the only piece of the future I'd brought with me that still worked, and it was a finite thing, it was draining whether I used it or not, and every dumb panic call to a tower that wasn't going to be built for nineteen hundred years was a piece of it I'd never get back.

I turned the brightness all the way down. I turned off the radios, all of them, airplane mode, which was a joke, an actual joke that I made to myself in the cell, airplane mode in 98 AD, and there was no one to tell it to and I think that was the first time the real shape of it landed on me, not the fear, the alone. I had a funny thing and the funny thing died in my chest because there was nobody.

Then I opened Notes and I started writing, fast, because the funny thing dying had scared me worse than the spears had.

I wrote like the building was on fire. I didn't organize it. I told myself I'd organize it later and later never came the way I meant it to. I wrote down the date the phone thought it was, which was wrong, which was my world's date, and then I wrote ROME 98 AD?? with the question marks because the Colosseum math was the only date I trusted and I trusted it about eighty percent. I wrote the things I was afraid I'd forget, and what scared me was how fast that list got long and how shallow each thing on it was. I knew water boiled at 212 and I wrote it. I knew it boiled lower up a mountain and I could not for the life of me sitting in that cell remember the rule for how much lower, I could see the shape of the graph in my head and the number wouldn't come, and I wrote down can't remember which is its own kind of horror. I wrote pi to the digits I had, 3.14159, and stalled, and wrote 26 and wasn't sure about the 26. I wrote the quadratic formula and got the sign wrong and fixed it and wasn't sure I'd fixed it right. I wrote GUNPOWDER and then sulfur, charcoal, and the third one, the white one, and I knew there was a third one and I knew it was the important one and I sat there with my thumbs over the glass and could not produce it. I wrote ??? and moved on, furious, because I could feel the percent ticking even with the screen dimmed, even though it wasn't, not really, not in a single session, but in my head every second of light was a coin I was burning.

I wrote down names. My mom's number. Our address. The fact of Marcus and the flickering light over the fastener aisle, which I will never understand why I wrote, except that it was real and mine and nobody could take it if it was on the glass. I wrote down that the President was, and stopped, because who was I writing it for.

I was writing it for whoever found my body. That was the answer and I knew it as soon as I asked, and I had to put the phone face down on my knee for a minute.

Then I picked it back up, because there was one more thing.

I took a picture of my own face.

I don't know exactly why. I held the phone out at arm's length, both hands, the front camera, and there I was, this kid, hair stuck to my forehead, dirt on me, eyes too wide, the high barred window a gray smear behind my head, and I looked at myself looking at myself and I took the picture, and the fake shutter clicked in the stone room, and now there was a record that on this day a person named Daniel Mercer who knew the quadratic formula and could not remember saltpeter was alive and seventeen and scared in a cell outside Rome. Two photos on the phone now. The white building and my face. The proof and the proven.

I turned it off all the way, holding the button, watching it go black, and that took a real effort of will, like letting go of a hand.

Now the hard one.

Because they were going to want to know what I was. The leader had asked me already, four or five words, and somebody bigger than him was going to ask again, and at some point the asking was going to stop being polite, and I was going to have to be something. I lay on my back on the dirt with my tied hands on my chest and I worked the problem the way I'd work any problem, which is to say badly and in circles, but I worked it.

Option one. The truth. I'm from the future. I come from two thousand years from now and I know how your story ends and I can read and I can do things you can't. I lay there and I ran it forward like a chess line and every branch ended badly. There was no way to prove it. Not one thing on me proved it. The phone proved I had a strange object, and a strange object that lights up and is never wrong about the hour and shows little pictures, in a world I already knew did augury off bird flight and read the future in animal guts, that did not say "scholar," that said something with a worse word attached, something you burn or stone or feed to the thing in the round building. The truth was unprovable and it was mad and it was the kind of mad that gets you killed by frightened people, and I was surrounded by people I had frightened just by walking down a lane. I crossed off the truth.

Option two. Lie. Be from somewhere. Somewhere far enough that no one could check, far enough to explain the clothes and the not-talking and the strangeness, somewhere that made me odd instead of damned. A traveler. Better, a scholar, because a scholar is a man with value and reasons, a man you keep and feed and ask things of, instead of a vagrant you throw away. From the edge of the world. From up north, way up, past wherever north ran out for these people, a cold far place at the end of the map where the clothes are fine and strange and the men know numbers. I didn't have the word Thule yet. I want to be honest, because the rules of this telling are honesty, I did not lie there in the dark and pick the perfect cover story like a spy. I lay there and got as far as I am from very far away and I am learned and I am worth keeping alive, and the rest of it, the name of the cold place and how to sell it, that came later and it came hard, with help, and it cost me. But the spine of it I decided on the dirt floor of that cell on the first day, which was this: never the future, never the truth, only the far country. A man from a far country can be ignorant of your language and your manners and still be a man. A man from the future is a horror or a god, and both of those die young.

I'd just gotten that far when the hunger arrived, and it arrived like a separate event, a fist closing slowly under my ribs, and I realized I had not eaten since the night before in a world that no longer existed. The hunger and the needing-to-pee and the thirst all showed up around the same time, the body keeping its own schedule, indifferent to the fact that everything else was over. There was a clay pot in the corner that I'd been ignoring and I understood, with great dignity, what the clay pot was for.

The light on the wall went orange and then gray and then it was just the square of the window, paler than the dark around it, and I sat against the cold stone with my knees up and my tied hands hanging off them and waited for nothing in particular, because waiting was the only verb I had left.

The bar on the door lifted near full dark.

A man came in carrying a small clay lamp and a bowl, and he was younger than the others, or harder-used in a different way, with a soldier's build and a soldier's economy of movement, and he had a strip of something dark, leather or braid, on his shoulder that the spear-carriers hadn't had, which I took, correctly as it turned out, to mean he ranked them. He set the lamp in a niche. He crouched down in front of me, balanced on the balls of his feet, and looked at me with a directness that wasn't kind exactly but wasn't cruel either, the look you give a horse you've been told to evaluate, and he held out the bowl.

It was warm. It was some kind of grain mush, gray, with the sour-sharp reek of the fish sauce cut into it, and it was, in that moment, the most beautiful object I have ever been handed. I went to take it and couldn't, my hands tied, and I made a fumbling useless grab and the bowl tipped and he caught it before it spilled, fast, no fuss, and he looked at my wrists, and then he looked at my face for a long moment, working something out.

He took a short knife off his belt. I went very still. He turned it and put the edge against the rope, not my skin, between my wrists, and sawed through it in two strokes, and the rope fell off and my hands came apart and the blood went back into them in a hot pins-and-needles rush that made me hiss.

He put the bowl in my hands. He stood up, picked up the lamp, and at the door he stopped and pointed at his own chest with two fingers and said a word, slow and clear, the way I'd tried to say my own name to the other one and failed. He said it again. Two syllables, the second one falling. Then he tipped his head at me, waiting, and I understood, and I tapped my chest with a hand that didn't fully work yet and said, "Daniel," and he tried it back, mangled, the middle of it gone soft and wrong, and something in his mouth almost moved toward a smile and didn't get there.

He took the lamp with him. The bar dropped. I sat in the dark with the warm bowl in both my freed hands and ate the worst food of my life with my fingers, fast, scraping the clay, and I licked the bowl after, and I was not ashamed.
