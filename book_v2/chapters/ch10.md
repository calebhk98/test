# Chapter 10: The Workshop

The yard came with a smell before it came with anything else.

Macer's man walked me down to the wharf on a wet gray morning at the back end of the year, the river running fat and brown past the timber rafts, and the smell got into the back of my throat from fifty paces off and stayed there for the next year of my life. Wet pine. River mud. The cold tar they swabbed on the rafts to keep them from drinking the Tiber. Under that the tannery two lots up, which is a smell I will not put on paper because it would ruin the paper, and under that the general perfume of a city of a million people who all use the same river to drink from and to throw things into. I learned to stop noticing it inside a week. That is its own kind of horror, when I think about it.

The yard itself was better than I had any right to expect, which I have learned is exactly how you should feel right before you understand the price. A wall of mortared rubble higher than my head on three sides, the river side closed with a plank fence and a gate that barred from inside, a long open-fronted shed roofed in old tile with the gaps stuffed with rag, a smaller lockable room at the back that had been a tally-clerk's office and still had the pigeonholes, a yard of beaten earth that turned to soup when it rained, and a well. My own well. I stood and looked at the well for a while like an idiot. After a year of begging coals and water off a kitchen woman who threw spoons at me, a well I was allowed to draw from felt like being handed a country.

There was a man at the gate when we came. A guard, leaning on a stick that was a stick the way a dog is a friendly animal, with a short blade on his hip that he wore like he'd used it. He looked at me the way the broad men looked at me. Macer's man said a name at him and he stood aside, and I understood that the wall I had been glad of went both ways, and that I was now the most expensive thing inside it, which is not the same as being a guest.

Tyche came down the next morning with the other one.

I had asked the steward, on the tablet, in my careful broken way, for the two hands I'd been promised, and I had spent the night lying on a straw pallet in the tally-room imagining a couple of strong patient apprentices — the kind you get in the version of this where I am a clever man building a clever thing. What came through the gate was a girl of fourteen with her hair hacked off short and a face that had already decided not to be surprised by anything I did, and behind her a man.

The man's name was Pamphilus. He was maybe thirty, maybe forty, you couldn't tell, with the heavy stooped shoulders of someone who had carried things for a living since he could walk, and an iron collar.

The collar stopped me, even though it shouldn't have, even though I had filed that whole account under things-I-cannot-do-anything-about and told myself the filing was necessary and not a choice. I had seen collars. You cannot walk a hundred paces in Rome without seeing collars, the thin ones riveted shut with a little bronze plate that says, in letters, hold me I have run, return me to my master for a reward. By the back end of that year I had stopped flinching at them the way I'd stopped smelling the river. But Pamphilus stood in my yard with his collar and his hands hanging and waited to be told what to do with his day and his back and his life, and the steward's note said, in effect, here are your tools, and I stood there with my own neck bare only because nobody had bothered to put metal on it, and I could not file it.

"Take that off him," I said.

Tyche looked at me. The gate-guard looked at me. Pamphilus did not look at me, because looking at me was not his to do, and I watched him decide whether I was the kind of madman who got slaves beaten with talk like that.

"I can't," Tyche said. Flat. City-quick, no slowing it for the foreigner, which I'd already learned was how she told you she respected you and how she told you she didn't, both at once. "It's not yours to take off. It's the master's collar. He had it riveted." She let that land. "You take it off him, you've broken the master's thing. They'll think he ran and you helped."

She was right and I knew she was right. The collar was a piece of Macer's property fastened around another piece of Macer's property, and I was a third piece, and pieces do not unfasten one another. I had asked for free hands. I had been clear about it, as clear as my Latin let me be, the day of the deal: I'd rather pay men. Macer had looked at me the flat way he had and said slaves don't cost wages and slaves don't quit and slaves don't talk, and he'd sent me Pamphilus, who cost nothing and would not quit and could not talk, to make the point in the cheapest available way.

So I did the only thing the situation left me, which was nothing, which was small.

"Then sit," I said to Pamphilus, and pointed at a bench, and made my voice ordinary. "You'll cut cloth. You eat when I eat." I said the last part loud enough for the gate-guard to carry it back. It was not a kindness anyone would write down. It was the only lever I had and I pulled it, and Pamphilus sat on the bench and looked at his hands like a man waiting for the joke.

---

The work, at least, I could do.

Or rather, the work I could fail at on a much grander scale than before, which is what money buys you. I had cloth now, real close-woven linen by the bolt, as much as I could ruin, and I ruined a lot of it. The first show bag for Macer, the one he wanted to wave at senators' freedmen, taught me what I already knew and had been hoping was a lie: that everything I'd solved at small scale came undone at big scale. A bag twice as tall holds eight times the air and weighs four times as much and leaks along eight times the seam, and the heat to fill it wants a fire that wants to eat the bag. I burned one. Not badly, a corner, a brown blossom up one panel before Tyche beat it out with a wet sack, but I stood there afterward with my heart going and understood I was going to be fighting fire for the rest of my life.

What changed was the hands. I'd never had hands before. I'd been the hands.

Pamphilus, it turned out, could cut cloth like a tailor once you drew him the line, slow and dead straight, never wasting a thumb's width, the patience of a man who has nothing waiting for him at the end of the day worth hurrying toward. And the stitching I gave to free women.

That was the one piece of the arrangement I could buy. The *peculium*, the money-jar that was mine and not mine depending on the day, had coins in it now, Macer's coins that I was permitted to spend as if my hand were his hand. And Macer permitted piecework. He permitted it because it was cheaper than I expected, because freedwomen in the wharf insulae would stitch a seam for a few asses and feed their kids on it and ask no questions about why a foreigner wanted forty feet of doubled grease-sealed seam, and because a sewn seam from a woman who'd sewn sails was worth three of mine. I told myself I preferred free hands for the quality. That was half of it.

The other half I am putting down here honestly: I walked up the lane on pay-mornings with the jar and counted coins into the palms of women who could leave when they wanted to, and I paid them and they nodded and went, and that was the whole transaction. Four women and a wharf, and a slave on a collar cutting cloth at the bench, and the slave was free of charge and the women were not, and every accountant Macer owned could have told me which way that arithmetic bent in the long run. It bent the wrong way. It bends the wrong way still. That is all of it and I am not going to dress it up.

---

The numbers were the thing that spread, and even the numbers spread like cold honey.

Tyche I noticed by accident, which is how you notice the things that matter. I had her keeping the cloth tally, because someone had to, and because I'd shown her the new figures in an idle hour, a card trick, something to do. Ten marks, a sign for nothing, and the place a mark stood in told you what it was worth. I'd done it slowly, with my bad Latin, expecting to do it four times.

I did it once.

I came back from the lane the third pay-morning to find she'd taken the day's piecework, four women, different seam-lengths, different rates, an advance to one of them against next week, a bolt and a third of linen drawn against the steward's tablet, and she'd laid it all out down a wax tablet in a column, every figure in the new hand, carried clean, the asses summing to a denarius and the denarius carried up into its own column, and a remainder set off to the side with the little *r* I'd shown her once, in passing. She'd got the long division. The thing I'd flubbed in front of Heras and had to grind out by hand with my ears burning. She'd watched me do it slow and wrong and slow and right, exactly the one time, and she'd kept it.

"You checked it?" I said, because I am a coward and could not say the true thing, which was something closer to fear.

"Twice," she said. She did not look up. "The Helena one's short. She'll say you owe her two more asses on the long seam and she'll be lying, it's here." She turned the tablet so the column faced me. The two asses sat in their place, accounted, judged. "She does it to the new clerks."

I stood there in my own yard and looked at a fourteen-year-old slave who had taught herself in a week the thing that took the whole Roman accounting profession a generation, and I felt two things at exactly the same time, stacked, both real.

The first was the thing you'd feel. Look at this. Look at what's in there.

The second was Macer's voice from the day of the deal, dry as a ledger: teach the clever one anything costly and I'll sell her, because a skill is worth money and money walks. He'd said it flatly, like a weather report. And I understood, standing there, that the surest way to get Tyche sold down the river was to do exactly the thing I most wanted to do, which was hand her the whole ladder, every rung, and watch her go up it. A bright mind in a body that the law called a thing. The value I could put on her by teaching her was a price somebody could pay.

So I took the tablet back and said, "Good. Do the next one alone," and I kept my face shut, and that was the first lie I told to protect her instead of myself, and not the last.

---

Macer's accountants did not learn it in a week. Macer's accountants did not want to learn it at all.

He sent them to me in the cold weeks after the new year, because the new figures were the part of me he could sell to other men without anyone catching fire. There were three of them, freedmen, gray men who ran his contracts, and the senior one was named Onesimus and he had been adding sums on an abacus since before I was a smudge in the future, and he sat across the steward's old table from me with his counting-board in front of him like a shield.

I showed them the trick I'd shown Tyche. Place value. The zero. A long multiplication done down a column in a quarter of the strokes the board took, no beads, no sliding, the working left on the wax for anyone to check after, which the board never left, because the board forgets the instant your thumb moves.

Onesimus did the sum on his board faster than I did it on the wax. That was the first thing that happened, and I should have seen it coming. He'd been doing it for forty years. His thumb was faster than my pen. He sat back and glanced at the two young clerks with him as if to say, see.

But the working stayed on my wax. And one of the young ones, a thin nervous boy, leaned over it after, when Onesimus had gone to relieve himself, and put his finger on the carried figures and traced them up the column, and I watched him see the thing Onesimus would not: that the board gives you an answer and the new hand gives you an answer and a witness. You can find where you went wrong. You can hand the sheet to another man and he can find where you went wrong. In a contracting house that lives and dies on whether the grain tally matches the wagon tally, a number you can audit is worth more than a number you got fast.

The boy took to it. The other young one took to it slower, when he saw the first one getting trusted with the checking. Onesimus never did. Onesimus kept his board till he died, I heard later, and trained two more boys on it on his own time out of spite, and the house ran both systems side by side for years, the new figures creeping in at the bottom where the young men were and the old numerals keeping the top of every contract because a Roman document with Thule-marks on its face looked like a foreigner's joke and no senator would sign it.

Patchy. The honest word is patchy.

---

The plow went out to Macer's land the same winter and I never saw it work. I gave the smith a drawing, a moldboard to turn the soil over instead of just scratching it, and a scheme for resting fields in a rotation with beans to put the heart back in the dirt, and Macer's bailiff took it up-country on a mule and I got, two months later, a single line back through the steward: the soil there was thin, the new plow pulled heavy, and the beans were beans, they already grew beans. Maybe it helped. Maybe in three years a field somewhere yielded a little more and no one thought of me at all, which is the best a thing like that can hope for. You don't get to watch the field.

---

The blocks I started in the dark months, and the blocks half-worked, which felt, after the plow, like a triumph.

I had the idea whole, which was the rare thing. I had seen presses. The whole city was presses, olive and wine, a great screw biting a flat bed down onto a thing to squeeze it. And I had, in the wharf lanes, men who carved, sign-cutters and seal-cutters and a Greek who cut letters into stone for tombs, men whose whole craft was making a shape backward in a hard surface. Put the two together and you have the thing the West would not get for thirteen more centuries and I knew it and I could not quite get it to work, which is the story of my entire life here in one sentence.

The first block was a disaster. We cut a line of Greek, a single line, a tag of Homer the letter-cutter picked because he could do it in his sleep, into a flat of close-grained pear-wood. Inked it with the soot-and-gum ink they write with. Pressed a sheet of papyrus down by hand. Got a smear. The ink was too thin and ran in the grooves and the high parts and the low parts all printed the same gray fog. We cut it deeper. Mixed the ink thicker, more gum, less water, more soot, until it sat on the raised letters and not in the troughs. Pressed again, harder, with a board and my whole weight. Got, on the fourth try, the worst-looking line of Homer in the history of the world, gray and crooked, one letter doubled where the block had skidded, the left end fat and the right end starved because the bed wasn't true.

I have it still. I kept it. It is genuinely ugly.

It is also the same line, twice, three times, ten times, off one carved block, in the time it would take a copyist to write it once and a tenth of the cost. A copyist makes his own errors, different ones each time. This made the same error every time, which a copyist cannot do, which means you can fix it in the block and then it is fixed forever. Heras came down to the wharf to see it and held a damp gray crooked sheet up to the light and was quiet a long time, which from Heras is a whole oration, and then he said the most Heras thing he has ever said: "Now make it not ugly, and you will have done something, and not before." He was right, and it stayed ugly for years.

---

The salon was Macer's idea, which is to say it was Macer showing off the mule that talked.

A man named Mettius, an equestrian with money and a wife with ambitions, kept a house on the good side of the hill where men of letters came to recite and be admired, and Macer got me an evening there — lending his remarkable horse, except the horse was me and the favor was owed to Macer afterward. He had Tyche scrub me and put me in a clean tunic that was almost the right length, and he came himself, in his good wool with no stripe, to stand at the back with his arms folded and watch his investment perform.

I will set down how it felt to walk into that room, because it is the loneliest I have been in Rome and I have been very lonely here. Warm. Lamp-lit, the good oil that doesn't smoke, couches, a floor with a sea-creature done in little stones, men reclining with watered wine in cups worth more than my yard, the talk going in two languages over my head like birds I could see but not catch. My Latin had gotten good enough to order cloth and pay women and curse at a fire. It was nowhere near good enough for this, for the allusions, the half-lines of poetry everyone finished in their heads, the Greek tags dropped in to show they could. I stood in a warm bright room full of the cleverest men in the world and had never in my life had less to say.

Then a man unfolded himself off a couch to look at me, and I met Lucius Vettius Crispus, and the loneliness got worse and also, for the first time that evening, interesting.

He was around forty, beautifully kept, the kind of trim that takes a slave an hour every morning, his hair oiled into careful waves, his tunic the soft white of cloth that has never carried anything. He held his wine without drinking it. He looked at me like a collector appraising a piece another collector has bragged about, already deciding it's a fake.

"So this is the Hyperborean," he said, to the room, not to me. He gave the word a little spin, the old poets' word for the people beyond the north wind, and got a small laugh, because of course they'd all read the poets and of course I hadn't. "Macer's marvel. The scholar from Thule." He said Thule at arm's length, two fingers. "Tell me, friend. You'll forgive an old reader his pedantry." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm, practiced, the smile of a man who has never once meant it. "Pytheas of Massalia claimed to have sailed to Thule. Six days' sail north of Britain, he said, where the sea and the land and the air hang together in a kind of pulp, like a sea-lung, neither walkable nor sailable. You'll know the passage."

"I don't read Greek," I said. True, and the wrong thing, and I watched it please him.

"Ah." He turned a little to the couches, bringing them in, a man warming to his instrument. "He doesn't read Greek. The scholar from the land of scholars. Well. Strabo read Greek, and Strabo, who is no fool, who measured the world, calls our friend Pytheas the greatest liar who ever lived." A ripple of pleasure through the room; he rode it. "He says the man invented his Thule out of the northern fog to sell a story to the gullible at Massalia. A far country no one can reach, that conveniently no one can check." He let his eyes come back to me, soft, delighted, lethal. "And here you stand. From a country no one can reach. With marvels no one quite saw fly. Drawing seas on the far side of the world, where, how fortunate, no ship will ever go to find you out. I do admire the symmetry."

I had nothing. That is the truth of the scene and I'm not going to invent a comeback I didn't have. My Latin failed me at exactly the speed I needed it and I stood there feeling the heat come up my neck while the cleverest men in Rome enjoyed it, and Crispus watched me have nothing and his smile got wider and easier. I had just given him a gift. He'd come in ready to be threatened by Macer's famous foreigner and found a tongue-tied boy in a too-short tunic, and the relief of that turned, even as I watched, into contempt, and the contempt was already growing teeth, because a man like Crispus cannot leave a thing alone once he's decided it's beneath him and getting attention anyway.

"He flew a bag of hot air over the Subura," I said. My voice came out flat and accented and I hated it. "Four hundred men saw. A priest saw."

"I heard," Crispus said gently. "I heard it caught fire and fell on a washerwoman. The gods, perhaps, expressing an opinion of the new philosophy." More laughter, the warm easy kind, and he turned his shoulder to me, which in that room was an execution, and went back to his couch and his cup and a line of Callimachus he and another man finished together in Greek, and I was furniture again.

Across the room Macer was watching, arms folded, and his face had not moved, and I could not tell, then or after, whether he was angry that his marvel had wilted or pleased that a sharp-tongued man of letters had just spent his whole performance proving Macer owned something worth attacking. With Macer it was usually both.

Tyche was waiting at the gate when they walked me back down to the wharf in the dark, awake, the lamp lit, the cloth tally squared away in the new figures in a clean column on the table. She looked at my face.

"Bad?" she said.

I didn't answer her. I went to the back room and got the ugly gray line of Homer off the pigeonhole where I kept it, the crooked one, the doubled letter, the starved right end, and I sat down at the table under the lamp and started cutting a second block.
