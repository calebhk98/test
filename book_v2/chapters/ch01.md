# Chapter 1: Waking

The first thing I got wrong was thinking I'd fallen asleep on the job.

I came up out of nothing the way you do after a long shift, with my mouth dry and my neck wrong and the certainty that I'd missed something. My cheek was against the ground. The ground was cold and it had texture, little broken stalks pressing dotted lines into my skin, and I lay there working out, in a slow stupid way, that you do not fall asleep face down in a field. I had been in the stockroom. I had been counting deck screws into the little paper sacks because the scale at register three had been throwing tantrums all week, and I remember the buzz of the long fluorescent tube that flickered above the fastener aisle, the one that Marcus kept saying he'd replace and never did. That tube. The hum of it. I held onto the hum for a second because it was the last real thing I had, and then it was gone and there was only this.

I sat up.

My phone was in my hand. I was gripping it hard enough that my fingers had gone stiff, and the first thing I did, before I even looked around, was look at the screen, because that is what your hand does. Six fourteen. The battery was a fat green bar. Ninety-eight percent. No little dot of signal at the top left, just the empty bars and a small gray word where the carrier name should be. I remember thinking, in the flat dumb way you think before the fear arrives, that I must be way out somewhere. That somebody had driven me somewhere as a joke.

There were no roads.

I want to be careful here, because later on people would ask me about this morning and I would lie about how clear it was, how I knew right away. I did not know right away. What I knew was that the field went on. It was a harvested field, the wheat or whatever it was cut down to a stubble that came up maybe to my ankle, dry and pale gold, going on in rows to a line of trees a long way off. The rows were not straight the way our fields back home are straight. They wandered. And past the trees there were low hills the color of nothing in particular, brown going green, and over all of it the sky was doing that thing where it is the kind of blue that hurts to look at near the bottom and still gray-purple up at the top, the sun not up yet but close, lighting the edge of everything.

I stood. My knees were stiff. I had my work clothes on, all of them, which made no sense and at the same time was the only thing that did, because they were mine and they were familiar and I could touch them. Jeans. My gray T-shirt with the little hole at the collar. My work boots, the steel-toes, the brown leather ones I'd bought myself with the first paycheck so my feet would stop dying on the concrete floor, and I was even wearing the cheap nylon belt I hated. My keys were in my pocket. House key, the spare to my mom's Corolla, the brass one to the store's back door that I wasn't even supposed to have a copy of. They jingled. Such a small, ordinary, idiotic sound.

"Okay," I said out loud, to the field. My voice came out cracked. "Okay."

It smelled wrong. I think that was the first thing my body understood before my head did, because I had been standing there a while turning in a slow circle and trying to find a fence or a power line or the back of a barn, and underneath the dry-grass smell and the dirt there was something thick and low and animal, woodsmoke and something rotten and sweet at once, like a dumpster behind a restaurant in August, only spread out thin over everything. I had never smelled air with that in it. The air at home had a smell, sure, exhaust and cut grass and whatever, but it had a kind of nothing under it, a clean nothing, the smell of a planet with the windows open. This air was full. There was no clean nothing anywhere in it.

I told myself: you fainted. You hit your head. You are confused. I had read somewhere that a concussion can scramble you, can lose you a chunk of time, and I held onto that, I really did. I pressed my fingers around the back of my skull looking for a sore spot, a lump, blood, anything, and there was nothing. My head didn't even hurt. I was just standing in a field that went on forever, in my boots, at six fourteen in the morning, and the sun had not come up, and there were no roads, and the air was full.

Birds started.

That was the thing that made my chest go tight, and I couldn't have told you why at the time. Birds are birds. But these were not the birds from home. I don't mean I'm some birdwatcher who can tell a sparrow from a finch. I mean the sound was wrong in a way I felt in my teeth, too many of them, too loud, a racket coming up out of the trees all at once like somebody had thrown a switch, and not one of them was the flat two-note thing the mourning doves do on our gutter. I stood very still and listened to a thousand birds I did not know and I thought, with great calm, the way you think a thing right before you lose it: I am not where I was.

I started walking because standing was worse.

I picked the direction where the smoke smell was strongest, because smoke meant people and people meant a phone that worked or a road or a cop or my mom, somebody, anybody. The walking helped. I'm okay at staying calm if I have something to do with my hands or my feet, and I made myself a job: count steps, watch the ground, look for anything made by a person. The stubble crunched. After a while the field gave out into a strip of weeds and then a path, and the path was real, packed dirt with a rut worn down the middle and dried mud humped up on either side, the kind of path feet make, not machines. No tire tracks. I looked for tire tracks for a long time. There were ruts but they were the wrong width and there were no treads in them, just the smooth scoop a wheel makes, and beside them, pressed into the old mud, hoofprints. A lot of hoofprints.

I checked the phone again. Six twenty-six. I don't know what I expected. The signal was still the empty bars. I opened the dialer with my thumb shaking and I hit nine, one, one, and the green button, and it sat there a second saying it was calling and then it just stopped, the screen going back to the keypad with a little gray line of text I didn't read. No ringing. No operator. Nothing. I did it again. Same. I told myself the tower was far, that I'd walk to higher ground, and I put the phone away because looking at it was making it worse.

The settlement came up on me slow.

First it was one building off to the side of the path, and I almost laughed, because it was so clearly a fake. Mud walls, actual mud, dried tan and cracked, and a roof of red clay tiles that didn't sit flat, and no windows, just a low doorway with a cloth hung in it. A movie set. That was the word my brain reached for and grabbed and held with both hands. A movie set. They build whole towns and leave them out in the desert. I'd seen a video about it. Somebody was filming something and I had wandered onto the back lot, that was all, and any second a guy in a vest with a headset was going to come yell at me for being where I wasn't supposed to be, and I would have given anything in the world to be yelled at right then.

Then there were more buildings, and a smell of animals so strong it stung, and the cloth in a doorway moved and a woman looked out at me.

She was old, or she looked old, with a brown cloth over her hair and a long dress the color of dirt, and her face when she saw me did a thing I have never had a face do to me before. She did not look annoyed, the way you'd look at a kid who wandered onto the set. She went still, and then she pulled the cloth across the door so fast it snapped, and she was gone, and I heard her saying something behind it, low and quick, words I didn't catch and wouldn't have understood if I had.

"Hey," I said to the closed door. "Sorry. Hey. Do you have a phone?"

Nothing.

I kept walking into the place because what else was there. It was bigger than it had looked, a cluster of these mud and rough-stone buildings strung along the widening path, a couple of them with a second story, smoke coming out of holes in some of the roofs, and there were people now, and the people were the part I couldn't make a story out of. A man leading a donkey loaded with bundles. Two kids, filthy, barefoot, who stopped dead and stared at my feet. A man squatting by a wall doing something with a knife and a piece of leather, and when I came near he stood up and stepped back and held the knife where I could see it, not pointing it at me, just letting me know it was there. They were all in the same kind of clothes. Long shirts to the knee, belted, wool I think, dyed in colors that were really just shades of the dirt, brown and rust and grayish white, and sandals or bare feet, and not one zipper, not one logo, not one stitch of anything I recognized as having been made in a factory, and I work retail, I have refolded ten thousand T-shirts, I know what machine-made cloth looks like and this was not it. It was thick and uneven and it hung wrong.

They looked at my jeans and they looked at my boots and they looked at the gray shirt, and they did the woman's thing, the going-still, the stepping-back. A man said something to me. It had the shape of a question, sharp at the end, and then a word he said twice, hard, and the rest of it was just sound, fast and full of edges, nothing in it I could grab. Not Spanish. We get a lot of Spanish at the store and I can fake my way through a little and this was not it, not French from two years of it in school, not anything. I held up my hands the way you do, palms out, I come in peace, and I said, "I don't, I'm sorry, I don't understand, is there, do you have a phone? A telephone? Police?"

His face didn't change. Whatever I was making with my mouth was just more noise to him, the same as his was to me, and I felt the floor of it open up a little right then, the realness of it, that I could stand a foot from another human being and we were as cut off from each other as two animals.

I tried again, slower, louder, which is the stupidest thing you can do and I knew it even while I did it. "Help. I need help." I pointed at myself, at the ground, I mimed holding a phone to my ear. He took another step back and put his hand on a kid's shoulder and moved the kid behind him.

There was a thing painted on the wall above a doorway, a long word in letters, and I went to it because letters meant I could maybe at least figure out where I was, read a town name, anything. The letters were ones I knew. That was the worst part. They were our letters, an R and an A and what I thought was an N, capital, scratched on red, and I stood there and read them left to right and they did not make a word. They were letters and they were arranged like a word and they meant nothing, and I read them four or five times like the meaning would turn on if I just kept hitting the switch, and it didn't, and a feeling came up the back of my neck like cold water.

I needed to get higher. That was the plan, the only plan, get up high, find the signal, find the highway, see the city. The path ran on toward where the ground rose and I went that way fast, not quite running, past more of the still faces, past a pen of actual pigs, the pig smell hitting me like a wall, past a cart with wheels of solid wood and a wooden axle, no metal on it anywhere that I could see, and I got to the top of the little rise where the buildings thinned out and the land fell away in front of me into a long shallow valley, and I stopped.

There was a city.

It was a long way off, sitting in the haze at the bottom of the valley and up over the next hills, and it was big, bigger than it had any right to be out here in the middle of all this dirt and these mud huts, a smear of pale stone going on and on with smoke standing up over it in a hundred thin gray lines, and I felt a flood of relief so strong it almost put me on the ground, because a city meant everything, a city meant streets and signs and a hospital and a working phone, and I started down the path toward it and I was already half composing the thing I'd say to the first cop I found.

And then I saw the building on the near edge of it.

I want to be honest about what happened in my head because for a while nothing happened. I looked at it the way you look at anything. It was round. It was huge, even from here, a pale ring of stone sitting a little apart from the rest of the city, arches stacked in rows up its side, and it was clean, it was bright, the morning sun was just catching the top of it and the stone was so pale it was almost white and it had no cracks in it, no holes, no green stains, no scaffolding, it was whole and it was new and it was the most enormous thing made by people that I had ever seen with my own eyes. I had seen a picture of a building like that. I had seen a thousand pictures of a building like that, in a textbook, on a screen, on the side of a pizza box one time. Everybody has. And in every single one of them it was broken. It was a ruin, a ring of stumps, half of it fallen, the whole top gone, that's the only way it ever is, that's the only way it has been for longer than there has been a country I'm from.

And this one was whole.

I stood on the path with the wind moving the dry grass around my boots and I looked at the unbroken Colosseum and the bottom went out of everything.

I don't know how long I stood there. My knees did go, partway, and I ended up crouched on the path with my arms around them and my breath coming wrong, fast and shallow, the edges of my sight going sparkly the way they do when you stand up too quick except I was already down. I kept saying no. Just no, out loud, over and over, a little kid's word, the only one I had. Because there were explanations. There are always explanations. They built a new one. They built a fake one. It's a museum, a theme park, they reconstructed it, people do that, there's a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas, this is fine, this is a thing people do. But the mud huts. But the cloth. But the letters that weren't words and the man who put his kid behind him and the pigs and the cart with the wooden axle and the air that was full and the birds I didn't know and the path with no tire tracks, none, not one, in the whole world that I could see, not a single straight line that a machine had made except the lines on the screen in my hand.

The phone.

I got it out. My hands were doing something I couldn't fully control, a fine fast shake, and I had to try twice to wake the screen. Six fifty-one. Ninety-eight percent, the green bar still fat and smug, the empty signal bars at the corner. I opened the camera because some animal part of me needed proof, needed to make the building be a real thing that a real machine had recorded, and I pointed it down the valley and there it was on the screen, the round white building, sitting in the little glass rectangle in my hand exactly the same as it sat in the world, and I took the picture and the fake shutter clicked and I had it now, I had a photo of the thing, and that did not help at all. It made it worse. The photo made it a fact.

I opened the maps. I knew it wouldn't work and I did it anyway. The little blue dot spun and spun looking for itself and the map underneath was just the gray grid it goes to when it has nothing, the rivers and roads it had bothered to save sitting there with no me on them, a memory of a world the phone could no longer find. Searching. Searching. I closed it. I opened the dialer and I called nine one one again, knowing, and it died the same as before, and I called my mom, my thumb finding her without me even looking because I have called my mom from that screen ten thousand times, and the screen said calling and then it said nothing and the keypad came back and I sat down hard in the dirt and made a sound I'm not proud of.

I'm good at math. I know that sounds like a strange thing to reach for sitting in the dirt outside a city that couldn't exist, but it's true, it's the one thing I've always had, and when everything else in me was screaming my brain went to numbers the way it always does, because numbers hold still. I started measuring. The building down there, I told myself, look at it, estimate it, you can estimate anything. Those arches, count them across the part you can see, the curve, if that's the curve of a circle then the diameter, the thing has to be six hundred feet across at least, more, and the height, four rows of arches, call it forty feet a row with the stuff on top, that's a hundred sixty, hundred eighty feet of stone, and you cannot fake that, you cannot build that for a movie, that is a real building of real stone holding up its own real weight and it is standing there finished and clean in the morning sun, and the only place in the entire history of the world where that building stands clean and finished and new is a place that stopped being two thousand years ago.

Two thousand years. The number sat in front of me. I could not make it mean anything. I tried to and it just sat there, too big, the way a number gets when it stops being a count of anything and turns into a wall.

The sun came up over the far hills while I was doing the math, full up, and the light went from gray to gold and the unbroken building threw a long blue shadow across the valley, and somewhere behind me in the settlement a dog started barking, and then another, and there were voices, men's voices, more than one, coming up the path the way I'd come, fast and getting louder, and the word I'd heard the man say twice, the hard one, I heard it again now, called out, an order in it.

I got up. My legs were jelly and I got up anyway and I turned around.

There were four of them coming up the rise toward me, men in the dirt-colored shirts but with more to them, leather over the chest on two of them, and they had spears. Real ones, long, the heads of them catching the new sun in four small wet points of light, and they came at a walk, spread out a little so they had me from more than one side, and the one in front had his arm out flat toward the others, holding them at a distance from me, watching my hands, watching my feet, watching the strange bright cloth of me like I was something that might go off.

I put the phone in my pocket. I put my hands up, palms out, the only word I had in any language they could possibly want.
