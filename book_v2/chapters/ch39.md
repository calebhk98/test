# Chapter 39: Institutions

A man cannot leave money to a press. I learned this from Marcia in the storeroom, the way I learned most law, by proposing something stupid and watching her decide whether it was worth the breath to tell me why.

I had said I would put the press in my will, leave it Eros's wages and a sum to cut new blocks, and let it run. She asked who received the bequest. The press, I said. She said a press is a thing, like a hammer, and you do not leave a hammer money, you leave it to a man, and the man sells it when his son wants the timber for a boat. A will moves things to people. It does not make a thing that holds itself.

"Then how does a thing hold itself," I said.

"It doesn't. People hold it. You want a thing that goes on when the people who care about it are dead, and the law was not written by men who wanted that. It was written to move a dead man's property to his living kin without a fight." She pulled a fresh tablet toward her. "So we do not give the law a thing it hates. We give it one it knows. A burial club."

I did not follow, and said so.

"A man dies," she said, "and his trade fellows want him buried decent and his name said once a year over wine, so they make a *collegium*. They pay in monthly. They own a strongbox and a meeting room and a slave to sweep it, and they take in new members as the old ones die, and the thing goes on a hundred years burying men who weren't born when it started. The law likes a funeral. Nobody fears a funeral club." She tapped the wax. "A club can hold property. A club can take a gift. A club can pay wages out of rents and never die, because it is not a man. We will build a society of men devoted to printing, with you as their dead founder before you are even dead, and the press is the thing the society keeps, the way a burial club keeps the cup it drinks from."

"And I'm the corpse they toast."

"You're the corpse who left them a quarry's worth of pearwood," she said, "so they'll toast you sincerely a while."

---

It took the better part of a year, and most of it was not mine. I stood beside the thing while Marcia made it, because the work had stopped being mine to make and I had not stopped reaching for the tools.

She found the form in an old guild of letter-copyers, a sleepy society of a dozen freedmen who met four times a year, drank bad wine, and copied wills and petitions for the courts at fixed rates. They had a charter, a strongbox, two rooms behind a fuller's, and a god, which mattered. Marcia bought their goodwill with a hall of their own and a promise that the new craft would not undercut their court work, and folded the press into them as a new branch, the cutters and pressmen and inkers entering as members who paid their monthly coin and drew their wages from the society's rents and the sale of sheets. I endowed it. That dry word covered an hour in a notary's room while a man read out in formula-Latin that Marcus Ulpius Danihel conveyed to the society a half-share in the rents of two wharf-front shops, in perpetuity, the income applied to the wages of the printing-branch, the cutting of blocks, and the teaching of apprentices, and that I reserved no power to revoke it. That last clause was the whole point. To sign away the power to take it back is to admit, on a tablet, before a notary who does not care, that you have begun to plan a world you are not in.

I signed it. Eros witnessed it, because the law wanted citizen witnesses and he had been one since before I was, and when the notary finished he said, dry, that he had cut blocks for twenty years for a man who could be sold, and now he was a brick in a building, and he wasn't sure which of us had come up in the world. Then he asked, because Eros never let a sentiment stand without putting a tool in its hand, who would teach the boy.

---

There was no boy. That was the hole in the whole structure, the one Marcia had named in the storeroom.

Eros cut blocks. He had cut every block we ever printed from, and he cut them the way an old man does a thing he has done forty thousand times, well and slowly, his eyes worse than mine from twenty years of pearwood by lamplight, holding the gouge at the length of his arm now and feeling the line as much as seeing it. The second pressman could pull sheets. He could not cut. Cutting a block in reverse, a whole page of letters backward and clean so they print forward, is a thing you learn in years or not at all, and we had no one learning it, because I had never made it anyone's livelihood to spend three years cutting spoiled blocks before he cut a good one. I had needed Eros and I had had Eros, and a man who has what he needs does not train the man who will need it after him.

We took an apprentice the autumn the charter was sealed. His name was Vitalis, and he was twelve, a wood-carver's son from the Subura, freeborn, his father dead of the autumn fever, his mother glad of a place that fed him. He came with his own small knives wrapped in a rag and a way of holding wood up to the light to read the grain before he set the edge to it, which his father had taught him, and which Eros watched once and grunted at, and that grunt was the boy hired.

The bargain the society made with him was the thing I had not understood I was building until I watched it signed. Three years bound, fed and housed and taught, no wage; then journeyman's wages and a member's share; and a clause that he would in his turn teach two before he drew his pension from the funeral fund. He was bound to the society, which would outlast me, feed him when his eyes went the way Eros's had gone and mine were going, and which he would feed in turn. I had spent twenty years being the only man who knew how to do a thing so no one could afford to kill me. I sat in the fuller's back room and watched a freeborn boy bind himself to a club so the knowing would be in more than one pair of hands, and the cleverness I had lived by all that time turned over in my chest and lay the other way up.

The first month he ruined eleven blocks. Eros made him cut the same line of the times-table backward until the seven came clean, and the boy cut it eighty times, and on the eighty-first Eros pulled a sheet and laid it under the boy's nose and said nothing, and the boy looked at his own seven printed forward and true. I heard it from Eros that evening, the way I now heard most of what the press did, secondhand, from the men who did it.

---

The contest was harder, because the contest had a god in it, and the god had a tax man.

It ran, still, the way it had run for twenty years. I funded it from my own coin, Macer took the credit and gave the reading in his domus, and I stood as the tame barbarian judge. It was held every spring on the Quinquatrus, Minerva's festival, chosen long ago as cover, a thing crowned on the goddess of true craft so no haruspex could call it impious. And since the year they nearly broke me on the Caelian, since the white heifer and the dedication I had led with my own hand, the priestly colleges held a hook in it. A right to be consulted. A priest the colleges sent each spring, who watched the reading, took an offering, said his words over the winners before they were crowned, and was given a sum everyone called incense and everyone knew was for the colleges.

To outlive me the contest needed its own coin, the way the press now drew its wages from rents. And the moment it had its own coin it became a property with an income, and a property the priestly colleges held a standing right in was a thing they could, on a bad day, with the right enemy, claim to administer for the gods. Endow it as we had the press, Marcia said, and we would have built the colleges a permanent revenue and handed them, in the same act, the argument for taking it.

"So we don't endow it," I said.

"So we endow it carefully," she said, "and we feed the priest before he is hungry."

What she built I would not have thought of in ten years, and a Roman widow who had fought her husband's cousins for her own warehouse thought of in an afternoon. She did not endow the contest. She endowed Minerva. A sum was settled, through the same society dressed for the purpose, as a perpetual gift to the goddess for the keeping of her spring rite, the cleaning of a small shrine of hers on the Aventine, the incense, the priest's portion. And bound into the deed, as the founder's wish for how the gift be administered, was the contest: the prize said yearly on her festival, the winners blessed by her priest, the whole machine of it folded inside a pious endowment as the works a temple does with a temple's money. The colleges got their revenue named and fixed and theirs, which made them the contest's protectors against any later priest who might meddle, because you do not let another man tax what is already taxed to you. And the contest got walls. It became a rite, and a rite is harder to abolish in Rome than a funeral.

It cost more than funding it from my purse ever had. I said as much, that we had taken a thing I paid for out of pocket and built it a temple's overhead, a priest's stipend, incense by the pound forever.

"You paid for it out of pocket," she said, "and your pocket dies. Now Minerva pays for it, and she is very old and shows no sign of dying. She is expensive and she does nothing and she will outlast every man who ever tried to break you. For a freedman with enemies and a barbarian's name, she is the cheapest senator you will ever keep."

Vibenius was four years dead by then. I do not know what he would have made of a thing built half to honor the hook he had set in me and half to slip out of it, a prayer with a clause. I had told him once, in the worst hour of my life, that building the ladder was thanking the gods in the only language that did not lie, which was work, and I had not meant a word of it, and now I had built the contest a permanent priest out of that lie made law. He had said what I did with his words was not in my hands. Here was a thing I had done with them, and no one left who could say whether it counted as the lie coming true or the truth put to use.

---

The patronage fund was Marcia's whole skill and almost nothing of mine. The idea I had carried for years. No one man can afford to fail at steel for ten years, which is precisely why no man has the steel. Hermes had chased it since before I was free, gorgeous one batch and brittle garbage the next, and every wasted batch was bread out of his own mouth, so he chased it only in the slack hours, between the plowshares and gate-bands that paid. A man who must eat cannot fail long enough to win. So build a thing that pays a man to fail, a fund to keep a craftsman at one hard problem for ten years, fed, supplied, free to ruin a hundred crucibles, asked for nothing but the trying and the writing-down of what failed.

Saying it was the easy part. Making it a thing the law would hold and a man could not pocket and walk away from took Marcia three drafts and a quarrel with a notary. A foundation in trust, the income of a settled capital tied to a purpose, administered by the same society that held the press, with a board of three who chose the craftsmen, judged the writing-down, and paid the next year's coin only against the last year's labor honestly recorded, win or fail. The capital could not be touched, only its income spent. A craftsman drew a living wage, enough to keep him at the bench and not enough to make the bench worth abandoning. And he held his post against his record of attempts, not his successes, which made the whole thing strange and made it work, because the moment you pay a man for results he stops telling you about his failures, and his failures are the rungs.

I put Hermes on it first, which was no decision, only a debt paid. He was past sixty and his shoulder had gone bad and he took the steel commission the way an old soldier takes a pension that lets him keep marching, with no thanks and much complaint about the board's record-keeping. He would chase repeatable steel until he died, and fail, and how he failed, batch by batch, the iron and the charcoal and the color of the heat his eye could no longer trust, went into a book the society kept and Tyche kept a copy of in the cipher, worth more to whoever came after than any blade Hermes ever drew gorgeous and broke in the quench.

---

I had not seen Felix in years when he came to the wharf about the fund. He had been a boy when he won the contest, a counting-house slave nineteen or twenty, sharp and starved-looking, who had written a tale of a ship that fed its own fire and drove itself across the sea on a metal that did not yet exist, and who had said, at the year's end, that he thought he could fix the pot. I had laundered his prize toward his peculium because the law would not let me simply give a slave a hundred denarii, and had not thought hard about him since.

He was forty now, near enough, broad through the chest where the boy had been all elbows, a free man six years and a bronze-caster with a furnace of his own behind the cattle market. He had bought himself out at thirty-four with the peculium the prize had helped fatten, and carried his freedom lightly, without the wound the freed-by-favor carry. He had heard, the way the cattle-market end of the river hears everything, that the Thulean's widow was paying men to fail at hard things, and walked over to ask whether the pot counted.

I asked if he still had the tale. He had burned it years ago, he said, it embarrassed him, a boy's drawing of a ship with no oars and no sail and a stack belching smoke like a kitchen. What he had not burned was the principle, that fire makes water into a wind, and a wind can push a thing, and if you could catch the wind from a boiling pot and make it shove and shove again instead of just lifting a lid, you would have a horse that ate wood and never tired. He had built a dozen pots in the years between. He showed me his hands while he said it, the old burns layered under the new, the way mine had once. The pots all tore, or the wind got out the wrong way, or the bronze went soft at the seam where the heat lived, and that last was the wall, the same wall the old smith was at. You needed a metal that held its strength when it glowed, and there was none in Rome.

"There isn't," I said. "Hermes has been failing to make it for thirty years. We're paying him to keep failing at it now."

"Then I will fail next to him," Felix said, "and use whatever he gets."

I gave him a wage, a term of years, a furnace allowance, and an order to write down every pot that tore and how. I told him, because Marcia made me say it to his face so there could be no later grievance, that the board judged him by the writing and not the working, that he would almost certainly die at this, that I had a wall I would die at too, that the steel which held its strength in the fire might not come in his grandchildren's lives, and that he was being paid, in plain words, to climb a ladder whose top neither of us would see and leave the rungs cut clean for whoever came after.

He heard all of it. Then he asked the only question that mattered to him, how many pots a year the allowance bought.

I told him. He did the sum faster than I could now, holding nothing at arm's length, his eyes still good, and said it bought enough to fail honestly, which was all he had ever wanted, that he had spent six years failing dishonestly in the slack hours, lying to himself the next pot was the one, and a man paid to say the pot tore was richer than a man who had to pretend it hadn't. Then he went off to talk to Hermes about charcoal, two men who would fail at the same wall from opposite sides for the rest of their lives, on my coin, with my blessing. The strange thing was that I envied them, watching them go, both still able to read their work close.

---

Lucanus did not want the workshop. I let myself know it the spring of the apprentice, when he was sixteen and came to the press once because I asked him to and stood in the ink-smell with his hands behind his back the way a guest stands in a temple he does not worship in. He was a good boy, freeborn, citizen-clear where I would always carry the freedman's stain, and he wanted the things the freeborn want, the law and the rolls and a seat in the basilica, to be a man who decides cases and not a man who cuts blocks. He had Marcia's flat way of pricing a room and none of her hunger for the warehouse, and he looked at the press, the engine of everything I would leave, and saw a shed full of stained men, and was too kind to say so and too honest to pretend. I did not push him. I had a daughter who could read close.

Ulpia was eleven and did the sums Lucanus would not. She came to the yard when Marcia let her and stood at Tyche's elbow at the tally-bench, and Tyche, who suffered no one gladly and the child least of all at first, had begun to hand her real columns, the wharf's own, because the girl was faster than the second clerk and made fewer errors. There was no place for her in any of it. A freedman's daughter does not keep a counting-house. She marries where her mother can place her and brings her figures to her own kitchen accounts. Marcia said this without heat, the way she said the level of a thing, that the girl was wasted and would stay wasted and I should not fill her head with a future the law had bolted shut. I said the law had bolted a great many doors I had walked through anyway. Marcia said yes, and I had walked through them as a man, and the girl was not one, and would I please not teach my daughter to break herself against a wall I could see and she could not. I had no answer. I taught her the nine-times course and then the elevens, and did not teach her the cipher, not yet, though Tyche had begun to look at the child the way you look at a hand you might one day need.

---

I came to the press one evening late, the autumn the charter was a year old, to find Eros gone home and the lamp left burning over the cutting-bench, against every rule of the shed, fire and oil and a hundred dry blocks. The apprentice was asleep over his work, his head on his folded arm, a half-cut block under his slack hand and the gouge fallen on the boards. He had been cutting a prize-sheet for the spring, a tale of a cart that ran on a road of iron, and had gotten the wheels and one cart and was at the smoking stack when sleep took him.

I trimmed the wick to nothing and pinched it out, and took the boy by the shoulder, not hard, the way you wake a son, and told him to go to his pallet, the block would keep. He went mumbling that he had the stack nearly right. I picked the gouge up off the floor and felt the edge with my thumb in the dark, the way Hermes had taught me to read a blade I could not see, and it was keen, he kept it keen, the boy who would cut the blocks when Eros and I were under the ground. I set it on the bench where his hand would find it in the morning, and left, and did not look at the block, because I could not have read it close enough to spoil my own pleasure in not needing to.
