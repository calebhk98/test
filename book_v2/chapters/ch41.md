# Chapter 41: Teaching

The hardest part of teaching a language with no speakers is that there is no one to be wrong in front of.

When I learned Latin I learned it by saying a thing badly to a man who winced, and saying it less badly the next day because the wince had taught me where my mouth was lying. The homeland script had no winces in it. Tyche read it and wrote it cold and silent, the way you read a tally, and the two new hands I set down beside her that spring read it the same way, lips still, because there was no sound to put to it. I had spent thirty years making sure of that. The script was a wall, and a wall does not talk back. So they learned it the way you learn a lock, by the turning, and not one of them ever knew whether the words in their mouths would have made a man on the far side of the world laugh.

I had thought, when Tyche pressed me over the third copy, that the danger was time. It was time. But the thing I had not counted was that I would have to sit in a room and watch other people be handed the inside of my head, a piece at a time, and feel each piece leave.

---

We did it at the wharf in the slack hour, the same bench, the same cheap palimpsest, after Eros trimmed the lamps and the press went quiet under its oiled cloth. Tyche taught. I had wanted to teach it myself and she told me no, in the tone she used on a clerk, that my hand shook and my eyes were going and the children would learn my errors as gospel, and that a thing taught by the man who made it is taught wrong, because he teaches what he meant and not what he wrote. So I sat at the end of the bench and was a thing to be consulted, and mostly was not consulted, and that was correct, and I hated it the clean way you hate a true figure.

Three of them. Ulpia, who came most evenings now. Vitalis, who was fifteen by then and cutting blocks clean as any letterer in Rome, bound to the society for one more year and required by his indenture to teach two others, which made him a keeper before he knew the word. And a girl Marcia had bought that winter to keep the storeroom day-book, a Bithynian, eleven or twelve, collared when she came and uncollared within the month because Marcia would not have iron on a child who handled her ledgers, named Chloe by the man who sold her and kept by the name because she had had no other long enough to remember. Chloe could not read Latin when she came. By spring she read it better than Vitalis, having come to it hungry, the way Tyche had come to the figures in a week behind Macer's back, and Tyche watched her the first month the way a smith watches a heat he does not trust and then stopped watching, which from Tyche was a coronation.

They started where everyone starts. Tyche gave them the letters of the homeland script paired to sounds they would never hear spoken, and the first text she set them to break was not a poem and not a prayer. It was the bottom of the ladder. The numbers and the trick of writing them by place, the four workings, the long division in full. They learned the script by decoding the thing the script was built to carry, so that the lock and the room behind it came into them together. By the time Chloe could sound the homeland letters in her head she had already, without being told it was strange, read a thing no living person but the four of them and me could read, which was the order of the world set out for a reader not yet born.

Vitalis treated it as a craft, which it was for him, a third skill stacked on the gouge and the press, and he was sound and slow and never once asked what the words were *for*. Chloe treated it as a door, and went through fast and hungry and did not look at the frame. Ulpia treated it as a problem with a wrong answer in it somewhere, and she was the one who gave me trouble.

---

She found the order first. That was the thing.

She came to me on a night Tyche had set them to copy a clean page of the lower rungs, the counting, and she stood at my end of the bench with the palimpsest held out at the careful distance she had learned to hold a thing she wanted checked, and she said the script was too tidy.

I asked her what she meant.

She said a man making marks up to stand for sounds would make a mess of it. She had watched Vitalis invent a shorthand for his block-orders, she said, and it was a heap, a stroke here for a word he used often, a scratch there, no two parts of it built the same. The homeland script was not a heap. It had the same number of marks always, give or take, for a thing of a size; the marks for the small joining-words were short and the marks for the big words were long, the way a thing settles when it has been used by many hands for a long time and worn to fit the hand. She said the figures were tidy because numbers are tidy, the world made them so. But the words were tidy too, and words are not tidy, words are a mess everywhere, she had heard Greek and Syrian and the Subura's gutter-Latin and they were all a mess. Mine was not a mess.

I told her it had been used a long time where I came from. Which was true, and was the first true thing I had said to one of my own children about it, and it cost me nothing and it cost me everything, the way a true thing said sideways does.

She let it sit. She had Marcia's habit of letting a thing sit to see if it would say more on its own. Then she asked how long, and I said longer than Rome, and she looked at the page in her hand and you could see her doing the figure, the only figure she had ever met that she could not close, and not liking it, and putting it down half-worked the way she never put a tally down half-worked.

---

The argument with Marcia came the same week and it was not about the script. It was about all of it.

Marcia kept the storeroom warm with a brazier against the river damp, and the pepper and rope and hide smell of Caepio's old concern, which was hers, and she did her hardest talking there standing up with a ledger open under her hand because she liked to have a true thing in front of her while she said a thing that could not be made true. She said I was teaching the girl the whole ladder. The figures, the shapes, now the script, all of it, the same I would teach a son meant to run the thing after me. And there was no after-me the girl could run. A freedman's daughter could not keep a counting-house under her own name; could not hold the press-society's charter; could not stand in the basilica where Lucanus meant to stand. I was fitting Ulpia for a world that did not exist, Marcia said, and a child fitted for a world that does not exist is unfit for the one she has to live in, and that is a cruelty dressed as a gift, and she would not have it called a gift in her storeroom.

I said the bottlenecks had never been permission. I said I had walked through every bolted door I had ever met and that the locks had all been the same lock, which was a man's fear and a man's ignorance, and that nobody had handed me the key, I had been too stupid to know the door was bolted and walked at it and found the bolt was rust.

Marcia said I had walked through them as a man, and a strange one, a marvel the emperor had a use for, and that the law did not bolt a marvel's door the way it bolted a girl's. She said the rust was rust for me. For Ulpia it was iron, and teaching her to read iron as rust was how you got a girl to walk at a thing that would not give and break herself against it, smiling, because her father had told her doors were rust.

She was not wrong. That was the trouble with Marcia, she was almost never wrong about the world as it stood, and I was arguing the world as it might be moved, and there is no honest way to weigh a thing you can hold against a thing you only believe in. I had spent thirty years on the second kind of weighing and lost most of the bouts and gone on. But I was not the one who would do the breaking, and I had no answer to that, and I did not pretend to have one. I said I would not stop teaching her. Marcia said she had not asked me to stop. She had asked me to know what I was doing, so that when the door held and the girl came home with her face broken I would not stand there surprised, as if the law were a thing I had only just heard of.

We left it there. Nothing was decided, because the only thing that could decide it was a world neither of us could see, and we both knew the other was partly right and neither of us would say so.

---

Ulpia decided it herself, which I should have known she would, having made her.

She had heard the storeroom through the wall, or some of it, the way children hear the thing meant to be over their heads. She did not come to me with it. She came to Tyche, at the bench, and I was at my end pretending to mend a stylus and heard the whole of it, because they did not lower their voices and a man my age learns to be furniture.

She asked Tyche straight whether it was true a woman could not keep the books under her own name. Tyche said it was true. Ulpia asked whose name the books were kept under now, the wharf's books, Caepio's old concern. Tyche said Marcia's, the widow's. Ulpia asked how, if a woman could not. And Tyche, who had been freed by a man whose name the law had stapled to her against her will and who signed nothing with it, set down her pen and gave the girl the real answer, which was that the law said a great many things a woman could not do and that the world was full of women doing them under a man's name, a dead husband's, a son's, a tame freedman's, a name borrowed and held in front of the work like a shield, and that the trick was never to own the door but to be the one hand in the house that knew how to work the lock, because a name can be killed and a knowing cannot, and there is always, always a name to be had if you have the knowing, and the names go cheap and the knowing does not.

Ulpia thought about that. Then she said she did not want a borrowed name. She wanted to know the thing so completely that whatever name stood in front of it, the knowing under it would be hers, and they could put any name they liked on the door and it would still be her counting-house behind it whether the law could see her or not. She said this flatly, the way she said a sum, and went back to her copying.

Tyche looked down the bench at me. I went on with the stylus. There is no answer to make to your own argument coming back out of a child's mouth in a harder form than you ever had the nerve for, except to be glad and to be afraid, which feel, when they come together, almost exactly the same.

---

So the script passed to her, and to the other two, that spring and the next, slow, the way Vitalis got the gouge. By the time the work was three years gone the lock opened to four living hands besides mine and Tyche's, and the books did not die when I did, which had been the whole point, and was a comfort, and was the strangest grief I knew, to sit at the end of a bench and watch the inside of my skull walk off in pieces inside three children, and to be glad of it, and to be, in the room where it was happening, more alone than I had been since the cell.

Because they would carry it all and they would never carry the one thing.

It came to a head over a word. Late, one of the upper pages, the cold sky, the part I had left half-dark on purpose. Ulpia was copying it and stopped on a word and brought it to me, the careful distance again, and asked what it was, because it was not a word for a thing. I had a word, in the homeland script, for a kind of weather that is not rain and not snow, a thing that falls grey and wet and freezing and is neither, a thing the homeland had so much of it had worn a short fat word smooth to name it, and Rome had never once needed the word because Rome had never once had the weather, and I had used it without thinking, on an upper page, describing cold, because my hand still reached for the homeland word for cold things the way your tongue reaches for the name of a street you grew up on.

She asked what it was. I told her a kind of cold rain. She said there was a word for cold rain three lines up and it was a different word, why two words. I said in the homeland there were many kinds of the cold falling, and many words. She asked why, if it was so cold there, anyone lived there, and where it was, and why I had a word for a thing Rome had no thing for, and how a place could be so far that no ship had ever brought one man of it but me, in thirty years, in a city that ate the whole world up the river every morning. She asked it the way she asked a sum that would not close, leaning into the wrongness of it, certain there was a true figure if she only pushed, and looking at me to push back, the way I had taught her to push back, because the pushing was the lesson.

And I had nothing to push with. I gave her the road-lie, Thule, the far north, the ice, the dark half the year, the same lie I had given a Greek physician's flat unconvinced face thirty-four years before in a cell with a wax tablet, and she took it the way Heras had taken it, which is to say she did not, quite, and was too well-mannered or too uncertain of her own arithmetic to say so. She put the word back on the page. She copied it faithfully, the smooth fat homeland word for a wet grey thing that fell on no street she would ever walk, into a book three children would carry a thousand years past a place that had the word and not the weather, and she went back to her bench.

I sat with that. There was a thing I could have said that would have closed her figure clean, and it was the one thing in the world I could not say to a living soul and least of all to her, and I had built a whole language to keep from having to, and the language had a word in it for sleet, and the word had given me away to my own daughter as far as it could give me away and no farther, which was: not at all, and entirely.

---

Heras came down to the wharf near the end of that season, which he did rarely now, seventy-nine and walking with two sticks and a boy at each elbow, to see the thing he had been arguing me toward for ten years, the man read by a lamp six hundred years on. He sat where I sat, at the end of the bench, and watched Tyche set the three of them a page, and watched Chloe break a line of the homeland letters into a true sum and not look up for praise because there was no praise to be had at that bench, only the next line. He watched the Bithynian girl, the bought child with the day-book hand, read a thing no senator in Rome could read.

He did not say anything clever. He had stopped, mostly, saving the cleverness for when his breath was up to it. He watched a long while, and the lamp burned down, and Eros came to trim it and Heras waved him off with a stick so as not to break the children's heads with the light, and we sat in the failing of it.

Then he said, low, that he had spent his life teaching boys what he knew, and that every one of them had got some of it and lost some of it and added a little wrong of their own, and that a thing taught is a thing handed to a thief, the student steals it and runs and you never see the whole of it together again, it lives now in the running.

I said I knew.

He said no, he did not think I did, not yet. He said the books were a fine thing and the lead box a fine thing, but that a book is a dead man's voice and the children were a live one, and that a live voice is the only kind that argues back, and that I should look at the small one with the borrowed name, the Bithynian, and consider that she had read my account of the order of the world before she was twelve and would spend forty years quarreling with it in her own head and teaching it to someone who would quarrel with her, and that this was the thing, not the lead box, this was the fire actually traveling, and that he was old and would not see where it went and neither would I and that this was, he had decided lately, the correct way for a fire to be carried, by people who would not see the far end of it, because a fire carried only by men who needed to see the far end would have gone out in the first generation that did not.

Then he asked Chloe, in Greek, what the sum on her page came to, to see if the child could turn her head from one tongue to another fast, and she answered him in the new figures, in Latin, without looking up, and got it right, and Heras laughed, the dry up-from-the-chest laugh I had not heard out of him in a year, and the two boys at his elbows startled, and the child finally looked up, at the old man laughing at her right answer, and went a little pink, and bent back to the next line.

I walked him to his litter. He took my arm on the wharf where it was uneven, which he had never done, and at the litter he held it a moment longer than the footing wanted, his hand light and dry and very cold even through my sleeve, and said only that I should keep the small one in figures, that one, she was the one, and then he was folded in and the bearers took him up the dark toward the Caelian, and I stood on the wharf in the smell of the cold river with my sleeve still cold where his hand had been, and went back in to where Tyche was sending the children home, one by one, into the streets that would never have a word for what fell on them, each carrying off a piece of a country that had no place on any map but the one I drew wrong on a wax tablet to save my life, and that none of them would ever miss, because you cannot miss a thing you were never told you were carrying.
