# Chapter 21: The Cannon That Bursts

I want to put down clearly that I knew what I was doing when I started keeping the manure.

I had nailed the lid on the manned balloon and meant it. I had told an emperor to his face that I would never hang another man under a bag of fire, and I had told myself a smaller version of the same thing about the other thing, the thing I had written "???" beside on the second night I was ever in Rome, when I couldn't remember the white powder. I had said it to myself on the bridge coming back over the Danube with my own water-knowledge fresh on my hands and a city dead of thirst behind me. Not yet. Maybe not ever. I'd been pleased with how grown-up it sounded.

What I had not understood about a vow is that you don't break it in one piece. You sell it off in slivers, each one cheap enough that you can pretend you haven't spent it.

The first sliver was the steel.

Hermes and I had been at the crucibles for going on two years and we were exactly where we'd been at the start, which is to say we could make a blade that would shave the hair off your arm and we could make, with the identical method on the identical day, a thing that came out of the quench in pieces like a dropped cup. I would stand in his forge under the brick arch behind the cattle market with the heat coming off the fire in a wall and watch him pull a glowing billet and judge it by a color, and I would have nothing to give him. No number. The whole problem was a number neither of us could see. There is a temperature, and we could not measure the temperature, and so we were two men trying to bake by the smell of the oven, and one of us at least should have known better.

"The fire is the master," Hermes said, which he had said before and which had stopped being a complaint and become a kind of prayer he muttered while he worked. He was past forty, gray as iron filings, and his forearms had a written history of every spark that had ever found him.

I told him, more than once, that if we could only get the powerful men of Rome to care about steel the way they cared about marble and frontiers and their own faces in bronze, we would have the money and the men to chase the number until we caught it. He didn't disagree. He also didn't see how a man got a senator to lie awake at night over the carbon in a crucible, and neither did I, for a long time.

That was the second sliver. The realization, which arrived slowly and which I welcomed because it was useful to me, that the steel chokepoint gated everything. Not just blades. Springs, which I'd wrecked a small fortune of soft Roman copper trying to make. Fine instruments. A clock that would keep time, which I could not build and was beginning to suspect I would never build. The whole ladder I told myself I was climbing for the saving things, the clean water and the medicine and the books, all of it ran up against the same wall, which was that nobody with money believed metal was a problem worth solving, because their metal held their swords and roofed their temples and did everything they had ever asked of it.

I needed to ask it to do something it couldn't.

The third sliver I am least proud of, because it was a lie I told myself on purpose to make the rest go down. I told myself the secret wasn't mine to keep. The Seres had it, I was fairly sure, the silk people at the eastern edge of the maps, China, they'd had fire-powder for centuries, I'd read it somewhere or seen it somewhere, in the half-life of facts a high-schooler carries, and if a whole civilization already had a thing then a man holding his breath in Rome was not the cork in the bottle. He was just a coward pretending to be a wall.

I believed that. I want to be honest that I believed it, standing in my own yard at the wharf with money in my peculium for the first time in my life, money I'd earned and not been allowed to keep until a year ago. I believed it the way you believe the thing that lets you do what you'd already decided to do.

I have wondered since whether the Seres had it then at all. I think now that I was wrong, that I'd done the thing I always did, slid a fact two or three hundred years out of its place because the timeline in my head was a smear. But I didn't know that then, and a man can build a great deal on a fact he can't check.

---

So. Niter.

I had three of the words. Charcoal I had by the cartload, the same charcoal Hermes burned. Sulfur came up from the volcano country, yellow and stinking, a curing-house and dyer's good that nobody thought twice about my buying, and I bought it through Pamphilus so my name stayed off it. The white salt I had nothing of. *Sal petrae*, Heras called it when I asked him sideways, the salt of the stones, the bloom you scrape off old cellar walls and the underside of stable floors, a curiosity that some cooks used to redden meat and otherwise good for nothing.

I sent men to scrape walls. It was the obvious move and it was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that cost me the better part of a year before I understood why. We boiled the scrapings and leached them and crystallized them and got a salt that would not work. It sucked water out of the air until the powder was a paste; it fizzled and smoked and would not bang. I didn't have the chemistry to know the word for what I'd made, but I know now it was the wrong nitrate, the calcium one, the lime in the old walls poisoning it. I only knew then that the white salt off a wall was no good, and that the white salt I needed came from a different and far worse place.

Dung.

You make a niter bed the way you make the worst compost heap in the history of the world and then you tend it like a vineyard. Manure, and the bedding straw, and ash from the wood fires, and lime, and over all of it, urine, as much as you can collect, which means you become a man who buys other men's piss. We built the beds in a leased shed two streets back from the wharf, far from anything I cared about burning, and we turned them and watered them and waited, and the smell got into the timber and the timber held it and the whole street learned to walk on the other side. You wait months. You wait through a summer that bakes the stink into a thing with weight. Then you leach the rotted beds through ash, wood-ash and water dripping slow through a barrel with a false bottom, and you boil the brown liquor down, and skim it, and let it crystallize, and you get, if the gods and the bacteria you don't have a word for are kind, a handful of long white needles that taste cold on the tongue and burn the right way.

It took most of two years to get a quantity worth the name. Two years, for a thing I'd half-promised a contest of children that Rome would have in a generation.

I told the men it was for medicine. For Heras, for a cooling salt, which was not entirely a lie because I was learning everything from Heras's books and Heras's patience and Heras knew exactly what I was doing and hated it and helped me anyway, in the careful way he did things he disapproved of, which was to make sure I did them less stupidly than I would have alone.

"You've decided," he said one evening, not a question. We were in the shed and he had a cloth over his nose and mouth and his eyes were watering above it.

"I've decided to be able to," I said. "Deciding to use it is a different thing."

"Is it." He looked at the beds, at the white forming at the edges where the wet liquor had splashed and dried on a board. "You're a man who built a wall and now spends his evenings learning where the gate is. Tell yourself the gate is still shut if you like. I've watched you find this many gates."

I didn't answer him. There wasn't an answer that wasn't the lie about the Seres, and I didn't want to say the lie out loud to Heras, who would have known it for one.

---

The ratios I had to find by killing powder.

I knew there were three parts and that the white salt was most of it and the other two were small, and that is genuinely all I knew, a shape with no numbers in it. I had a memory of a number, three-quarters, and I clung to it, and I was lucky that the memory was close. I made small batches in a stone mortar, ground fine, and burned a pinch on a tile, and most of it whooshed and stank and did nothing, and slowly, over a winter, the pinches started to crack instead of hiss.

I had a man on the grinding. His name was Geta. He was one of the wharf hands, a freedman like Pamphilus now, a wages man, broad and slow-spoken and good with a pestle, and I had told him the powder was dangerous and I had not told him enough, because I didn't know enough. I thought dry powder ground gently was safe to grind. I had read, somewhere in the smear, that you ground it wet or you ground the parts apart, and I had a memory of the warning and not of the reason, and I let Geta grind a dried cake fine in a stone mortar in a closed shed in February because I wanted a finer powder and I was impatient and I had begun, by then, to think of the thing as nearly solved.

I was across the yard when it went. There was a sound I felt in my chest before I heard it, a flat hard slap of the air, and then the shed door came off and a gout of dirty smoke stood up over the roof, and I was running before I'd decided to.

Geta was alive. I will say that first because it's the thing I most needed to be true while I was crossing that yard. He was alive and he was on his back in the spilled char screaming in a voice I'd never heard a grown man use, and his hands were wrong. The pestle had still been in the mortar and the mortar had still been mostly whole and the flash had gone up and out, and it had taken his face on one side and it had taken his hands, the left one worse, the fingers of it gone or going, the right one burned to the bone across the back. His eyebrows were gone. His beard was gone in a patch and smoking. The smell was the balloon smell, the burned-grease-and-hair smell I knew from the Loaf, the one I will not describe again because I described it once and that was enough for one life.

Heras came. Heras was always close in those years, thank God, and he packed the hands and the face and gave him poppy and worked through the night, and Geta did not die, which I had no right to and took anyway. He lost the left hand at the wrist in the end and three fingers of the right and the sight in one eye. He could not grind anymore. He could not do most things anymore. I put him on wages for life out of my own coin and I built him a room and I have paid it every month I've had a month to pay it in, and none of that is a thing I get to feel good about. I bought a man his hands and then I burned them off teaching myself a number I could have learned from a book if the book had survived to the century I needed it in.

When Geta could talk, days later, gray and shrunken in the cot, the bandaged stumps held up off the blanket on a roll of cloth, he asked me if the powder had worked.

I told him it had.

He said good, in the cracked voice, and shut his one eye, and I sat with him until he slept, and that is the part of this that I would undo if I could undo any of it, and I can't undo any of it. You can't un-know a thing for the world and you can't un-burn a man's hands. I had wanted, when I started keeping the manure, to make the powerful men of Rome feel something true about metal. The first person my powder made feel anything was Geta, and what he felt was his own hands going.

---

I went on. That is the worst sentence in this whole account and I'm going to leave it short.

I went on, because the powder had worked, and because a thing that has cost that much demands to be made worth it, which is a lie people tell themselves to keep from admitting the cost was paid for nothing. I ground the rest of it wet, with the parts kept apart until the last, the way I should have from the start, and I corned it, pressed it damp into cakes and broke the cakes back to grains so the powder breathed fire instead of smothering itself, which I'd remembered too late to do Geta any good.

And I built the cannon, knowing it would burst. That was the entire point of it. I want that understood, because what I staged was a kind of theater and the audience was not supposed to know the ending and I did.

Hermes cast it. We did it in bronze, the good gun-bronze the Romans used for the best of everything, because the argument I was making was about exactly that bronze. A short fat tube, closed at one end, the bore a hand's-width across, the walls thick as I could make Hermes pour them, mounted in an oak cradle so it couldn't leap. Hermes hated it. He'd cast ballista parts and temple doors and he knew metal in his hands the way Heras knew a fever, and he kept running his thumb along the cooling bronze and telling me the wall was too thin for what I was telling him would happen inside it, and I kept telling him that was the point and watching him fail to believe me, because no craftsman wants to build a thing to break.

"You're paying me to make a flawed casting," he said.

"I'm paying you to make a perfect casting of a thing that bronze can't do," I said. "When it fails, it fails the metal. Not you."

He didn't like that either. But he poured it true, beautifully true, a clean tube without a flaw in it, and that was important, because a man could look at it afterward and not say it broke because the smith was a fool.

---

I had Celer get the army men there, and Celer's word and my own name as the emperor's freedman got me people who mattered, officers of the Guard and an engineer or two and a man from the imperial household whose job I never learned, standing under an awning on a stretch of bank well downriver of everything, on a gray morning with the cold coming off the water. I'd had the cradle staked into the mud sixty paces out, and the line where the spectators stood I'd set myself, and then I'd moved it back twenty paces farther because I was the only one there who knew what was coming and I wasn't going to kill an officer of the Guard to win an argument about steel.

Celer stood by me. He looked at the squat bronze thing in the mud and then at me.

"This is the powder," he said. Not a question. He'd been on the Loaf. He knew the look of me building toward a cost.

"This is the powder."

"You swore off it."

"I swore off hanging men under fire," I said. "I didn't keep the rest of it." He let that sit, the way he let things sit, and I added, because he'd earned it, "There's no man on this one, Celer. Just the metal."

I loaded it heavy. I want to be honest: I loaded it heavier than the argument strictly needed, because I wanted no chance of a fizzle in front of those faces, a stone ball seated on a charge I'd packed to do exactly what it did. We touched it off with a slow-match on a long pole and everyone flinched back and the morning came apart.

I have tried to find the words for the sound and the closest I've got is that it wasn't a sound, it was a thing that happened to your body, a flat enormous blow that hit you in the chest and the teeth at the same instant, and the bronze tube was simply not there anymore, it had opened like a flower made of knives and gone in every direction, and a piece of it the size of a fist went over our heads with a noise like a torn sky and into the river, and the stake and the cradle were gone, and the stone ball, the stone ball had gone first and gone true, out over the water, and we heard later it had crossed the whole width of the river and struck the far bank hard enough to throw mud.

There was a long quiet after, the kind of quiet that comes when a lot of frightened men are deciding not to have screamed.

Then they all started talking at once, and I let them, and I waited, and when they'd worked their way around to the question themselves, which was the whole craft of it, I gave them the answer they'd already arrived at. Here is a force that will throw a stone through a wall. Here is the best bronze in the world, poured without a flaw by the best smith I could find, and it could not hold the force for the space of a heartbeat. It tore like wet bread. So if you want the thing that throws the stone, and you have just seen with your own eyes that you want it, then you do not need a smith and you do not need bronze. You need a metal that does not yet exist, and the only place in Rome that anyone is trying to make it is a forge behind the cattle market run by a freedman named Hermes, who is out of money.

I had the wrecked pieces gathered up and laid out on a cloth so they could handle them. A Guard officer picked up the fist-sized chunk that had gone over our heads and turned it in his hands and went pale around the mouth, and I knew I had them, and I knew exactly what I had cost to get them, and the two facts sat in me side by side and did not cancel.

---

It is loose now. That is the thing I can't take back and the thing I keep coming around to in the dark.

Before that morning, the powder was a secret in a stinking shed and a number in my head and a man with no hands. After it, it was a thing that powerful men had seen with their own eyes throw a stone across a river, and powerful men do not unsee a weapon. They went away from that bank knowing it could be done, and knowing roughly what it ate, charcoal and brimstone and a white salt that comes from a slow filthy patience, and that knowledge was no longer in one frightened freedman's keeping. I'd told myself the Seres already had it so it wasn't mine to hold. Whether or not that was ever true, it is true now that Rome has it, and Rome got it from me, and I did the arithmetic on that the way I do arithmetic on everything and the sum did not come out the way I'd wanted.

The steel question is theirs now too, which was the whole point and which has turned out to be a heavier thing than I bargained for. Men who would not have crossed the street for a crucible are sending to ask about Hermes's forge. The household man whose job I never learned came back twice. I had wanted them to care about metal and now they care about metal the way an army cares, which is the way that ends with the metal pointed at someone, and I have made myself the man who can be asked about it, which means I am more useful and more watched than I was, and being either of those things in Rome is a way of being closer to the edge of a high place.

Hermes was glad, in his way. The money is coming and the money buys fuel and the fuel buys attempts and the attempts might, someday, with men and luck I didn't have before, catch the number. He has not caught it. I want to be clear that the steel is exactly as unsolved this morning as it was the morning we cast the cannon to argue for it; I have spent a man's hands and a great deal of my own peace to buy the right to keep failing at it with better funding. That is the honest shape of what I did.

I went to see Geta after, the evening of the demonstration, because I didn't want to drink wine with officers and be congratulated. He was sitting up. The room I'd built him had a window and he had it open to the cold and the river smell, and the bandages were off the worse arm finally, the wrist healed over smooth and wrong. He heard me come in and turned the good side of his face to me.

I sat down on the stool by the cot and didn't say anything for a while, and neither did he, and the wharf made its evening noises down the lane, the carts starting up now that the day-ban was lifting, the river slapping the pilings. After a while he asked me whether the powerful men had been frightened.

I told him they had.

He was quiet, working at something. Then he lifted the smooth healed stump a little off the blanket, the way you'd lift a hand to make a point, out of an old habit the body hadn't lost, and he said that was good, then, and he hoped they stayed frightened, because frightened men are careful, and I should make sure I stayed frightened too.

I sat with him until the light went. There was a wax tablet in my lap that I'd carried from the bank without remembering picking it up, and on it, in my own English that no one in the world but Tyche could read, I'd written the ratio I'd killed his hands to learn, the three numbers, finally right, and I sat in the dark with my thumb over the figures and did not rub them out.
