# Chapter 45: What It Serves

The accounts came to me out of Judaea the way everything came to me now, late and on someone else's errand. Eudemus sent them down to the wharf with a clerk and a covering note that asked me to cast a column the fiscus had got into a tangle, because the war had thrown the eastern books out of true and the man who balanced them had died of a flux, and the figures were mine and so was the only living hand that could be trusted to find where a province had stopped adding up.

I had not asked to see them. I did not go looking. The leaves came to me because I had taught Rome to keep its sums in marks a tired clerk could not fudge, and a war is the largest sum an empire ever does, and so the war came to my bench in my own figures, fair-copied in Ulpia's clean hand off the field rolls, the two and the circle and the rest, the marks they had cut into the milestone, adding up everything.

I read them at arm's length by the window because my eyes would no longer take small marks by lamp. Ulpia read the smaller hands to me where the field clerks had crowded them. We sat at it three mornings.

---

The reports came in pieces over that winter and the next, secondhand, thirdhand, worn smooth by the men who carried them.

A discharged man at the wharf, an auxiliary mustered out and working a grain-hull for his passage home, told Pamphilus something about the country that Pamphilus brought to me because Pamphilus knew the eastern books had crossed my bench. The man said the legions were not fighting battles any more, that there were no battles to fight, that the rebels would not stand. He said they had gone into the ground, into the dry hills, into caves and old cisterns and the diggings under the towns, whole families of them with their grain and their water and their goats, and that the army had stopped trying to dig them out, which cost men, and had instead sat down at the cave-mouths and waited. He said it took a long time. He did not say it as a horror. He said it as a tedium, a sitting of a season at the mouth of a hole in the rock on short rations himself while the people inside it were brought to the end of theirs. He had the contempt of a man who has been frightened and lived, for the people who frightened him and did not. Pamphilus stood in my tally-room and gave it to me in the man's own words, and then said, because Pamphilus is a kind man and had watched my face, that it was a long way off and none of my doing, and went back to the cloth.

A dispatch reached the city in the spring, read out and chalked up and gone over in the porticoes, that the war was as good as won and that the emperor would refound the rebel capital. Aelia Capitolina, after his own name and the god of the Capitol, a colony on the bones of the old city, the plough run round its boundary the old way, a temple where their temple had been, and a standing prohibition that no Jew set foot in it on pain of death. The men in the portico thought it a fine settlement of a tiresome matter. A city was a city; a colony was prestige and land for veterans; the emperor was a great founder of cities, he had founded them all across the world and put up his wall in the north and his temples in Greece, and here was another, and the only difference any of them remarked on was the strangeness of a people who would rather die in a hole than live under a new and well-drained street plan.

I knew the street plan they meant. I had drawn that grid for other colonies, the cardo and the decumanus square to the compass, the falls worked out so the drains ran, the aqueduct sited off a survey done to the one scale in my figures. I did not draw this one. But I had drawn the kind of it, and taught the men who drew it, and a colony laid out clean over the place where a city had been razed and its people driven off or dead is a survey's work as well as any, because a survey does not know what was on the ground before the chain was run across it.

---

I tried to take it to Heras and he was a year and more in the ground.

I do not mean I forgot. I mean the want of him came up in me the way a tongue goes to a pulled tooth, a reaching to the place where he had been. He would have sat on the bench under the window with his bad chest and his arms folded and let me talk myself out, and then he would have found the soft place in what I said and put his thumb on it. He was good at that. He was the only one who had ever been good at that with me, because Marcia weighs a thing for what it will do and Tyche is on my side past the point of use to me and the rest of them think I am the Thulean and cannot help it.

So I did it without him, badly. I argued it on the cold bench in the empty room after the day shift had gone. I said, to no one, in the Greek I used to use with him because it kept my own voice strange enough to hear, that a man is not answerable for the use another man makes of a true thing, that the figures are only counting and counting is good, that a clean column is a defense against the tax-farmer as much as a tool of him, that I had fought slavery with the only weapons I had and a slave market crashing under its own weight is what I had wanted, the market made too cheap to be worth keeping, except that this was not the market dying of a rail cart that did the work of twenty men, this was the market drowned in the bodies of a destroyed people, and the figure was the same figure either way, falling, and the figure did not know the difference and neither, it turned out, did the world.

I asked the empty bench whether a man who carries water into a camp so the men do not die of the flux is answerable for what the men he kept alive then do with their hands. Heras would have made me say what I meant by answerable. He would have asked me whether I was looking for the truth of it or for permission, and I would not have been able to lie to his face as I can lie to a bench, and that was the use of him, and it was gone.

I had taught four children to read my secret script so that the knowing would outlive me. I had not taught one of them to argue with me, because there had been no time, and because I had not wanted it. A man wants a knowledge kept and does not want it questioned, and now I had no one to question it and the questioning was the part I could not do without.

---

The powder I could not settle, and the not settling it was the worst of the work.

The powder is loose. It has been loose since the day Hermes cast a bronze tube thick enough to burst and we burst it on purpose downriver before the Guard's officers, to argue that the force wanted better metal than any we could make. Rome learned that day that dung and ash and the yellow rock from the volcano country, ground fine and mixed and corned, will throw a stone across a river. The steel to make a tube that does not burst has never come, not from Hermes in thirty years, not from Felix after him, and it will not come in my life; there is no gun in the world, no cannon a gunner can trust, the thing I am most often credited with and have most completely failed to make. So no one threw a stone at a wall in Judaea out of a tube of mine. Of that much I am sure, and it is a small sure thing.

But a wall does not have to be thrown down from a distance. It can be undermined. A gallery is dug under it and the props are fired and the wall comes down into the hole, and men have done that with timber and fire since before Rome was a village. And a charge of the gray powder set in a bored hole will crack rock and bring down an overhang and seal or open a way into the ground far faster than a man with a pick, which is a thing any quarryman would see in an afternoon, and Maximus saw it at his quarry years ago when I let a little of it loose to split a block, and quarrymen talk, and the powder is loose. I do not know that anyone carried a keg of it into the dry hills of Judaea to bring down the roof of a cave or to open one that men did not want to enter with their bodies. I do not know that they did not. The accounts that crossed my bench were grain and captives and pay; no clerk puts a line like that in a revenue column. I asked no one, because the only way to learn it was to ask in a way that named me to it, and I found that I would rather not know than be told, and I despise that in a man and found it in myself.

So it sits, and will sit. Either my powder helped close a hole in the rock over a family that had gone into it to live, or it did not, and I will go to my grave not knowing which, and the not knowing is its own kind of weight, heavier some nights than a certainty would be. A certainty has edges. You can set your hand on it. This had no edges. It was everywhere the powder might be and nowhere I could point.

---

They call me the Thulean still, in the markets and the porticoes, the man who flew, the man who taught the figures, the wonder-worker from the far cold north. It is a good name to have. It opens doors and it has more than once kept me out of a cell, and I built it on purpose, day by day, out of a balloon and a map and a string of demonstrations, because a man with no city and no people in this world needs a name that does the work a city does.

I had thought of the name as mine, a garment I had cut and put on. I sat on the cold bench that winter and understood it the other way. A man useful to power is a tool in power's hand, and a tool is used for the work the hand is doing, and the hand in Judaea was doing what it was doing, and my figures were in its grip, and my drains were under its camps keeping it healthy enough to do it, and my survey was square to the compass over the razed ground, and somewhere out past the edge of what I would let myself ask the gray powder might be cracking the rock. The Thulean is not a man who hands the world gifts and stands clear. The Thulean is the name a slaughtering empire gives the foreigner who makes the slaughter run cleaner, and I had spent thirty-seven years polishing that name and presenting it at every door, and the doors had opened, and this was the inside of the house the doors led to.

I had wanted to be useful and forgotten. I had got the useful. The forgotten I had wanted for vanity's sake, the small clean immortality of a mark with no name on it, and now I wanted it for cover, and could not have it, because a man does not get to lay down a name once it has started doing work without him, any more than he gets to call back a figure once it is cut in the public stone.

---

I went back to the accounts on the fourth morning.

The first leaves were grain. So many men eat so much bread a day, and the bread is so much grain, and the grain is so many ships, and the ships are so many days, and the days were now into the fourth year. The column balanced. It was a good column. Whoever had drawn it had used the carry-mark correctly and had not once hidden a shortfall in a Roman tail where it could rot unseen, which was the whole of what I had built the figures to prevent, and they had prevented it, and the work they had made legible was the feeding of the army that was burning Judaea down to its roots.

There is a kind of reading where the eye runs ahead of the man. I had read three more leaves before I understood what the fourth column was.

It was headed in the field clerk's abbreviation, which Ulpia read out flat because she did not know the country and the word was only a word to her. Captives. The fiscus took its tithe of the captives as it took its tithe of everything, and the captives were a revenue, and a revenue is reckoned, and so they had been counted and valued and the value entered, and because the price falls when the market floods, there was a second column beside the first that tracked the price, and the price was falling.

I sat and watched the price of a human being fall down a column of my own marks across the seasons of the war. The clerk had noted, in the margin, in a tradesman's shorthand, that the markets at the staging ports could not absorb the supply and that lots were being driven north and west to find a price, and that even so the price was a fraction of the standing rate, and he had given the fraction in my figures, exact, because my figures are exact. That is what they are for.

Ulpia stopped reading. She had got to the bottom of the column and there was nothing under it but the rule and the total, and she did not read the total. She put her finger on the rule under the last figure and waited.

I could not tell, sitting there, and cannot tell now, writing it, whether I had been a man doing the only duty a man with my knowledge could do, which is to lift the bottom rungs and trust the climbing to better hands than mine, or a boy who decided in a cell to stay alive and thought he knew better than the whole turning world what the world should have, and gave it, and it served. Both arguments are good arguments. I have made them both to the empty bench in the dark and neither has ever once won, and I no longer have the man who could have told me whether either was even the right question.

Ulpia sat with her finger on the rule and I sat with the column and neither of us said anything for a long time.

---

I went up to the house late, past the hour the gate is barred, and Marcia was awake.

She was at the storeroom table with a single lamp drawn close and the day's tallies under it, working them because her eyes were still good enough to and mine were not, and she did not ask where I had been. She moved the lamp so it would not be in my face when I sat down. That was all. She finished the column she was on, and ruled it off, and set the stylus down, and we sat a while in the smell of pepper and rope and warm oil that her dead husband's warehouse had never lost.

After a time she said the price of eastern grain would come down hard once the province was settled and the levies eased, and that we should not be long on grain contracts this season, and I said no, we should not. We talked about grain, the price of it, the ships, the season. Two people who reckon for their living, talking the language they both knew how to talk, while the lamp burned down. Neither of us said the other column, the one beside the grain, the one that had been falling. I held the grain the way a drowning man holds a plank, and she knew that, and let me hold it, and we sat with the figures that meant only themselves until the oil was low and she banked the lamp and we went in.
