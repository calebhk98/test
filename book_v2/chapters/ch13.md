# Chapter 13: The Fall

The hill had a name in Dacian that nobody in our camp could say, so the men called it the Loaf, because it sat on the far side of the valley with a round brown back like a loaf left too long in the oven. The Dacians were behind it. That was the whole problem, the whole reason any of this happened. They were behind the Loaf and Trajan's scouts could not get a man up high enough to count them without the man being seen and killed, and somebody up the chain had remembered that the tribune Celer had a Thulean and a balloon.

So they sent for me to put a man over the Loaf.

Pretending they were stupid would be a comfort and a lie. They were in a hurry, and a hurry is a different animal. There was a column that was going to move up the valley in three days whether anyone had counted the Dacians or not, and the men in that column were going to walk into whatever was behind the Loaf, and the difference between knowing and not knowing was going to be measured in how many of them came back down. Celer laid it out to me flat in the command tent with a wax tablet and his thumbnail for a stylus, the valley, the Loaf, the column, the three days, and he did not raise his voice once, and at the bottom of it he set the tablet down, weighing exactly what it could do and not an inch more.

"Tell me what it can't do," he said. "All of it. Now, while there's time to hear it."

"It can't clear that hill on a tether," I said. "Not from this side. The valley's too wide. To see over the Loaf from where we can stand a man you'd need him up past three hundred feet, and the tether to hold him there weighs more than the bag can lift, and the wind off the river will lay him sideways before he gets half that high. You saw it lie down in the wind last week. That was at a hundred and twenty with no one in it."

"So not a tether," Celer said.

I had known he was going to say it. I had heard it coming before I could name it. "No," I said. "Don't."

"Free, then. Cut him loose, let him go up clean, let him count, bring him down on the far side or wherever the wind drops him."

"Down where," I said. "On whose side of the Loaf."

That landed. I watched it land. He didn't like it, and Celer's dislike was a man already doing the arithmetic of how to do it anyway.

---

Apollodorus was in the camp because the bridge was in the camp's reach and Trajan kept his engineer close. Somebody thought the great man should look at the flying business before the army bet a column on it. He came down to my patch of trampled ground with his thick scarred hands behind his back and looked at the bag laid out on the hides and the fire-bowl and the willow, as if it had personally claimed it could do his job.

"You want to put a soldier on a fire under a sack of greased cloth," he said, "and let the wind have him."

"I don't want to," I said. "They want to."

"They always want to." He crouched, with the ease of a man whose knees had carried him up scaffolds for thirty years, and he picked up a fold of the linen and rubbed it between finger and thumb, feeling the grease, the weight, the give of it, and his mouth did something sour. "This holds heat the way a sieve holds water. You light a fire under it and you climb up away from the ground, which is the only thing that ever puts a fire out, the ground, the sand, the water. Up there you have none of those. You have a man and a flame and the wind, in a basket." He let the cloth drop and stood and brushed his palm on his tunic. "A clever toy that does one thing. I told you that at the bridge. The one thing is not this."

"I know," I said.

He looked at me then, and for a moment there was something in it that wasn't rivalry, that was just one man who builds things looking at another man who builds things across a problem that neither of them could stop. "Then say so to the man who can stop it," he said, "if such a man exists in this camp, which I doubt." And he went back to his bridge, where the things he built held, because he built them out of stone and his own stubbornness and never once out of greased linen, and I have hated him and envied him in the same breath more times than I can count and that morning I did both at once and wished he'd stayed.

There was no man who could stop it. I was a slave on loan. Celer could be talked to, and Celer had already heard me, and hearing me was not the same as obeying me, and obeying a slave was not a thing a tribune did in front of an army three days from a fight.

---

I built it as safe as a thing like that gets. I am going to set down what I did because it is the only part of this I get to keep.

I made the envelope bigger than anything I'd flown, so it would rise slow and grudging, a walk not a leap. I built a longer basket, Macer's design from Rome, so the man could sit low and keep his weight under the bag. I lined the fire-bowl in wet clay and set it on a wet plank and rigged a lid he could drop by pulling one cord - choke the fire from where he sat, no standing, no reaching, one pull. I soaked the willow. I soaked the hides. I made him a hood and gloves of wet leather. I tied a long light line to the basket, knowing the wind would take it for breakfast, so that when he came down we'd have a thread to follow.

I made the rule again: one thing goes wrong, you don't fix it, you pull the cord, you come down. I made him say it back to me twice.

His name was Sabinus. Marcus Aurelius Sabinus. He was a real soldier, not a recruit, maybe twenty-five, with a farmer's forearms and a scar through one eyebrow that pulled the eyebrow up so he always looked faintly amused, and he volunteered, which I have never forgiven the army for letting a man do. He volunteered because there was extra pay in it and because the man who went up and counted the Dacians and saved the column would have his name said by the legate, and a soldier's whole life is his name being said by the right man.

Naso was there. They'd brought him up north with the baggage because a half-burned recruit who can't grip a shield with his ruined hand is no use in a line but he can still tend mules, and he came and stood at the edge of my trampled patch with his left hand curled against his chest as it had healed, a claw of shiny skin, and his ankle still putting a hitch in his walk. He watched me fit the hood on Sabinus. He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, to me, low, "Make him keep his seat." His face when he said it was not a young face anymore. "Tell him I said. The standing up. Tell him."

I told Sabinus. He looked over at Naso's hand, at the claw of shiny skin curled against the chest, and something went out of his amusement, and he said he'd keep his seat, and that was the closest thing to a good omen I got that morning.

---

The wind was wrong from the start and I knew it was wrong and I said it was wrong and the legate's man said the column moved in three days, and that was the end of the conversation, because three days is a fact and my fear was just a slave talking.

We filled it in the lee of a baggage cart, and it filled slow and sullen and good, swelling up off the hides into the tall lopsided teardrop, and the men hauled it upright with poles in the wet leather mitts I'd made them, and Sabinus climbed into the long basket and sat, and I checked the cord to the fire-lid myself, put it in his right hand, closed his fingers on it. His eyes over the wet hood were steady. The wind came across the valley in a long cold shove and the whole bag leaned and the men dug in.

"On your word," Celer said. He was at the line himself. He was always at the line.

I did not have a word. I had every reason in the world and no power and a fact about a column, and the thing about being property is that your no is a sound you make, not a thing that happens. I looked at Sabinus and Sabinus looked back at me with the cord in his fist and said, "Let it go, Thulean, before I lose my nerve and you lose your morning," and the men laughed because soldiers laugh, and I lifted my hand, and they cut it loose.

It went up clean. That is the cruelty of it. The first part went exactly the way the whole idea promised, the bag rising slow and huge and Sabinus going up over the camp sitting low and small in the long basket with one hand on the cord and one on the rim, up past the cart-tops, up past the standards, up past the height a man falls from and lives, and the camp went quiet, that held breath, and for a count of maybe forty he was a man flying and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever built.

The wind had him at a hundred feet. I'd told them. The river wind came down the valley in a sheet and it caught the bag broadside and pushed, and the bag, which wanted to go where the wind went, leaned and ran with it, and Sabinus and the basket swung out under it on the long swing of a thing hung from a single point, out and up, like the seat of a swing at the top of its arc. And the fire-bowl, wet clay and wet plank and all my careful weight, leaned with the swing, because clay and plank don't care how careful you were, they care about which way is down, and which way is down had just changed.

I saw the flame come out of the bowl. From the ground it was a small bright tongue, no bigger than a candle at that distance, leaning off the lip of the bowl and laid sideways by the same wind that was laying the bag, reaching, and it touched the cloth.

He pulled the cord. I have to believe he pulled the cord. The lid was for a fire sitting straight in its bowl and the fire was not in its bowl anymore, it was a sheet of flame being pressed flat against the side of the bag by the wind, and a lid does nothing to a fire that has already left the dish and found the grease. I watched a brown line open up the side of the bag into a bright one, the seam going, the same seam, always the same seam, and the bag that was holding him up stopped being a bag.

It came apart in fire. The top of it, the part still full of hot air, that part went on rising for a breath even as the rest of it burned, so that for one moment Sabinus hung under a crown of fire with the basket trailing flame, and then the air went out of the top through the burning and there was nothing holding him up at all.

He fell with the burning cloth coming down around him and over him, a hundred feet, into the trampled ground at the edge of the camp, and the sound of it reached me a moment after I saw it, a sound I am not going to write down, the sound of all of him arriving at the ground at once. The cloth came down after him, still burning, and laid itself over him like something being tucked in.

---

The camp was silent for about three breaths and then it was not. Men ran. Somebody was already throwing dirt, the right thing, the only thing, dirt and a wet hide, and the burning cloth was pulled off, and there was no question to ask of what was under it, no alive, he's alive to hold up in front of myself this time, nothing to bind in wet linen, nothing for Heras's clean hands even if Heras had been within fifty miles. The fall had done it. The fire was just the fire.

I do not remember crossing the ground to him. I remember being there, on my knees in the dirt, with my hands out and nothing for them to do, and somebody, an optio, taking me by the shoulder and moving me back, not unkindly, just clear of the work, because there was work, there is always work, a body to carry and a column that still moved in three days.

I stayed on my knees. I picked up a fistful of the burned cloth, scorched stiff and still hot enough to hurt, and my old burn-scars screamed where I'd grabbed it and I held it tighter. The grease blackened into the weave was my grease. I had sealed that seam to hold the heat that lifted the man so the army could see over the Loaf. I had built every link of that chain and there was a man at the end of it in the dirt.

He had a wife in a town in Pannonia and two children. He'd told me their names while I was fitting the wet hood on him, a boy and a girl, the boy old enough to mind the goats, and I had made myself not hear them and I heard them anyway and I have them still.

I did not make a sound because there was no sound that was the right size.

Naso came and stood over me. I felt him before I looked up, the hitch in his step. He didn't say I told you to make him keep his seat. He reached down with his good hand and closed it over the rag in my fist, not to take it, just to put his hand over mine on it, and then he took it away and limped off toward the baggage, because a man with a ruined hand can still tend mules and the work does not stop for anybody.

---

Celer found me still kneeling there when the body was gone and the ground had been turned over and dirt thrown until the burned smell was mostly under the smell of churned earth. He crouched in front of me. His palms had fresh rope-burn. He looked at the rag in my hand and at my face and he did not do the arithmetic, or if he did I didn't see it. I had wanted to see it. I had wanted him to be cold so I could hate him and hating him would have been somewhere to put it.

"You said it," he said. His voice was rough and low and steady. "You said the wind. You said all of it. I heard you and I let it go anyway. That's mine." He didn't say it like a confession. He said it like a fact he was entering into a ledger, the way you'd note a wagon lost in a river crossing. "He volunteered. They'll write it down that he volunteered. It won't be a lie."

He stood up. His knees cracked. He looked out across the valley at the Loaf sitting there brown and round with the Dacians still uncounted behind it. I watched the soldier come back into his face. A column moved in three days and Sabinus had not even gotten high enough to count them.

"No more men go up in it," Celer said. "Not from me. I'll tell the legate it can't be done from this side and I'll be telling him the truth, because you told me first." He looked back down at me, at the rag, and something crossed his face that he didn't let stay. "It's a good thing you made," he said, "and it's a butcher, and now it's killed a man, and you're going to have to decide what kind of man builds a thing like that. I can't decide it for you." He went to send the litter and write the letter.

---

I did not go back to the trampled patch for the rest of that day. Pamphilus found me near the river after dark, where I'd gone to be where the noise of water could be louder than the inside of my head, and he didn't say anything. Pamphilus had a riveted collar around his neck that I could not take off and he knew better than anyone in that camp what it was to build the thing your owner told you to build. He sat down near me on the bank and after a while he started the small fire I'd taught him to start for boiling water, because the flux was still in the camp and we still boiled our water, because that was a thing that worked and did not lie on a good day.

I watched him feed the little fire. The same orange. The same lick of it up off the coals. I had spent two years teaching myself not to be afraid of fire so I could make it lift a man into the sky, and I sat on the bank of a river whose name I couldn't say and watched a small clean fire boil water that would keep men alive, and I held the scorched corner of cloth that I had carried all day and not been able to set down, and I put it, finally, into the fire, and watched the grease in it catch and the bright line run up the weave one more time until there was nothing left of it but the smell, and that smell I will not get out of the back of my throat as long as I live.

I thought about what Celer had said. About what kind of man builds a thing like that.

The envelope was right. The fire-lid was right. The long line was right. Sabinus had done everything I told him. None of that had been the problem. The problem was I had put a man who had never flown into a design that had never been flown free. I had put a farmer's son into something I had only flown on a rope in a Rome courtyard, against a Rome wall, in Rome wind. I had done it because I was property and the column moved in three days. I had done it because a man said to and I did not know how to make my no into a thing that stopped anything.

I was not going to do it again.

The vow was a rule, stated to myself, stated plain: I will not put untrained people into an untested design. I would test it myself first until I knew exactly what it did in every wind, every load. I would train anyone who flew until they knew what the wind felt like and what to do when it changed. An engineer owes the people under his work better than I had given Sabinus.

Not yet, I thought. Maybe not ever in a war where three days is a fact and a slave's no is a sound. But not until I'd proved it. Not until whoever went up had been up a hundred times and knew what they were in.

The fire burned low. Pamphilus fed it a stick. The water started to bubble at the edges, the first small bubbles before the full boil, and he watched it with the patience of a man who has been told exactly when to take it off and trusts the instruction.

I held the piece of cloth until it was cold, and then I put it in the fire.
