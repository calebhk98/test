# Chapter 33: The Four Consulars

The first I heard, I did not hear it. I felt the city stop talking.

It came on over a span of days, a slow change in the air before you can name what changed. The wharf was loud as it always was, carters cursing and gulls fighting over guts, and then it was loud with a different quality - men talking close and short and not laughing at the ends of things, because that laughter is how a man on the street shows a stranger he means him no harm, and that season nobody was showing anybody anything. The bread-woman who would not gossip in the winter now would not take the coin from your hand without looking at who was behind you in the line. A grain-factor I had dealt with for years, a fat easy man who told the same three jokes, came to settle an account and said nothing else, and left a gift he had brought to give me sitting in his own basket, having decided on the walk over that it was a bad year to be seen giving the Thulean a gift.

I did not know the names yet. I knew the weather of them. All of Rome had taken a step back from every doorway at once, and I stood in the middle of it not knowing which way the wind that moved them was blowing, which is the worst place a frightened man can stand, because he cannot even make himself small in the right direction.

---

Marcia had the names before I did. She had them as she had everything, off the rolls and off the men who kept the rolls, the factors and the bank-table men who lend at the bridge and hear a thing the morning it is true.

"Four," she said. We were in the tally-room with the door shut, which we did not do, the lying clock dripping into its bowl. "Four of the consulars. Killed."

I did not understand the word at first in the sense she meant it. I knew consulars. Men who had held the consulship, the top of the heap, the names you put on a year. I had stood in rooms with them and not been spoken to.

"Tried?" I said. It was a fool's question. I asked it because I wanted the answer to be yes.

"No." She wrote nothing. That was how I knew. Marcia writes when a problem can be worked. "Taken and killed. On the prefect's word. Attianus. While the boy is still on the road." She said the names the way you set down stones, to be sure of the count. Palma. Celsus. A third I had heard in the winter as a soldier's name, the Moor, the one the army out east had loved, Quietus. A fourth she gave and I lost, a long name, Nigrinus, that I could not have picked out of a crowd if you had paid me, and that is the one that frightened me most, because I understood I could die for being on the wrong side of a man whose name I could not even hold in my head.

"For what," I said.

"A plot." She let the word sit there with us. "There is always a plot. Four of the best men of the dead emperor's making, and a plot was found, and they were dealt with before the new one is even in the city to be asked whether he wanted it done." She put her two hands flat on the table, which she does when she is making herself be still. "This is the house being cleaned before the master gets home. By the steward, so the master's hands are clean of it. Whether the boy ordered it or will arrive and grieve over it and lay it at Attianus's door does not matter to the men in the ground. It is being done by men with no name on the year who want to be useful to the one coming, and a man trying to be useful to a new master before the master arrives is the most dangerous animal there is, because he has to guess what will please, and he guesses big."

I had built a life on guessing what would please a man with power. It is the only trade I have ever truly had.

"They were senators," I said. "Consulars. I'm a freedman who flew a bag."

"You're a freedman who flew a bag," she said, "with the dead emperor's own name. Whose work is dedicated into a god's keeping with a college that can ask a question any day it likes. Who is, on three rolls I could fetch you before the lamp burns down, written as the man of a senator of the conquest party." She picked the stylus up, and put it down again without writing, which I had never once seen her do. "They are not coming for you. You are not a fish that size. But the net does not care what it catches up on the way to the fish it wants. And there is a man in this city who has wanted you in a net for fifteen years and now has a season where nobody is asking too closely why a man dies."

She meant Crispus. She did not need to name him. We had both lived under that name long enough that it had stopped needing saying, the way you stop saying the name of the river when everyone in the room is wet.

---

I went to Scaeva.

Scaeva had leaned in on the Caelian and tipped a room and made me his. I had hated the public collar of it, *Scaeva's man*, but a collar that a strong hand holds the other end of is a comfort when the dogs are out. He held my sulfur secret, and we held debts to each other, and the arithmetic of all that was tangled but it came to something I could lean on, or I had thought it did, for five years.

I went at the dawn hour, with the clients, in a clean toga, as a client goes. His house on the Quirinal stood open with a great man's morning openness, to show the world how many men want a word.

I was not let in.

It was done so that no one could ever point at the doing of it. The doorman knew me. He had taken my name into that house a dozen times. He looked at me as though working out where he had seen the face, and then a freedman came out, a sleek young one I half knew from the dinners, with a face arranged into regret, and he did not take my hand.

"The senator is not receiving this morning," he said.

"I'll wait," I said. There were thirty men waiting. Waiting is what the dawn is for.

"He is not receiving," the freedman said again, and there was a small weight on the second saying that I understood in my body before my head caught it up, "for some while. He has a great deal of business this season. He has asked that those who can manage their own affairs manage them, and not trouble the house until the city is quieter." He looked, very briefly, at the purple I did not wear and could never wear, at the freedman's clothes under my citizen's toga, and the regret on his face did not change at all, which was how I knew it had never been regret. "I'll tell him you came. He'll be glad you're well."

Glad I was well. A man tells you he is glad you are well when he has decided he will not stand between you and not being well.

I sent a tablet, two days on. A short one, nothing in it a clerk could read sideways, only that I would value a quarter of an hour on a matter of the house's business, the river-gate business, said in the words we had used for years. It came back, in the hand of a slave I did not know, the seal unbroken, with a word from the freedman that the senator's affairs at the gate were settled and would need no further attention from me, and that he advised me, as a friend advises a friend, to attend to my own and trouble no one with what could be carried alone.

The river-gate business was the sulfur. *Settled and would need no further attention from me.* He was telling me, in the clean words of a man who does not put a knife in writing, that the favor was withdrawn, the gate no longer smoothed. And the other thing under it, which a man hears with the back of his neck. *Trouble no one.* You are not my man. You were never my man. If a clerk drawing a line through the rolls comes to this door and asks was the Thulean yours, this door will be surprised to hear the name.

He had not denounced me. A denunciation that season was a coin you could spend on your own safety, and he did not spend it. He let go of the rope and stepped back and dusted his hands where men could see him do it, so that anyone watching the senator Scaeva that spring would see a man with no barbarian on his leash. He was a conquest-party man with a dead emperor's wars all over his career, exactly the kind the net was out for, and he had looked at me and seen a smell on his own clothes, and taken off the coat. I had spent two months drawing up the very same sums about myself in Marcia's tally-room. I had just not yet thought of myself as a smell on someone else's coat.

I walked home with my own toga feeling like a costume a child had put on, and did not go down the wide way, and kept finding myself reading the faces of men coming the other way to see whether any of them were reading mine.

---

Macer I went to last, because Macer was the one I could least afford to lose, which is the order a coward does things in, saving the worst for when there is nothing else to try.

Twenty years I had gone to that door, the door of the man who owned me, then of my patron, and somewhere in there, without either of us ever saying a soft word about it, it had become a door I went to the way you go to a thing that will be where you left it. He was past seventy now and built like a beam that has carried a roof so long the roof would fall before the beam did. He kept me standing in the atrium, which he had never done, and came out himself, which he also had never done, he sent the steward, and the two wrong things together told me before he opened his mouth.

"You shouldn't have come up the hill," he said. No *boy*. No *Thulean*. He had a way of saying those that was the nearest thing to fondness he let himself, and they were gone, and the room was colder for the lack of them than for anything he said after.

"I needed to talk to you."

"I know what you need." He turned his ring, the heavy gold one, his thumb working it round and round, and I thought of Lucanus on the press-room floor turning the dead man's buckle the same way, learning a frightened man's trick off the men around him. "You need an old man with frontier names in his account-books to stand in a door this spring and say the Thulean is a good fellow, leave him be. That's what you need." He stopped turning the ring. "I'm not going to do it."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You came up the hill. That's the asking." He came a step nearer and dropped his voice, and that was the worst of it, because Macer does not drop his voice, Macer says the crude thing at the volume of a man selling timber, and a Macer who has learned to be quiet is a Macer who has been made afraid, and I had only seen the edge of that in the winter and now I was seeing all of it. "Listen to me, because I'm going to say it once and then you're going to go home the back way. There are four of the best men in this city in the river or under quicklime this month, not one of them tried, and I am a leather-contractor whose name is on the legion rolls for thirty years of feeding the dead man's army. I am exactly the size of fish a hungry steward keeps for the new master. I have spent the winter making myself small and dull and loyal to nobody anyone can name, and I am not undoing it by being seen a friend to the one man in Rome whose every brick has the dead emperor's name baked into it."

"Titus." I had not called him that in years.

"Don't." He said it flat. "I sold you to Caesar fair and I was glad, you know I was glad, you ungrateful barbarian. I'm not your enemy. If a man comes to my door and asks was the Thulean ever mine, I'll tell him the truth, that the emperor bought you off me a long time ago and I've had nothing to do with your sky-bags and your burning tubes since, and that's no lie, and it'll cost you nothing and me nothing, which is the only kind of true thing worth telling this year." He looked at me, and there was an old man behind the fear of his, one who had taken a cheap oddity for less than a season's mules and watched it turn into this. "But I will not burn for you. I've got a household and grandsons and a name to leave them and I am not setting a torch to all of it to keep one clever foreigner out of a net he flew himself into the middle of. So stop coming to my door. Make yourself small. Make yourself dull. Make yourself the kind of nothing nobody bothers to denounce, because that, boy," and the *boy* came out then, the only one, and it landed like a hand on the back of the neck, not unkind, "is the one thing that's going to save you, and the one thing you have never once in your life known how to be."

He turned the ring once more. Then he had the steward show me out, by the back, through the yard where the hides were stacked, into a lane that smelled of tanning, and the door shut behind me with the ordinary sound a door makes.

He had warned me. That was what I carried down the hill, turning it as I had turned the buckle. He had looked at the smell of me and chosen not to denounce, only to warn, and the difference between those two things was the whole bond we had, worn to a wire, and the wire had held. It was not nothing. In the lane, with the door shut, it only felt like nothing.

---

The scare came at the yard, eleven days later, and it came in through the front, smiling, which is how it comes.

He was a man of forty or so, compact, the kind who moves without appearing to decide to move, the way of a man who has spent years watching a room without being watched in return. His tunic was good cloth but chosen plain, nothing that would catch an eye at either end of the street. He had a tablet under one arm and a slave behind him of the useful, forgettable sort, and a manner that was a hair too easy, the ease of a man who has practiced it until he cannot put it down. He said he wanted his account looked at, got up as a small importer, oils and the like.

Marcia took him, because Marcia takes the front, and I was at the back of the press room and saw him through the door and felt my skin go before I knew why. He was not buying oil. His questions wandered. He admired the press, asked could he see it work, asked what we printed, asked whether it was true the Thulean's marks were said before a god each spring on the Aventine, asked it the way a man asks a thing he already knows, to watch your face while you give it. He let drop that a friend of his in the colleges had said an interesting thing about the dedication, about whether everything off the Thulean's press fell rightly under the goddess's keeping or whether some of it went somewhere the rite did not cover. He used the word *impiety* once, lightly, setting it on the table to see if anyone's eyes went to it.

I did not let my eyes go to it. I do not know how. I think I was too frightened to do anything but the thing I had done my whole life, which is to put a stupid pleasant Thulean over my face and stand behind it.

Marcia was faster than I was, and colder, and magnificent in a fashion that does not look like anything, which is the only fashion that works. She did not throw him out. She bored him. She answered everything long and dull, the prices of cloth, the cost of pearwood, the schoolmasters who bought the times-tables, the exact denarius the heifer had cost on the Aventine and who the priest was and how the rite went and how proud the house was, how very proud, to have its little marks under the goddess's eye, and would the gentleman like a sheet, here, a gift, a true account of wheat-weights any honest grain-man could use. She buried him in the harmless until the harm could not breathe in it. The press she walked him to was the school press; the man who ran the other one, the one Eros kept the cut blocks for, had been sent on an errand the moment Marcia heard the front-door manner; and the room where I keep the work that is mine, the box under the floor, the leaves in the homeland script with the ratios in them, was shut, with a girl sitting against the door of it mending a basket as if she had sat there all her life.

That was Tyche. She had moved the box the night the city went quiet, without being told, and she sat against the shut door mending a basket with her short-cut hair and her face that gives away exactly nothing, a freedwoman of no account at all, and she let the oil-man's slave glance over her head into the room and see a girl and a basket and shelves of ordinary scrolls, and she did not look up.

The man left. He took the gift sheet. He said he would think about the oil. We never saw him again, and I never learned for certain whose he was, the colleges' or the prefect's or Crispus's or some independent delator's own, a man who sniffed at doors on his own account hoping to sell what he found to whoever would buy that season, which was the worst possibility, because a hungry man with no master cannot be reasoned with.

I did not win it. I did not best him with a clever word. Marcia bored him and Tyche hid the dangerous half of my whole life behind a basket and a girl's blank face, and the rest was the pure dumb luck that he found, on the day he came, a dull cloth-house with a school-press and a proud little rite and no purchase for a man's hand to close on. If he had come the week before, with the second press running and the wrong leaves out, I would have had no clever word for that either. I sat on the floor of the press room after, my heart going like a fist on a door.

For a month after, I made myself small as Macer had told me to. I sent no tablets to senators. I let the contest rite be prepared exactly to the colleges' liking and stood at the back of my own enterprise and thanked the goddess in the words a priest gave me, and bowed lower than I had ever bowed. I took my name off things. Marcia put the new contracts under hers, *Marcia, liberta*, the widow of the merchant Caepio, a respectable nobody of a name, and where mine had been the security on an agreement she found another to be it. I went down to the wharf in a plain tunic and let men forget they had been afraid to be seen near me, by giving them less of me to be seen near.

I had drunk foul water in front of an emperor to make that name. Now I was teaching myself to walk down a lane in such a way that no one would think to put the name to me, and I did it, because a man who will not be a beetle when the boot is coming down is a dead one.

---

The press was running when I came up from the wharf in my plain tunic at the end of it, the school press, the only one I dared run that spring, putting wheat-weights and times-tables onto cheap gray sheets for an as apiece, the smallest and safest work the house made.

I had caught myself reaching for Heras out of habit and stopped. I had stopped going to his door too, because a man who loved me was something I could no longer afford to be seen to have. Celer was three years in the eastern ground for a province struck off a map. Scaeva had taken off the coat. Macer had warned me through a shut door, which was the most a friend had given me in a month. No emperor knew my name except as a line a tired clerk wanted to draw through. The life I had spent twenty years building, the un-ignorable Thulean who flew and counted and burst tubes for great men, was the very asset that could get me killed, and the only freedom I had left was the freedom to be forgotten, which I now wanted with my whole body, the way I had once wanted to be seen.

Lucanus came and leaned on me while I checked a sheet. He had the buckle. He always had the buckle now. He was sounding out the figures under his finger, slow, getting the seven and the one the wrong way round as they all did at first.

"That's a seven," I said, and turned his finger to it.

He fixed it and went on counting, and did not ask whose anything was, because he had not heard a frightened thing said over his head that week, we had been careful, and a child who hears no fear asks no questions about it. He counted the sheet to the bottom and got it right and looked up to be told so, and I told him, and he leaned his whole small weight against my arm as they do when they have decided the leaning is allowed, and went back to the top to do it again.

I let him count, and made myself as small and dull and nothing as a man can make himself while a child counts against his arm, and the press came down, and came down, putting the safe true small thing onto the cheap gray page, an as at a time, in a city that had shut its doors, and no one was coming up the hill to mine, which was the thing I had wanted. The room with the one press running was about the size of a cell, and I knew exactly how that compared, having begun in one.
