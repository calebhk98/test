# Chapter 29: Overreach

The word came up the road the way good news always came, ahead of the proof of it, carried by men who had heard it from men, so that by the time it reached the camps behind Antioch it had been polished smooth as a coin passed through too many hands. Ctesiphon was taken. The great city on the river, the Parthian's own seat, had fallen, and Caesar had stood in the king's own hall. The legions had gone down the rivers to the place where the rivers ended and the water turned bitter and went on forever, the southern sea, the edge of the maps, and there was no more east to take.

I heard it from a wine-factor at the quay and then from a wounded optio in a returning cart and then from a tribune's slave who had it, he swore, from a man who had been there. In every telling it got bigger and emptier at once. They had reached the sea. They had loaded a ship and seen it stand out toward lands that paid no tax to Parthia or to Rome or to anyone. And the old man, they said, the emperor, had stood on the shore in the heat with the bitter water at his feet and watched a merchant ship go out toward India and said he wished he were younger, that he might cross over after Alexander, and the men around him had laughed because it was the kind of thing he said, and then had not laughed, because of the look on him.

I did not see it. I want that plain. I was a great many miles behind the shore where the old man stood wishing he were a boy, standing over a fire telling a centurion to wait for the bubbles, and everything I know of the high-water mark of the world I know the way the people in a town know about a flood that took the next valley, from the brown line it leaves and the things it floats down to you afterward. What floated down to me was the strangeness in the men who told it. They told it as a victory and their faces told it as something else, the way a man's face does when he has eaten until he is sick and is still being handed plates. They had taken everything. There was nowhere left to point. A Roman with nowhere to point is a frightening thing, like a draft horse in a stall too small to turn around in, and I stood at the bottom of the world's longest supply line and felt the whole weight of it arrive at a wall it had not known was there and begin, very slowly, to lean back on the people who had pushed it.

---

The leaning back was a smell before it was anything else.

I had learned to read a camp the way a sailor reads water, by the things under the surface, and what I read first was the grain. There was less of it coming up the road, and then it was coming up the road wrong, in guarded clumps with too many soldiers walking beside too few carts, and the soldiers walking beside it had the look of men who had been shot at. A supply line is a held breath. You do not notice it until it catches. Ours caught somewhere behind us, down toward the rivers and the great taken cities, and the catching came up to me as rumor and short rations and a quartermaster swearing over a tally that no longer balanced because the figures going out the far end were not coming back as anything but trouble.

The cities had risen. That was the shape of it as it reached me, and I will not pretend I understood it better than I did, because I did not understand it at all, and the men who told me understood it less and were more certain. The taken places would not stay taken. They had bent the knee while the legions stood in their streets, and the legions had gone on east to the bitter sea, and there were too few of them left in the rear to hold what the front had grabbed. Garrisons cut off. A column ambushed in a place that had been Roman a month before. Killing in towns whose names I heard three different ways, and a kind of killing I understood even less, a rising of peoples I had only heard of as words, somewhere south and west, whole communities turning on their neighbors and being turned on, a fire that had nothing to do with Parthia and burned hotter than Parthia ever had. I heard it as a Roman heard it, which is to say wrong, as madness and treachery and the gods withdrawing their favor. I knew only that the line I kept alive with clean water now ran through country that wanted it dead, and that the further the army had reached, the thinner the hand was that held the ground behind its back, until the ground simply closed up behind it like water behind a swimmer.

The army was a fist that had punched all the way through and now could not pull its arm back out.

---

Celer's cohort was in the rear, on the supply road, which had been a dull safe duty in the summer and was the most dangerous place in the east by autumn, because the road was the thing the rising wanted to cut, and a cohort spread thin along a road is a cohort spread thin along the exact thing everyone is trying to kill.

I knew this the way I knew most things, by arithmetic, lying awake. So I was not surprised when they brought him to me. I had been half waiting to be surprised by it for weeks, which is a different and worse way to wait.

They brought him on a cart, with four of his men around it who would not leave even when an optio told them to, in the gray before a dawn, the way the carts always came. He was awake. That was the first thing, and I let it be a good thing for the space of one breath before I made myself look properly. He had taken a thrust low on the left side, below the ribs, in a night action on the road where his men had held the carts and lost six and held them. Somebody had bound it on the road with whatever was to hand, which was the trouble, because whatever was to hand on a road in that country was filth. It had been two days and a half. The binding when I cut it away came off in a way I had smelled in other men and learned to dread, and under it the edges of the wound were the wrong color and the wrong heat, and the skin around it had gone a tight shine that ran further than it should, and I knew, with the part of me that had spent fifteen years keeping armies from dying of exactly this, what I was looking at.

"Don't," Celer said. He was watching my face the way he watched everything, weighing it. "I've seen that look on enough surgeons. Save it for someone who hasn't seen the inside of a tent before."

"It needs cleaning."

"It needs the gods," he said, "but clean it, by all means, if it gives you something to do with your hands. I always said you'd kill me with washing."

I cleaned it. I am going to set down what I did, because it is the only part of this I get to keep, and because a man should be able to say exactly how far short everything he had reached.

I had water boiled fresh and cooled, more of it than the camp could spare, and I washed the wound and the country of skin around it until the cloths came away less foul, and I cut away what my knife told me was already dead, which made him swear at me steadily in three languages and grip the cart-rail with a hand that went white, and I packed it with linen boiled and dried clean and bound it loose so it could weep instead of holding the rot in against him the way the road-binding had. I gave him posca to drink and washed it on the wound besides, the sour wine that I half believed and half only hoped did something. I built him a place off the road, by clean water I had sited myself, and I changed the dressing twice a day and boiled everything between, and I made the men who tended him scald their hands until they hated me, and I did every single thing I knew.

I knew a great deal. I knew more than any man within five hundred miles about keeping the invisible climbing things out of a body. And I knew, washing his side at noon on the first full day, watching the shine on the skin reach a thumb's width further than it had at dawn, that I had come to the wall I had spent my life pretending I could not see clearly, the one Heras had named for me a lifetime ago and I had not listened. The little creatures were already inside him. They had been put inside him on a dark road by an iron point and two days of a dirty rag, before I ever cut the binding, and there is no clean water in the world that washes out a thing that is already in the blood. Everything I had was for keeping them out. I had nothing for getting them back. I never had. I had been able to save the army from this for fifteen years only by reaching every man before the road did, and the road had reached Celer first.

---

He went down the way that wound goes down, which is to say not steadily, which is the cruelest part of it, because it leaves you room to be a fool.

The first day he was himself and angry at being on his back. He had his optio come and report and he gave orders about the cohort and the carts and where the road was weakest and he was right about all of it, his voice flat and clipped and exactly as it had always been, and I let myself think, against everything the skin was telling me, that he was a tougher animal than the rule. The second day the fever came up the way the tide had come up around the old man's feet at the bitter sea, slow, certain, with nowhere built into it for it to stop. He sweated through the bedding and I changed it and boiled it. He drank the water I gave him. The shine on his side went up past the wound and around toward his back and the smell of it came through the clean dressings no matter how often I changed them, and I changed them, by lamp and by sun, and the changing did nothing but let me look at it and know.

Naso came. They had kept him in the rear with the baggage and the mules, his ruined hand curled to his chest, his ankle still putting its hitch in his walk, and he came and sat where he could be useful, which was holding water and carrying out the foul cloths to be burned, the one good hand doing the work of the bad one's absence. He did not say much. He had buried things in his own lid years ago and he knew the weight of a man sitting with another man's dying. He kept the small fire I needed and he kept it clean and he kept his face still, and once, when I came back from being sick behind the tent where I thought no one could see me, he had a cup of the posca ready and he put it in my hand without a word, the way I had once watched him put his one hand over my fist on a burned rag, a long way north, a long time ago.

On the third day Celer stopped being angry, which frightened me worse than the fever, because a Celer who has stopped fighting the thing in front of him is a Celer who has done his arithmetic and got an answer. He was clear-eyed between the fevers. That is the thing about that death, that it leaves the mind alone for too long. He looked at the dressing when I changed it and he looked at the shine that had reached his back and he looked at my face doing the thing faces do when they are working very hard to be steady.

"You've gone gray over it," he said. "When did you go gray. You were a boy."

"You've gone grayer."

"I've earned mine." He let his head go back. "How long. And don't lie to me to be kind, I'll know, and I'll think less of you, and I've thought a great deal of you, which is a thing I'd as soon not undo at the end of it."

I did not say it for a moment. The water clock in Rome would have measured the moment; out there I had nothing to measure it with but my own pulse going in my ears. "Not long," I said. "A day. Two. The fever takes more of you each time it comes."

"Good," he said. "Not the fever. The knowing." He worked his dry mouth. "I've watched men go who didn't get told. They spend their last day on plans. It's an obscene thing to watch, a dying man making plans." He looked at me. "Tell me a thing. The bag. You never put another man up, after the boy. Did you ever want to? After. When they pressed you."

"Every time," I said. "I wanted to give it to them. To be done arguing."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"There," he said, and something eased in his ruined face that was as close to peace as I saw on it. "You did decide, then. What kind of man builds a thing like that. You decided it with your no, every time, for fifteen years, and never once told yourself that was the deciding." He closed his eyes. "Mind your drain," he said, which was a joke we had, and then he was asleep, or under, and the fever came up again that night and took more of him, and the day after that he was not clear anymore, and talked to men who were not there, a centurion long dead, his mother by the sound of it, and I sat with him and washed him and did the useless tender work that is all there is left to do, and Naso fed the fire, and the road outside went on moving in the dark.

---

He died before dawn on the fifth day, while I was holding the cup to his mouth and he was past drinking from it, the water running down his chin onto the clean cloth I had boiled, and there was no last word, because the wound that kills you that way does not leave you a word, it takes the words first and the breath after. The breath went out long and ragged and did not come back in, and I sat with the cup still at his lips for longer than made any sense, the way I had knelt by the burned cloth with my hands out and nothing for them to do, and then Naso reached over with his one good hand and pressed my arm, and I set the cup down.

I washed him. There was no need to wash a dead man and I washed him anyway, the way I had washed him living, the wound that had won and the rest of him the wound had wasted, and I closed his eyes with my thumb, and I straightened the kit-strap he had worried at the last night we ate together, the one his hands had wanted to keep busy on, and I left it lying neat across him because his hands could not do it now and somebody's should. His optio came and his men came, the four who would not leave the cart, and they stood in the gray with their helmets off, and I gave him over to them, because he was theirs more than mine. They wrote it down that the prefect Quintus Marcius Celer fell of a wound taken in a night action holding the supply road, which was true, every word of it, and which left out the two days and the dirty rag and the wall I had broken myself against, the way the stone in Rome leaves out the soldier in the fishing-float, the way the writing-down always leaves out the part that is the whole of it.

---

I went back to the drains. There is always work, and the work that morning was a centurion's century waiting for clean water on a road that wanted them dead, and I went and stood over the fire and waited for the bubbles, and my hands shook on the dipper so that the centurion noticed and looked away from it to give me the manners of not having seen, which was the kindest thing a soldier did for me that month.

I did the arithmetic, because I could not stop doing the arithmetic, it is the thing my hands do when the rest of me cannot be trusted. I had kept thousands of men from dying of the thing that killed him. I had it in figures, near enough, the men who would have gone down to the flux and the foul water in two wars and had not, because of clean cloth and cooled boiled water and a tedium of standing over fires. Strangers, every one, men whose names I never learned and whose children's names I was spared. And I could put every one of those saved strangers in one pan of a scale and the one man in the other and the scale would not balance, it would slam, because the strangers were saved before the thing got into them and Celer was brought to me after, and there was no cleverness in any head in that century of the world that could reach back across that little distance of two days and pull the climbing things out of his blood. My whole life's gift was a wall built to keep them out. I had no door in it for going in after the ones already inside. I never built one. I did not know how. No one would know how for longer than my life, longer than the lives of everyone burning a foul dressing on that road, and I stood over the fire and understood that the very thing that made me worth sending east, the saving, had a floor in it, and the floor was where my friend was, and I had been standing on it for fifteen years calling it the top of a ladder.

The army would not hold what it had taken. I knew it the way I had known the wind was wrong over the Loaf, in the body before the mind would say it, watching the grain come up the road in guarded clumps and the empty carts come back full of the gray-faced and the worse-than-gray-faced from places that had been ours a month before. A thing reaches as far as it can hold and then it reaches a little further, because reaching is the only thing it knows how to do, and the little further is the part that kills it, the part that closes up behind it like water. Someone, some day, was going to have to stand at the front of all that overreached length and do the thing no Roman in my hearing had ever once said aloud, which was to let go of ground that had been taken, to pull the arm back out of the wall, to draw a line somewhere short of everywhere and write here, and not past here. I did not know who. I did not know it was coming. I knew only that it had to come, the way I knew Celer had been right that you run out of world or out of us and one of the two always comes first, and that out here, on this road, in this autumn, we had run out of both at once.

---

I did not point the hunger west to anyone. The thought was in me harder than it had been, hardened by the smell of a clean dressing over a wound that had already won, by the brown enormous emptiness the empty basket had hung over, by the figures of grain that would not come back as anything but trouble. The east was a dead end. It was desert and rivers with foreign names and cities that would not stay knelt, and it had eaten the old man's wish to be young again and it had eaten Celer below the ribs, and every instinct I had, the saving instinct and the turning-the-wheel instinct both, said the want would have to go somewhere or it would go on grinding up the people it could reach, and I knew a place it could go that was empty, because I had drawn it empty on a tablet in a cell before I could speak. But there was no one to say it to. Celer was the one man in the east I might have tried it on, the one who would have heard the arithmetic in it and the obscenity in it both and told me which it was, and the road had reached him first.

So I kept it. I kept it the way I kept everything, in the only homeland I had left, which was the inside of my own skull, and I went on standing over fires.

Naso left with the next baggage train going back toward Antioch, mended enough, alive, his hand curled to his chest, his ankle hitching him up onto the cart. He stopped beside me before he climbed up. He looked at me a while with the face that had stopped being young a long way north. Then he took, from inside his tunic, the kit-strap I had left lying neat across the body, the one Celer's hands had worried at, that I had not noticed anyone take, and he put it into my hand and folded my fingers over it with his one good hand, the way you close a child's fist around a thing it is too tired to hold, and he climbed up onto the cart and did not say anything, because there was nothing the right size to say, and the train pulled out east to west along the road that the rising would cut again before the moon changed.

I did not put the strap in the fire. I had burned the last thing I carried, on a riverbank a world ago, and I was older now and had learned that burning a thing is only another way of keeping the smell of it. I worked it into my own kit instead, where my hands could find it in the dark and worry at it the way his had, and I went back down the line to the next century and the next fire and the next grown men who had killed other men and would now stand and wait, because I told them to, for the water to boil.
