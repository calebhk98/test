## SECTION 1: THE V1 FAILURE

V1 committed a systematic error: **everyone who speaks sounds too smart, too
eloquent, and too perceptive.** No character is dull, inarticulate, crude-mouthed,
or confidently wrong. Class is shown through power, deference, and content — never
through rough grammar, thin vocabulary, or halting syntax. When a character would
have had to sound uneducated, V1 made them silent. When a character would have
had to swear, V1 reported it indirectly ("something soldier's-rough I didn't catch")
rather than quoting it. Even a 10-year-old gets the chapter's thematic capstone.
The one "slow" character was secretly performing slowness — a genius in disguise.

V2 fixes this by: (1) giving inarticulate characters inarticulate dialogue;
(2) quoting at least some crude speech rather than muting or reporting it;
(3) including characters who are wrong and stay wrong; (4) resisting the habit
of side characters landing the scene's sharpest line.

## SECTION 1.5 — THE NARRATION/DIALOGUE SPLIT (CRITICAL FOR DANIEL)

**This is the single most important technical rule in the book for Daniel.**

Daniel narrates retrospectively as an old man writing late in his life. He has had
sixty years to process what happened, to find the right words, to achieve perspective.
His **narration voice** CAN be mature, dry, ironic, clause-heavy, and sophisticated.
The old man looking back earns that register. Do not flatten it.

**But his quoted DIALOGUE must sound like the 17-year-old (or 25-year-old, or
40-year-old) he was at the time of the scene.**

These are different people. The narration is written by the old man. The dialogue was
spoken by the young man. Agents must not bleed the narrator's sophistication into what
Daniel actually said at the time.

### The failure mode this rule prevents:

A writer reads the narrator's voice — sophisticated, controlled, dry — and lets it
bleed into Daniel's spoken words. The result is dialogue that is too clean, too
formed, too complete. A 17-year-old who has just been told his invention will kill
people does not say "I know." He says "Yeah. I know. I know, okay? I —" and stops.
A 25-year-old selling an idea to a skeptic does not produce a polished three-point
argument aloud. He hedges, backs up, says "I mean —" and tries again.

### The test:

Read Daniel's dialogue aloud in your own voice. If it sounds like something a writer
polished, it's wrong. If it sounds like something a real person actually said, out
loud, in a situation — it might be right.

### What the split looks like in practice:

**NARRATION (old man, retrospective — CAN be sophisticated):**
> "I did not understand yet that the most dangerous thing I could become, in that
> city, was valuable. I thought I was solving a problem. I was, in fact, making one."

**DIALOGUE (the young man at the time — must sound like it was spoken):**
> "Look — okay, so the thing is, I think this part works. I've actually tested it,
> kind of. So it's not just — I'm not just guessing. The part I'm not sure about is
> the other part. Which, yeah, is a problem."

**WRONG (narrator voice bleeding into spoken dialogue):**
> "I do not know whether to be glad it already exists or to be sad that I did not
> think of it."
> "I know." *(flat, as sole response to being told his work will cause deaths)*

**RIGHT:**
> "Huh. Okay. That — huh. I mean, that's actually — wait, why didn't I — okay,
> this is good. This is fine. I just need a minute."
> "Yeah. I know. I know, okay? I — yeah."

### Required features of Daniel's spoken dialogue (especially chapters 1–25):
- Contractions, always. "I don't" not "I do not." "It's" not "it is."
- Hedging: "I think," "kind of," "sort of," "I mean —", "maybe," "probably"
- Incomplete thoughts: trailing off mid-sentence when scared or uncertain
- Filler as texture: "like" as a marker, "you know," pauses written as dashes
- Second-guessing mid-statement: "It's gonna — I mean, it might not — yeah, okay,
  it's probably gonna work but I've never actually done it at this scale."
- Mild swearing under stress (shit, damn, hell, occasionally stronger; PG-13 level)
- Asking questions he should know the answer to because he's nervous
- Starting sentences with "I mean —" and not finishing the thought cleanly
- Restating himself: saying the same thing twice in slightly different words
  because the first version didn't sound right

### Explicitly forbidden in Daniel's spoken dialogue:
- "I know." as a flat complete response to hard truths — too adult, too settled
- "Whether to be glad... or sad..." constructions — no teenager phrases it this way
- Perfectly formed arguments with clean rhetorical structure
- Philosophical summations that land too cleanly
- Rhetorical questions that arrive already answered
- Formal diction or elevated vocabulary that the narration uses but the speaking
  voice wouldn't have
- Delivering the chapter's thematic mic-drop line himself

### What he CAN do that sounds authentic:
- "I mean —" starts that don't finish grammatically
- Trailing off mid-sentence: "It's just — yeah."
- Swearing under his breath when scared: "Oh, shit. Okay. Okay."
- Asking questions he should know the answer to: "That's — that's not permanent,
  right? That can come out?"
- Hedging every claim: "I think this works. I'm pretty sure. Mostly sure."
- Second-guessing himself mid-statement: "This is good — I mean, it's fine, it's
  not great, but it'll — yeah, it's fine."
- Repeating a key word twice when landing a difficult point: "It works. It actually
  works. I just need — it works."

### As Daniel ages (chapters 30+):

His spoken voice CAN mature — he has spent thirty years speaking Latin and living
in a culture that prizes rhetorical precision. His dialogue becomes more controlled,
more deliberate. But even at 50, his narration is more polished than his dialogue.
The teenager does not disappear — he becomes better at hiding. Under stress or grief,
the teenage register should surface. The "I mean —" comes back. The trailing off
comes back. He reverts to type when the stakes get high enough.

## SECTION 2: THE VOICE RANGE SPECTRUM

From most to least educated/eloquent. Every character should be writable from this
map without reference tags; voice alone should sort them.

---

### TIER 1 — SENATORIAL (Scaeva, occasionally Hadrian's formal register)
**Markers:** Long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences. Allusion as social currency.
Favor-language; obligation-language. Never states a threat directly — it is always
the implication hanging in a pause. Quotes other writers. References history
and precedent. Comfortable with ambiguity because ambiguity is power.

**Example:**
> "I have mentioned your name in three rooms this week, all of them rooms you
> would not be invited to unmentioned. I do this because I find your work
> genuinely interesting, which is a rarer thing than you might suppose. There is
> no obligation attached, of course. There is never any obligation attached."

**What NOT to write for this tier:**
> "I want something from you and you'll pay if you don't give it."
(Too direct; senatorial men never say the transaction out loud.)

---

### TIER 2 — EDUCATED GREEK (Heras, Apollodorus, Eudemus, and Crispus's affectation)
**Markers:** Dry, ironic, compressed. Aphorism is natural but not performed.
Quotes come easily. Speaks from a position of intellectual authority that does
not need to announce itself. Disagrees by taking apart your reasoning, not by
raising his voice. Uses Greek tags or Greek concepts as reference points.

**Heras specifically:** teasing warmth underneath the dryness; asks questions
rather than issuing verdicts; admits what he was wrong about, but never while
you're watching.

**Apollodorus specifically:** no warmth; serene total contempt; repeats his
verdict forever without needing you to agree; trade metaphors (stone, water,
plumb). He is not teasing — he is indifferent.

**Heras example (DO):**
> "I notice you have come back to my house three times this month. I notice
> also that each time you have a question that could have been sent by letter.
> I do not mind. I have always preferred the question that didn't fit a letter."

**Heras example (DON'T — too warm, too plain):**
> "It's good to see you, Daniel. I'm glad you came."

**Apollodorus example (DO):**
> "The rope holds. The fire does not. You have built a thing that knows this
> better than you do and will demonstrate it at the worst possible moment."

**Apollodorus example (DON'T — too collegial):**
> "You're onto something, but the fire's the problem. Let me know if you figure it out."

---

### TIER 3 — EQUESTRIAN BUSINESSMEN (Macer, Marcia)
**Markers:** Blunt, transactional, everything priced. No performance. Correct about
money nearly always. Does not quote anyone. No philosophical register. Humor is
mean or deflating. The difference between Macer and Marcia: Macer states and
exits; Marcia argues and structures.

**Macer specifically:** states cost, moves on. Never argues. "The Thulean" or
"boy" as address. Gold ring on the thumb. Mean joke as a form of approval. His
cruelty and his usefulness are the same instrument. ONE crude oath somewhere in
the book, when genuinely angry.

**Marcia specifically:** lays out the whole case, every side, having pre-decided.
Tests people before she trusts them. "Don't be charming at her... she counts the
spoons after." Long paragraphs, structured arguments. Less terse than Macer.

**Macer example (DO):**
> "Forty thousand. And I'm being kind, which you should remember later when you
> tell yourself I wasn't."

**Macer example (DO — crude oath, one occasion):**
> "Gods-rot that carter. Three seasons I've paid him and he's been shorting me
> since the second. Get him in here."

**Macer example (DON'T — too warm):**
> "I know this is hard for you, boy. I respect what you're doing here."

**Marcia example (DO):**
> "Everything is a toy until it makes money. Then it's a business, and a business
> has a hundred ways to die, and the ones you didn't see coming are the interesting
> ones. So: what haven't you seen coming."

**Marcia example (DON'T — too terse, sounds like Tyche):**
> "It'll work. Money's the problem. Fix the money."

---

### TIER 4 — FREEDMEN CRAFTSMEN (Hermes, Felix, Vitalis)
**Markers:** Terse, physical, technical vocabulary within their trade; thin
vocabulary outside it. Grammar roughly correct but not ornate. No subordinate
clauses of any complexity. No allusions. No quotations. Humor absent (Hermes)
or straightforward (Felix). Disagrees with a specific factual objection, not a
rhetorical framework. Speaks about what he can do and measure; silent about what
he cannot.

**Hermes specifically:** hammer-paced fragments. Demands a number. "Tell me how
much / how hot / how long." "Then you've told me nothing." Reduce every problem
to a production variable. Not cruel — just has no room for theory.

**Felix specifically:** more talkative than Hermes. Gets enthusiastic and needs
to be brought back. Tells a story when a number would do. Warmer.

**Hermes example (DO):**
> "Black sand. How much."
> [Daniel gives a ratio.]
> "How hot."
> [Daniel gestures vaguely.]
> "Then you've told me nothing."

**Hermes example (DON'T — too polished):**
> "The ratio matters less than the temperature. If you can give me a replicable
> heat, I can work with whatever ratio you bring me."

**Felix example (DO):**
> "I tried the first batch the way you said and it blew the mold, which —
> honestly — it was something to see. Not useful. But something to see.
> Second batch I went slower and got nothing. Third batch—"
> [Marcia: "Felix."]
> "Right. Third batch: nothing useful."

---

### TIER 5 — ARMY (Celer, Naso, walk-on soldiers)
**Markers:** Clipped. Imperatives. Asks one practical question. Books consequences
as ledger entries ("the men I bury are the ones who weren't"). Gallows humor
expressed as matter-of-fact. Frontier vocabulary. One crude oath per soldier,
somewhere, when under stress.

**Celer specifically:** clipped flat, no music, no ornament. States what is true
and what will be done. Tenderness only as a clipped order. "Said something low
and soldier's-rough I didn't catch" — V2: quote it once.

**Naso specifically (pre-accident):** cocky, dim, fearless in the way of someone
who has not yet been hurt. Coarse. Certain of things he has no basis for. Not
evil — just young and stupid.

**Celer example (DO):**
> "How far down the river could that be seen."

**Celer example — the quoted oath (V2 minimum):**
> [After something goes badly wrong with the fire]
> "Hercules' *balls*. Get them back. All of them, now."

**Celer example (DON'T — too considered):**
> "I understand your concerns, but the tactical value outweighs the risk we've
> just seen demonstrated."

**Naso example, pre-accident (DO):**
> "Any man can hold a rope. What's the sky going to do, fall on us?"

**Naso example (DON'T — too self-aware):**
> "I'm not afraid. I've been in worse situations."
(Too adult; Naso is specifically dim and fearless, not calm and brave.)

---

### TIER 6 — URBAN WORKING CLASS (Tyche's early register, Pamphilus)
**Markers:** Tyche starts here; Pamphilus stays here.

**Tyche (early):** flat, city-quick, declarative, no slowing for the foreigner.
Short sentences. States the fact without wrapping it in sentiment. Not
inarticulate — she is sharp — but economical to the point of bluntness.
As she develops: grows more complex but the fragments remain.

**Pamphilus:** below Tyche even at her most guarded. Very short sentences.
Ungrammatical. Gets words wrong. Swears tiredly at objects. No subordinate
clauses. No abstractions. Understands more than he can say; says it badly.

**Tyche (early) example (DO):**
> "The yard is by the wharf. Long walk. You should eat the bread while you walk.
> He won't give you time to eat it there."

**Tyche (early) example (DON'T — too much explanation):**
> "If you don't eat before you get there, you won't have time once you arrive,
> so I'd suggest eating on the way."

**Pamphilus example (DO):**
> "That wall. Three times I done it. Don't hold."
> (Later, to the wall:) "Gods-cursed thing."

**Pamphilus example (DO — misunderstanding an instruction):**
> "You said the big ones. These are the big ones."
> [They are not the ones Daniel meant.]

**Pamphilus example (DON'T — sounds like Tyche or Hermes):**
> "I put the heavy stack where you said. The far wall. It's not holding."

The difference: Pamphilus gets the object wrong, not just the method. He
misunderstands what was asked, then defends the misunderstanding without
being hostile about it. He is not stupid in a mean-joke way — he is
a man with limited language navigating a world that asks for more.

---

### TIER 7 — CROWD WALK-ONS (hecklers, bystanders, wharf crowds)
**Markers:** Rough, dumb, superstitious, coarse. Short. Does not understand the
technology or the situation, fills the gap with what they know (the supernatural,
the sexual, the dangerous). Not violent necessarily — just unfiltered.

**V1 failure:** "A man in the crowd shouted something I did not catch." This
recurs at ch08, ch16, ch26. Every crowd heckle is reported and softened.
**V2 fix:** Quote 2-3 actual crowd lines in at least one scene.

**Crowd example (DO):**
> "Thulean witch!" 
> "Give us the flying man — the real kind, not this goat-bladder thing!"
> "I had a dream like that once, ask my wife what I did with it!" [laughter]

**Crowd example (DO — superstitious, dumb, specific):**
> "The smoke went north! North is the bad direction!"
> "He's got the flame hidden somewhere on him. Search him. Go on, search him."

**Crowd example (DON'T — too perceptive):**
> "It's just hot air rising — he found a way to trap it."
(A crowd member who correctly explains the principle is V1's fish-seller's son
problem. Crowd hecklers should be wrong, loud, and specific.)

## SECTION 3: PER-CHARACTER QUICK REFERENCE

### DANIEL (narrator / spoken dialogue)

**THE CORE RULE:** Two registers, deliberately split. See Section 1.5.

Narration: ornate, clause-heavy, retrospective, dry, modern. The old man's voice.
Spoken dialogue: short, hedged, flat, incomplete, often concedes mid-sentence.
The two registers must never bleed into each other.

**DIALOGUE BY STAGE OF LIFE:**

*Age 17 — just arrived (chapters 1–8):*
> "I — okay. Yeah. I don't — I'm trying to explain this in a language I barely
> speak to someone I've known for like three days, so. Can we — can we just try it?
> The thing. Just try it and see."
> "I don't know, man. I have no idea. I don't know what I'm doing."
> "Okay so this is gonna sound — just hear me out. Please."

*Age ~25 — established, beginning to have standing (chapters 15–25):*
> "Okay so — I know how this sounds. I know. But I've actually done the math on
> this like four times and it keeps coming out the same, so either I'm wrong —
> which, possible — or the thing is actually gonna work. Probably."
> "I don't — look, I'm not saying it's safe. I'm saying it's the least bad option
> I can think of right now, and I've been thinking about it for three days, so."
> "I build things that float and burst, Caesar. I know enough to know that I do
> not — which about roofs is the most I have ever known."

*Age ~40 — powerful, but still himself under pressure (chapters 30–42):*
> "I'm not — this isn't a negotiation. I just want to be clear about that. I know
> it looks like a negotiation. It's not. I've already decided."
> "Yeah, I know what I said. I'm saying it differently now. That's allowed."
> "Look. Here's the thing. The thing is — okay, the thing is complicated. Give me
> a minute."

**DO:**
> "I don't read Greek." [flat, wrong-footed]
> "Thank you." [which was always the wrong size]
> "Yeah. Yeah, I know. I know, okay? I — yeah."
> "Probably." [as a complete answer to a yes/no question]
> "Oh, shit. Okay. Okay." [discovering something has gone wrong]

**DON'T:**
> "I know." *(flat, alone, as complete response to hard news — too settled)*
> "I do not know whether to be glad it already exists or sad that I did not do it."
> "What I've discovered is a method of controlled combustion that allows lighter-
> than-air flight by manipulating the density differential between the interior
> gas and the surrounding atmosphere." *(Daniel never lectures in spoken dialogue)*
> Any perfectly-formed argument he delivers in full without hedging or backtracking.
> Any line that lands the chapter's thematic point and he clearly knows it landed.

**FORBIDDEN PHRASES (dialogue only — fine in narration):**
- "I know." *(alone, flat)*
- "Whether to be glad... or sad..." constructions
- "The [noun] where you [elegant subordinate clause]" used as spoken speech
- Any sentence that sounds like it came from a paragraph rather than a mouth

**WHAT HE CAN DO (authentic teen-to-middle-age voice):**
- "I mean —" starts that don't finish grammatically
- Trailing off: "It's just — yeah." / "I don't — yeah."
- Swearing quietly when scared: "Oh god. Okay. Okay."
- Asking things he should know: "That's not — that heals, right?"
- Hedging: "I think this works. Mostly. Probably."
- Restating: "It works. I mean — yeah. It works."
- Second-guessing mid-sentence: "This is fine — I mean it's not great, but it'll—"

### TYCHE (mature register, ch20+)
DO:
> "How much." [flat response to being freed]
> "I'm not thanking you."
> "He's careless with money but he's not a liar, watch the first and trust
> the second."
> "The box is where you left it. I move it when the floor needs sweeping and
> I put it back."
> "And the army." [two words, ahead of Daniel's reasoning]

DON'T:
> "I appreciate everything you've done for me, even when it wasn't done
> perfectly." [sentimental; Tyche does not perform appreciation]
> "What you need to do is think about the whole problem from the beginning."
> [too long; Tyche states, does not instruct at length]

Tyche vs. Marcia test: Tyche says "The eleventh load." Marcia says "The
eleventh load is the one that hangs him, because if you follow it back to
the booking date, you'll find he was paid twice, which is the fraud, and the
fraud is what you'll need in front of a magistrate before he moves the
money again." Both are right. One stops; one argues.

### HERMES
DO:
> "Tell me how much black. Tell me how hot. Tell me how long. Tell me one

> "Macer says. You'll make my iron harder. Than I make it." [hammer pacing]
> "We try again." [as a weather statement, not a decision]
> "Then you've told me nothing."

DON'T:
> "The problem is temperature consistency. Without a reliable way to measure

DO:
> "How far down the river could that be seen."
> "Good. The men I bury are the ones who weren't. Don't love it."
> "Build me something that doesn't need a man hung off it. Or build me nothing.
> Either one."
> [quoted oath, once] "Hercules' balls. Back them up. All of them."

DON'T:
> "I want you to understand what you're getting into here. The army isn't just

### CRISPUS
DO:
> [Using Strabo to undermine Daniel's Thule claim]

> edge of the frozen sea. You will forgive my curiosity about a man who claims
> to have come from a country that Pytheas did not find."
> [Building toward the fraud charge, publicly, with footnotes]

DON'T:
> "You're lying and I know it." [direct; Crispus never accuses without a citation]

### SCAEVA
DO:

> I mention it only because you seem to know something about sulfur compounds,
> and a word in the right office would be a very small thing." [the favor-trap]
> [The leash, later:] warm words, and under them: the length of the leash.

DON'T:
> "I need you to do something for me. Here's what I want." [too direct; Scaeva
> never names the transaction]

### MARCIA
DO:
> "Everything is a toy until it makes money. Then it's a business, and a business
> has a hundred ways to die, and the interesting ones are the ones you didn't see."
> "That was a test. You failed it. Now I know what I'd be dealing with."
> "A way to be worth more without being bigger. There is an emperor coming who
> reads." [completes Daniel's strategic thinking before he does]
> "Go wash your feet. You smell like the portico."

DON'T:
> "Trust me." [Marcia does not ask for trust; she demonstrates and then waits]
> "I think you're right about this." [She tests before she agrees; agreement
> without prior testing is not her register]

---

### HADRIAN
DO:
> "You bow to the room when you do not know which of us is worth bowing to.
> I like to see a man I am talking to."
> "Do not flatter me by agreeing too quickly. I dislike it."
> "A man who asks to be forgotten is asking to be remembered for the asking.
> I will remember you for it."

DON'T:
> "What do you want from me? Be direct." [Hadrian never asks for directness;
> he constructs traps and watches which ones you step in]

### PAMPHILUS (V2 new material — must be written fresh)
DO:
> "That wall. Three times I done it. Don't hold."
> "Boss said stack here." [after being told he stacked wrong:] "I stacked here."
> [quietly, to the dropped thing:] "Gods-rot you."
> "Big ones. You said the big ones." [defending a misunderstanding]
> "It's heavy." [when asked why he didn't move something]

DON'T:
> "I've repaired this wall three times and it still doesn't hold structurally."
> "I thought you meant the larger crates, based on what you said earlier."
(These versions are grammatically correct, sequentially logical, and verbally
articulate. Pamphilus is none of these things when speaking.)

DO:
> "Any man can hold a rope. What's the sky going to do, fall on us?"
> "I been up in trees twice that height. This is nothing."
> "The Thulean's more of a mother than my mother." [reported in V1 as a near-

DON'T:
> "I'm not scared, but I'm not stupid either." [too measured; Naso is stupid

## SECTION 4: CLASS-MARKING GRAMMAR RULES

Apply these by tier. The goal is texture, not mockery.

**Tiers 1–2 (Senatorial/Educated Greek):** Full complex sentences; subordinate
clauses; passive constructions for elegant distancing; allusion and quotation
natural; comfortable with the subjunctive; pauses before the key word.

**Tiers 3–4 (Equestrian/Craftsman):** Shorter, more direct; no allusion;
humor blunt or functional. Marcia uses "because" and "which means" — she argues.
Hermes cannot; Hermes strings imperatives. No rhetorical figures unless physically
demanded by the work.

**Tier 5 (Army):** Imperatives dominate. Pronouns dropped where possible
("Body count?" not "What is the body count?"). Numbers without units
("Forty. North road."). Crude oaths natural under stress, not performed.

**Tier 6 — Pamphilus specifically:** Use every construction a grammar teacher
would reject. Wrong past tense ("I done it"). Missing articles ("Stack is wrong").
Missing pronouns ("Went there. Came back."). No multi-clause sentences — ever. One
fact per sentence. Noun rather than verb where possible. Tyche (early) is NOT the
same: she is economical, not ungrammatical. The distinction matters.

**Tier 7 (Crowd):** Rhetorical questions as conclusions ("What's he got hidden?
Something. Has to be."). Superstition as fact ("The smoke went north. North is
bad."). Physical specificity without understanding. Volume in short phrasing,
not exclamation marks.

## SECTION 5: CRUDE SPEECH REFERENCE

### Guidance on frequency
Not every scene; not zero. The goal is to crack the V1 clean-mouthed seal — a
total of 4-6 crude moments across the book is enough to make the silence elsewhere
feel like choice rather than avoidance.

### Characters who must have at least one crude line

| Character | Context | What it should sound like |
|---|---|---|
| **Macer** | Genuine anger (money, contract, betrayal) | A Roman contractor's oath — see below |
| **Celer** | Under stress, battlefield or accident | Short, specific, a soldier's oath |
| **Pamphilus** | Tired, at an object, not a person | "Gods-cursed thing" / "rot you" |
| **Naso** | Pre-accident cockiness | Crude comparison, not an oath |
| **Crowd hecklers** | At least one scene | Something sexual or scatological, specific |

### What oaths fit the period and register
Latin originals and register-neutral translations:

- **Hercule!** / "By Hercules!" — common, mild-to-medium soldier's oath; Celer's range
- **Cacator!** ("shitter!") — crude insult; dock/crowd register
- **Mentula!** (anatomical; "cock!") — strongest; graffiti-grade; crowd or Macer at peak anger
- **Futue te ipsum!** (go ——— yourself) — graphic; use sparingly; crowd or extreme moment
- **Di immortales!** ("Immortal gods!") — medium; can be used without full vulgarity
- **Malum!** / "Damnation!" — mild; crosses all registers
- **Stercus!** ("shit!") — moderate; craftsman/soldier range; Pamphilus level

For V2, a period-neutral English translation is acceptable; what matters is that
the register is rough and the speech is quoted, not muted. "Gods-rot him" (Macer)
and "Hercules' balls" (Celer) land the texture without requiring untranslated Latin.

### What crude speech is NOT:
- It is not performative. Characters who swear do so because that is what comes
  out under stress, not because they are being characterized as coarse for
  the reader's benefit.
- It is not sustained. One line, one moment. The roughness lands and the
  narrative moves on.
- It is not comic. Pamphilus swearing at the wall is not a joke. It is what
  a tired man with limited language does when a thing fails him again.

## SECTION 6: THE MUTING PROBLEM

V1 solved "how do I write an inarticulate person?" by not writing them at all.
This is the book's single most consequential voice failure. The following
characters were muted or near-muted in V1 and must be given real dialogue in V2.

### Characters to unmute — minimum line counts

| Character | V1 status | V2 minimum | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pamphilus** | Effectively zero direct lines | **4 direct spoken lines** | Primary class-voice vehicle; see Section 3 and his profile in 02_characters.md |
| **Naso** | One reported near-quote | **1 direct line, pre-accident** | Must be crude and dim; the reader must hear what the narration claims |
| **Crowd hecklers** | "Shouted something I didn't catch" (3+ scenes) | **2-3 quoted lines in at least one scene** | Pompeii-graffiti register; crude, superstitious, wrong |
| **Celer's oath** | "Something soldier's-rough I didn't catch" | **1 direct quoted oath** | Once; then muting can resume |
| **Davus** (the "big slow household slave," ch08) | Zero lines | **1 direct line if he appears in V2** | Brief; inarticulate register |

### How to write the unmuting
The key move is not to give these characters more words — it is to give them
their words, in their register, however short or rough.

Instead of: *"Pamphilus watched me try to fix the wall and did not say anything."*

Write: *"Pamphilus watched me try to fix the wall. 'Done it three times,' he said,
to the wall more than to me. 'Don't hold.' Then he went back to carrying."*

Instead of: *"A man in the crowd shouted something I didn't catch."*

Write: *"A man in the crowd shouted that the smoke had gone north, and that north
was the bad direction, and that someone should search me. Two or three others
agreed with the part about searching me."*

The second version is not longer. It is more honest.

### A note on dignity
Giving Pamphilus ungrammatical speech and Naso a dim brag is not contempt —
it is the opposite. V1's clean-mouthed eloquent cast is the condescending version:
it implies only characters who speak like educated Romans are worth quoting directly.
V2 quotes all of them, in their own registers, however rough.
