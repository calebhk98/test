# Chapter 7: "Iterations" — Review

## Summary
Spring of year 2: Daniel solves the seam problem by watching an old man fold grain-sack edges (triple thickness, greased fold). The bag now holds 140+ breaths. He learns the "fill then release" method — fill the bag to bursting off a large open fire, pinch the throat, let it go free. A small free test succeeds: the rag bag drifts up over the midden, past sight, gone. Side threads this chapter: a sketched wheelbarrow for a cart-man (built in two days, works), a crop-rotation suggestion to an estate manager (legumes before grain, one field, mechanism unremembered), kite-making with Stichus (Stichus sells them at market, coins negligible, children watching the sky is the point). First trip to Rome for the emperor's triumphal entry — Trajan, walking on foot. In the crowd: a diviner named Aulus Vibenius notices Daniel with an expression of alarmed recognition. Heras shuts down hard: "Do not let him point at you." On the road home, Heras reveals he's spoken to the household owner about Daniel. A demonstration is being arranged. The chapter ends with Daniel sewing the best bag he's ever made, by feel, before dawn.

## The Fill-Then-Release Solution
The central technical breakthrough of the arc — fill the bag on the ground from a big, unconstrained fire, then seal and release it rather than keeping fire underneath — is arrived at through observation (the heat column in still air, the way it breaks when wind hits). This is the right kind of solution: it bypasses the open-flame-under-cloth problem entirely.

The test works. The free flight is brief and described well — "it kept going up for a while after I was sure it should have stopped" — and then it drifts off and falls in someone's field. Heras witnesses it. Heras has no face for it. The line he produces to reclaim his composure is perfect: "You have made a thing that flies away from you. Congratulations. It is the most useless miracle I have ever seen."

And then: "A man who knew numbers would walk a mile and pay a copper to watch it." Heras is simultaneously dismissive and invested. He's been deploying Daniel's credibility in background conversations the whole time ("I have spoken to him about you").

## Three Side Inventions — Handled Brilliantly
The wheelbarrow, crop rotation, and kites are presented in about three paragraphs each, without fanfare. This is exactly right. Daniel doesn't pause to explain their historical significance. He sketches a one-wheel cart, a carpenter builds it in two days, it works, he doesn't go back to watch. That's the whole story. The kites are Stichus's project; Daniel fixes the balance and makes two more; Stichus sells them; children stand in a lane looking at the sky. "I could not explain why this felt important. It just did."

**The crop rotation detail is the most interesting:** Daniel knows legumes help subsequent crops. He doesn't know why (nitrogen fixation, 19th century discovery). He offers it as "I saw this in Thule" — the lie as cover for knowledge without mechanism. The estate manager is not convinced. Daniel files it under "will prove itself or not."

This is the correct intellectual humility — and the correct pattern of how actual agricultural change spreads. Not through proclamation; through one field, one season, and whoever notices.

## The Haruspex: Aulus Vibenius
The most narratively efficient scene in the book so far. "He lifted his head and looked across two hundred people directly at me, at my burned hands, or my barbarian clothes... He looked at me the way you look at something that has appeared in the entrails and should not be in the entrails."

That simile is genuinely good. A diviner looking at someone as though they're an anomaly in the reading — that's the perfect frame for what Vibenius is doing. He's not curious. He's not friendly. He's recognizing something that doesn't belong.

Heras's reaction is the correct alarm level: not dramatic, just shut-down. "Do not let him point at you." Four words of genuine warning from a man who doesn't waste words.

This is clearly the seed of the impiety trial mentioned in ABOUT.md. A haruspex who decides the Thulean is a bad omen has real power to act on that belief. Filed as a future threat.

## Trajan's Entry
Historically vivid and accurately characterized. Trajan was genuinely known for his accessibility — he would get off his horse to talk to veterans, he walked among crowds. "A big plain soldier in a clean toga walking on his own two feet through the gate like any man." The crowd's reaction ("the enormous shared hunger to be ruled by someone who came in on foot") is accurate: the contrast with Domitian (his predecessor's predecessor, paranoid and reclusive) and with the contrast of grand imperial theater would have been striking.

**Historical flag:** Daniel has been in Rome approximately one year (spring 98 → spring 99 AD). The "emperor coming home from wars on a river at the edge of the world" suggests a triumphal military return. Trajan's major wars (Dacian campaigns) were 101-102 and 105-106 AD. In 99 AD, Trajan had been emperor only a year — he was on the Rhine/Danube border in 98, came to Rome in early 99. There may have been minor military actions, but the framing ("won his wars") reads more like a major triumph than a first year accession visit. This is a potential timeline compression or anachronism. The author may be fudging the timeline by 2-3 years to get Daniel in Rome for a more dramatic moment.

## Research Check
- **Trajan's personality**: Accurate. Famous for walking among citizens.
- **Haruspicy**: Accurate Roman religious practice. Reading animal organs (especially liver) for omens. Etruscan in origin, practiced throughout the Republic and Empire.
- **Crop rotation/legumes**: The nitrogen-fixation mechanism is correctly identified as beyond Daniel's recall. The three-field rotation was known in Rome but not systematically practiced. Legume benefits were empirically observed by Roman agricultural writers (Columella, Varro, Pliny). This is accurate.
- **Kites**: Roman-period Europe had no kites. Kites were Chinese (known from ~200-300 BC). They weren't in the Roman world. This is a small anachronism for the region — Daniel introducing kites to Rome is planting something that wouldn't have arrived for centuries. The author doesn't flag this, which suggests either it's deliberate (one of Daniel's knowledge seeds) or it wasn't caught.
- **The wheelbarrow**: Han Dynasty China (~100-200 AD) — roughly contemporary with this story, but unknown in the Roman world. Daniel introducing it is one of his "ladder" contributions. Accurate as an introduction.
- **Dice gambling probability**: Romans used knucklebones (astragali) and later cubic dice. Weighted dice (loaded) were known. The probability angle Daniel notices is real — a man who knows probability has an edge over those who don't.

## Hidden Message Pattern
The wheelbarrow invention in an aside: one sketch, two days, done. Daniel doesn't frame this as significant. He doesn't say "I just introduced the wheelbarrow to Rome." He notices a problem, suggests a solution, watches it get built, doesn't go back. Meanwhile he's obsessing about the balloon. The author is again hiding a consequential act inside Daniel's apparent preoccupation with something else.

The kites are similar: Daniel frames them as "children who think the sky is possible," not "I just introduced kite-flying to Europe." His frame is always behavioral/psychological (how it changes what people believe is possible), not technological (what object I introduced).
