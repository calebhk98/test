# Chapter 18: "The Siege" — Review

## Summary
Spring before the Second Dacian War. Four thousand sesterces buys Pamphilus from Macer. The freeing is done badly — household manumission, no praetor, Heras feeds Daniel the words. The collar is riveted; Hermes cuts it with a cold chisel ("a freed man bleeds out on his first free morning" if the chisel slips). Pamphilus runs his hand along the pale band where the iron had been. "You free," he says — using "you" when he means "I": eleven years of Latin learned in a house where someone else was the subject. He asks: "Go where." Daniel offers wages. Heras takes the collar halves away. Pamphilus picks up the shears. Goes to the cloth. Daniel holds four thousand sesterces of freedom that "still came up light." The Second Dacian War: Daniel marches north as Marcus Ulpius Danihel, with a warrant and a mule. The army doubled and more than doubled. Apollodorus's stone bridge at Drobeta — "twenty piers standing in the river that had drowned his cofferdam-men." Apollodorus: "It will outlast the province." Camp siting — latrines downstream, water-points upstream, barrel filters, boiling by century. The tethered eye on still mornings. At Sarmizegetusa: two teams trained, 50+ hours tethered each, two-basket design (second man below to manage the fire-bowl, counter the sway). Manned flights. Crossbowmen in baskets reading troop concentrations; Dacian cavalry find bolts falling from a barely-visible basket; the passes empty ahead of the column. Celer alive through the whole war; they never say Sabinus's name. From the balloon, Daniel reads the slope south of Sarmizegetusa's walls, traces the city's water supply. The question comes up the chain: "Could it be cut." He says cut here and here, and foul it above the intake. "The exact inversion of every word I had said to the emperor in the cold room." The city breaks. Decebalus cuts his own throat. Head and right hand carried south. The gold: cart after cart. Daniel does the figures on part of the haul — "the same marks, the same hand." The question of determinism: was he watching history he remembered or changing it? He has no way to know. Crosses back over the stone bridge in the cold. "There was a winter of figures waiting, and a city full of gold, and a girl at the wharf I had promised, next, to buy."

---

## "Go Where": The Freeing That Comes Up Light

The Pamphilus scene is the chapter's most important ten paragraphs, and they're about what freedom is not.

"You free," Pamphilus says, using "you" when he means "I." Eleven years of Latin learned in a house where someone else was the subject. He has learned the grammar of not being the subject. It doesn't leave in a morning.

Daniel imagined a scene from a story — the chains coming off, the weeping, the grip of an arm. "What I wanted was the clean feeling, the one clean thing." What Pamphilus does is ask whether he is still to cut the cloth. When Daniel says he can go, Pamphilus asks "Go where." A plain question. A man past forty with a band of pale skin and no family that survived being sold twice and no trade but carry and lift and cut bolt-cloth. The question has no good answer. Daniel freed him into being a client. "I had moved him one rung and called it the sky."

"Nobody owns you. That is all I have."

This is the most honest thing Daniel says in seventeen chapters. The freedom is real and it is also not the thing he imagined giving. Pamphilus picks up the shears and goes to the morning's work, because that is the morning's work, and freedom has not changed the morning or the cloth. Tyche watches from the shed wall, learning what free turns out to be. She asked for "us" before herself. She is watching what she asked for.

Heras takes the collar halves away. He says he wants them for something. He never says what. The explanation the text offers — that he didn't want them in the yard — is the right one. It's not a sentiment. It's a physician's practical removal of something that would keep the wound visible.

## The Water Cut: The Moral Center

"The exact inversion of every word I had said to the emperor in the cold room."

This is the book's thesis fulfilled. From the balloon, Daniel reads the fall-lines south of Sarmizegetusa's walls — the engineering lines of a hilltop water system. He sketches it from altitude. A young Greek engineer confirms on the ground. The question comes up the chain: "Could it be cut."

Daniel says cut here, and here, and where you can't cut, foul it — put the camp's filth above the intake. "Do to their water what I spend my life stopping us from doing to ours."

The same knowledge. The filter that saves Roman soldiers from the flux is the inversion of the fouled water that breaks the Dacian city. Same principle, same man, different end of the rope. The city "broke." The fight went out. "A city full of people ran dry on a high cold hill because a man from a country that did not exist had read the slope right."

"I did not see them die of it. That is what I keep coming back to: I did the water and never saw the water do its work, the same as I have never seen the boiled water save a man, no number either way, only the result."

This is the book's most honest sentence about the epistemological problem that runs through all of Daniel's work. He has never counted the men the boiled water saved. He has never counted the people the cut water killed. "Only the result that came down the mountain in the end." The ledger is invisible in both directions. He does the work. The result happens. The column balances.

The inch: "There was an inch where I could have been slow, vague, wrong on purpose — the inch I had used for two years to drag my feet on the powder. I did not use it." He chose to live. "I am not calling that a moral act."

This is the most important thing Daniel has said about his own actions. He is not claiming virtue. He made a calculation about what a freedman of the emperor's house does when he's slow and vague at a siege, and he read the slope. The calculation is self-serving. Calling it self-serving is honest. The book does not let him off with the rationalization (fewer Romans dead, it balances) — it makes him state the actual reason and then refuse to call it moral.

## Apollodorus: "Don't Make the Face"

Apollodorus comes to see the cut professionally. He looks at the fouled run with his plumb-line still in his hand out of habit. He says "Neat," then:

"It's the same knowledge. The same hand that keeps your men off the flux fouls theirs. You didn't think it would stay on the one side."

"No."

"Then don't make the face." And walks off down the slope.

This is the Apollodorus scene the book has been building to since ch12. He said "a demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day" — now he says "it's the same knowledge." Both lines are delivered flat, as facts, with no attempt to soften them. Apollodorus's function in the book is to state the thing accurately and then leave Daniel to carry it. He's never kind and he's always right.

The bridge footnote: "It will outlast the province. They'll take the country and lose it in two lifetimes, three, and the bridge will still stand when there's no Rome on either bank to use it." This is the cold long view, and it's also historically accurate — Trajan's bridge was demolished by Hadrian (to prevent barbarian crossings), but the stone piers remained in the Danube for centuries. Apollodorus, building for permanence, is correct that the engineering outlasts the politics.

## The Gold: The Same Marks, The Same Hand

"The column of grain that had kept a cohort off the flux and the column of looted gold that the same cohort had died and killed to take — the same marks, the same hand. I balanced the column, because it balanced."

The numerals are morally neutral — they'll total any column you bring them. The food that kept men alive for the siege and the plunder they took at the end of it are both rendered in the same figures. The machine of war and the machine of accounting are both served by the same innovation. "I balanced the column, because it balanced."

This is the cleanest statement of the ABOUT.md's thesis: "his gifts serve power's cruelty as readily as its good."

## The Balloon at Sarmizegetusa: The Vow Kept and Its Cost

"I won't rush untrained men into untested designs" — kept. Two teams, 50+ hours tethered, voluntary at every step, double-basket construction (second man below to manage the fire and counter the sway). "I had built the design past the point of failure. I had built it until it did not need the vow anymore. That is either the honorable version or the story a man tells himself it is, and I have not decided which."

The crossbow deployment from altitude is the military application Celer wanted: seeing behind ridges, standardized bolts, passes emptying ahead of the column. "I have wondered whether those men were grateful or terrified and I think probably both." The Dacians who emptied the passes before the column were not killed in those passes. The chapter does not say they were killed elsewhere. It doesn't not say it either.

Celer checks the basket-rigging on the first day, finds the two-man rig, says "Longer gap between the baskets next time." They never say Sabinus's name. This thread — Celer's quiet professionalism after Sabinus — is handled with the same restraint as the collar halves.

## The Historical Determinism Question

"Had it ended that way. Was I remembering it or watching it. Had any of it gone otherwise than it would have without a barbarian by the timber wharf who read a slope, and I had no way to know, no number, no second world to lay beside this one and check the column."

This is the book's first explicit engagement with the determinism question, deferred from the very beginning. Daniel doesn't ask whether he could have prevented the war; he asks whether the outcome he's watching is the one he half-remembers from a history class two thousand years off. He cannot know. There is no control condition. The column has no second version to balance against.

"I chose to live, and I am not calling that a moral act." — "Had any of it gone otherwise" — both questions, in the same chapter, without resolution. The book is not going to resolve these. That is the right decision.

## Research Check
- **Trajan's Second Dacian War (105-106 AD)**: Accurate in shape. The First Dacian War ended with Decebalus as a client king; this was widely understood as temporary. The second war was a full annexation campaign. Sarmizegetusa Regia fell; Decebalus did cut his own throat rather than be captured (attested). His head was carried to Rome. Accurate.
- **Trajan's Bridge at Drobeta**: Real and historically remarkable. Twenty stone piers in the Danube, timber arches, wide enough for army columns. Apollodorus designed it (~105 AD). It was later demolished by Hadrian. The stone piers remained visible in the Danube for centuries. "It will outlast the province" — accurate.
- **Dacian gold**: The haul was enormous — estimated as the largest single looting event in Roman history. Rome funded years of building and games from it. The mood change in the army as the gold carts came down is historically appropriate.
- **Murus dacicus**: The Dacian fortification style (stone-faced rubble wall with timber tie-beams) is accurately described. Roman engineers did admire and study it. The ram-resistance of the construction is correct.
- **Water supply at Sarmizegetusa**: The hillfort's water supply came from springs via channels. Cutting a besieged city's water supply was a standard Roman siege tactic (attested at many sieges). The fouling-above-the-intake method is the logical extreme of that tactic. Plausible.
- **Household manumission vs. formal manumission**: Accurate. Informal manumission (household ceremony, no praetor) created a Junian Latin — technically free, fewer rights than full citizen freedman. The text is correct that this is "the cheap door, which makes a free man but a lesser-free man." This detail is precisely right.
- **Imperial freedman status**: Accurately rendered. Freedmen of the imperial household (liberti Augusti) occupied a unique and powerful social position, administering vast amounts of imperial business, often wealthier and more influential than freeborn citizens.

## Pamphilus: The Finest Small Character Moment in the Book

The rope-coil scene: "That man. Loud." A practical offer to make the centurion trip on the ropes, delivered flat, not as a joke. "The kindest thing anyone said to me that week."

Pamphilus has now been in the book for eight chapters. He's heavy-shouldered, empty of urgency, reliable. He carried in the Dacian War, grabbed the tether rope in the wind and didn't let go. He asked "go where" when he was freed. He went back to the cloth. He offers a trip-hazard as kindness and is told no and accepts no and goes back to the rope-coils. He is the book's most quiet and most honest peripheral character.

## Hidden Message Pattern
The collar inversion: for eleven chapters Daniel has been failing to remove a collar he has no power to remove. In this chapter he removes it, with a cold chisel held by the smith he met in the steel chapters. The collar comes off in two halves and leaves a band of pale skin, soft, never weathered, "like the underside of a stone lifted from damp earth." The same image type as always — specific, sensory, not symbolic on the surface — but the undertone is the stone lifted, and what's under it is tender.

The inversion of knowledge: Daniel's water knowledge has been presented as pure good for 17 chapters. In this chapter it's used to kill a city. The hidden message is that there is no pure good. Apollodorus states it. Daniel already knows it. The reveal isn't a surprise; it's a confirmation.

## Overall: The Book's Most Morally Complete Chapter

Ch13 was the book's turning point. Ch18 is its moral completion. The water cut is the thing that the book has been promising since the beginning: the same gift serves the same two hands. Daniel has no clean ledger. He freed one man and the freedom came up light. He kept his vow about the balloon and still can't name whether the honorable version is the real one. He chose to live and isn't calling it a moral act. He balanced the column because it balanced.

"I came down off that mountain a different man than the one who had gone up, and I do not entirely have the measure of the difference even now, writing it."

That last phrase — "writing it" — is the retrospective narrator surfacing. An older Daniel, writing this down, still doesn't have the measure. This is what the memoir structure is for: not resolution, but honest account of what remains unresolved after a long time.
