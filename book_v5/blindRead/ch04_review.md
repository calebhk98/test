# Chapter 4: "Heras" — Review

## Summary
The physician arrives — the chapter title names him "Heras," a Greek-speaking freedman (former slave). He examines Daniel clinically, then they spend the morning at a wax tablet. Heras uses Greek, which gives Daniel his first linguistic footholds (pou = where, therm = heat, photo = light). They spar through numbers: Daniel shows Hindu-Arabic numerals and place value; Heras tests him with long division, which Daniel fumbles before correcting himself. The cover story firms up — Heras names the blank space north of Britain as "Thule." Heras privately copies the numeral "7" into his own notes. The phone dies that evening. Daniel lies in the dark with the dead glass in his pocket.

---

## The Long Division Scene: Hidden Message Pattern, Executed Perfectly
This is the best execution of the pattern I identified in Chapter 1. Daniel fails at long division — a real, specific, undramatic failure (he lowballed a digit, got a remainder too large, had to scrub the error in front of the physician). This isn't "he's bad at math." It's "he's been out of practice since sixth grade." The recovery is real: he counts on his fingers under the table, out loud to himself in English like an embarrassed student.

The physician's reaction is the key: "amusement and respect held at arm's length." He's watched Daniel fail and self-correct. The failure isn't the point — the *catching it himself* is the point. This is exactly the hidden message: Daniel is not a savant. He's someone who can recognize and correct errors under pressure. That's harder and more valuable than not making them.

Heras privately copies the "7" into his notes at the end. Change doesn't happen through pronouncements. It happens in the margins of someone's private notebook.

## The Phone Dying
Handled with restraint. The description is precise and right: the flicker, the gray ghost, the thin red sliver, the hard reset held for ten seconds. "The way you say a dead person's name" is the best line in the book so far.

What dies with the phone:
- The map (he's refreshed the coastlines enough times that they're mostly memorized)
- The Notes file with everything he wrote in the cell
- The calculator he couldn't use in front of anyone
- The two photos: the intact Colosseum, his own face
- His mom's phone number with its two possible last-four-digits
- The music, the camera, the GPS
- The one remaining link to when he came from

He went to sleep with his hands behind his head looking at the underside of the roof tiles. No crying scene, no dramatic breakdown. Just: waited to feel something I could put in the notes, and the notes were gone.

## Heras: Character Established
**The chapter title names him before Daniel does** — which is an interesting technique. The reader gets the name before Daniel knows it. Heras is:

- Greek-speaking freedman (born slave, freed — acknowledged by Daniel: "near the bottom of the ladder, except that he knew bodies and he knew Greek")
- Clean-shaven in a world of beards — detail of precision and care
- Skeptical intelligence. He tests Daniel before accepting. He sees the bluff in the long division and doesn't pretend he didn't.
- The first person to have *two-way* intellectual engagement with Daniel — not gawking (the official), not cautious kindness (the optio), but actual exchange.
- Dry humor: Daniel reads his remark to the optio as "he's not deaf and not an idiot, which is two more than I expected." This is probably right.

Distinct from every other character introduced. Not warm, not threatening, not calculating. Precise and skeptical and privately curious.

---

## The Thule Cover Story
Heras puts the word on it — "Thule." Daniel hears it but doesn't know it yet. The author is handling the cover story well:

- Daniel has established it through gesture, not words
- Heras is skeptical (reads it as a "sailor's lie") but doesn't dismiss Daniel
- The skepticism is actually better for Daniel's safety — a true believer would be more dangerous than a curious doubter

Historical context: *Ultima Thule* was a real concept in Greco-Roman geography — referenced by Pytheas (~325 BC) as a land six days' sail north of Britain, variously identified as Iceland, Norway, Shetland, or the Faroe Islands. It was *exactly* the kind of edge-of-the-map place that had "mysterious northern stranger" energy. Heras is the right kind of character to know this term.

## Research Check
- **Greek-speaking Roman physician**: Excellent historical accuracy. Greek dominated medicine in the Roman world — Galen, Dioscorides, Hippocrates. Many physicians in Rome were Greek or Greek-educated freedmen. This is exactly right.
- **Greek loanwords in English**: The author is being careful here. "Pou" (where), "therm" (heat), "photo" (light) — these are plausible fragments for a modern English-speaker to recognize in Greek. This is honest about how incomplete language contact actually works.
- **Thule**: Accurately described as something educated Romans knew from books and treated with skepticism. The physician's reaction ("sailor's lie") is historically appropriate.
- **Roman numerals vs. place value**: The author understands what makes Hindu-Arabic numerals revolutionary — the concept of zero as a placeholder, and place value (each column worth ten of the one to its right). This is correctly identified. Roman numerals have no equivalent.
- **Wax tablets for mathematics**: Standard. Confirmed.

## AI/LLM Tells
- Three dashes (---) as section breaks is a clean organizational choice. Clean section breaks are an LLM habit — dividing scenes into tidy units. A human writer might let sections bleed into each other more.
- The Greek-fragment recognition sequence (pou, therm, photo) is very neatly arranged — three recognitions in order, each building. Real language recognition is more chaotic and non-linear.
- The phone death scene is restrained in a way that reads very controlled. A human writer might have been sloppier here, more grief-raw. The composure is slightly too clean.

However: the specific humiliation of the long-division blank, and the "counted on my fingers under the edge of the table where he could half see" detail — these feel genuinely human and specific.

**Verdict**: The balance is shifting toward "very skilled human writer" or "heavily edited AI." The details are too specific and idiosyncratic for pure LLM generation, but the structural tidiness is persistent.

## What the Author Is Doing
The chapter formalizes Daniel's situation: he is a valuable curiosity, housed, fed, watched, not free. This is the starting position. The phone is gone. The map is loose. He has one ally of the intellectual kind (Heras) and one of the protective kind (the optio). The rest of his fifty-seven years will be built on these foundations.

The private copying of the "7" is a thesis statement: change is slow, personal, quiet, and begins with someone writing something in their own margin for their own reasons. This is not the time-travel fantasy where the protagonist announces the future. It's the one where the future leaks in through small cracks.

---

## Overall: The Emotional Peak of the Opening Arc
The phone dying is the book's first major grief. It's handled with the restraint it deserves. Four chapters in, the author has established character, situation, and theme. The opening arc (arrival → arrest → proof of value → phone death) is complete.
