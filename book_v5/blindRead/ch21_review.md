# Chapter 21: "The Cannon That Bursts" — Review

## Summary
The niter shed, two streets back from the wharf. Daniel crosses the two-column line into the powder — not in one piece but in three slivers: the steel problem (two years with Hermes, cannot catch the temperature without measurement, everything else stacks on it), the funding calculation (nobody with money will care about metal tolerance until they have a reason, and the reason is the weapon), and the lie to himself (the Seres already had it, so he isn't the cork in the bottle — he now thinks this was probably wrong, a timeline smeared). Niter production: wall scrapings don't work (calcium nitrate, sucked water from the air, won't bang). Niter beds: the worst compost heap in history, buying men's piss, turning the beds through a summer that bakes the stink into a presence with weight. Eventually: long white crystals that taste cold and burn the right way. Geta's accident: dried powder ground fine in a closed shed. A flat hard slap, the door comes off, the smoke stands up. Geta loses the left hand at the wrist, three fingers of the right, sight in one eye. On wages for life. When Geta can talk he asks if the powder worked. Good. The cannon: bronze, deliberately flawed design, built to argue. Hermes pours it true and beautiful, no flaw in it, because a man could look at it afterward and not say it broke because the smith was a fool. Loaded heavy on the bank downriver. "The bronze tube was simply not there anymore, it had opened like a flower made of knives." The ball crossed the river. The pitch to the Guard officers and the household man: the design failed the metal, not the metal the design — what you need is a metal bored to tolerances that don't exist yet, and the only place in Rome chasing that question is Hermes's forge, which is out of money. The boring prize posted, and the valve prize, one skill unlocking three doors: the cannon, the pump, the clock. "It is loose now." The powder is no longer in one frightened freedman's keeping. The lingering engineer with a measuring face. That evening, Geta by the window with the stump raised by habit: "I hope they stayed frightened." Daniel sits until the light goes, a wax tablet in his lap with the ratio written in English, thumb over the figures. He does not rub them out.

---

## "You Don't Break a Vow in One Piece. You Sell It Off in Slivers."

This is the chapter's thesis and its most honest sentence. The two-column list from ch14 had the powder on the right side — "not yet, maybe not ever." Daniel has spent chapters arguing that he was keeping it there. The three slivers:

**Sliver one** — the steel problem is real, the funding problem is real, nobody cares about metal until they have a reason: true, and a valid argument for doing something.

**Sliver two** — the funding would come if powerful men could see what the steel had to hold: true, and the demonstration is the logical next step.

**Sliver three** — the lie: the Seres already have it, so it's not mine to hold. "I think now that I was wrong, that I'd done what I always did, slid a fact two or three hundred years out of its place because the timeline in my head was a smear." He knew the lie was a lie. He told it to himself anyway. He names this clearly, years later, writing it down.

The structure of the vow-selling is the most psychologically accurate thing in the book. No one crosses their own ethical line in one deliberate step. They build a path of small reasonable steps, each defensible, until they look back and the line is far behind them. Daniel maps this with honesty rather than self-exoneration.

---

## Geta: The Book's Second Sabinus

Geta is Naso and Sabinus rearranged. Naso survived and served as a face of cost. Sabinus died and served as the book's moral turning point. Geta survives and is worse than either, because Daniel had the warning (he knew wet grinding was safer) and let impatience override it.

"I had a memory of the warning and not of the reason, and I let Geta grind a dried cake fine in a stone mortar in a closed shed in February because I wanted a finer powder and I was impatient and I had begun, by then, to think of the work as nearly solved."

The accident sequence is precise: flat hard slap to the chest before the ears register, the door, the smoke, Geta on his back screaming in a voice he'd never heard a grown man use. Hands wrong. The smell "the balloon smell, the burned-grease-and-hair smell from the Loaf, the one I will not describe again."

He puts Geta on wages for life. "None of that earns me anything." When Geta can talk, he asks if the powder worked. "Good." Then shuts his one eye.

Geta's "good" is not forgiveness. It's something simpler and harder: a man accepting the cost of his own role in a project he understood he was participating in, even if not all its risks. He isn't naive — he was a free man, on wages, who knew the powder was dangerous. The "I told him it was dangerous and I did not tell him enough" is the real accounting: not that Geta was deceived entirely, but that the information Daniel had was incomplete, the information he passed on was more incomplete still, and the difference between those two levels of incompleteness cost Geta his hands.

The evening visit — the stump raised off the blanket by old habit, the good side of the face turned, asking if they were frightened, hoping they stayed that way — is the chapter's best quiet moment.

## The Cannon Demonstration: Science Fiction as Engineering Pitch

The deliberate design: too long, bore too wide, walls too thin, mobile cradle. Built to fail. Hermes pours it true and beautiful because the failure has to indict the design, not the pour. The argument: this is what bronze does with this force in this configuration. To hold the force, you need a different configuration. The different configuration requires tolerances that don't exist yet. The tolerances require the boring prize. The boring prize is what Hermes needs.

"When it fails, it fails the design. Not you."

The demonstration sequence is one of the book's great set pieces. "The bronze tube was simply not there anymore, it had opened like a flower made of knives." The piece the size of a fist going over their heads into the river. The long quiet of men deciding not to have screamed. The Guard officer pale around the mouth, asking what killed it.

"The design. That bronze was poured without a flaw."

The argument: the force is real, the material is insufficient, the problem to solve is the boring tolerance, the only place solving it is Hermes's forge, which is out of money. Daniel has staged a failure to argue for a material problem. He has used the cannon to make the case for the boring that makes the cannon, the pump, and the clock. One prize for three doors.

---

## "It Is Loose Now": The Unboxable Knowledge

This is the chapter's moral completion. Before the demonstration, the powder was in one head, one shed, one man's keeping. After it:

- Geta knew the rough ratios before the accident (Daniel talked while he ground)
- Two workers turned the niter beds for a year and asked no questions he could hear
- The officers of the Guard saw the demonstration
- The lingering engineer picked through pieces with "the face of a man measuring distances in his head"
- The boring prize specification implies the purpose to anyone who thinks carefully about it

"I had thought I was deciding when to release it; by the time of the demonstration, some of it was already out, already moving through hands I hadn't counted."

The powder has escaped the two-column list not through one decision but through accumulation — the grinding, the beds, the workers, the demonstration, the prize. Each moment was controllable in isolation; the sequence was not. This is the honest account of how knowledge spreads and how "keeping it back" is always a temporary and partial proposition.

"The knowledge was no longer in one frightened freedman's keeping." The "frightened" is important — he was frightened, it was reasonable to be frightened, and the fear didn't stop him. Fear that doesn't stop you is a different kind of fear from fear that does.

## The Heras Scene: The Gate

"You're a man who built a wall and now spends his evenings learning where the gate is. Tell yourself the gate is still shut if you like. I've watched you find this many gates."

Heras knows. He hates it. He helps anyway. "In the careful way he did things he disapproved of, which was to make sure I did them less stupidly than he would have alone." This is the most precise sentence about Heras in any chapter. He is not neutral. He is not compliant. He helps where he disapproves because the alternative — Daniel doing it alone, more stupidly — is worse. This is the ethics of a physician who understands harm-reduction.

The cloth over his nose, the watering eyes, the one sentence that is a complete reading of Daniel's situation — Heras says what Heras always says: the accurate thing. Not the comfortable thing, not the validating thing. The accurate thing.

## The Steam Engine Connection: Three Doors

Daniel has been building a steam pump in secret, in the same shed, alongside the niter work. "I did not want to be on record as failing at something as old as Heron's toy engine in the library." Three cylinders cut by different smiths, each better, all leaking. A greased piston shows exactly where the fit fails. The piston rattles. The valve timing goes wrong.

The morning the third cylinder ran three strokes: Tyche in the yard. "It moved." "Three times." "Three more than yesterday."

She's right. It annoys him. Three strokes pumped a cup of water and then everything went wrong again. But the principle is not fantasy. The cycle completed itself three times.

The boring prize is the key: same tolerance requirement for the cannon bore, the steam cylinder, and the clock gears. One smith mastering one skill unlocking three technologies. This is the convergence the book has been building — Hermes's forge, Felix's steam derivation, Daniel's pump attempts, the calipers, the measuring rods — all pushing at the same dimensional tolerance problem.

## Research Check
- **Gunpowder chemistry**: Accurate. The calcium nitrate vs. potassium nitrate distinction (the former absorbs moisture and won't detonate reliably) is correct. Niter beds using urine and manure are the historical method for producing saltpeter (potassium nitrate) before the industrial era. The process of leaching through wood ash (which provides potassium) and crystallizing is accurate. The timeline (nearly two years) is appropriate for an initial production batch.
- **Gunpowder ratios**: The historical ratio is approximately 75% potassium nitrate, 15% charcoal, 10% sulfur (varies). Daniel's "three-quarters" memory is close. Wet grinding as a safety measure is correct — dry grinding of blended gunpowder is a major explosion hazard.
- **Corning (pressing into cakes then breaking to grains)**: Accurate. This is how gunpowder was historically improved — corned powder burns faster and more completely than serpentine (unmixed) powder. The technique was developed in the 14th-15th centuries. Daniel's description is correct.
- **Bronze cannon burst**: The early history of guns includes many catastrophic burst events, particularly in European history (14th-16th centuries). Bronze guns were eventually superseded by cast iron and then steel for exactly the tolerances Daniel describes. The burst pattern (walls spreading the force sideways) is accurate for a thin-walled tube under internal pressure.
- **Steam engine thermodynamics**: The valve timing problem Daniel describes (exhaust valve closing and intake opening simultaneously) is a real challenge in early steam engine design. The Newcomen engine (1712) and Watt's improvements (1765) were solving exactly these valve and cylinder problems. The piston-leather wrapping for better seal is historically accurate.
- **China and gunpowder**: Daniel's "Seres had it" belief and his later doubt are both appropriate. Chinese gunpowder use in military contexts is documented from the 10th century AD. Whether he's "moving the fact two hundred years" depends on what he thinks the date is (ca. 110-115 AD, roughly), and whether he's thinking of the earliest Chinese experiments or later developed military applications. He may be off by several centuries.

## Hidden Message Pattern

The cannon demonstration is the hidden-message pattern inverted and made public. Daniel has always downplayed before demonstrating. Here he builds a demonstration of failure — a failure staged so precisely that it can only argue for what he wants to argue. The failure is the message. What the cannon tells you, bursting "like a flower made of knives," is the exact case for the boring tolerance. Daniel has weaponized failure as argument.

---

## The Thumb Over the Figures

The chapter's closing image: the wax tablet with the ratio in English, the thumb over it. He does not rub them out.

This is the final word on the two-column list. The numbers are written. They're real. He has found the gate, as Heras said. The gate is open. He is not rubbing out the ratio. The knowledge exists now, in English, on a wax tablet in his lap, and only Tyche can read the script — which means the ratio is as private as he can make it and is also no longer his alone. The not-rubbing-out is the decision. He made it by staying until the light went and not rubbing them out.

## Predictions Updated
- **The boring prize**: Will be won by someone — possibly Felix, possibly Hermes's apprentice, possibly an unknown craftsman. The timeline is a year of practice. The winner unlocks the cannon (more investment from powerful men), the steam pump (the Felix convergence), and the clock.
- **The lingering engineer**: He will come back. He measured distances in his head. He is the first person outside Daniel's immediate circle who has the pieces to reconstruct what was seen.
- **Geta on wages for life**: A permanent presence, a permanent cost, a permanent weight. He will probably appear once more.
- **The "it is loose now" problem**: The powder is moving through hands Daniel hasn't counted. The empire will develop the technology whether Daniel manages it or not. The question is whether he can shape the development (boring prize → better cannon → safer deployment) rather than simply watch it escape.
- **Heras**: "In the careful way he did things he disapproved of, which was to make sure I did them less stupidly than he would have alone." Still the most important sentence about the most important relationship. The clock is still running.
- **The Mercator Thulensis mystery (ch19)**: Still unresolved.
- **Tyche's formal manumission**: Still outstanding from ch20's commitment.

## Overall: The Darkest Chapter Since ch13

ch13 was the death of Sabinus — a cost imposed by the war's logic that Daniel couldn't fully own or fully escape. ch21 is different: the cost is fully his. He knew wet grinding was safer. He knew impatience was overriding caution. He let it happen. And he went on — did the powder, did the cannon, posted the prizes, released the knowledge.

"I had not understood about a vow is that you don't break it in one piece. You sell it off in slivers."

This is the book's most important sentence since "the fall had done it." It's the truest thing Daniel has said about how people actually lose the commitments they make. Not in a moment of moral collapse, but in a chain of defensible steps that look reasonable individually and lead somewhere they said they wouldn't go.

The chapter ends with his thumb over the ratio and the light gone and the wharf making its evening noises. He writes the numbers in English, in the only language that's still his, and he does not rub them out.
