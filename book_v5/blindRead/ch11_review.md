# Chapter 11: "The Army's Eye" — Review

## Summary
Military tribune Quintus Marcius Celer arrives at the yard (Macer arranged it). His first question about the balloon: how far can it be seen? — a reconnaissance question, not a wonder question. A money conversation: Celer at 40,000 sesterces, Macer counters at 80,000, Daniel negotiates 40,000 for himself. Celer makes visits, asks precise questions, receives sketches for stirrups and a crossbow-cocking device. The Eratosthenes stick-shadow experiment with Heras; Daniel adds heliocentrism in cipher ("Earth circles sun. For me, not for them. Useless until the machines need explaining."). Counterweight trebuchet sketch goes to Celer. First balloon-with-basket trial: Naso (young soldier) stands up in the basket, bag heels, fire catches, bag burns, Naso falls with fire on him — back, arms, neck, one hand, ankle. He survives. Macer: "Smaller fire next time. Work it out." Celer: "It works and it's a butcher and we'll do it again." Daniel stands in the wet ash holding the broken willow line.

---

## Celer: The Book's Second-Best New Character
The first thing Quintus Marcius Celer asks about the balloon is not "how does it work" or "what is it" — it's "how far down the river could that be seen." Every other person who saw the balloon asked about altitude, wonder, or magic. Celer asks the watchtower question. This establishes him instantly as someone who doesn't experience the world as spectacle.

His recurring visits — not summoning Daniel, but coming to the wharf himself — and his method of asking the hard question after two easier ones are the best character development technique in the book so far. He listens when Daniel says something is impossible. He doesn't dismiss it. He asks one precise question that reframes it. "How does a man get a message down?" — Daniel hadn't thought about that. Celer had.

"The men I bury are the ones who weren't [afraid]." This line does a lot of work. It establishes him as a man who has buried men, who is practical about fear, and who is not trying to comfort Daniel. He means it literally.

The most morally complex moment with Celer is the end: "It works. It works and it's a butcher and we'll do it again with a smaller fire and a longer basket." No cruelty in this. Just the cold arithmetic of military utility. Naso is in the bad-kind-of-burns and Celer is already planning the next trial. This is the book's first clear "gifts serve power's cruelty as readily as its good" moment. And it's not even cruel — it's just the honest calculation of what an army does with a working tool.

## The Balloon Accident: The Book's Most Visceral Scene
"It is so fast. There is no moment where you might still put it out."

The sequence is well-executed: Naso stands, basket swings, bag heels, fire licks sideways from the bowl, Daniel shouts haul, bag comes down heeled and fast, fire finds the grease, fire along the seam. Then Naso trying to climb out while fire comes down on him, half-jump half-fall, basket on top of him.

Heras working on Naso's back: "the bad kind, doesn't hurt at the center because the part that feels is already gone." This is the correct clinical description of a third-degree burn. The author knows what this looks like.

Daniel's response: "alive, he's alive" as a soaked hide, something to put between himself and what's on the ground. He built this. He's responsible for what it's used for. The willow line he checked twice, held in silence at the end — this is the chapter's best image, and the best final image in the book so far.

## Three Military Inventions Buried in Celer's Visits

**Stirrups:** Two loops of leather hung from the saddle, sketched when a cavalry rider mounts wrong. One of the most significant military innovations in history — transforms mounted combat by allowing full-body force against targets. Not in Roman cavalry; arrived in Europe roughly 6th century AD. Celer takes it to "Hermes" the smith.

**Crossbow cocking device:** An L-shaped iron hook bolted to the stock; foot in hook, pull string back with both hands and bent knee. The medieval "goat's foot lever" or stirrup for crossbow cocking (stirrup for the bow in a different sense — the same principle). Daniel sketches it after watching a soldier struggle. Celer: "Hermes again."

**Counterweight trebuchet:** Sketched on a tally-sheet in response to a siege engine question. A falling counterweight rather than twisted rope — no rope to rot, no limit to weight if the arm is long enough. The Romans had torsion engines (ballista, onager). The counterweight trebuchet, historically, arrives in the 12th century and is significantly more powerful.

All three are slipped in across two pages, as asides during Celer visits. The author is making these look like improvised observations rather than deliberate donations. This is exactly the "hidden message" pattern at its most efficient — world-historical military innovations described in two paragraphs each, while the chapter is nominally about the balloon.

## The Heliocentrism Note
The stick-shadow demonstration is Eratosthenes's method — known since ~240 BC, so Heras would know it. What's new is Daniel adding heliocentrism (Aristarchus's theory, ~280 BC, but generally rejected). Heras's response is exactly right: he asks how the circumference number is known, not whether the conclusion is true. He's interested in the epistemology, not the assertion.

Daniel's cipher note: "Earth circles sun. For me, not for them. Useless until the machines need explaining." This is what the book does best — Daniel has true knowledge that the world isn't ready for and files it honestly. He can't deploy heliocentrism. There's no practical use for it. The machines that will need it don't exist yet. This is the same pattern as the lead pipes, the same as variolation-not-yet.

## Research Check
- **Stirrups in Roman cavalry**: Correct — no stirrups. Their absence is documented. The impact of stirrups on medieval cavalry is well-documented.
- **Crossbow cocking aids**: The medieval cocking aids are well-documented; applying one to the Roman arcuballista is a plausible extension.
- **Counterweight trebuchet**: Historically accurate as a 12th-century development. Daniel's introduction is one of the book's bigger "ladder" donations.
- **Third-degree burns**: The "doesn't hurt at the center because the part that feels is already gone" is medically accurate for full-thickness burns. The author knows this.
- **Eratosthenes**: Historical, ~240 BC. His method (shadow angle difference at noon, measured distance between cities) is correctly referenced. Heras as an educated Greek would know his work.
- **Aristarchus's heliocentrism**: Historical, ~280 BC. Rejected by most ancient scholars in favor of geocentrism. The "file it for when machines need explaining" is a reference to the machinery of astronomy that will eventually require a heliocentric model.

## The Moral Weight of Military Use
This chapter is the first time Daniel's inventions are explicitly being turned into military tools. He's been doing it all along — maps, numbers, balloon for reconnaissance — but this is the first chapter where it's unambiguous. Celer takes the stirrup sketch to Hermes. He gets the trebuchet drawing. He's planning to use the balloon with soldiers in it.

Daniel's response to Naso's burns is to hold the willow line and say nothing. He doesn't refuse to continue. He doesn't articulate an objection. He just stands in the ash. This is realistic — he has no power to refuse, and he knows it — but it's also the book's first sustained moral ambiguity. The ABOUT.md's thesis ("his gifts serve power's cruelty as readily as its good") is now clearly in motion.

## Hidden Message Pattern
The stirrups: sketched because a cavalry rider mounts wrong, in an offhand moment. Two paragraphs. Handled as a minor aside during a Celer visit. This is one of the most consequential military inventions in history, described as a reaction to watching one rider's bad mount. The author consistently hides the most consequential things in the smallest-seeming moments.

The Naso accident changes what the book is. Before: Daniel building things and deploying knowledge to establish credibility. After: Daniel's inventions hurting real people in real time, with real people (Celer, Macer) viewing that as an acceptable cost.
