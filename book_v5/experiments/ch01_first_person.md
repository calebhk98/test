# Chapter 1: Waking (first-person draft)

My alarm went off at six-fifteen and I hit snooze with my eyes still shut. I gave myself the usual twenty seconds to wonder whether I could call in sick, and the answer was the usual one. Rick had cut my hours twice already this month, and there were like forty dollars in my checking account, and forty dollars did not stretch to skipping a shift over nothing. I pulled the orange Brentwood Hardware shirt off the floor, the one with the bleach spot on the hem and the logo half cracked off the chest, and dragged it on. The house was still dark. I stepped over the creaky board outside my sister Maya's door so I wouldn't wake her, grabbed a granola bar off the counter, and left the lights off.

Jess had sent a meme at eleven the night before. It was a good one. I'd get back to her. I pocketed my keys - house, the Civic, and the brass copy of the store's back door I wasn't technically supposed to have - and went out to the car.

Fifteen minutes, the same road as every morning, past the Dairy Queen and the long light that never changed when you wanted it to, the radio down low and my brain half on the fastener aisle Rick wanted faced before open and half on whether the gas could wait until Friday. I pulled into the lot a little past seven-fifteen, grabbed my phone off the passenger seat, and started across the asphalt toward the doors with my keys swinging off one finger. My foot caught on something, a pothole or the lip of a parking block, I never did sort out which. The ground came up fast and my hands flew out.

Then nothing.

When I pushed myself up off my hands and knees, the parking lot was gone.

I was in a field. A real one, cut down to stubble, brown and flat, running a long way out to a line of trees with a thin early sky over the top of it. No store, no road, nothing I could put a name to. The air came in wrong, too clean, all cut grass and dirt and none of the gas-and-hot-blacktop smell of the lot I'd been standing in a second ago. A bird I didn't know made a call I didn't know, and somewhere another one answered it. Without meaning to I looked up, and the sky was just blue and empty, not a plane or a wire on it anywhere.

Marcus. This had to be Marcus - exactly the kind of stunt he'd spend two weeks planning - and I grabbed the idea and held on with both hands. Marcus, and whoever he'd talked into helping.

"Marcus!" I scrambled up off the ground, turning, scanning the tree line. "Okay, ha ha, you got me, real funny, come out!" Nothing came out. A breeze moved the stubble and went still. I kicked a clod of dirt as hard as I could and it burst apart and helped nothing. "Marcus, come on, man, I'm gonna lose my whole shift, this isn't - " My voice went up and cracked and I let the rest of it drop.

The phone said seven-forty. Ninety-eight percent, because I'd pulled it off the charger on my way out the door. No bars, not even the little searching circle. I called Marcus and it died before it rang. My mom, the same. 911, which sat there saying *connecting*, *connecting*, and then quit on me. I'd gone down at seven-fifteen and now it was seven-forty. Twenty-five minutes, gone, and nothing in them I could find.

It came up all at once then. My chest pulled tight and my breath went fast and useless, and I spun a full circle looking for one normal thing, a building or a wire or a road, and there was none of it, and I could hear myself saying oh my god, oh my god, the words tripping over each other. My legs wanted to go and I let them, half sat and half dropped into the stubble, and ground the heels of my hands into my eyes until the dark behind them was only dark. "Okay," I said, and it came out shaking, so I said it again, slower, and that time it almost held. My thumb flicked the phone into airplane mode on its own, the old reflex from a hundred low-battery afternoons, and that was the one clear thing I managed before the rest of it closed back over me. It was something to do. I made myself find the next thing.

I felt the back of my head. No lump, no blood, not even an ache, which should have been a relief and wasn't, because I'd gone down hard enough that there should have been something. A concussion, maybe. A concussion would explain all of it - I'd watched a video once about a guy who came out of an eleven-day coma swearing the whole stretch had felt exactly like being awake. Maybe my mom was sitting by a hospital bed right now and none of this was real. Then the stubble bit into my palms, sharp and real, and the bird would not stop, and I couldn't make myself believe the hospital anymore.

Sitting there was worse than moving, so I got up, picked the direction with the trees, and turned the walk into a job, because a job I could do: watch my feet, count the rows off, find one single thing a person had made. The stubble crunched under my boots. The field gave out into a band of weeds and then a path, packed dirt with two grooves worn down the middle of it, smooth scoops about a stride apart, too narrow for a car, no tread anywhere in them. I crouched over them longer than I meant to. Just the smooth grooves, and pressed into the dried mud beside them, the cut shapes of hooves.

If I didn't show before the store opened, Rick was going to call my mom. The thought arrived plain and stupid and familiar, the same work-late guilt as any other morning, and sat on top of everything else, and under it was the worse one I kept trying not to look at straight: there was no road here. Not in any direction. I walked a long time after that, longer than any field back home went without hitting a fence or a wire or a phone pole, until my mouth dried out and the low sun came off the stubble in a flat glare, and the whole way I kept catching myself about to say something to somebody who wasn't there.

The settlement showed up a little at a time. The first building almost made me laugh. The walls were mud - actual dried mud, gone tan and split with cracks - under a roof of red clay tiles laid crooked, a cloth hung over the doorway where a door should be. I'd watched enough YouTube to know the back side of a movie set, or one of those living-history villages where people dress up and churn butter for tourists. Somebody was filming something out here, or playing pretend at something, and I'd come in the wrong way, and any second a guy in a vest and a headset was going to round the corner and chew me out for wandering into the shot.

I'd have paid money to get chewed out right then.

More buildings came up along the path, mud and rough stone leaned together, smoke leaking out of holes in some of the roofs. It smelled like animals and woodsmoke and something cooking I couldn't place, and off behind a wall a goat kept up a flat, complaining bleat. A cloth over a doorway twitched and a woman put her head out. Old, or worn down to looking old, a brown cloth over her hair, a dress the color of dust. She saw me and went still, the kind of still that nobody acts, and then she snapped the cloth shut so hard it cracked and she was gone. I could hear her behind it, low and fast.

"Okay," I said, low, just to hear it. "Okay." And I kept walking, because it was that or stand still.

People were out now. A man led a donkey stacked with bundles and swung wide around me, eyes straight ahead the whole time, careful not to look at me. Two barefoot kids stopped dead in the path and stared at my boots with their mouths open. A man crouched against a wall working a strip of leather got to his feet as I came near and turned the knife in his hand so it showed - not aimed at me, just there, just so I'd know it was. My heart, which hadn't really come down since the field, slammed back up into my throat, and I lifted both hands a little, no problem, no trouble, and kept moving, because stopping felt worse than going.

The clothes were the part I couldn't get past. Long shirts belted at the waist, down to the knee, sandals or nothing on the feet, and on every single person not one zipper, not one tag, not one printed word. I'd folded ten thousand T-shirts at work, and whatever this cloth was, no machine had made it: thick, a little lumpy, hanging heavy, dyed in colors that were really just whatever brown the dirt nearby was doing.

A bigger building had its door wedged open with a rock. The hinges were held on with nails, and I slowed up without meaning to - square, lumpy, each one a little different, nothing like the boxes of identical ones at work. Brentwood kept a few like these in a bin in the back that nobody ever bought. A door somebody actually used should not have them, and that landed wrong before I could say why, and I kept moving.

A man stepped into my path and said something with the shape of a question. Not Spanish - I'd bailed on Spanish in eighth grade over the rolled R's, but I knew the sound of it, and this wasn't the sound of it, or of French, or of anything my ear could catch a corner of.

"Do you speak English?" He held steady and watched me. "I'm lost. Do you have a phone?" I held mine up and pointed at it. He only said something slower, his eyes on my hands. "Danny. I'm Danny." I tapped my own chest. "I fell, I was in a parking lot, and then I'm just - here, and I don't even know where here is." My voice climbed and cracked and I made myself stop and drag a hand down my face. "Does anybody here speak anything? Anything I know?" Nobody answered, because nobody could, and being a foot from another person and locked all the way out of him turned out to be worse than the empty field had been, and for a second the urge to grab his shirt and shake him, just to make something happen, came up so hard my hands shook with it instead.

Somebody had painted a word over one of the doorways in big capital letters, and I went toward it because letters were at least letters. I read it left to right, then again. The shapes had been with me my whole life, an R and an A and one that was probably an N, but lined up like that they didn't spell anything in any language I'd ever met, and a third read didn't fix it. I knew how to read. I'd always known how to read, my whole life, and these letters just sat there over the door meaning nothing at all, and that scared me worse than the dirt houses had.

Past the last houses the ground climbed. I wanted to get up high and see one thing that made sense, so I went for the rise - past a pen of pigs that put out a smell thick enough to stop me breathing through my nose, past a cart riding on wheels of plain wood, just wood, banded and pegged and turning on a wooden axle - and at the top I stopped.

There was a city.

It sat out in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and ran up the hills behind it, bigger than anything had a right to be out here, a pale sprawl of stone with smoke standing off it in a hundred thin lines. Something in my chest unclenched so fast it left me light-headed. A city had streets. It had a hospital and somebody in charge and some way back to my mom. I started down toward it, already half-writing what I'd say to the first cop I found.

Then my eye snagged on the building at the near edge of it, and everything in my head stopped at once.

It was round, and it was huge even from up here, a pale ring of stone with arches stacked in rows up the face of it. I counted the rows before I knew I was counting. Four. The sun had just reached the top, and the stone was nearly white, clean and unbroken the whole way around, every arch sharp-edged and whole, like it had been finished that same year. I'd seen this building my whole life - on screens, in a textbook, in the eighth-grade class I'd mostly spent thinking about other stuff, on the side of a pizza box one time - and so had everyone, and in every single picture of it the thing was broken. Half fallen down, the top gone, chunks of it missing. That was the only way it ever looked.

This one was new.

My legs folded under me and I ended up on the path, knees up, arms wrapped around them, staring at the thing, and I couldn't look away and couldn't make it stop being there.

"No," I said out loud, soft, and then again, harder, "no." Somebody built a fake one. People did that. There was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But not the mud houses. Not the woman's face when she saw me. Not all of it, not at the same time. My stomach rolled over and I was suddenly certain I was going to be sick, and then, sideways and out of nowhere, I thought of my mom at the kitchen table that night asking how my shift went, and Maya asleep behind her creaky door, and the dark ordinary house I'd walked out of that morning, and I had no idea, none, how a person got back to any of that from here.

I got the phone out, almost dropped it, opened the camera, and aimed it down the valley. There it was in the little rectangle, tiny and steady and completely real. I took the shot. The fake shutter clicked, loud and stupid in all that quiet.

I zoomed in and went hunting the edges of it for the trick - a seam, a crane, the flat gray of a screen, the spot where a movie set always gives itself away. The picture only got grainy. There was nothing in it to catch.

The size of it wouldn't fit in my head. What came back instead was the timeline poster that ran the whole front wall of my eighth-grade history room, forty feet of it, and how far to the left you had to go to find the year 100. Almost off the end of the paper. Barely on the wall at all.

A dog started barking, back down in the settlement. Then voices, more than one, coming up the path fast.

I got up, my knees not all the way under me, and turned. Four men were climbing the rise in the same dust-brown shirts as everyone else, leather strapped over the chest on two of them. They had spears. Long ones, real ones, the points throwing back four small chips of sun. They came on at a walk and fanned out to put me in the middle, and the one in front held an arm out flat to keep the others off, his eyes moving over my hands, my boots, the cracked orange logo across my chest.

I didn't run. I made my feet stay where they were.

The phone went back into my pocket. I raised both hands, palms out, and held them there.
