# Chapter 5: Latin and the Little Ones

The first word I owned outright was *aqua*. Water.

I got it by being thirsty and stupid in front of people, which was how I got everything that winter. The boy who carried water for the workshop - a knock-kneed kid called Stichus, maybe ten, who did not like me - set a jug down by my bench, and I pointed at it and made a noise, and he looked at me like I'd asked him to explain fire and said the word slow, the way you talk to a dog. *Aqua.* I said it back wrong. He said it again. I got it closer. He picked up the jug and walked off, and I sat there saying the word to myself, because it was the first one I'd taken whole instead of guessing at the front of it.

That was how it went, for months. No wise old man teaching me by lamplight. Being thirsty and cold and humiliated, pointing at things, getting the word wrong, getting laughed at by a ten-year-old, trying again. The workshop ran on talk, the two men at the benches arguing about a joint or a price, the woman of the household, the boy, the people who came through the courtyard - ten hours a day of it, over me and around me, and some of it stuck. You can't help it. After a while you just know that *salve* means hello and *abi* means go away and the noise the cook made when she was annoyed meant *out of my kitchen.*

I built a grammar in the loft at night, on the inside of my own skull, because I had no tablet of my own and nothing to write with that wasn't theirs. I'm decent at patterns. That's the math talking. Two years of high-school French turned out to be the most useful thing my education handed me, and it had nothing to do with the French words. It was one idea: that a word could change shape depending on its job in the sentence. *Amo, amat* - same word, different work. Latin did it harder. It did it six ways and the six ways had exceptions. For weeks I used the form that meant someone was doing a thing *to* Daniel when I meant Daniel was doing the thing, so I went around announcing myself as the object of every sentence, which, fair.

The sounds were their own private joke at my expense. Their *c* was always hard, a *k*, no exceptions, so every soft *c* I'd ever read came out wrong in my mouth and right in theirs, and I had to unlearn a thousand words I'd never said out loud, only read. And the *v*. The *v* killed me. They said it like a *w*. I'd point at the wine, *vinum*, and say it with the buzzing *v* of home, and they'd squint, and Heras would say it back soft, *winum*, and the whole language tilted half a degree away from everything I'd assumed. I kept slipping the *v* back in for a year. It marked me foreign before I'd finished the first word, and all I could do was flatten it down a degree at a time.

Heras came every few days. I had his name by the time the rains set in, and getting it was its own small idiot triumph. He'd been "the physician" in my head for weeks, the clean-handed Greek who'd caught me lying about long division and decided he liked me for it. He came and went and I never had the language to ask the one thing you ask a person, which is who you are. Then one grey afternoon he was leaning over my bench correcting a figure I'd written, and the man who ran the workshop called across the courtyard to him by name, and I heard it land clean. Two syllables. I'd heard it a dozen times and never cut it out of the noise. This time I had enough words around it to know it was a name.

I pointed at him. "Heras."

He straightened up, and I got the dry uptick of the mouth gone wider than I'd ever earned. He put a finger on his own chest and said it again, properly, the Greek shape of it, and made me say it until I had the vowels close, and then he touched my chest and said my name, the mangled middle of it, *Danihel*, the version they could all pronounce, and we stood there formally introducing ourselves four months after we met. He found this funny. He had a way of finding things funny that never once involved laughing. The mouth went up a hair on one side and the eyes did something dry, and that was the whole performance, and I'd already learned to chase it.

He talked to me in Latin mostly, slow and clean, because Latin was what I needed to survive in the street. But when a Latin word annoyed him he slid into Greek, and I picked up Greek too, slower, sideways. The Greek words had pieces of English in them, or the other way around - *therm* and *photo* were already half-mine before I got here, and I caught them in his sentences and held on. I couldn't tell him I knew the roots. I couldn't tell him anything I knew.

The egg man showed me that. The marketplace, Heras buying, a fishmonger's neighbor watching, and out of nowhere, out of four months of swallowed words, I hear myself say it. I point at the eggs. In my broken collar-wrong Latin, the case probably wrong, I ask whether the hen laid them or Caesar did. Because it was an emperor's price. That was the joke. Barely a joke. But it lands, the case wrong and the *v* buzzing and all, because the fishmonger next door barks out a laugh and the egg man goes red, and Heras turns with that dry uptick gone wider than I'd earned. I got my eggs at the right price and laughed all the way back to the workshop like a man let out of a cell. Up in the loft that night the high came off it and left the drop. The people I'd have called to tell about the egg man would never be born. I lay there with the dead phone against my hip out of habit and didn't take it out, because there was nothing on it anymore, and went to sleep counting eggs in Latin to keep from counting anything else.

The little ones nearly got me killed, and I walked into it on purpose, because a kid was dying and I thought I knew something.

It was deep winter, the wet kind, everyone coughing, and the boy who worked the next bench cut his hand badly on a chisel and the cut went wrong. A wound here was a coin flip with death on the other side, and this one came up death's face. The hand swelled and went red and the red ran up his arm in a thin angry line crawling toward the elbow, and the boy went hot and stupid with fever, and they sent for Heras, and I, with my four months of Latin and my half a memory of a science class, decided this was the moment.

I'd been trying to get the idea across for weeks, in pieces, badly. Now I had a sick boy and a reason. When Heras came I waited until he'd examined the arm and done the things he did, the smelling, the pressing, the muttering about the humors, and then I got the wax tablet and tried to draw it. You can't draw what I was trying to draw. I drew dots. Crowds of them, swarming, going into the cut, into the red line, up the arm. I said the words I had. *Small. Living.* I made them with my fingers, tiny, crawling, walked my fingers up his arm like little legs, and said, in the Latin of a four-month-old, that they were small living ones, that they went in, that they climbed inside you and ate. I didn't have the word for invisible, so I said small past seeing. And because I'd spent four months listening to people swear by gods at every turn, I added the only cover I had. I pointed up. The gods send them. Tiny living creatures the gods send to climb inside you and eat you from the middle out.

Heras let me finish. He let me get all the way to the end of it. And when I was done he took the stylus out of my hand, gently, and he took me apart.

"*Aer*," he said. He pointed at the swollen arm and then up at the air around us. "*Aer malus*." Bad air. He swept his hand low under his face, slow, like a fog over a marsh. The wet, rotten kind, the smell from the standing water out past the wall. *That* was what climbed into a wound.

"No - " I had three words and none of them were going to get me there. "Heras. Not air. Small. Living."

He didn't even pause. He pressed his four fingers to his own forearm and named them, one at a time, twice each so I'd catch it. *Humor*. The body had four of these in it, and they were either wet, dry, hot, or cold, and they were supposed to balance, and a fever was the balance gone wrong. He held up four fingers. He held up two. Two of them were off. He drew the imbalance in the air with his hands like a pair of scales tipping. Then he said a name and the man at the bench actually nodded.

"Hippocrates."

I knew the name from a poster in a doctor's waiting room. That was the whole of what I knew. "Yeah," I said. "Hippocrates. I - yeah."

He waited a beat to see if I was going to argue with Hippocrates. I wasn't going to argue with Hippocrates. He went on. The third thing was harder, longer, and I lost most of it. I caught *deus* and *ira*, gods and anger. A wrong done at an altar. He pointed up. I'd pointed up too, ten minutes ago, but his up and mine had not meant the same thing at all, and I felt my whole little story tip sideways.

He tapped my drawing of the dots. He said a word I didn't know.

"What?"

He said it again, slower. The man at the bench snorted, low, into his hand. Heras said it at me a third time and I understood from the shape of how he said it that it was the kind of word old women in the hills said when a baby died in the dark. Witch-talk. The kind of word the law had teeth about. He was telling me, as a friend, to shut up about my little animals before I said it where it mattered.

"You understand me?" he said.

"Yeah," I said. "I - yeah. I understand."

"Good." He set the stylus down on the tablet over my drawing of the dots, covering it. "These things you believe. Keep them to yourself."

I'd known it was a risk. I hadn't known how much. I'd reached for the one true thing I had, the half-memory of a video, swarming dots under a microscope I'd seen once on a screen, and I'd reached for it because the kid was dying and I was sure. And Heras had just shown me that being sure was worth nothing here. I had a story and no way on earth to make him see the dots. I picked up the chores I'd set down and didn't raise it again.

I couldn't make him believe in the little ones. But I could make him *do the practices*, because the practices worked, and Heras, whatever else he was, would do what worked even if he hated the explanation.

I took the water he meant to wash the boy's arm with and wouldn't let him use it cold from the jug. I made him boil it. I had no word for *sterilize* and there's no such concept here anyway, so I framed it the one way that didn't sound like witchcraft: hot water is clean, fire cleans, you wouldn't eat off a dirty plate, don't wash a wound with dirty water, the fire makes it clean past what the eye can judge. That, he could take. Cleanliness he understood. He boiled it, let it cool, and we washed the arm properly and washed it again, and washed our own hands first, which I made a thing of, which he thought was fussy and did anyway because the fussy foreigner wouldn't stop until he did. We kept the wound open and clean instead of packing it with the muck they'd packed it with. He knew his own herbs, the ones that helped, and I didn't argue any of that, because he'd cut up more bodies than I'd seen and I wasn't about to play doctor at him. I owned the water and the hands. He kept everything else.

The boy lived. Boys with red lines up their arms sometimes live no matter what you do and sometimes die no matter what you do. One kid was not proof of anything. He might have lived either way. I knew that even lying awake glad he was breathing.

But Heras saw the boy keep his arm. And weeks later, a different patient, he sent the household girl to boil the water before I'd said a word, and washed his hands first without being told, and when I caught his eye he gave me nothing, no credit, no concession, just went on as if he'd always done it this way. He never once said I was right about the little ones. To the end of that winter and probably the end of his life he thought my invisible animals were a superstition I'd carried down off the ice, a barbarian's ghost story. He just quietly started boiling his water and washing his hands, because he could *see* it helped, and the story he hung on it was his own business.

I told him about the soldiers' *posca* one afternoon, sour wine and water, that it washed a scrape cleaner than plain water did. I half-remembered the acid did something and couldn't tell him what.

"How do you know this?" he said. Not hostile - just the physician's question, flat.

"I - uh." I turned my cup in my hands. "I heard it. From someone. Back home."

"From someone," he said. I didn't say anything else and he didn't push it. He went on without comment. We both did.

Around this same time I noticed there was no soap in the household. There was oil, used for cleaning the skin the Roman way - rub, scrape, rinse. There was ash, used to clean pots. But no soap. Soap, I half-remembered, was fat and ash and water cooked together, more or less. I melted down the fat trimmed off the meat that would otherwise go to the dogs. I ran water through a bowl of wood ash, the way you'd brew the world's worst tea, and boiled what came out down to a sludge. I combined the two and heated them, and got a soft gray-brown mess that smelled like something had died in a swamp and burned like a second wound on any cut it touched. I gave some to Heras with no explanation. He smelled it, made a face, touched it to the back of his hand, made a different face, and set it down. "What is this," he said. "Soap," I said, which was an English word and meant nothing to him. "I mean - it's for washing. Like oil, but - it lathers. With water. I'll make a better batch." He set it back on the shelf. "Make a better batch," he said.

Three tries later I had a cake that was merely unpleasant rather than actively hostile. It cleaned well. It didn't dissolve on contact with water like the first batch. Heras started using it without acknowledging it had come from me. The household eventually started using it too, and I never heard anyone call it anything but "the Thulean's muck." Which was fine. I didn't need them to call it soap.

This was the same winter I noticed the pipes. The pipes were lead. So were a lot of the pots. Soft gray metal everywhere the water touched, in the household and every house I'd been in since I'd got here, because lead was cheap and easy to bend and held a seal. I knew lead was bad for you. I didn't know all of why, exactly. Something about your nerves and your head, the dose-over-time thing they tell you when you're seven and someone's painting your house. And here I was eating off it and drinking out of it and there was not a single thing I could do about any of it at scale. What I could do was stop using the lead pot in my corner of the kitchen for my own food. I borrowed a clay one instead.

"Heras," I said one afternoon. "Defrutum. The grape-wine syrup. Made in lead pots." He said yes without looking up from his tablet. "I'm - okay, so. I think lead is bad for you. Like, if you eat off it and drink off it your whole life. I think it does something to your head." I touched my temple. "Over time."

He kept the particular expression he had for my witch-talk. Patient. Courteous. Absolutely unpersuaded.

"I can't prove it," I said. "I just - I'm not using lead pots anymore. For my own food. I thought you should know."

"I will consider it," he said, which was his way of ending a conversation he found exhausting. I moved the lead pot to the floor and set the clay one on the shelf, and added the pipes themselves to the list of things I couldn't get to yet.

Spring came around again. I'd been here a year. Eighteen, now, by my own count off the seasons, though no one here cared and there was no candle and no cake and no one to tell.

I could hold a conversation about a thing in front of us - a tool, a price, a body, the weather, the work. I could follow Heras at his slow and lose him entirely when he sped up with another educated man, the Greek flying, the long words stacking, and I'd be back to catching one word in five. I made the kind of mistakes that cost me. Wrong cases that turned a sentence sideways. The *v* that buzzed and marked me foreign in the first three words. I could barely read at all, only the figures and a few words. And people heard the bad Latin and figured my whole head was about the same size as my worst sentence, which meant most days I was talking to people who thought I was slow. Heras had skipped that whole thing. He'd priced my head off a long-division problem in week one, and the bad Latin hadn't dragged it back down, and that was the only reason I could stand it.

I was lodged in a workshop off a household that wasn't mine, fed by people who watched the door, useful enough to keep and not free to leave, a foreigner with a homeland nobody believed in. So I made my first quiet move, and it cost me nothing, which was the whole point. Somewhere in the weeks after the arm healed, I started cooking for myself in the evenings when the kitchen fire was still lit and the household had eaten. I had eggs, always eggs, the hens in the yard producing reliably. Oil. Whatever herbs the cook left bundled by the wall. I beat the eggs in a clay bowl and poured them into the pan in the hot oil and watched them set from the edge inward - eggs over a gas burner, the most ordinary thing I'd ever done, over a burner I would never see again. I folded one side over. I slid it out.

The cook's assistant, a girl of maybe twelve, had been watching from the doorway. She looked at it on the plate, then at me.

"That," she said, in the considered manner of someone making a judgment, "is how barbarians eat."

She said it as if this settled something. Then she asked if there was more. I gave her half. She ate it standing up and didn't say anything else about it.

The garum was its own education, slower. My first month I ate around it - moved it to the edge of whatever I was handed, breathed through my mouth. The smell was honest-to-god rotting fish, or that was how I read it, and somewhere in my head was the knowledge that this was intentional, this was the seasoning, this was what went on everything. That knowledge and the smell were two different facts that refused to merge. They merged eventually. I can't say exactly when. There was a morning that winter when they put a bowl down in front of me, lentils and cumin and something I didn't bother to identify, and I ate it all the way to the bottom without noticing I'd done it. Later I thought back and realized: garum. The whole depth of it, the back of the throat, the quality that had been missing from everything I'd cooked when I tried to cook without it. Just fish sauce. Rome had figured it out and I was the last one to get there.

That morning I was making my eggs with a little lovage - which was available and sharp and tasted like celery had a fight with pepper - and I seasoned them with garum without making a face. Three drops from the small jug Stichus kept near the fire. The eggs came out better. And sometimes, eating something this far from what I'd grown up eating, I thought about where the food I actually missed was growing. Tomatoes. Potatoes. Corn. Chocolate, if you could get to the right coast. I'd drawn those western landmasses on the magistrate's map. I knew roughly where they were. A ship, the right kind of guidance, enough time. Another one for the pile.

Outside the kitchen, sometime that same season, I saw a horse choking in its harness. A cart horse, loaded with timber, collar tight at the throat so every pull pressed the windpipe. The animal was pulling badly and the driver was laying into it for pulling badly. I stood in the street for a second thinking - wait, that's not how horses pull stuff back home. The Amish carts I'd seen on a field trip once, the draft horses in some documentary, the harness sat low across the chest, not jammed up under the throat.

I went over. I had it in my head I was going to fix it. I had no business going over. I didn't even have the words. I pointed at the horse's neck and said "*malum*," bad, and I made a choking motion with my own hand at my own throat, and the driver gave me a flat tired look that meant I was the part of his morning he hadn't earned. He said something fast that I caught maybe one word of. Something about my mother, possibly. He turned his back on me and laid into the horse again. I backed off with my hands up. The horse went on choking.

That night I tried to draw the chest-and-shoulder version from memory, the shape of it more than anything, and the next morning I took the sketch to the leather-worker two streets over.

The man turned the sketch over in his hands. He was a square-faced freedman with a workbench full of bridles and thick-fingered hands that read the drawing without any effort.

"It'd work," he said. He sounded slightly annoyed about it. "More leather to stitch. More fitting. People don't want more fitting." Then he held the tablet up at an angle and went over it again, and the annoyance went into something else. "Where'd you see one of these?"

I had no answer ready. "I - back home."

"Back home." He didn't say anything for a second. His finger went down the lines I'd drawn - the curve, the strap placement, the spread across the chest. They were too clean. They were the kind of clean that meant somebody, somewhere, had already worked the thing out and showed it to you. "You see a lot of harness, back home?"

"I saw - some. Sometimes."

He handed the tablet back. "It'd work. Nobody's going to want it." He went back to the bridle he was stitching, and he didn't ask me anything else, but he had a face on him now I hadn't put there at the start. I left.

I took the sketch home and wrote it up in English that night, and put it on the pile.

The small wins were not going to be enough. One person who heard past the accent, an arm that healed, some laughter at a fish stall, a jug of garum I'd stopped avoiding. That was my whole dent in the world. I needed something I could just show people. Heras had taught me that, cold, with the little ones - a story wasn't going to do it. It had to be a thing they could see.

Around this same time I started thinking about the glass. In the workshop there were always scraps of the poor greenish stuff, thick and full of bubbles, next to useless for anything fine. I needed better and knew I'd need better for a long time, and I had no particular means to produce it myself. I wrote out what I wanted on a wax tablet in my broken but improving Latin: a pane of glass, one handspan across, through which a man could read a line of text from twice arm's length without the letters swimming in color. I added a figure. One month's skilled wages to the first man who brought me such a thing and told me how he'd made it.

I didn't sign it with my name. I gave it to a quiet capable man named Drusus at the guild contacts - the kind of person whose function seemed to be knowing where things were sourced - and said I'd like it posted among the glassworkers through whichever channels he used. Drusus asked if I wanted to know the attempts as they came; I said only when someone succeeded. He gave me a look that meant nothing in particular and took the tablet. Three craftsmen asked after it over the following months, according to what I heard back through him. None of them had the answer yet. The question was out there now, working on its own.

It was still winter when I landed on the other thing. I was up in the loft, lying close to the floor, and a draft came up under the tiles - the smoke off the household fire below, warm air pushing through the gaps so you could feel it on your face if you lay there. The heat rising, the smoke rising, always up. I'd known the reason for that since I was twelve. Hot air is lighter. Hot air climbs. The gods don't send it up, it just goes, and nobody in this entire world knew the reason and I did, I had that one for free, and I lay there with my hand in the rising warm and thought about a bag.

A bag full of hot air. Light enough, full enough of fire-warmed air, that it would do what the smoke did. The same thing I'd watched a thousand times over a campfire and a candle and never once stopped on. It would go *up*. Off the ground, into the sky, in front of everyone. I didn't know how to build it. I didn't know what it cost or what it weighed or whether the cloth would hold or burn. But I knew it would rise. The same way I'd known the eggs were an emperor's price - except this time the thing I knew was true.

The first problem was the cloth. I needed to know what I had access to and what it cost before I said a word to anyone. I lay there a while longer with my hand in the warm draft, running through what I'd seen at the wharves and what I hadn't.

I fell asleep thinking about linen weights.
