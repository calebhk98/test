# Chapter 45: What It Serves

The accounts came to me out of Judaea the way everything came to me now, late and on someone else's errand. Eudemus sent them down to the wharf with a clerk and a covering note that asked me to cast a column the fiscus had got into a tangle, because the war had thrown the eastern books out of true and the man who balanced them had died of a flux. The figures were mine and so was the only living hand that could be trusted to find where a province had stopped adding up.

I had not asked to see them. I did not go looking. The leaves came to me because I had taught Rome to keep its sums in marks a tired clerk could not fudge, and a war is the largest sum an empire ever does, and so the war came to my bench in my own figures, fair-copied in Ulpia's clean hand off the field rolls, the two and the circle and the rest, the marks they had cut into the milestone, adding up everything.

I read them at arm's length by the window because my eyes would no longer take small marks by lamp. Ulpia read the smaller hands to me where the field clerks had crowded them. We sat at it three mornings.

Four days after the first leaves arrived, a discharged man at the wharf, an auxiliary mustered out and working a grain-hull for his passage home, told Pamphilus something about the country. Pamphilus brought it to me because Pamphilus knew the eastern books had crossed my bench.

Pamphilus stood in my tally-room in the afternoon light. He had the man's words by memory, as he always did.

"Says the rebels gone in the ground," Pamphilus said. "Whole families, grain and water and goats all, into the holes under the towns. And the soldiers, they just sat. Mouth of the hole. Sat there the whole season." He looked at his hands, then at me. "Long way from here, that. None of your doing." He went back to the cloth.

I sat a while with what he had brought me. The man who told it had the contempt in his voice a man gets when he has been frightened by someone and then survived them: a whole season at the mouth of a hole in the rock, on short rations himself, while the people inside were brought to the end of theirs. That was the part of the story I was not in. I had not, of course, done only that.

A dispatch reached the city in the spring, read out and chalked up and gone over in the porticoes, that the war was as good as won and that the emperor would refound the rebel capital as Aelia Capitolina, after his own name and the god of the Capitol, a colony on the bones of the old city, the plough run round its boundary the old way, a temple where their temple had been, and a standing prohibition that no Jew set foot in it on pain of death. The men in the portico thought it a fine settlement of a tiresome matter. A city was a city; a colony was prestige and land for veterans; the emperor was a great founder of cities, and here was another, and the only difference any of them remarked on was the strangeness of a people who would rather die in a hole than live under a new and well-drained street plan.

I knew the street plan they meant. I had drawn that grid for other colonies, the cardo and the decumanus square to the compass, the falls worked out so the drains ran, the aqueduct sited off a survey done to the one scale in my figures. I did not draw this one. But I had drawn the kind of it, and taught the men who drew it. A colony laid out clean over the place where a city had been razed and its people driven off or dead is a survey's work as much as any other.

Heras was a year and more in the ground. The want of him came up in me the way a tongue goes to a pulled tooth, a reaching to the place where he had been. He would have sat on the bench under the window with his bad chest and his arms folded and let me talk myself out, and then he would have found the soft place in what I said and put his thumb on it. He was good at that. He was the only one who had ever been good at that with me, because Marcia weighs a proposal for what it will do and Tyche is on my side past the point of use to me and the rest of them think I am the Thulean and cannot help it.

So I did it without him. Badly. I argued it on the cold bench in the empty room after the day shift had gone. I said, to no one, in the Greek I used to use with him because it kept my own voice strange enough to hear, that a man is not answerable for the use another man makes of a true skill. That the figures are only counting and counting is good. That a clean column is a defense against the tax-farmer as much as a tool of him. That I had fought slavery with the only weapons I had, and a slave market crashing under its own weight was what I had wanted. The market falling in the accounts was the market drowned in the bodies of a destroyed people, and the figure was the same figure either way, falling, and the figure did not know the difference and neither, it turned out, did the world.

I asked the empty bench whether a man who carries water into a camp so the men do not die of the flux is answerable for what the men he kept alive then do with their hands. Heras would have made me say what I meant by answerable. He would have asked me whether I was looking for the truth of it or for permission, and I would not have been able to lie to his face as I can lie to a bench. I sat in the empty room until the coals went cold, then I got up and went to bed, which was what he would have said to do.

The bronze siege bombards are real and have been real since Felix and Hermes and the foundry-men worked out the casting weight that would hold. Twenty-seven years since the first tube burst on the river-bank, and now they do not burst; they sit on their oak frames at twenty paces from a fortified wall and they put a hundred-pound stone through it in the time it takes to re-lay the charge. Rome knows how to use them. The army learned at Dacia and Parthia and learned again in Judaea, faster than before, because they had had the practice and the trained crews and the powder kegs with the waxed seals Hermes's foundry stamps. My bronze. My ratios. My primer cord design.

There are no field guns yet, no cannon a horse-column can bring to a moving line and trust not to kill its own crew when the axle jolts wrong. That problem is still the steel problem, which is still the thermocouple problem, which I will not solve. Mobile artillery is what I have not done. But I did not have to do it. A wall is not a moving target. A cave-fortress is not a moving target. A dry-rock hillside village behind a wall is not a moving target, and Bar Kokhba's fighters had built their strength in the dry-rock hillside villages, and my bombards came for the walls.

This I know. Not from the accounts, though the accounts have it there for any man who can read the quartermasters' abbreviations. I know it from a letter a Guard tribune sent me after, to say the work I had done on bronze densities had proved useful in the eastern theater, and that the army was in his personal debt. He did not say for what, specifically. He did not need to. The polite specificity of a soldier to an imperial freedman on a matter of public record is its own kind of saying.

I know what my bombards did to those walls. I know the walls came down in days where without them they would have held for months, and I know that the families who went into the ground under those walls came out of the ground faster than they would have otherwise, into the army's hands, and I know the number of surviving prisoners in the accounts because that is the column I was asked to balance and I balanced it.

As for what else the powder did in that country, I cannot inventory every keg. Quarrymen have known the cave-demolition use since Maximus split the block at his quarry and stood watching the dust come down. Soldiers are always long on kegs once the walls are down, and soldiers know what quarrymen know, and I know what siege weapons do when a commander points them at a fortified position, and the dry-rock villages of Judaea were full of fortified positions. I have turned this over long enough to know that the gap between knowing about walls and not-knowing about individual caves is the gap between a man who can be precise and one who has chosen a comfortable stopping point. I sat on the cold bench and put the tablet down and did not reach for the comfortable stop.

They call me the Thulean still, in the markets and the porticoes, the man who flew, the man who taught the figures, the wonder-worker from the far cold north. It is a good name to have. It opens doors and it has more than once kept me out of a cell, and I built it on purpose, day by day, out of a balloon and a map and a string of demonstrations, because a man with no city and no people in this world needs a name that does the work a city does.

I had thought of the name as mine, a garment I had cut and put on. I sat on the cold bench that winter and understood it the other way. A man useful to power is a tool in power's hand, and a tool is used for the work the hand is doing, and the hand in Judaea was doing what it was doing, and my figures were in its grip, and my drains were under its camps keeping it healthy enough to do it, and my survey was square to the compass over the razed ground, and my bombards had come for the walls.

The Thulean is the name a slaughtering empire gives the foreigner who makes the slaughter run cleaner, and I had spent thirty-eight years polishing that name and presenting it at every door, and the doors had opened, and behind those doors the empire had been doing its work, and my hands were in it up to the wrist. I sat on the cold bench in the dark and the lamp on the table had gone out and I had not lit another.

I went back to the accounts on the fourth morning and worked through until the first leaves were done.

The first leaves were grain. So many men eat so much bread a day, and the bread is so much grain, and the grain is so many ships, and the ships are so many days, and the days were now into the fourth year. The column balanced. It was a good column. Whoever had drawn it had used the carry-mark correctly and had not once hidden a shortfall in a Roman tail where it could rot unseen, which was the whole purpose I had built the figures to serve, and they had served it, and the work they had made legible was the feeding of the army that was burning Judaea down to its roots.

There is a kind of reading where the eye runs ahead of the man. I had read three more leaves before I understood what the fourth column was.

It was headed in the field clerk's abbreviation, which Ulpia read out flat because she did not know the country and the word was only a word to her. Captives. The fiscus took its tithe of the captives as it took its tithe of everything, and the captives were a revenue, and a revenue is reckoned, and so they had been counted and valued and the value entered, and because the price falls when the market floods, there was a second column beside the first that tracked the price, and the price was falling.

I sat and watched the price of a human being fall down a column of my own marks across the seasons of the war. The clerk had noted, in the margin, in a tradesman's shorthand, that the markets at the staging ports could not absorb the supply and that lots were being driven north and west to find a price, and that even so the price was a fraction of the standing rate, and he had given the fraction in my figures, exact, because my figures are exact. That is what they are for.

Ulpia stopped reading. She had got to the bottom of the column and there was nothing under it but the rule and the total, and she did not read the total. She put her finger on the rule under the last figure and waited.

I could not tell, sitting there, and cannot tell now, writing it, whether I had been a man doing the only duty a man with my knowledge could do, which is to lift the bottom rungs and trust the climbing to better hands than mine, or a boy who decided in a cell to stay alive and thought he knew better than the whole turning world what the world should have, and gave it, and it served. Both arguments are good arguments. I have made them both to the empty bench in the dark and neither has ever once won, and I no longer have the man who could have told me whether either was even the right question.

Ulpia sat with her finger on the rule. I sat with the column. Neither of us said anything for a long time.

I went up to the house late, past the hour the gate is barred, and Marcia was awake.

She was at the storeroom table with a single lamp drawn close and the day's tallies under it, working them because her eyes were still good enough to and mine were not, and she did not ask where I had been. She moved the lamp so it would not be in my face when I sat down. That was all. She finished the column she was on, and ruled it off, and set the stylus down, and we sat a while in the smell of pepper and rope and warm oil that her dead husband's warehouse had never lost.

After a time she said: "Eastern grain will come down hard once the province is settled and the levies ease. Don't be long on grain contracts this season."

I said no, we shouldn't.

We talked about grain, the price of it, the ships, the season. Two people who reckon for their living, talking the language they both knew how to talk, while the lamp burned down. Neither of us said the other column, the one beside the grain, the one that had been falling. I held the grain figures for what they were, and she knew that, and let me, and we sat with the sums until the oil was low and she banked the lamp and we went in.
