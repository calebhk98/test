# Chapter 6: Cheap, Mine, Undeniable

I sat on the idea for three days before I touched a single thing. The idea was the only part I had to myself, and I didn't want to burn through it before I had a plan.

And I was a slave in everything but the word, and a slave can't just walk out and buy cloth. The fire belonged to the household. The cloth belonged to the household. The rope, the oil, the time, the hands - everything I would need belonged to somebody else. I had nothing to build it out of and no money to get the nothing.

So the first work was argument - reasoning my way to why this and not something else, because I'd already burned a year on things that didn't take.

I'd given them the numbers, the best thing I had, a whole machine for thinking handed over for free, and what had it bought me. Heras showed them to a few men who kept accounts and they squinted and said the figures were clever and went on cutting their tallies into wood the way their fathers had. Same with the little ones. I'd had the truth in my hands and Heras had taken it apart in an afternoon because I couldn't show it to him. Everything I'd tried, a man could wave away. The numbers were a trick. The creatures were witch-talk. The map was a curiosity in a drawer.

A thing that flew was different. If a bag rose up over the town, there was no haruspex alive who could stand under it pointing up and tell the crowd it hadn't.

It would also be cheap, which mattered more than anything. I could not buy a workshop. I could not buy a single craftsman's day. But hot air was free, fire I could beg, and cloth, even scrap, even rag, I could maybe scrounge a piece at a time without anyone deciding I was worth stopping. I could build it slow, with my own hands, out of what fell off other people's work, and nobody had to give a beggar permission to collect rags. Cheap, and mine, and impossible to argue with.

What I did not have was the faintest idea how to actually do it.

I started with the lightest material I could find, except the lightest material in Rome was already someone's garment. There's no paper here. I kept forgetting and reaching for it. There's papyrus, and papyrus is money, sheets of it, scraped and pressed and sold, and the household had exactly none lying around for a foreigner to set on fire. What they had was an old man's worth of accounts on wax and a few brittle scroll-ends too stained to keep, and I wasn't getting near those.

So the first model was nothing. Was rag. I begged Stichus for it.

The boy hauled water and ran errands and was ten years old and treated me, on the good days, like a half-broken dog he'd been told to feed. I'd spent the winter making him laugh, which is a thing you can do across a language if you're willing to be the joke, and I cashed some of that in. I wanted the scraps off the cloth the women cut, the trimmings, the bits too small to mend with, the ravelings that swept up off the floor. He thought I'd lost my mind, which was the going opinion.

"Rags," he said. No question in it. Just verifying.

"Yeah," I said. "The small ones. Off the floor."

He looked at me. Then he shrugged and brought me a fistful of linen offcuts anyway because I'd promised him I'd show him a thing the gods do, and at ten a thing the gods do beats almost any reason not to.

Linen is wrong for this. The first time I found it out was small and almost gentle. I cut and I had nothing to cut with but a borrowed knife that I had to give back, and I tried to sew a little bag out of four scraps of linen the size of my hand, and it came out a sad puckered article the size of a wineskin, and I held it over the household fire by the neck and felt the warm push up into it and the whole sorry thing just sat in my hand and got hot. It didn't even try. Linen's heavy. The cloth weighs more than the warm air inside a thing that small can ever lift, and I knew that, I think, in some back part of my head, I'd just hoped the knowing was wrong.

Stichus laughed at me. Fair.

But it had pushed. Held over the fire it had filled, gone taut, wanted to go somewhere. It had only failed because it was too heavy. I lay awake that night oddly steadied. The failing had told me the problem. Bigger. Lighter. I'd been keeping a stub of a ledger in the margins of an old wax tablet - what I'd spent on each try, what I'd learned. I scratched a new line: one scrap linen, 0 asses, one lesson. The list of what I still needed ran to the bottom of the tablet.

I needed something lighter than linen and there isn't anything, not here, not that I could get. No silk a beggar can touch, no oiled paper, no nothing. So I did the cleverest thing I managed all month, which tells you how thin the month was for cleverness.

I made it out of the thinnest stuff in the building, which was the worn-through cloth nobody would miss, the parts of old tunics so threadbare you could see the fire through them. Stichus and I picked them apart for a week. I built the second model the size of a feed sack, light panels of see-through cloth, and I shaped it like the campfire smoke wanted to go, narrow at the bottom where the heat came in and belling out above. No equations. I had the vague idea that a shape like a teardrop standing on its small end was right because that was the shape smoke made when it rose calm, and that was the entire engineering, a memory of smoke.

I needed sustained heat under it and an open mouth to let the heat in, and that was the part that nearly ended the project on a charge of arson. You can't lift a hot-air bag with a brazier you carry over. The air cools the second it's in the bag and the thing wants to come down as fast as it went up, so you have to keep feeding warm into the mouth while it's tethered low, and the only way I had to feed warm was open flame, and open flame under a bag of see-through rag is precisely as stupid as it sounds.

The first time the thin model went up it was the best moment I'd had in the world in over a year.

I did it in the little yard behind the workshop, early, before the household was properly awake, with Stichus holding a string tied to the bag's mouth and me feeding a small fire under it through a cone of green wood I'd rigged to funnel the heat up. It took forever. I burned my knuckles twice getting the fire to behave, and the bag just hung there filling, going round, going taut, and then the string came up tight in the kid's fist and the whole light see-through thing stood up off the ground and pulled, lifted, hung in the morning air at the end of the line with the fire-shimmer rising into it, a made thing, my made thing, floating.

Stichus screamed. Good scream, the gods-do-a-thing scream. I started laughing like an idiot.

It flew for as long as I fed it and not one breath longer. The second the warm stopped coming the thing sagged and sank and settled back onto the dirt. The heat was the whole fight - feeding it, keeping it alive under cloth, the fire I'd have to keep going. There was no safe version of that. The warm I'd worked to push into the bottom poured right back out through every gap in the weave.

Heras came around the corner of the workshop while it was still up. He'd been sent for the household, some morning complaint, and he stood there in the gate with his satchel and watched the rag bag hang at the end of the string over a small dangerous fire in a yard that wasn't mine. His face did nothing on purpose.

"Very pretty," he said, in the careful voice you'd use to tell a child his mud horse was very lifelike. Then he looked at the green-wood cone scorched black on its inner face, and the cloth gone brown at the bag's mouth. "Are you planning to burn down this household, or did you just want a fire."

I looked at the cone. At the bag's scorched lip.

"I mean - it's kind of under control," I said. "Mostly."

Heras grunted at "mostly" and walked back through the gate. I let the fire die.

The household had a point too, and they made it less kindly. You cannot run an open flame in a city of wooden lofts without people noticing, and Rome's towns burn, everyone's terrified of fire, and rightly, the whole place goes up like kindling on a dry month. The woman who ran the kitchen, who I'd never gotten a name out of and who ran more of the place than the master did, came out the second morning when she smelled the smoke wrong and saw the scorch and the rag and the foreigner crouched over a fire too close to the wall. She didn't need much Latin to make her point.

"Out," she said. She pointed past the gate with a wooden spoon. "Out. Out of this yard. Take your fire somewhere it can burn somebody else's roof."

The spoon came past my ear when I didn't move fast enough. I lost the yard. I lost the morning fire. If I wanted to play with flames I could do it well away from anything that mattered, which meant the open lot past the midden where they slaughtered, and it meant I'd lost the easy fire and now had to beg coals and carry them.

It cost me, all of it, in the small steady way that everything cost me there. I'd burned through a month of Stichus's goodwill and a fistful of rag and the kitchen woman's tolerance, and what I had to show was a feed-sack of worn-out cloth that flew for as long as I fed a fire I wasn't allowed to feed anymore. I'd grabbed the hot cone once and had a blood-blister across two knuckles to show for it. Small wound. I washed it and watched it and went on.

But the thin model proved the lift, and now I had to make it big, and big meant linen.

There's no getting around the linen. Rag flies because it weighs nothing, and it weighs nothing because it's half holes, so a rag balloon can only ever be a toy on a string. A real one has to hold the heat, and holding the heat means a tight weave, which means linen. And linen is heavy, and leaks at every seam, and burns.

And linen, the good cloth, I could not just have. This was the wall again, the one a free man with a coin never even sees. I needed an arm's length of decent linen and the household was not going to hand a length of decent cloth to a foreigner to set on fire. So I bargained, owning nothing to bargain with.

I went to Heras. He was the only man who'd ever paid full price for my mind and the only one with a denarius to his name who'd hear me out, and I made the pitch over a sick man's leg one afternoon, in pieces, in my bad Latin, with my hands. I told him I needed a length of close-woven linen and grease to seal it and rope and a brazier I could leave standing, and I told him why, the whole why, the flying, the man-cannot-call-the-sky-a-lie of it.

Heras listened, finished what he was doing with the sick man's leg, then wiped his hands on a cloth.

"Linen burns," he said. "Grease burns worse. You will weigh it down sealing it and then you will set the seal alight and then you will have a heavy bag that is also on fire." He looked at me. "I want to be standing well back when you do."

"Yeah, I know," I said. "I know. But - okay, can you just - the rag one worked. You saw it. You were standing there when it -"

"It was a rag on a string that sagged when the heat stopped."

"Right, but it lifted. It actually lifted, and if I can get a bigger one that holds the heat better -"

"The bigger one will hold the heat until the grease catches. Which it will."

"I think I can maybe - the seam, if I sew it right -" I stopped. He was looking at the sick man's leg and not at me. "Okay. Look. I know you think I'm going to set something on fire."

"I am certain of it," Heras said. "I am funding it anyway." He picked up his satchel. "One length of linen. One pot of mutton grease. Not out of belief, you understand. Out of curiosity. If you burn the slaughter-lot down I have not heard of you."

He gave me the cloth and he was right about all of it, which I'd demonstrate thoroughly over the next hour, in the slaughter-lot past the midden.

I sealed the linen with mutton grease rubbed in warm, because I half-remembered that you have to close the weave or the heat pours through, and grease was the only sealant I had. The cloth went from cloth to a heavy, stinking, greasy hide that the heat couldn't pour through. It solved the leak and made everything else worse. The sealed panels weighed nearly double. The bag I sewed out of them, big as a man could reach around, sat on the dirt of the lot like a dead animal, and when I got the brazier going under it through the green-wood cone and fed it steady and fed it and fed it, it filled, slowly, sullenly, and it did not lift. It got hot. It strained. The whole greasy mass swelled and trembled and wanted to and could not, because I'd added so much weight in the sealing that the warm air inside, even good and hot, could not carry the skin that held it.

So I made the fire bigger. Of course I made the fire bigger. That's what you do, you're cold so you build the fire up, you want lift so you make more heat, and a man with no equations and a thing that almost flew is going to feed the fire until the thing flies or stops him. The flame came up off the brazier higher than I meant, into the cone, up into the mouth of the bag, and the greased linen at the lip caught.

The grease at the mouth lit with a low blue-edged crawl. The flame walked up the inside of the bag along the seam. The linen browned and split where it burned, and the heat I'd worked an hour to trap came howling out the new hole, and the whole thing collapsed onto the brazier. I grabbed the board Stichus had left by the wall and started beating it. The fire was hot enough to push my face back. There went the afternoon. There went a length of Heras's linen and a pot of grease and most of my faith in myself.

I put it out. I burned my forearm doing it, a real burn this time, a line of shiny red up the inside of my arm where I'd reached past the flame. It blistered and it hurt for a week. I cleaned it the way I'd taught Heras to clean things and I sat down next to the smoking ruin. I'd just set fire to something I'd begged for. I wanted, badly, to be done.

Heras had, as promised, been standing well back. He came and looked at the wreck, his prediction confirmed in every particular and too decent to enjoy it more than a little. He held out his hand for my arm. I held it out. He turned it in the light, grunted at the cleaning - which from Heras is praise - and let go.

"Well," he said. "Fire goes up. We knew that."

"Yeah."

"And the bag did not."

"Yeah. I noticed."

He crouched beside the smoking remains and looked at the blackened seam. He started to say something. He didn't.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing useful." He shouldered his satchel and stood. "Wash the arm tonight. The cooled boiled water, twice, the way you taught me. You will not lose it."

He walked back toward the gate. I stayed where I was.

My arm hurt. The wreck smoked. The light cloth had flown and the heavy cloth hadn't, and I couldn't see how to make one bag be both. The seal burned. The seams leaked. Bigger only made all of it worse. I sat in the dirt with the brazier still warm against my shin and tried to think of the trick that solved both at once, and I couldn't think of one.

I needed money. That was the bottom of it. I needed a free man's ability to walk into a market and buy good cloth by the bolt and a craftsman's day by the dozen, and the balloon was supposed to be how I'd get there. So the balloon needed money to build, and the money came from the balloon. My arm throbbed. I couldn't see a way out of it.

But the little one had flown. The rag one, the toy on a string, it had flown, and the big one burning down didn't unsay that. Hot air rises. I'd watched it carry a made thing off the ground. The rest of it - the cloth and the seal and the heat and the money and the hands - I could maybe chip at, piece by piece. The balloon was going to be a long grind: scorched cloth, begged coals, a doctor funding linen to see if it worked.

I built one more before the spring was out, because I couldn't not. Smaller than the burned one and bigger than the toy, a modest thing of linen I'd lightened by giving up half the seal, hoping I could split the difference between leaking and burning and find one breath of cloth that did neither. I made it in the lot in the long evening light with Stichus holding the line and Heras leaning on the midden wall pretending he had somewhere better to be. I fed the brazier careful this time, low, no higher than the cone, coaxing the warm in steady.

It filled. It went round and stood up and the linen drew taut and held, and the warm went in and stayed in, and the line came up tight in the boy's fist, and the whole linen weight of it lifted off the trodden dirt of the slaughter-lot, a hand's height, then a forearm, then higher than the boy's head, hanging on the warm with the fire-shimmer pouring up into it. My heart was going crazy.

It held maybe ten breaths. I counted them. Six, seven, eight, the linen straining at the seam I'd sewn by lamplight with a borrowed needle, nine, and on the tenth the seam let go in a long soft tearing, and the warm punched out the rip in one gust I could feel on my face, and the bag folded along the seam and came down. Not fast. Just a settling, down past Stichus's face onto the dirt.

Stichus was still holding the string in both fists.

"It went up," he said.

"Yeah."

"For a while."

"Yeah, I saw."

He waited for me to say something else. I didn't have anything else. After a minute he wound the slack around his hand and started to coil it. I watched him and didn't move to help.
