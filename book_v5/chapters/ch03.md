# Chapter 3: The Map

They came for me in the gray part of morning, before the town had finished being asleep. Two of the spearmen this time, and the optio behind them with his hand resting on the doorframe like he owned it, which maybe he did. He'd fed me. That counted for something with me and probably nothing with him.

One of the spearmen made a short upward motion with his free hand. Get up. The other fact about my situation, the fact I kept relearning every hour, was that I understood gestures fine and words not at all. A whole language was happening around me all the time, wet and fast and full of those hard k-sounds where I expected soft ones, and none of it stuck. It washed off. I'd spent the night listening to two men talk in the lane and I could not have told you whether they were arguing or joking.

I got up. My back had a knot in it from the dirt floor that I would have killed to crack. I thought, for one stupid second, about my bed at home, the mattress with its Daniel-shaped dent, and then I shut that door because there were better uses for the morning than crying in front of soldiers.

The phone was in my front jeans pocket, screen-in, the safest place I had. Eleven percent. I'd checked it in the dark and made myself put it away. Eleven percent of the only device I owned.

They walked me through the town. By daylight it was smaller and uglier than the noises had made it sound, three lanes of leaning brick and timber with a gutter running down the middle of each one carrying things I didn't look at. People stopped to watch me go by. A woman with a baby on her hip pulled the baby's face into her shoulder. A man selling something out of clay pots said a word at me, sharp, and the men around him laughed, and I felt my ears go hot even though I had no idea what he'd said. That was the worst part, honestly. You can't even be properly insulted. You just stand there glowing while strangers enjoy themselves.

The building they took me to had a real door, painted, and two more spearmen outside it who came to a kind of attention when the optio appeared. Inside it was cooler and dim, smelling of lamp soot and old smoke and, under that, the fish-rot smell that was apparently just the smell of the entire world now. There was a table. Behind the table sat a man.

He was maybe fifty, going soft in the jaw, in a tunic with a band of dull red at the hem, and he had the particular stillness of a person who is used to other people waiting on his mouth to open. A clerk sat to one side with a folding wooden frame across his knees, two leaves of it, the inner surfaces darkened. The clerk had a little bronze stick. I watched him press the flat end of it across the dark surface, smoothing it, and I thought, wax, that's wax, they write on wax, and felt absurdly pleased with myself for figuring out one true fact.

Someone had set a bowl near the door, on a low ledge, grain and something poured over it. I could see the liquid had a color to it, amber-dark. While the optio spoke to the seated man I edged close enough to see into the bowl. I picked up a pinch of the grain. It was fine, just grain, and I was hungry enough that hungry had started eating itself, so I ate the grain around the edges and left the rest. The smell came off on my fingers. I wiped them on my jeans. That did nothing. I got most of the grain. The liquid pooled underneath.

The optio said something to the seated man. The man started asking questions.

I know they were questions from the lift at the end, the little rise in pitch, the pause after where he waited and I gave him nothing. He asked one. Pause. He tried again, different words, slower, as you'd talk to someone elderly or dim. Pause. He leaned back and asked the optio something and the optio answered at length, and I caught exactly one sound in all of it that I recognized, which was the noise the optio had made last night when he failed to say my name, that mangled middle thing that wasn't quite Daniel. So they were talking about me. Brilliant deduction. Sherlock lives.

The man pointed at my clothes and said a word and made a small circular motion taking in the jeans, the T-shirt, the boots. He wanted to know about the clothes. Of course he did. I'd want to know about the clothes too. I had no way to tell him they came off a rack in a store under fluorescent lights for nineteen dollars. I plucked at the hem of the shirt and turned both palms up, the universal sign for I cannot help you, and his face did something cold.

I was being graded. I understood that much. I'd watched enough of these scenes in movies to know the categories, even if the movies were all wrong about everything else. Spy. Lunatic. Runaway slave. There'd be some word here for each and the man was sorting me into one. None of them ended with me going home, but some of them ended with me alive, and I wanted very badly to push myself toward the alive ones, and I had no tools. No words. A dead language I'd never even learned, except it wasn't dead yet, it was extremely alive and sitting at a table judging me.

He asked something else, longer, with an edge on it now, and when I didn't answer he made a flat gesture toward the door, a take-him-away gesture, dismissive, done. The optio shifted his weight.

That's what did it. The little movement of the optio's foot, the sense of a door closing. I'd been told no answer and I was about to be filed under whatever you file the useless under. I thought, in a rush: the one thing none of them had was something I couldn't say. I could only show it.

I lifted my hand and mimed it. I held an invisible stick and dragged it across an invisible surface. I looked at the clerk, at the wax frame on his knees, and did it again. Writing. Drawing. Give me that.

Everyone went still. The man at the table narrowed his eyes. I thought I'd blown it, that I'd just done something that read as a spell or a threat, that miming was how barbarians cursed you. Then the man said one short word and flicked two fingers at the clerk, and the clerk got up, not happy about it, and crossed the room and handed me the folded frame and the bronze stick, holding them out at arm's length like I might be contagious.

The wax was the color of dark honey, pressed smooth, in a wooden surround about the size of a paperback splayed open. The stick had a point at one end and that flat blade at the other for smoothing out mistakes. My hands were not steady. I knelt down because there was nowhere to set it, balanced the frame on my own knee, and pressed the point in.

It cut a thin clean line. Easier than I expected.

A kid who got a B-minus in the one art class he was ever forced to take drew this map. But I knew one thing cold, and it was this: I knew every coastline on earth. I'd seen them ten thousand times. On the back of cereal boxes. On the wall of every classroom I'd ever sat in. On my own phone, the little blue planet you spin with your thumb. I had those lines in my hands the same way I had the alphabet. They were already there, a pattern, something my hands could do before my brain caught up with what I was doing.

So I started with the part they'd know.

I drew the sea in the middle. I drew it as a long horizontal pool, fat in the center, and I put the boot in. Italy. I got the boot pretty good, actually, the kick and the heel, and as the point came down through it I heard the optio make a sound, a soft grunt, and when I glanced up his eyes had changed. He'd recognized it. He was a soldier, he'd seen maps, military maps, road maps, and there under my hand was the shape of the ground he stood on.

I kept going. I hooked the long top edge of the Mediterranean, the south coast of what I called France and Spain in my head and had no name for here, the heel and instep of the European underbelly. I put in the big island down by the boot's toe, Sicily, a fat triangle. I dropped Greece in, all those torn-up fingers of land reaching down, and I knew I was getting Greece wrong, too clean, too few islands, but it was the right idea in the right place. I ran the line east and curled it down toward where Egypt sat, where the sea ended and the land took over.

The man had stood up.

He came around the table. He bent over the frame on my knee and I had to angle it up for him, and I could smell the wine on him, the morning wine they all drank instead of water. He put one thick finger down near the boot, not touching the wax, hovering, and said a word, soft, almost to himself. A question in it. I didn't know the word. I knew what he was pointing at. I tapped the boot with the back of the stick. Yes. Here. Then I tapped the floor between us. Here. Same place.

His head came up and his eyes settled on me for the first time, and the contempt that had been sitting on his face all morning wasn't gone exactly, but it had something else next to it now.

I should have stopped there. If I'd had any sense I'd have stopped at the sea they knew and let that be my whole magic trick. But there's an effect that happens when a room finally turns its attention to you after a day of looking through you, and I was seventeen and I'd been afraid for two days straight, and I wanted more of it. I wanted them to keep looking at me.

So I kept drawing.

I took the land below the sea, the African coast they traded along, and instead of letting it trail off into rumor at the edge of their known world, I pulled it down. I drew the whole continent. The big shoulder of the west bulging out into the ocean, the long taper down to the point at the bottom, the eastern coast curving back north above a narrow point. I drew Africa as a continent with an end to it, a coast you could sail around, and I knew I was getting the proportions wrong even as I did it, too narrow probably, the bottom point too sharp, but the wholeness of it, the fact of it being a closed shape, was something nobody in that room had ever seen rendered, because nobody in that room knew it had a bottom.

The clerk had come close without my noticing. He said something to the seated man, fast, and the seated man answered without looking away from my knee.

I went east. This is where it got bad, where my hand was guessing, and I knew it. I put in the wedge of Arabia okay, the Red Sea as a long thin gash, and then past that I was running on fumes. I knew there was a big subcontinent that hung down, a triangle, India, and I put it in too small and too far north, I'm sure, jammed up against everything. I knew China was east of that and huge and I gave it a fat coastline curving up and away, and I have no doubt it was wrong in every particular except the one that mattered, which was that it was there and it was enormous. I drew islands off the east edge, Japan, a guess on a guess. The southeast, all those islands and peninsulas below China, I just stippled in a scatter and hoped. It was the kind of map you'd draw for a friend to win a bet, fast and confident and fundamentally a lie in the details and true in the bones.

The seated man was pointing at India now, asking, and I didn't have the word and couldn't give it, so I just kept the point moving, because the point moving was the only sentence I could speak.

Then I came back to the western edge. To the boot, to the coast of France and Spain, to where their world ended at the open ocean. The edge of everything. I'd seen in some article, some half-watched video, the word they had for it, the ocean as the great encircling river at the rim of the map, the place you didn't sail past because there was nothing to sail to.

I left a gap of blank wax. A wide one, the width of two fingers, open sea.

And on the far side of it I drew two more continents.

I drew the long thin tail of South America first because it's the easy one, the way it dangles, the bulge on its east shoulder. Then North America above it, the wide top, the gulf scooped out underneath, the long western run. I connected them with the thin waist in the middle. I drew them small, off at the edge of the frame, badly, the coastlines vague because nobody draws the coast of Brazil from memory, but unmistakably two big lands, with shape, with an end and a far side, sitting out there in the ocean Rome thought was empty.

The room had gone very quiet. Even the noises from the lane outside seemed to have stepped back.

The seated man wasn't pointing anymore. He was just looking at the blank gap of wax and the two shapes beyond it, and his hand had come up to his mouth, and I watched something move across his face that I can only call calculation. The clerk said a word, and the seated man said one back, low and short, with a cutting-off quality, shut up, and the clerk shut up.

I knelt there with the frame on my knee and the bronze stick going slippery in my fingers and said it, quiet, in English, because nobody here knew English: "You idiot." And then, because once wasn't enough: "You absolute idiot."

Because here is what I knew that they didn't, and what I shouldn't have shown them I knew. There was nothing across that ocean for them. Not yet. Not for fourteen hundred years. I had just drawn a treasure map to two continents that no one alive would reach, and I'd drawn it for a man with the kind of face already doing sums, already thinking about who he reported to and what it was worth, and the part of me that read too much science fiction was screaming about consequences and timelines and knowledge you don't put back in the box.

And somewhere in the middle of that, I thought about the bread I'd been eating since I got here, the coarse heavy rounds of it that kept me standing upright. No tomatoes. No chocolate. No potatoes. The crops I'd grown up tasting every day of my life were right over that blank strip of ocean, growing without anyone to pick them, and for one beat I understood in my stomach rather than my head the real pull behind those two shapes I'd drawn. I'd been hungry before I even knew what I was drawing.

And the other part of me, the part that had been cold and bound and laughed at and one wax-flick away from the dungeon or worse, that part was just glad. Glad they were looking at me. It had felt important to draw those continents. It felt important the same way any lever feels important when you're seventeen and you finally have one. I'd shown them the world had more of it than they knew, and that I knew where, and now I was a curiosity worth keeping alive.

I didn't have a word for any of that. So I just held the map up a little so he could see it better.

The man took the frame out of my hands. He didn't ask, he took it, and he held it close to his face and tilted it toward the light from the door, and then he carried it back to his table and set it down and stood over it with both hands flat on the wood. He said something to the optio without turning around. A string of it, decisive, the voice of a man who has stopped wondering and started arranging.

The optio answered with a single word that I would learn, later, was a kind of yes, the army yes, and he said it the way you speak to someone whose authority you don't argue with.

Then the man said one more thing, and it had a name in it. I'm sure of that much, because of how it landed differently from the rest, a hard little stone in a stream of water. He said it and the optio repeated it back as a question and the man said it again, sharper, and made a gesture with his hand like he was sending something out the door and a long way off. Go get this. Go get him.

I didn't know who. I picked up, much later, that he'd said two things about the man he was sending for: one of them meant something like one who heals, and one of them I would eventually understand was just a slur for foreigner, the same flavor of word the guy at the pots had spat at me, except this time it wasn't aimed at me. There was someone else in this town, or near it, who was strange enough to be useful for understanding strange things. A man who knew bodies, and tongues, and the far edges of the world. They were going to bring him to me and see what the two strange creatures made of each other.

A spearman left at a fast walk. I heard his sandals slap off down the lane and fade.

The seated man spoke once more to the optio, shorter, and waved his hand toward me, and I understood I was being given back into the optio's keeping. A possession returned to its shelf.

They didn't tie my hands. I noticed that. Yesterday the rope, today nothing. The math of how they saw me had changed in the space of one drawing, and I'd done all of it on my knees on a dirt floor with a stick.

The optio touched my shoulder, not rough, and tipped his head toward the door. We were going. I stood up, and my knee had gone half-asleep under the frame, and I stumbled, and one of the spearmen snorted, and there it was, the glow in my ears again, the great inventor of worlds tripping over his own dead foot.

At the door I looked back. The clerk had crossed to the table and the two of them, clerk and master, were bent over my map, heads almost touching, and the clerk had his own stick out and was, I realized, copying it. Copying my coastlines onto a fresh leaf of his own. Already. Whatever it was, they weren't going to let it stay only in my crooked hand. It was loose now.

Outside the light had come up hard and white and the lane had filled with the day, donkeys and shouting and the smell of bread somewhere doing its level best to cut through everything else. The optio walked me back the way we'd come, slower this time, and partway along he stopped at a stall and bought something with a coin, two of those flat dense rolls, and put one in my hands without looking at me, like he didn't want it remarked on.

I ate it walking. It was better than the mush. I'd have eaten a tire.

I had eleven percent of battery left in my pocket and I'd spent none of it doing what I was about to need it for, which was making sure the next map was better, because there'd be a next map, I could feel it coming. Tonight, in the cell, in the dark, with my back to the door and the brightness down as low as it would go, I'd open the dead useless map app, the one that couldn't find me but still remembered the coastlines it had loaded once, and I'd stare at the real shapes for as long as I dared. Refresh the boot. Fix Africa's bottom. Get India hanging where it actually hangs. It wasn't honest, drawing from a glowing rectangle and passing it off as something I carried in my head. But I was past worrying about honest. I was a learned man from somewhere cold and far away who happened to know the outline of the whole world, and a learned man checks his sources, and mine had eleven percent of battery left and was the last library on earth that spoke my language.

There was one more thing I wanted to write down, while I still had the power for it. I typed it in English, a single note: variolation - not yet. No standing to try it. No physician, no language, no trust. But I'd remember. I'd have to remember.

The optio said something to me as we reached the cell door. Easy, calm, four or five words. I didn't understand any of it. But he tapped his own chest and said a sound, the sound he'd given me last night for his name, and then he pointed at me and said the mangled middle of Daniel, the part he could manage, and waited.

I said it back to him. His sound, as close as I could get it. His mouth went sideways, not quite a smile, more like a man acknowledging a decent throw.

Then he opened the door and put me back in the dark, and slid the bar across, and I sat down against the wall with half a roll still in my hand and my heart going like I'd run a mile, and I thought about the two continents I'd drawn into the empty ocean, and ate the rest of the bread.
