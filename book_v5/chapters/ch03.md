# Chapter 3: The Map

They came for me in the gray part of morning, before the town was really awake. Two spearmen this time, and the optio behind them with one hand on the doorframe like he owned it, which maybe he did. He'd fed me last night. That meant something to me and probably nothing to him.

One of the spearmen jerked his hand up. Get up. That was the thing I kept relearning every hour here: I understood gestures fine and words not at all. A whole language was going on around me all the time, fast and full of hard k-sounds, and none of it stuck. I'd lain awake half the night listening to two men talk in the lane and I couldn't have told you whether they were fighting or laughing.

I got up. My back had a knot in it from the dirt floor. For one stupid second I thought about my bed at home, the mattress with the me-shaped dent in it, and then I shut that off, because crying in front of soldiers wasn't going to get me anywhere.

The phone was in my front jeans pocket, screen turned in, the safest spot I had. Eleven percent. I'd checked it in the dark and made myself put it away.

They walked me through the town. In daylight it was smaller and uglier than the noise had made it sound, three lanes of leaning brick and timber with a gutter down the middle of each one carrying stuff I didn't look at. People stopped to watch me go by. A woman pulled her baby's face into her shoulder. A man selling something out of clay pots called out at me, "Quid indutus es?", or something close to it, and the men around him laughed, and my ears went hot even though I had no idea what he'd said. That was honestly the worst part. You can't even get insulted properly. You just stand there glowing while strangers have a good time.

The building they took me to had a real door, painted, with two more spearmen outside who straightened up when the optio showed. Inside it was cooler and dim and smelled of lamp soot and old smoke, and under that the fish-rot smell that was under everything in this place. There was a table, and behind the table a man.

He was maybe fifty, going soft in the jaw, in a tunic with a band of dull red at the hem, and he had the stillness of a man used to being waited on. A clerk sat off to one side with a folding wooden frame across his knees, the two inside faces of it dark. The clerk had a little bronze stick and was pressing the flat end of it across the dark surface, smoothing it, and I thought, wax, that's wax, they write on wax, and felt stupidly pleased with myself for working out one true thing.

Somebody had set a bowl by the door on a low ledge, grain with something amber-dark poured over it. While the optio talked to the man at the table I edged close enough to get a pinch of the grain off the top. Just grain. I was hungry enough that the hunger had teeth by now, so I ate the dry part around the edge and left the rest sitting in the liquid. The smell came off on my fingers, and I wiped them on my jeans, and that did nothing.

Then the man started in on me with questions. "Quis es?" A pause, waiting. Then again, slower, different words, the way you'd talk to somebody old or slow. "Unde venis?" I gave him nothing, because I had nothing to give. He leaned back and asked the optio something and the optio answered for a while, and in all of it I caught exactly one sound I knew, the mangled thing the optio had made last night trying to say my name, the noise that wasn't quite Daniel. So they were talking about me. Great. Real useful thing to figure out.

He pointed at my clothes and made a little circle with his finger, taking in the jeans, the shirt, the boots. "Quid est?" He wanted to know about the clothes. Of course he did. I'd want to know too. There was no way to tell him they came off a rack under fluorescent lights for nineteen bucks. I plucked at the hem of my shirt and turned my palms up, the universal I can't help you, and his face went cold.

I was being graded. That much I got. I'd seen enough movies to know the categories. Spy. Lunatic. Runaway slave. There was a word here for each one and he was sorting me into a box, and none of the boxes ended with me going home, but some of them ended with me alive, and I had no way to push myself toward those. No words. Just the dead language from school, except here it wasn't dead at all, it was alive and sitting at a table deciding what to do with me.

He asked something longer, with an edge on it, and when I didn't answer he flicked his hand toward the door and said one short flat word. "Auferte." I didn't need the word; the hand said it all. The optio shifted his weight.

That's what did it, the little scrape of the optio's foot, the feeling of a door swinging shut. I was about to get filed under whatever they filed the useless under. And the one thing none of them had was a thing I couldn't say to him. I could draw it.

I lifted my hand and mimed it, held an invisible stick and dragged it across the air. I looked at the clerk, at the wax frame on his knees, and did it again. Writing. Drawing. Give me that.

Everybody went still. The man at the table narrowed his eyes, and for a second I was sure I'd blown it, that miming was how barbarians put a curse on you. Then he said one short word and flicked two fingers at the clerk. The clerk got up, not happy about it, crossed the room, and held the frame and the stick out to me at arm's length, like I might be catching.

The wax was the color of dark honey, smoothed flat, in a wooden surround about the size of a paperback laid open. The stick had a point at one end and a flat blade at the other for wiping out mistakes. My hands weren't steady. There was nowhere to set it down, so I knelt and balanced the frame on my knee and pressed the point in. It cut a thin clean line, easier than I expected.

A kid who got a B-minus in the one art class they ever made him take drew this map. But I knew one thing cold, and it was this: I knew every coastline on earth. I'd seen them ten thousand times. On the back of cereal boxes. On the wall of every classroom I ever sat in. On my own phone, the little blue marble you spin with your thumb. The lines were in my hands the same way the alphabet is, already there before my brain caught up.

So I started with the part they'd know. I drew the sea in the middle, a long pool fat in the center, and I put the boot in. Italy. I got the boot pretty good, the kick and the heel, and as the point came down through it the optio made a sound, a soft grunt, and when I glanced up his eyes had changed. He'd recognized it. He was a soldier, he'd seen maps, and there under my hand was the shape of the ground he was standing on.

I kept going. I hooked the top edge of the Mediterranean, the south coast of what I called France and Spain in my head and had no name for here. I dropped in the big island down by the toe of the boot, Sicily, a fat triangle. I put Greece in, all those torn-up fingers of land reaching down, and I knew I was getting it wrong, too clean, not enough islands, but it was the right idea in the right place. I ran the line east and curled it down to where Egypt sat, where the sea ended and the land took over.

The man had stood up.

He came around the table and bent over the frame on my knee, and I had to tilt it up for him. I could smell the wine on him, the watered morning wine they all drank instead of water. He put one thick finger down near the boot, not touching the wax, hovering, and said something soft, almost to himself, a question in it. I didn't know the word. I knew what he meant. I tapped the boot with the back of the stick. Then I tapped the floor between us. Same place. Here.

His head came up and his eyes settled on me for the first time, and the contempt was still there, but now there was something else mixed in with it.

I should have stopped. If I'd had any sense I'd have stopped at the sea they already knew and let that be the whole magic trick. But there's a thing that happens when a room finally pays attention to you after a day of looking straight through you, and I was seventeen and I'd been scared for two days, and I wanted more of it. So I kept drawing.

I took the African coast they traded along, and instead of letting it trail off into rumor at the edge of the world, I pulled it down. I drew the whole continent, the big shoulder of the west bulging out into the ocean, the long taper down to a point at the bottom, the eastern coast curving back up. Africa as a thing with an end to it, a coast you could sail all the way around. I knew the proportions were off even as I did it, too narrow, the bottom too sharp, but the shape of it as a closed thing was something nobody in that room had ever seen, because none of them knew it had a bottom.

The clerk had come up close without my noticing. He said something fast to the man at the table, and the man answered without looking up from my knee.

I went east, and this was where it got bad, where my hand was guessing and I knew it. Arabia came out okay, the Red Sea a long thin gash. After that I was running on fumes. There was a big subcontinent that hung down in a triangle, India, and I put it in too small and too far north, jammed against everything. China I knew was east of that and huge, so I gave it a fat coast curving up and away, wrong in every detail except the one that mattered, that it was there and it was enormous. Japan I guessed at, a scatter of islands off the edge. Everything below China I just stippled in and hoped. It was the kind of map you'd draw a friend to win a bet, fast and confident and wrong in all the small parts.

The man was pointing at India now, asking, and I didn't have the word and couldn't give it, so I just kept the point moving, because the point moving was the only sentence I could say.

Then I came back to the western edge. To the boot, to the coast of France and Spain, to where their world stopped at open ocean. I left a gap of blank wax, a wide one, two fingers across, the open sea their maps stopped at.

And on the far side of it I drew two more continents.

I did the long thin tail of South America first, the easy one, that dangling shape with the bulge on its shoulder. Then North America above it, the wide top, the gulf scooped out underneath, the long run of the western coast. I joined them at the thin waist in the middle. I drew them small and badly, off at the edge of the frame, the coastlines vague because nobody draws Brazil from memory, but unmistakably two big lands with shape and an end and a far side, sitting out there in the ocean Rome thought was empty.

The room had gone very quiet. The noise from the lane outside had dropped away too.

The man wasn't pointing anymore. He was just looking at the blank gap of wax and the two shapes past it, and his hand had come up to his mouth, and I watched something move over his face that I can only call calculation. The clerk said a word. The man said one back, low and short, with a cut-off on the end of it. Shut up. The clerk shut up.

I knelt there with the frame on my knee and the stick going slippery in my fingers and said it, quiet, in English, because nobody here knew English. "You idiot." And then, because once wasn't enough, "You absolute idiot."

Here's what I knew that they didn't, and what I should never have let them see I knew. There was nothing across that ocean that anybody in this room would reach. I'd drawn a treasure map to two continents nobody alive would get to, and I'd drawn it for a man whose face was already doing sums.

And part of me, the part that had been tied up and laughed at and one flick of the wax away from a dungeon, was just glad they were looking at me at all. Whatever I was now, I was something they wanted to keep.

I'd been eating their bread for two days, the coarse heavy rounds of it that kept me on my feet, and there were no tomatoes in it. No chocolate. No potatoes. Everything I'd grown up eating was over that blank strip of ocean, growing with nobody to pick it, and I'd been hungry the whole time I was drawing.

I didn't have a word for any of that. So I just held the map up a little so he could see it better.

He took the frame out of my hands. He didn't ask, he took it, and held it close to his face and tilted it toward the light from the door, and then carried it back to the table and set it down and stood over it with both hands flat on the wood. He said something to the optio without turning around, a whole string of it, decisive, the sound of a man who's stopped wondering and started arranging.

The optio answered with a single word, "Ita," short and flat, the sound of a man agreeing with an order he wouldn't think of arguing with.

Then he said one more thing, and there was a name in it. I'm sure of that much, because of how it landed, sharper than the rest, the one piece of it I could tell was a name. He said it, and the optio repeated it back as a question, and the man said it again, sharper, and made a gesture with his hand like he was sending something out the door and a long way off. Go get this. Go get him.

I didn't know who. What I pieced together later was that he'd named two things about the man he was sending for: a word for someone who heals, and a word that was just a slur for foreigner, the same one the guy at the pots had spat at me, except now it wasn't aimed at me. Somewhere in this town there was somebody else strange enough to be useful for understanding strange things. A man who knew bodies, and tongues, and the far edges of the world. They were going to bring him to me and see what the two of us made of each other.

A spearman left at a fast walk. I heard his sandals slap off down the lane and fade out.

The man said one more short thing to the optio and waved a hand at me, and I got that I was being handed back into the optio's keeping, put away again like a thing on a shelf.

They didn't tie my hands. I noticed that. Yesterday the rope, today nothing. How they saw me had changed in the space of one drawing, and I'd done all of it on my knees on a dirt floor with a stick.

The optio touched my shoulder, not rough, and tipped his head at the door. We were going. I stood up, and my knee had gone half-asleep under the frame, and I stumbled, and one of the spearmen snorted, and there it was again, the heat in my ears, the big visiting genius tripping over his own dead foot.

At the door I looked back. The clerk had gone to the table, and he and the man were bent over my map, heads almost touching, and the clerk had his own stick out and was copying it. Copying my coastlines onto a fresh leaf of his own. Already. Whatever this was, they weren't going to let it sit in my one crooked copy. It was loose now.

Outside, the light had come up hard and white and the lane had filled with the day, donkeys and shouting and, somewhere, the smell of bread doing its level best to cut through everything else. The optio walked me back the way we'd come, slower this time, and partway along he stopped at a stall and bought something with a coin, two of the flat dense rolls, and put one in my hand without looking at me, like he didn't want it noticed.

I ate it walking. It was better than the mush. I'd have eaten a tire.

I had eleven percent of battery in my pocket and I'd spent none of it on the thing I was about to need it for, which was making the next map better, because there'd be a next map. I could feel it coming. Tonight, in the cell, in the dark, with my back to the door and the screen turned down as low as it went, I'd open the dead useless map app, the one that couldn't find me but still remembered the coastlines it had loaded once, and I'd stare at the real shapes as long as I dared. Fix the boot. Fix Africa's bottom. Get India hanging where it actually hangs. It wasn't honest, drawing off a glowing rectangle and passing it off as something I carried in my head. But I was past honest. I was a learned man from somewhere cold and far away who happened to know the shape of the whole world, and a learned man checks his sources, and mine had eleven percent left and was the last library on earth that spoke my language.

The optio said something to me when we got to the cell door, easy, calm, four or five words. I didn't understand any of it. But he tapped his own chest and made a sound, the sound he'd given me last night for his name, and then he pointed at me and said the mangled middle of Daniel, the part he could manage, and waited.

I said his sound back to him, as close as I could get. His mouth went sideways, not quite a smile. More like a guy giving you a nod for a decent throw.

Then he opened the door and put me back in the dark, and slid the bar across, and I sat down against the wall with half a roll still in my hand and my heart going like I'd run a mile, and I thought about the two continents I'd drawn out in the empty ocean, and ate the rest of the bread.
