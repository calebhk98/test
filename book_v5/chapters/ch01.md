# Chapter 1: Waking

Danny Mercer's alarm went off at six-fifteen and he hit snooze without opening his eyes. He lay there for about twenty seconds genuinely calculating whether he could call in sick. He couldn't - Rick had cut his hours twice already this month and his bank account had like forty dollars in it. He grabbed his work shirt off the floor.

His phone had a meme from Jess at 11pm he hadn't answered yet, pretty good, and he'd get back to her. He shoved his keys in his pocket - house key, the key to his Civic, the brass one to the store's back door that he was definitely not supposed to have a copy of - and headed out to the car.

Fifteen-minute drive, nothing morning, thinking about the fastener aisle that needed restocking before opening. He pulled into the Brentwood Hardware lot around seven-fifteen, got out, grabbed his phone off the passenger seat, and started walking. His foot caught on something at the edge of the lot, the ground came up fast, and his hands flew out.

Then nothing.

He pushed himself up onto his hands and knees and found a big harvested field in every direction he looked, dry stubble running on to a tree line somewhere far off with pale morning sky over it all and no parking lot anywhere, no hardware store, nothing he recognized.

Marcus, was his first thought. Marcus and whoever he'd pulled into this.

"Marcus!" He got to his feet and yelled it at the trees. "Come on, you got me! Come out!" He waited, and a bird called somewhere and nothing else did. He tried the other direction: "I'm going to be SO late right now, come on!" When nothing moved he grabbed his phone and checked the time - six-forty, ninety-eight percent battery, no signal. He tried calling Marcus and it died before it rang, then his mom and got the same nothing, then 911, which just sat there saying connecting until it gave up.

"MARCUS." He put real volume into it, the kind that scraped his throat. "I don't know where I am. Tell me where I am!" His voice hit the stubble and died and nobody answered and he stood there for a moment with his jaw tight and his hands trying to become fists, and then switched to airplane mode, because forty dollars in a bank account meant he couldn't afford a replacement phone if this one died out here.

He pressed two fingers to the back of his skull. Nothing, his head didn't even hurt. He looked at the lockscreen - still the photo of his cousin's treehouse, rope ladder coiled at the bottom - and then at the field. Maybe he'd fainted and hit his head, because concussions could scramble everything, make it all feel real. Or a coma - he'd watched a video once about a guy who'd been under for eleven days and said the whole time felt completely real, like actually being awake. Maybe his mom was sitting next to him in a hospital right now and none of this was happening.

He started walking because standing there wasn't doing anything, and smoke meant people. He made himself a job of it: watch the ground, count steps, look for anything made by a person. The stubble crunched under his boots. After a while the field gave out into a strip of weeds and then a path, packed dirt and worn ruts, the smooth scoop a wheel makes but the wrong width for a car, no tread marks at all. He stopped and looked at the ruts for a long time. Just the smooth groove, and beside it, pressed into the old mud, hoofprints.

He kept walking, and if he wasn't at work by seven, Rick was going to call his mom. He thought this very clearly, the practical fact of it sitting right on top of everything else, and then he thought: there are no roads in any direction. And then, right behind that: where is the stockroom.

The settlement came up on him slow. The first building made him almost laugh. Mud walls, actual dried mud, cracked tan, with a roof of red clay tiles that sat crooked. A low doorway with a cloth hung in it. He'd seen enough YouTube to know what a historical movie set looked like from behind. Or a historical village - one of those places where people dressed up and churned butter. Either way, somebody was filming something and Danny had wandered onto the back lot, and any second a guy in a vest with a headset was going to come around the corner and yell at him for being where he wasn't supposed to be.

He would have given anything in the world to be yelled at right then.

There were more buildings now, the settlement widening along the path, a cluster of mud and rough stone strung together with smoke coming from holes in some of the roofs. A cloth hanging in a doorway moved and a woman looked out. She was old, or she looked old, with a brown cloth over her hair and a long dress the color of nothing in particular. Her face when she saw Danny went still, the held-breath kind, animal stillness, someone startled by something that wasn't supposed to be there. Then she pulled the cloth across the door so fast it snapped and was gone, and he heard her saying something inside, low and urgent.

Danny stood there thinking about the woman's face, about how she'd gone pale and moved fast, the way people move when they're actually scared. People at movie shoots didn't do that.

"This is fine," he said, and kept walking, which was the only thing he had.

There were people now: a man leading a donkey loaded with bundles who stepped wide around Danny without making eye contact, two kids filthy and barefoot who planted themselves in the path and stared at his boots with their mouths open, and a man squatting by a wall working a piece of leather who stood when Danny came near and held the knife where Danny could see it, not pointing it, just showing it was there.

The clothes were wrong on every person - long belted shirts to the knee, sandals or bare feet, not a zipper or a logo anywhere in sight. Danny worked retail and had refolded ten thousand T-shirts and knew what machine-made cloth looked like, and this wasn't it. This was thick and uneven and hung different, the colors just different shades of whatever dirt was nearby.

A door on one of the bigger buildings was propped open with a stone, and he could see the hinges, square-headed nails, which made him slow down. He'd seen square nails before in the antique bin at the back of Brentwood Hardware, novelty stuff they sold to people building replica barn doors, and you didn't see them in actual use because they stopped making nails by hand a long time ago. His manager had mentioned it once and Danny had not paid much attention, and now he kept moving.

A man stopped him and said something, a question by the shape of it. Not Spanish - Danny had taken two years of Spanish in middle school and dropped it because he couldn't roll his Rs and the teacher made him feel stupid about it, but he'd at least heard what Spanish sounded like, and it wasn't that, or French, or anything else he recognized.

"Do you speak English?" Danny said. The man watched him steadily. "I'm lost. I need a phone, do you have a phone?" He held his up and pointed at it. The man said something else, slower, watching Danny's hands. Danny pointed at himself: "Danny. I'm Danny. I came from - I was in a parking lot and I fell and now I'm here and I don't know where here is." The man's face showed nothing that looked like understanding.

Danny crouched down and drew in the dirt. A rectangle with a circle on top - a phone, roughly, the way a little kid would draw one. He pointed at it, then at his own phone, then at the man, trying to ask: do you have one. The man studied the drawing and then looked back up at Danny and said something to the woman watching from the doorway nearby. She answered without taking her eyes off Danny.

"Okay," Danny said, standing. "Forget the phone. Road? Is there a road?" He made a steering wheel with his hands and turned it. "Cars? Highway?" The man said something short, twice, the same word both times. Danny had no idea what it meant. He tried pointing in different directions, miming walking, shrugging like a question. He tried "help" with his hands palm-up, the universal gesture for I have nothing. He held up the phone one more time so the man could see the screen, the clock, the battery bar, the empty signal indicator. The man studied it for a long moment and then turned to Danny with an expression that wasn't quite anything, and Danny understood that you could stand a foot from another person and be completely cut off, which was something he had never actually felt before.

There was a word painted above a doorway in capital letters and Danny went to it because letters were letters, stood there reading them left to right three times. He knew these letters - an R, an A, what might have been an N - but arranged like that they didn't make a word in any language he'd ever seen. He read them again and they still made nothing, and the cold feeling at the back of his neck got worse.

He'd had three years of English, two of Spanish he'd dropped because he couldn't roll his Rs, one semester of French before his schedule changed. He'd seen signs in Chinese and Arabic in the window of the noodle place near his house. He'd played video games in Japanese. None of that had prepared him for the specific wrongness of looking at letters you know and having them say nothing.

There was a rise at the far end of the settlement and he needed to get higher, so he went for it past pigs in a pen that hit him with a smell so strong he held his breath, past a cart with wheels of solid wood with no rubber or metal on them anywhere, and got to the top and stopped.

There was a city.

It was far off, sitting in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and up over the hills beyond, and it was bigger than it had any right to be out here in the middle of all this, a long smear of pale stone with smoke standing up over it in a hundred thin gray lines. Danny felt a flood of relief so strong his legs went a little shaky with it, because a city meant streets and signs and a hospital and getting back to his mom. He started down the path toward it, already composing the speech he'd give to the first police officer he found.

Then he saw the building on the near edge of it and for a moment nothing happened in his head at all. It was round and enormous, even from this distance, a pale ring of stone with rows of arches stacked up its side, and he counted the rows before he knew he was doing it, and got to four. The morning sun was just catching the top and the stone was almost white, clean and whole, without a crack or a hole or the green stains that ran down every picture he'd ever seen of it, no missing sections, no scaffolding, no rebar where chunks had fallen away. He had seen a picture of a building like that his whole life, on screens and in textbooks and in his eighth-grade history class that he'd mostly spent thinking about other things, on the side of a pizza box once, and everyone had, and in every single picture it was a ruin. That was the only way it ever was, had been for longer than the country he was from had existed.

And this one was whole.

Danny's legs stopped being reliable and he ended up crouched on the path with his arms around his knees and his breath coming wrong, fast and shallow, the edges of his vision going sparky, looking at the unbroken Colosseum sitting in the morning light at the bottom of the valley, and the bottom went out of everything.

No. He said that out loud, just the word, over and over, because it was the only one that fit. They'd built a fake one, they'd reconstructed it, people did that, there was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But the mud huts. But the woman's face. But the letters that weren't words and the square nails and the cart with the wooden wheels and the clothes and the birds and the air and the path with no tire tracks in the whole world he could see, not one, not a single straight line made by a machine except the lines on the screen in his hand.

He got the phone out with shaking hands, nearly dropped it, opened the camera and pointed it down the valley, and there was the building in the little rectangle of the screen, small and still and completely, impossibly real. He took the picture and the fake shutter sound clicked.

He tried 911 and the call sat there saying connecting and then the keypad came back with no ring, no operator, nothing. He tried his mom next because his thumb knew where her name was without looking, and got the same thing, calling and then nothing and then the keypad, and he made a sound he wasn't proud of and sat down hard in the dirt.

He sat there and counted arches, staring at the rows and trying to estimate the scale of what he was seeing, but he didn't get anywhere useful because his reference points were all wrong and he had no idea how to convert what he was seeing into anything that meant something, except that it was large in a way a fake thing couldn't afford to be.

At some point in the last year, in some class he'd barely paid attention to, he'd learned when the Colosseum was built, first century AD, and had probably forgotten it immediately because he was thinking about video games. He would have traded a lot, right now, for a better memory.

Two thousand years was not a number he could hold in his head, and when he tried it just slipped away and wouldn't mean anything. He stared at the building and tried, and what kept coming back was the timeline poster in his eighth-grade history classroom, forty feet of it across the front wall, and how far back year 100 was on that thing. Almost off the left edge, barely on the poster at all.

The dog started barking first, somewhere back in the settlement. Then voices, more than one, coming up the path fast.

Danny got to his feet, his knees not entirely his yet, and turned to find four of them coming up the rise, men in the same dirt-colored shirts as everyone else but with leather over their chests on two of them. They had spears, long ones, real ones, the tips catching the new sun in four small bright points. They came at a walk with purpose in it, spread out to have him from more than one side, and the one in front had his arm out flat to hold the others at distance, watching Danny's hands and feet and the strange bright cloth of him, the way you watch something that might go off.

Danny put the phone in his pocket. He put his hands up, palms out.

It was the only word he had in any language they could possibly want.
