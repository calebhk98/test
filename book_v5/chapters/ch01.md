# Chapter 1: Waking

The alarm went off at six-fifteen and Danny hit snooze with his eyes still shut. He gave himself the usual twenty seconds to wonder whether he could call in sick, and the answer was the usual one. Rick had cut his hours twice already this month, and there were like forty dollars in his checking account, and forty dollars did not stretch to skipping a shift over nothing. He sat up and pulled the orange Brentwood Hardware shirt off the floor, the one with the bleach spot on the hem and the logo half cracked off the chest, and dragged it over his head. The house was still dark. He stepped over the creaky board outside his sister Maya's door so he wouldn't wake her, grabbed a granola bar off the counter, and left the lights off.

Jess had sent a meme at eleven the night before. It was a good one. He'd get back to her. He pocketed his keys - house, the Civic, and the brass copy of the store's back door that he was not technically supposed to have - and went out to the car.

Fifteen minutes, the same road as every morning, past the Dairy Queen and the long light that never changed when you wanted it to, the radio turned down low and his brain half on the fastener aisle Rick wanted faced before open and half on whether the gas could wait until Friday. He pulled into the lot a little past seven-fifteen, grabbed his phone off the passenger seat, and started across the asphalt toward the doors with his keys swinging off one finger. His foot caught on something, a pothole or the lip of a parking block, he never did sort out which. The ground came up fast and his hands flew out.

Then nothing.

When he pushed himself up off his hands and knees, the parking lot was gone.

He was in a field. A real one, cut down to stubble, brown and flat, running a long way out to a line of trees with a thin early sky over the top of it. No store, no road, nothing he could put a name to. The air came in wrong, too clean and too green, missing the exhaust and warm tar he'd never noticed breathing until it was gone. A bird he didn't know made a call he didn't know, and somewhere another one answered it. Without meaning to he looked up, and the sky was just blue and empty, not a plane or a wire on it anywhere.

Marcus. This had to be Marcus - exactly the kind of stunt his buddy would spend two weeks planning - and Danny grabbed the idea and held on with both hands. Marcus, and whoever he'd talked into helping.

"Marcus!" He was on his feet before he decided to be. "Okay, ha ha, you got me, come out already!" Nothing came out. A breeze moved the stubble and went still. He kicked a clod of dirt as hard as he could and it burst apart in the air and helped nothing at all. "Marcus, I swear to God, I am losing my whole shift over this. This is NOT funny!"

He got the phone out. Seven-forty. Ninety-eight percent, because he'd pulled it off the charger on his way out the door. No bars, not even the little searching circle. He called Marcus and it died before it rang. His mom, the same. 911, which sat there saying *connecting*, *connecting*, and then quit on him.

It came up all at once then, the thing he'd been holding back since he opened his eyes. His chest pulled tight and his breath went fast and useless, and he spun a full circle looking for one normal thing, a building or a wire or a road, and found none of it, and heard himself saying oh my god, oh my god, the words tripping over each other. His legs wanted to go and he let them, half sat and half dropped into the stubble, and ground the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark behind them was only dark. "Okay," he said, and it came out shaking, so he said it again, slower, and that time it almost held. His thumb found airplane mode and flicked it before the rest of him had decided to, because a dead phone was the one bad thing out here he could actually stop, and forty dollars did not buy another one. It was something to do. He made himself find the next thing.

He felt the back of his head. No lump, no blood, not even an ache, which should have been a relief and wasn't, because he'd gone down hard enough that there should have been something. A concussion, maybe. A concussion would explain all of it - he'd watched a video once about a guy who came out of an eleven-day coma swearing the whole stretch had felt exactly like being awake. Maybe his mom was sitting by a hospital bed right now and none of this was real. That one he held onto as long as he could. It lasted about four seconds. The stubble was sharp and real under him, and the bird would not stop.

Sitting there was worse than moving, so he got up, picked the direction with the trees, and turned the walk into a job, because a job he could do: watch his feet, count the rows off, find one single thing a person had made. The stubble crunched under his boots. The field gave out into a band of weeds and then a path, packed dirt with two grooves worn down the middle of it, smooth scoops about a stride apart, too narrow for a car, no tread anywhere in them. He crouched over them longer than he meant to. Just the smooth grooves, and pressed into the dried mud beside them, the cut shapes of hooves.

If he didn't show before the store opened, Rick was going to call his mom. The thought arrived plain and stupid and familiar, the same work-late guilt as any other morning, and sat on top of everything else, and under it was the worse one he kept trying not to look at straight: there was no road here. Not in any direction. He walked a long time after that, longer than any field back home went without hitting a fence or a wire or a phone pole, until his mouth dried out and the low sun came off the stubble in a flat glare, and the whole way he kept catching himself about to say something to somebody who wasn't there.

The settlement showed up a little at a time. The first building almost made him laugh. The walls were mud - actual dried mud, gone tan and split with cracks - under a roof of red clay tiles laid crooked, a cloth hung over the doorway where a door should be. He'd watched enough YouTube to know the back side of a movie set, or one of those living-history villages where people dressed up and churned butter for tourists. Somebody was filming something out here, or playing pretend at something, and he'd come in the wrong way, and any second a guy in a vest and a headset was going to round the corner and chew him out for wandering into the shot.

He'd have paid money to get chewed out right then.

More buildings came up along the path, mud and rough stone leaned together, smoke leaking out of holes in some of the roofs. It smelled like animals and woodsmoke and something cooking he couldn't place, and off behind a wall a goat kept up a flat, complaining bleat. A cloth over a doorway twitched and a woman put her head out. Old, or worn down to looking old, a brown cloth over her hair, a dress the color of dust. She saw him and went still, the kind of still that nobody acts, and then she snapped the cloth shut so hard it cracked and she was gone. He could hear her behind it, low and fast.

"Cool. Great. Love it here," Danny muttered to nobody, and kept walking, because it was that or stand still.

People were out now. A man led a donkey stacked with bundles and gave Danny a wide berth without once turning his head. Two barefoot kids stopped dead in the path and stared at his boots with their mouths open. A man crouched against a wall working a strip of leather got to his feet as Danny came near and turned the knife in his hand so it showed - not aimed at him, just there, just so he'd know it was.

The clothes were the part he couldn't get past. Long shirts belted at the waist, down to the knee, sandals or nothing on the feet, and on every single person not one zipper, not one tag, not one printed word. He'd folded ten thousand T-shirts at work, and whatever this cloth was, no machine had made it: thick, a little lumpy, hanging heavy, dyed in colors that were really just whatever brown the dirt nearby was doing.

A bigger building had its door wedged open with a rock. The hinges were held on with nails, and he slowed down looking at them - square, lumpy, each one a little different from the last, the kind hammered out one at a time by hand. Brentwood sold nails like that from a bin in the back, the ones people bought to make new things look old. Never once had he seen them holding up a door somebody actually used. He kept moving.

A man stepped into his path and said something with the shape of a question. Not Spanish - Danny had bailed on Spanish in eighth grade over the rolled R's, but he knew the sound of it, and this wasn't the sound of it, or of French, or of anything his ear could catch a corner of.

"Do you speak English?" The man held steady and watched him. "I'm lost. Do you have a phone?" Danny held his own up and pointed at it. The man only said something slower, his eyes on Danny's hands. "Danny. I'm Danny." He tapped his own chest. "I fell down in a parking lot and now I'm here and I don't even know where here is." His voice climbed and cracked on the last word and he made himself stop and drag a hand down his face. "Does anybody here speak anything? Anything I know?" Nobody answered, because nobody could, and being a foot from another person and locked out of him completely turned out to be worse than the empty field had been, and for a second the urge to grab the guy's shirt and shake him, just to make something happen, came up so hard that Danny's hands shook with it instead.

Over one of the doorways somebody had painted a word in big capital letters, and he went toward it because letters were at least letters. He read it left to right, then again. The shapes had been with him his whole life, an R and an A and one that was probably an N, but lined up like that they didn't spell anything in any language he'd ever met, and a third read didn't fix it. That was the one that got him, somehow, worse than the mud or the wool or the wheels - letters were supposed to hold still.

Past the last houses the ground climbed. He wanted to get up high and see one thing that made sense, so he went for the rise - past a pen of pigs that put out a smell thick enough to stop him breathing through his nose, past a cart riding on wheels of plain wood, just wood, banded and pegged and turning on a wooden axle - and at the top he stopped.

There was a city.

It sat out in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and ran up the hills behind it, bigger than anything had a right to be out here, a pale sprawl of stone with smoke standing off it in a hundred thin lines. Something in his chest unclenched so fast it left him light-headed. A city had streets. It had a hospital and somebody in charge and some way back to his mom. He started down toward it, already half-writing what he'd say to the first cop he found.

Then his eye snagged on the building at the near edge of it, and everything in his head stopped at once.

It was round, and it was huge even from up here, a pale ring of stone with arches stacked in rows up the face of it. He counted the rows before he knew he was counting. Four. The sun had just reached the top, and the stone was nearly white, clean and unbroken the whole way around, every arch sharp-edged and whole, like it had been finished that same year. He'd seen this building his whole life - on screens, in a textbook, in the eighth-grade class he'd mostly spent thinking about other stuff, on the side of a pizza box one time - and so had everyone, and in every single picture of it the thing was broken. Half fallen down, the top gone, chunks of it missing. That was the only way it ever looked.

This one was new.

His legs put him down on the path before he chose to sit, knees up, arms wrapped around them, and he stared at the thing and couldn't look away and couldn't make it stop being there.

"No," he said out loud, soft, and then again, harder, "no." Somebody built a fake one. People did that. There was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But not the mud houses. Not the woman's face when she saw him. Not all of it, not at the same time. His stomach rolled over and he was suddenly certain he was going to be sick, and then, sideways and out of nowhere, he thought of his mom at the kitchen table that night asking how his shift went, and Maya asleep behind her creaky door, and the dark ordinary house he'd walked out of that morning, and he had no idea, none, how a person got back to any of that from here.

He got the phone out, almost dropped it, opened the camera, and aimed it down the valley. There it sat in the little rectangle, tiny and steady and completely real. He took the shot. The fake shutter clicked, loud and stupid in all that quiet.

He zoomed in and went hunting the edges of it for the trick - a seam, a crane, the flat gray of a screen, the spot where a movie set always gave itself away. The picture only got grainy. There was nothing in it to catch. He'd been telling himself a story all morning, prank and then movie set and then whatever was supposed to come after that, and crouched on the path he felt the last of it quietly come apart.

He couldn't make the size of it mean anything. He tried, and there was nothing up here to set it against, and it stayed a huge pale shape at the bottom of the valley and wouldn't turn into a number. What came back instead was the timeline poster that ran the whole front wall of his eighth-grade history room, forty feet of it, and how far to the left you had to go to find the year 100. Almost off the end of the paper. Barely on the wall at all.

A dog started barking, back down in the settlement. Then voices, more than one, coming up the path fast.

Danny got up, his knees not all the way under him, and turned. Four men were climbing the rise in the same dust-brown shirts as everyone else, leather strapped over the chest on two of them. They had spears. Long ones, real ones, the points throwing back four small chips of sun. They came on at a walk and fanned out to put him in the middle, and the one in front held an arm out flat to keep the others off, his eyes moving over Danny's hands, his boots, the cracked orange logo across his chest.

He didn't run. Running was the worst thing he could do here, and he knew it, and he made his feet stay where they were.

Danny slid the phone into his pocket. He raised both hands, palms out, and held them there.
