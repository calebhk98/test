# Chapter 1: Waking

My alarm went off at six-fifteen and I hit snooze with my eyes still shut. I gave myself the usual twenty seconds to wonder whether I could call in sick, and the answer was the usual one. Rick had cut my hours twice already this month, and there were like forty dollars in my checking account, and forty dollars did not stretch to skipping a shift over nothing. I pulled the orange Brentwood Hardware shirt off the floor, the one with the bleach spot on the hem and the logo half cracked off the chest, and dragged it on. The house was still dark. I stepped over the creaky board outside my sister Maya's door so I wouldn't wake her, grabbed a granola bar off the counter, and left the lights off.

Jess had sent a meme at eleven the night before. It was a good one. I'd get back to her. I pocketed my keys - house, the Civic, and the brass copy of the store's back door I wasn't technically supposed to have - and went out to the car.

Fifteen minutes, the same road as every morning, past the Dairy Queen and the long light that never changed when you wanted it to, the radio down low and my brain half on the fastener aisle Rick wanted faced before open and half on whether the gas could wait until Friday. I pulled into the lot a little past seven-fifteen, grabbed my phone off the passenger seat, and started across the asphalt toward the doors with my keys swinging off one finger. My foot caught on something, a pothole or the lip of a parking block, I never did sort out which. The ground came up fast and my hands flew out. I felt the slap of my palms on the asphalt, the grit, the sting starting up.

Then nothing.

When I pushed myself up off my hands and knees, the parking lot was gone.

I was in a field. A real one, cut down to stubble, brown and flat, running a long way out to a line of trees with a thin early sky over the top of it. No store, no road, nothing I could put a name to. The air came in wrong, too clean, all cut grass and dirt and none of the gas-and-hot-blacktop smell of the lot I'd been standing in a second ago. Some bird I'd never heard before was going off, and another one answered it from somewhere. I looked up without thinking and the sky was just blue and empty, not a plane or a wire on it anywhere.

Marcus. This had to be Marcus - exactly the kind of stunt he'd spend two weeks planning - and I grabbed onto that and didn't let go. Marcus, and whoever he'd talked into helping.

"Marcus!" I scrambled up off the ground, turning, scanning the tree line. "Okay, ha ha, you got me, real funny, come out!" Nothing came out. A breeze moved the stubble and went still. I kicked a clod of dirt as hard as I could and it burst apart and helped nothing. "Marcus, come on, man, I'm gonna lose my whole shift, this isn't - " My voice went up and cracked and I let the rest of it drop.

The phone said seven-forty. Ninety-eight percent, because I'd pulled it off the charger on my way out the door. No bars, not even the little searching circle. I called Marcus and it died before it rang. My mom, the same. 911, which sat there saying *connecting*, *connecting*, and then quit on me. I'd gone down at seven-fifteen and now it was seven-forty. Twenty-five minutes, just gone, and I couldn't remember a second of it.

And then it hit, all of it at once. My breath went quick and useless, in and in and not doing anything, and I turned a full circle and there was field every way I looked, no road, no wire, no roof, nothing, and I could hear myself going oh my god, oh my god, fast and out loud and I couldn't stop. My legs gave and I dropped into the stubble and shoved the heels of my hands into my eyes until there was just dark and the burn of it. If I don't look. If I just don't look at it. But I could still feel the dry stalks under me and that bird kept on, a bird that was not any bird I knew, and it was real, all the way real, and for a second I thought I was going to be sick. I don't know how long I was down like that. Somewhere in there my thumb turned on airplane mode by itself, from doing it a hundred times when my battery was dying, and then I just held the phone against my chest and shook.

I felt the back of my head. No lump, no blood, nothing, and that should have been good but it wasn't, because I'd hit hard enough that something should have been there. Maybe a concussion. A concussion would explain it - I'd seen something once about coma patients who woke up swearing no time had passed, that the whole thing had felt exactly like being awake. Maybe my mom was sitting next to a hospital bed right now and none of this was happening. Then the stubble bit into my palms, sharp and real, and the bird would not stop, and I couldn't make myself believe the hospital anymore.

I couldn't just sit in that field. That much I knew, so that was the plan: get up, walk toward the trees because they were at least something, and give myself something to do on the way - watch my feet, count the rows, find one thing a person had made. My hands still weren't steady. I went anyway. The stubble crunched under my boots. The field gave out into a band of weeds and then a path, packed dirt with two grooves worn down the middle of it, smooth scoops about a stride apart, too narrow for a car, no tread anywhere in them. I crouched over them longer than I meant to. Just the smooth grooves, and next to them, pressed into the dried mud, hoofprints.

If I didn't show before the store opened, Rick was going to call my mom. Dumb thing to be thinking about. But thinking about Rick was easier than the other thing, the one I kept shoving back down: there was no road here. Not in any direction. I walked a long time after that, longer than any field back home went without hitting a fence or a wire or a phone pole, until my mouth dried out and the low sun came off the stubble in a flat glare, and the whole way I kept catching myself about to say something to somebody who wasn't there.

The settlement showed up a little at a time. The first building almost made me laugh. The walls were mud - actual dried mud, gone tan and split with cracks - under a roof of red clay tiles laid crooked, a cloth hung over the doorway where a door should be. I'd watched enough YouTube to know the back side of a movie set, or one of those living-history villages where people dress up and churn butter for tourists. Somebody was filming something out here, or playing pretend at something, and I'd come in the wrong way, and any second a guy in a vest and a headset was going to round the corner and chew me out for wandering into the shot.

I'd have paid money to get chewed out right then.

More buildings came up along the path, mud and rough stone leaned together, smoke leaking out of holes in some of the roofs. It smelled like animals and woodsmoke and something cooking I couldn't place, and off behind a wall a goat kept up a flat, complaining bleat. A cloth over a doorway twitched and a woman put her head out. Old, or she just looked old, a brown cloth over her hair, a dress about the color of dirt. She saw me and froze, really froze, like I'd actually scared her, and then she snapped the cloth shut so hard it cracked and she was gone. I could hear her behind it, low and fast.

"Okay," I said, low, just to hear it. "Okay." And I kept walking, because it was that or stand still.

People were out now. A man led a donkey stacked with bundles and swung wide around me, eyes straight ahead the whole time, careful not to look at me. Two barefoot kids stopped dead in the path and stared at my boots with their mouths open. A man crouched against a wall working a strip of leather got to his feet as I came near and turned the knife in his hand so it showed. Everything in me went cold, and I lifted both hands a little, no problem, no trouble, and kept moving, because stopping felt worse than going.

The clothes were the part I couldn't get past. Long shirts belted at the waist, down to the knee, sandals or nothing on the feet, and on every single person not one zipper, not one tag, not one printed word. I'd folded ten thousand T-shirts at work, and whatever this cloth was, no machine had made it: thick, a little lumpy, hanging heavy, dyed in colors that were basically just dirt.

A bigger building had its door wedged open with a rock. The hinges were held on with nails, and I slowed up without meaning to - square, lumpy, each one a little different, nothing like the boxes of identical ones at work. Brentwood kept a few like these in a bin in the back that nobody ever bought. Nobody put nails like that on a door they actually used. Something about it felt wrong, and I couldn't say why, and I kept moving.

A man stepped into my path and said something that sounded like a question. Not Spanish - I'd bailed on Spanish in eighth grade over the rolled R's, but I knew what Spanish sounded like, and it wasn't that, or French, or anything else I'd ever heard.

"Do you speak English?" He held steady and watched me. "I'm lost. Do you have a phone?" I held mine up and pointed at it. He only said something slower, his eyes on my hands. "Danny. I'm Danny." I tapped my own chest. "I fell, I was in a parking lot, and then I'm just - here, and I don't even know where here is." My voice climbed and cracked and I made myself stop and drag a hand down my face. "Does anybody here speak anything? Anything I know?" Nobody answered, because nobody could. He was a foot away and I couldn't get one word into him, and somehow that was worse than being alone in the field had been. For a second I wanted to grab his shirt and shake him, just to make something happen, and my hands shook with it instead.

Somebody had painted a word over one of the doorways in big capital letters, and I went toward it because letters were at least letters. I read it left to right, then again. They were letters I knew - an R, an A, one that was probably an N, the same letters I'd read my whole life - but lined up like this they didn't spell anything in any language I'd ever seen, and reading it a third time didn't help. I knew how to read. I'd always known how to read, my whole life, and these letters just sat there over the door meaning nothing at all.

Past the last houses the ground climbed. I wanted to get up high and see one thing that made sense, so I went for the rise - past a pen of pigs that put out a smell thick enough to stop me breathing through my nose, past a cart riding on wheels of plain wood, just wood, banded and pegged and turning on a wooden axle - and at the top I stopped.

There was a city.

It sat out in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and ran up the hills behind it, bigger than anything had a right to be out here, a pale sprawl of stone with smoke going up off it everywhere, a hundred thin lines of it. I was so relieved for a second that I got dizzy with it. A city had streets. It had a hospital and somebody in charge and some way back to my mom. I started down toward it, already working out what I'd say to the first cop I found.

Then I saw the building at the near edge of it, and everything in my head just stopped.

It was round, and huge even from up here, a pale ring of stone with arches stacked in rows up the face of it. I counted the rows before I knew I was counting. Four. I knew this building. Everybody knew this building - on screens, in a textbook, on the side of a pizza box once, in the eighth-grade class I'd spent mostly thinking about other things. And in every single picture of it, ever, it was a wreck. Half fallen down, the top gone, chunks of it missing, dark and pitted with age. That was the only way I'd ever seen it.

This one was whole, every arch sharp and bright in the early sun. This one was new.

My legs just gave out and I ended up sitting in the path, knees up, arms wrapped around them, staring at it, and I couldn't look away and I couldn't make it not be there.

"No," I said out loud, soft, and then again, harder, "no." Somebody built a fake one. People did that. There was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But not the mud houses. Not the woman's face when she saw me. Not all of it, not at the same time. My stomach rolled over and I was suddenly certain I was going to be sick, and then, sideways and out of nowhere, I thought of my mom at the kitchen table that night asking how my shift went, and Maya asleep behind her creaky door, and the dark ordinary house I'd walked out of that morning, and I had no idea, none, how I was ever getting back to any of it.

I got the phone out, almost dropped it, opened the camera, and aimed it down the valley. There it was in the little rectangle, tiny and steady and completely real. I took the shot. The fake shutter clicked, loud and stupid in all that quiet.

I zoomed in and went hunting the edges of it for the trick - a seam, a crane, the flat gray of a screen, the spot where a movie set always gives itself away. The picture only got grainy. There was nothing in it to catch.

The size of it wouldn't fit in my head. What came back instead was the timeline poster that ran the whole front wall of my eighth-grade history room, forty feet of it, and how far to the left you had to go to find the year 100. Almost off the end of the paper. Barely on the wall at all.

A dog started barking, back down in the settlement. Then voices. More than one of them, and getting closer, coming up the path fast.

I got up. My knees weren't all the way mine yet and I almost went straight back down. Four men were coming up the rise. Same dust-colored shirts as everyone else, except two of them had leather strapped across the chest, and all four carried spears, long ones, the iron points throwing back the sun in four hard little chips that did not look fake at all. They weren't hurrying. That was the part that got me worst, that they didn't have to. They came up at a walk and spread out as they came, smooth and unbothered, like they'd done this exact thing a hundred times and knew how it ended, and one of them drifted wide and slow to get around behind me and I lost him and could not make myself turn to find him.

Then my body just decided. Run. Everything in me went tight and light at the same time and my weight rolled up onto the balls of my feet before I knew it was happening, the trees right there, the open field right there, and then my brain caught up: four of them, spears, open ground, my back. I would not make ten steps. I knew it before I finished thinking it, and I made myself stay. Every part of me wanted to bolt and I almost did. I don't know how I didn't.

I couldn't tell them anything. That was the worst of it. I couldn't say I was nobody, couldn't explain, couldn't make one single sound that would mean a thing to any of them, and I had no idea what they did out here with a stranger who walked in from nowhere wearing clothes that didn't exist. I got the dead phone into my pocket and put my hands up, both of them, open and high as they would go, and held them there and tried to look like I wasn't anything to be scared of.

The one in front came in close. Close enough to smell, wool and smoke and old sweat. His eyes went over me one more time, my hands, my shoes, the orange logo gone gray with dust, and whatever he decided he decided fast, because he said something short and flat over his shoulder and then they were on me. A hand clamped around my arm hard enough to leave a mark. Fingers dug into the back of my neck and pushed my head down. I heard myself make a sound I didn't mean to make. And then they had me turned and moving, down the hill, toward the city and the clean white ring of stone sitting at the heart of it, and there was nothing I could do about any of it at all.
