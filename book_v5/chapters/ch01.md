# Chapter 1: Waking

My alarm went off at six-fifteen and I hit snooze with my eyes still shut. I spent the usual twenty seconds wondering if I could call in sick. I couldn't. Rick had cut my hours twice already this month, and I had like forty bucks in my checking account, and forty bucks wasn't enough to skip a shift over nothing. I grabbed the orange Brentwood Hardware shirt off the floor, the one with the bleach spot on the bottom and the logo half cracked off the chest, and pulled it on. The house was still dark. I stepped over the creaky board outside my sister Maya's door so I wouldn't wake her, grabbed a granola bar off the counter, and left the lights off.

Jess had sent a meme at eleven the night before. It was a good one. I'd get back to her. I pocketed my keys - house, the Civic, and the brass copy of the store's back door I wasn't technically supposed to have - and went out to the car.

Fifteen minutes, the same road as every morning, past the Dairy Queen and the light that's always red when you're late, radio down low, my head half on the fastener aisle Rick wanted faced before open and half on whether the gas could wait till Friday. I pulled into the lot a little past seven-fifteen, grabbed my phone off the passenger seat, and started across the asphalt toward the doors with my keys swinging off one finger. My foot caught on something, a pothole or the lip of a parking block, I never did sort out which. The ground came up fast and my hands flew out. I felt my palms hit the asphalt, the grit, the sting.

Then nothing.

When I pushed myself up off my hands and knees, the parking lot was gone.

I was in a field. An actual field, cut down to stubble, brown and flat, stretching way out to a line of trees, the sky barely light over the top of it. No store, no road, nothing I knew. The air smelled wrong. Too clean. Like cut grass and dirt, none of the gas and hot blacktop from the parking lot I'd been standing in a second ago. Some bird I'd never heard before was going off, and another one answered it from somewhere. I looked up without thinking and the sky was just blue and empty, not a plane or a wire on it anywhere.

Marcus. This had to be Marcus - exactly the kind of stunt he'd spend two weeks planning - and I wanted it to be Marcus so bad. Marcus, and whoever he'd talked into helping.

"Marcus!" I scrambled up off the ground, turning, scanning the tree line. "Okay, ha ha, you got me, real funny, come out!" Nothing came out. Wind went through the field and quit. I kicked a clod of dirt as hard as I could and it busted apart and didn't help anything. "Marcus, come on, man, I'm gonna lose my whole shift, this isn't - " My voice went up and cracked and I let the rest of it drop.

The phone said seven-forty. Ninety-eight percent, because I'd pulled it off the charger on my way out the door. No bars, not even the little searching circle. I called Marcus and it died before it rang. My mom, the same. 911, which sat there saying *connecting*, *connecting*, and then quit on me. I'd gone down at seven-fifteen and now it was seven-forty. Twenty-five minutes, just gone, and I couldn't remember a second of it.

And then it hit, all of it at once. I couldn't breathe right, sucking air in and in and it wasn't doing anything, and I spun around and it was field every way I looked, no road, no wire, no roof, nothing, and I was saying oh my god, oh my god, out loud, fast, and I couldn't stop. My legs went out and I dropped into the stubble and pushed the heels of my hands into my eyes till there was just dark and the burn. If I don't look. If I just don't look at it. But I could still feel the dry stalks under me and that bird kept going, and it was real, all the way real. I don't know how long I was down like that. At some point I turned on airplane mode without even thinking, from doing it a hundred times when my battery died, and then I just sat there holding the phone and shaking.

I felt the back of my head. No lump, no blood, nothing, and that should have been good but it wasn't, because I'd hit hard enough that something should have been there. Maybe a concussion. A concussion would explain it. I'd seen something once about people coming out of comas swearing the whole thing had felt completely real. Maybe my mom was sitting next to a hospital bed right now and none of this was happening. Then I felt the ground under my hands, solid and not going anywhere, and the bird would not stop, and I couldn't make myself believe the hospital anymore.

I couldn't just sit there. So I got up - my hands still weren't steady, but I got up - and started toward the trees, because at least they were something. I watched my feet. I counted the rows as I went. I looked for anything a person had made. The stubble crunched under my boots. Then the field ran into a strip of weeds and then a path, packed dirt with two grooves worn down the middle, close together, too narrow for a car, no tire tread at all. I stared at them too long. Just the grooves, and next to them, pressed into the dried mud, hoofprints.

If I didn't show before the store opened, Rick was going to call my mom. Dumb thing to be thinking about. But thinking about Rick was easier than the thing I didn't want to think about at all: there was no road here. Not anywhere. I walked a long time, longer than any field back home would go without a fence or a wire or a phone pole, until my mouth went dry. The granola bar was still in my pocket and I couldn't have eaten it if you paid me. The sun climbed and threw a glare off the field, and the whole way I kept almost saying stuff to somebody who wasn't there.

Buildings started showing up, a few at a time. The first one almost made me laugh. The walls were mud - actual dried mud, gone tan and split with cracks - under a roof of red clay tiles laid crooked, a cloth hung over the doorway instead of a real door. I'd watched enough YouTube to know what the back of a movie set looks like, or one of those living-history villages where people dress up and churn butter for tourists. Somebody was filming something out here, or playing pretend at something, and I'd come in the wrong way, and any second a guy in a vest and a headset was going to round the corner and chew me out for wandering into the shot.

I'd have paid money to get chewed out right then.

More buildings came up along the path, mud and rough stone leaned together, smoke leaking out of holes in some of the roofs. It smelled like animals and woodsmoke and something cooking I didn't recognize, and a goat was bleating somewhere behind a wall. A cloth over a doorway twitched and a woman put her head out. Old, or she just looked old, a brown cloth over her hair, a dress about the color of dirt. She saw me and froze, really froze, like I'd actually scared her, and then she snapped the cloth shut so hard it cracked and she was gone. I could hear her behind it, low and fast.

"Okay," I said, low, just to hear it. "Okay." And I kept walking, because it was that or stand still.

People were out now. A man led a donkey stacked with bundles and swung wide around me, eyes straight ahead the whole time, careful not to look at me. Two barefoot kids stopped dead in the path and stared at my boots with their mouths open. A man crouched against a wall working a strip of leather got to his feet as I came near and turned the knife in his hand so it showed. Everything in me went cold, and I lifted both hands a little, no problem, no trouble, and kept moving, because stopping felt worse than going.

The clothes were the part I couldn't get past. Long shirts, belted, down to the knee, sandals or bare feet. No zippers. No tags. Nothing printed on any of it. I'd folded a lot of shirts at work and this was nothing like any of them - thick, a little lumpy, hanging heavy, dyed in colors that were basically just dirt.

A bigger building had its door wedged open with a rock. The hinges were held on with nails, and I slowed up without meaning to - square, lumpy, each one a little different, nothing like the boxes of identical ones at work. Brentwood kept a few like these in a bin in the back that nobody ever bought. Something about it felt wrong, and I couldn't say why, and I kept moving.

A man stepped into my path and said something that sounded like a question. Not Spanish - I'd bailed on Spanish in eighth grade over the rolled R's, but I knew what Spanish sounded like, and it wasn't that, or French, or anything else I'd ever heard.

"Do you speak English?" He held steady and watched me. "I'm lost. Do you have a phone?" I held mine up and pointed at it. He only said something slower, his eyes on my hands. "Danny. I'm Danny." I tapped my own chest. "I fell, I was in a parking lot, and then I'm just - here, and I don't even know where here is." My voice went thin and I made myself stop and drag a hand down my face. "Does anybody here speak anything? Anything I know?" Nobody answered, because nobody could. He was a foot away and I couldn't get one word through to him, and that was worse than being alone out in the field. For a second I wanted to grab his shirt and shake him, just to make something happen. My hands shook instead.

Somebody had painted a word over one of the doorways in big capital letters, and I went toward it because letters were at least letters. I read it left to right, then again. They were letters I knew - an R, an A, one that was probably an N - but lined up like that they didn't spell anything, not in any language I'd ever seen, and reading it again didn't help. I could read. I'd been reading my whole life. And these letters didn't mean anything.

Past the last houses the ground went up. I wanted to get up high and see one thing that made sense, so I went for it - past a pen of pigs that stank bad enough I started breathing through my mouth, past a cart on wheels made of plain wood, just wood, no metal on it anywhere - and at the top I stopped.

There was a city.

It sat out in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and went up the hills behind it, bigger than anything had a right to be out here, a big pale spread of stone with smoke going up off it everywhere. I was so relieved for a second I got dizzy. A city had streets. It had a hospital and somebody in charge and some way back to my mom. I started down toward it, already working out what I'd say to the first cop I found.

Then I saw the building at the near edge of it, and everything in my head just stopped.

It was round, and huge even from up here, a pale ring of stone with arches stacked up the side, row over row. I knew this building. Everybody knew this building - on screens, in a textbook, on the side of a pizza box once, in the eighth-grade class I'd spent mostly thinking about other things. And in every picture I'd ever seen of it, it was a wreck. Half fallen down, the top gone, chunks missing, dark and old. That's the only way I'd ever seen it.

This one was whole. Every arch there, clean and bright in the sun. This one was new.

My legs just gave out and I ended up sitting in the dirt, knees up, arms around them, and I couldn't stop looking at it.

"No," I said out loud, soft, and then again, harder, "no." Somebody built a fake one. People did that. There was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But not the mud houses. Not the woman's face when she saw me. Not all of it, not at the same time. My stomach rolled over and I was suddenly sure I was going to throw up, and then out of nowhere I thought about my mom at the kitchen table that night, asking how my shift went, and Maya asleep behind her creaky door, and the meme from Jess I still hadn't answered, and the whole dark house I'd walked out of that morning, and I had no idea how I was ever getting back to any of it.

I got the phone out, almost dropped it, opened the camera, and aimed it down the valley. There it was on the little screen, small and clear and right there. I took the shot. The fake shutter clicked, loud and stupid in all that quiet.

I zoomed in and looked for the trick - a seam, a crane, the flat gray of a screen, the place a fake would show. The picture only got grainy. There was nothing in it to catch.

The size of it wouldn't fit in my head. What I kept thinking about instead, dumb as it was, was the timeline poster on the wall in eighth-grade history, and how far back you had to go to get to the Romans. Almost off the end of it. Barely on the wall at all.

A dog started barking, back down in the settlement. Then voices. More than one of them, and getting closer, coming up the path fast.

I got up. My knees weren't all the way mine yet and I almost went straight back down. Four men were coming up the rise. Same dirt-brown shirts as everyone else, except two of them had leather strapped across the chest, and all four carried spears, long ones, the metal points catching the sun, and they did not look fake at all. They weren't hurrying. That was the part that got me worst, that they didn't have to. They came up at a walk and spread out as they came, easy, like they'd done it plenty of times and it always went the same way, and one of them went wide and slow to get around behind me and I lost him and couldn't make myself turn to look.

Then my body just decided. Run. Everything in me wanted to and I was up on the balls of my feet before I decided anything, the trees right there, the open field right there, and then my brain caught up: four of them, spears, open ground, my back. I wouldn't make ten steps. I knew it right away, and I made myself stay. I don't know how I didn't bolt anyway.

I couldn't tell them anything. That was the worst of it. I couldn't tell them I was harmless, couldn't explain, couldn't make one sound that meant anything to them, and I had no idea what they did out here with some stranger who showed up out of nowhere in clothes nobody had ever seen. I got the phone into my pocket and put my hands up, both of them, open and high as they would go, and held them there and tried to look like I wasn't anything to be scared of.

The one in front came in close. Close enough that I could smell him, wool and smoke and old sweat. He looked me over one more time - my hands, my shoes, the orange logo gone gray with dust - and he made up his mind fast, because he said something short over his shoulder and then they were on me. A hand clamped around my arm hard enough to leave a mark. Fingers dug into the back of my neck and pushed my head down. I made a sound I didn't mean to. And then they had me turned around and moving, down the hill, toward the city and that clean white ring of stone in the middle of it, and there was nothing I could do about it.
