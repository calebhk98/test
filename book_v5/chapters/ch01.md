# Chapter 1: Waking

The alarm went off at six-fifteen and Danny hit snooze with his eyes still shut. He gave himself the usual twenty seconds to wonder whether he could call in sick, and the answer was the usual one. Rick had cut his hours twice already this month, and there were like forty dollars in his checking account, and forty dollars did not stretch to skipping a shift over nothing. He sat up and pulled the orange Brentwood Hardware shirt off the floor, the one with the bleach spot on the hem and the logo half cracked off the chest, and dragged it over his head. The house was still dark. He stepped over the board outside Maya's door that always gave him away, grabbed a granola bar off the counter, and left the lights off.

Jess had sent a meme at eleven the night before. It was a good one. He'd get back to her. He pocketed his keys - house, the Civic, and the brass copy of the store's back door that he was not technically supposed to have - and went out to the car.

Fifteen minutes, the same road as every morning, past the Dairy Queen and the long light that never changed when you wanted it to, the radio turned down low and his brain half on the fastener aisle Rick wanted faced before open and half on whether the gas could wait until Friday. He pulled into the lot a little past seven-fifteen, grabbed his phone off the passenger seat, and started across the asphalt toward the doors with his keys swinging off one finger. His foot caught on something, a pothole or the lip of a parking block, he never did sort out which. The ground came up fast and his hands flew out.

Then nothing.

When he pushed himself up off his hands and knees, the parking lot was gone.

He was in a field. A real one, cut down to stubble, brown and flat, running out a long way to a line of trees with a thin early sky stretched over the top of it. No lot, no store, no road, nothing he could put a name to. The air came in wrong. Too clean, too green, missing the exhaust and warm tar he hadn't known he breathed every day until it wasn't there. Off in the trees a bird made a call he didn't recognize, and a second one somewhere answered with another he didn't recognize either. Under all of it ran a quiet he had no name for. No engine anywhere, no hum coming off a highway, no plane dragging its white line across the sky. The sky had nothing in it at all.

Marcus. That was the first thing that made any sense, so he grabbed it and held on. Marcus, and whoever Marcus had talked into helping.

"Marcus!" He was on his feet before he decided to be. "Okay, you got me. Come out!" Nothing came out. A breeze moved the stubble and quit. "I am going to be so late. This isn't funny!"

He got the phone out. Six-forty. Ninety-eight percent, because he'd yanked it off the charger on his way out the door. No bars, not even the little searching symbol. He called Marcus and it died before it rang. His mom, same. 911, which sat there saying *connecting* and *connecting* and then gave up. He stood with the dead call in his hand and his jaw set, and then thumbed the phone into airplane mode, because if the battery ran out here he was not buying a new one on forty bucks.

He felt the back of his head. No bump, no blood, no headache. He'd half wanted a concussion, because a concussion would explain this - he'd watched a video once about a guy who came out of an eleven-day coma swearing the whole stretch had felt exactly like being awake. Maybe his mom was sitting by a hospital bed right now and none of this was happening. The thought lasted about four seconds. The stubble was sharp and real under his hands, and the bird kept making that sound.

Standing there wasn't doing anything. He picked the direction with the trees and turned it into a job: watch your feet, count the rows off, find one thing a person made. The stubble crunched under his boots. The field gave out into a band of weeds and then a path, packed dirt with two grooves worn down the middle of it, smooth scoops about a stride apart, too narrow for a car, no tread anywhere in them. He crouched over them longer than he meant to. Just the smooth grooves, and pressed into the dried mud beside them, the cut shapes of hooves.

If he wasn't clocked in by seven, Rick was going to call his mom. The thought came complete and ordinary and sat on top of everything, and underneath it crept a quieter one he liked even less. There was no road here. Not in any direction he could see. He walked a long time after that, longer than any field back home ran without hitting a fence or a wire or a phone pole, until his mouth went dry and the low sun came off the stubble in a flat glare, and the whole stretch he kept wishing, stupidly, that there were one single person around he could say all of this out loud to.

The settlement showed up a little at a time. The first building almost made him laugh. The walls were mud - actual dried mud, gone tan and split with cracks - under a roof of red clay tiles laid crooked, a cloth hung over the doorway where a door should be. He'd watched enough YouTube to know the back side of a movie set, or one of those living-history villages where people dressed up and churned butter for tourists. Somebody was filming something out here, or playing pretend at something, and he'd come in the wrong way, and any second a guy in a vest and a headset was going to round the corner and chew him out for wandering into the shot.

He'd have paid money to get chewed out right then.

More buildings came up along the path, mud and rough stone leaned together, smoke leaking out of holes in some of the roofs. It smelled like animals and woodsmoke and something cooking he couldn't place, and off behind a wall a goat kept up a flat, complaining bleat. A cloth over a doorway twitched and a woman put her head out. Old, or worn down to looking old, a brown cloth over her hair, a dress the color of dust. She saw him and went still, the kind of still that nobody acts, and then she snapped the cloth shut so hard it cracked and she was gone. He could hear her behind it, low and fast.

"This is fine," Danny told nobody, and kept walking, because it was that or stand still.

People were out now. A man led a donkey stacked with bundles and gave Danny a wide berth without once turning his head. Two barefoot kids stopped dead in the path and stared at his boots with their mouths open. A man crouched against a wall working a strip of leather got to his feet as Danny came near and turned the knife in his hand so it showed - not aimed at him, just there, just so he'd know it was.

The clothes were the part he couldn't get past. Long shirts belted at the waist, down to the knee, sandals or nothing on the feet, and on every single person not one zipper, not one tag, not one printed word. He'd folded ten thousand T-shirts at work, and whatever this cloth was, no machine had made it: thick, a little lumpy, hanging heavy, dyed in colors that were really just whatever brown the dirt nearby was doing.

A bigger building had its door wedged open with a rock. The hinges were nailed on with square nails, and that slowed him down before he understood why. Brentwood sold square nails out of a bin in the back, novelty things for people building fake-old barn doors, and one of the guys had told him once that nobody used them for real anymore, that they'd stopped hammering nails out by hand a couple hundred years back. He hadn't really been listening at the time. He kept moving.

A man stepped into his path and said something with the shape of a question to it. Not Spanish - Danny had bailed on Spanish in eighth grade over the rolled R's, but he knew the sound of it, and this wasn't the sound of it, or of French, or of anything his ear could catch a corner of.

"Do you speak English?" The man held steady and watched him. "I'm lost. Do you have a phone?" Danny held his own up and pointed at it. The man said something slower, eyes on Danny's hands. "Danny. I'm Danny." He tapped his own chest. "I fell down in a parking lot, and now I'm here, wherever here is." Nothing crossed the man's face that meant he'd understood, and for a second Danny wanted, badly, for this one guy to know what a parking lot even was.

Over one of the doorways somebody had painted a word in big capital letters, and he went toward it because letters were at least letters. He read it left to right. He read it again. The shapes had been with him his whole life, an R and an A and one that was probably an N, but lined up like that they didn't spell anything in any language he'd ever met, and a third read didn't fix it. The cold patch at the back of his neck spread.

Past the last houses the ground climbed. He wanted to get up high and see one thing that made sense, so he went for the rise - past a pen of pigs that put out a smell thick enough to stop him breathing through his nose, past a cart riding on wheels of solid wood with no rubber and no metal anywhere on them - and at the top he stopped.

There was a city.

It sat out in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and ran up the hills behind it, bigger than anything had a right to be out here, a long pale spread of stone with smoke standing off it in a hundred thin lines. Relief went through him so hard his knees loosened. A city had streets. It had a hospital and somebody in charge and some way back to his mom. He started down toward it, already half-writing what he'd say to the first cop he found.

Then his eye snagged on the building at the near edge of it, and his head went quiet.

It was round, and it was huge even from up here, a pale ring of stone with arches stacked in rows up the face of it. He counted the rows before he knew he was counting. Four. The sun had just reached the top, and the stone was nearly white, clean the whole way around, every arch sitting where it belonged, the top of it still on. No green stains down the sides. No gap punched out of the wall. No scaffolding, no rebar standing up out of a fallen chunk. He'd seen this building his entire life - on screens, in a textbook, in the eighth-grade class he'd spent thinking about other stuff, on the side of a pizza box one time - and so had everyone, and in every last picture of it the thing was a ruin. Half collapsed. That was how it always looked, longer than his whole country had been a country.

This one was new.

His legs quit and he went down to a crouch on the path with his arms around his knees, breathing too fast and too shallow, the corners of his sight going bright and grainy.

"No," he said out loud, because it was the only word that fit, and then again, "no." Somebody built a fake one. People did that. There was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But the mud houses, and the woman's face, and the square nails, and the wooden wheels, and the letters that wouldn't turn into a word, and the birds, and the air, and the whole spread of land in front of him without a single straight machine-made line in it except the screen in his own hand.

He got the phone out, almost dropped it, opened the camera, and aimed it down the valley. There it sat in the little rectangle, tiny and steady and completely real. He took the shot. The fake shutter clicked, loud and stupid in all that quiet.

He zoomed in and went hunting the edges of it for the trick - a seam, a crane, the flat gray of a screen, the spot where a movie set always gave itself away. The picture only got grainy. There was nothing in it to catch. He'd been telling himself a story all morning, prank and then movie set and then whatever was supposed to come after that, and crouched on the path he felt the last of it quietly come apart.

He couldn't make the size of it mean anything. He tried, because numbers were the one thing he'd always been able to hold, but he had nothing up here to measure it against and the math slid off and wouldn't catch. What came back instead was the timeline poster that ran the whole front wall of his eighth-grade history room, forty feet of it, and how far left you had to go to find the year 100. Almost off the end of the paper. Barely on the wall at all.

A dog started barking, back down in the settlement. Then voices, more than one, coming up the path fast.

Danny got up, his knees not all the way under him, and turned. Four men were climbing the rise in the same dust-brown shirts as everyone else, leather strapped over the chest on two of them. They had spears. Long ones, real ones, the points throwing back four small chips of sun. They came on at a walk and fanned out to put him in the middle, and the one in front held an arm out flat to keep the others off, his eyes moving over Danny's hands, his boots, the cracked orange logo across his chest.

He didn't run. Running was the worst thing he could do, and some cold flat part of him knew it a second before the rest of him caught up.

Danny slid the phone into his pocket. He raised both hands, palms out, and held them there.
