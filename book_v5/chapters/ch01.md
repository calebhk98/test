# Chapter 1: Waking

Danny Mercer was in a field.

He registered this in pieces: ground against his cheek, knees cold through his jeans, the scratch of cut stalks under his palm. His phone was in his hand. His thumb found the button on its own and the screen lit up. Six-fourteen. Battery ninety-eight percent. No signal. He sat up.

The field went on to a line of trees a long way off. Behind him, nothing. No barn, no fence, no parking lot. Pale gold stubble cut down to ankle height, running in uneven rows, not machine-cut, and over all of it a sky still going from deep blue to the pale edge of morning. He had his work clothes on, all of them. Jeans, his gray hardware-store T-shirt with the Brentwood Hardware logo cracked to a ghost across the chest. His work boots, the steel-toes he'd bought himself with his first paycheck so his feet would stop dying on the concrete floor. His keys were in his pocket: house key, the spare to his mom's Corolla, the brass one to the store's back door that he wasn't technically supposed to have. They jingled when he moved, a small, ordinary sound that had no business being in a field in the middle of nowhere.

His stockroom shift at Brentwood Hardware started at seven. If he wasn't clocked in by seven, Marcus was going to call his mom.

He switched the phone to airplane mode to save the battery and turned in a slow circle. No roads.

The sound was wrong. Not the birds themselves, he wasn't a birdwatcher and didn't know sparrows from anything, but the volume of them, a noise coming up out of the trees all at once like someone had thrown a switch, layered and loud with nothing in it he could name. And under that, just silence. No highway hum. The kind of background nothing that you stop hearing at home and only notice when it stops. Then the smell hit: thick and low and animal, woodsmoke and something older underneath it, something like a restaurant dumpster in August except spread thin over everything, no clean air anywhere under it.

"Okay," he said, to the field. His voice came back flat. "You fainted. Hit your head. Concussion can scramble you. That's a thing." He pressed two fingers to the back of his skull and found nothing. His head didn't hurt at all. He stood for a minute working through other possibilities: sleepwalking, some kind of episode, a coma and this was what the inside of one felt like. He'd read something once about how coma dreams feel completely real. That could be it. He was in the hospital. His mom was probably sitting next to him. He opened his phone and looked at it. The photo of his cousin's treehouse was still there on the lockscreen. The battery was still ninety-eight percent. He started walking because standing was worse.

He picked the direction where the smoke smell was strongest, because smoke meant people, and people meant roads or a working phone or someone who could explain what was happening. He made himself a job of it: watch the ground, count steps, look for anything made by a person. The stubble crunched under his boots. After a while the field gave out into a strip of weeds and then a path, packed dirt and worn ruts, the smooth scoop a wheel makes but the wrong width for a car, no tread marks at all. He stopped and looked at the ruts for a long time. Just the smooth groove, and beside it, pressed into the old mud, hoofprints.

He kept walking, and if he wasn't at work by seven, Marcus was going to call his mom. He thought this very clearly, the practical fact of it sitting right on top of everything else, and then he thought: there are no roads in any direction. And then, right behind that: where is the stockroom.

The settlement came up on him slow. The first building made him almost laugh. Mud walls, actual dried mud, cracked tan, with a roof of red clay tiles that sat crooked. A low doorway with a cloth hung in it. He'd seen enough YouTube to know what a historical movie set looked like from behind. Or a historical village - one of those places where people dressed up and churned butter. Either way, somebody was filming something and Danny had wandered onto the back lot, and any second a guy in a vest with a headset was going to come around the corner and yell at him for being where he wasn't supposed to be.

He would have given anything in the world to be yelled at right then.

There were more buildings now, the settlement widening along the path, a cluster of mud and rough stone strung together with smoke coming from holes in some of the roofs. A cloth hanging in a doorway moved and a woman looked out. She was old, or she looked old, with a brown cloth over her hair and a long dress the color of nothing in particular. Her face when she saw Danny went still, the held-breath kind, animal stillness, someone startled by something that wasn't supposed to be there. Then she pulled the cloth across the door so fast it snapped and was gone, and he heard her saying something inside, low and urgent.

Danny stood there for a second. Not the building. Not the smell of animals or the smoke. The woman's face. The specific stillness of someone startled by something wrong. People at movie shoots didn't look at you like that.

"This is fine," he said, and kept walking, which was the only thing he had.

There were people now. A man leading a donkey loaded with bundles who stepped wide around Danny without making eye contact. Two kids, filthy and barefoot, who planted themselves in the path and stared at his boots with their mouths open. A man squatting by a wall working a piece of leather with a knife, who stood when Danny came near and held the knife where Danny could see it, not pointing it, just showing it was there.

The clothes were wrong. All of them. Long belted shirts to the knee, sandals or bare feet, not a zipper or a logo anywhere in sight. Danny worked retail. He had refolded ten thousand T-shirts. He knew what machine-made cloth looked like and this wasn't it. This was thick and uneven and hung different, the colors just different shades of whatever dirt was nearby.

A door on one of the bigger buildings was propped open with a stone, and he could see the hinges. Square-headed nails. He slowed. He'd seen square nails before, in the antique bin at the back of Brentwood Hardware, novelty stuff they sold to people building replica barn doors. You didn't see them in actual use because they stopped making nails by hand a long time ago. His manager had mentioned it once and Danny had not paid much attention. He kept moving.

A man stopped him and said something. It had the shape of a question, sharp at the end. Not Spanish, not French from two years of it in middle school that he'd mostly spent wishing he'd picked a different language. Not anything. Whatever it was had the edges of something old, something he couldn't place, something he might have learned once in a class he'd stopped paying attention to.

Danny said, "Do you have a phone? Can you help me?" The man's face didn't change. Danny tried again, slower and louder, which he knew was the stupidest thing you can do and did it anyway: "Help. Help me." He pointed at himself and at the ground and mimed holding a phone to his ear. The man took a step back and put his hand on a kid's shoulder and moved the kid behind him.

"I just need a signal," Danny said. He held up the phone so the man could see the screen. The man looked at the screen and then at Danny's face and then stepped back again.

He could stand a foot from another person and they were as cut off from each other as two animals. He'd never felt that before.

There was a word painted above a doorway in capital letters and Danny went to it because letters were letters. He stood there and looked. He knew these letters. An R, an A, what might have been an N. He read them left to right three times. They didn't make a word in any language he'd ever seen. They were arranged like a word and made nothing. He read them again. Nothing. The cold feeling at the back of his neck got worse.

He'd had three years of English, two of Spanish he'd dropped because he couldn't roll his Rs, one semester of French before his schedule changed. He'd seen signs in Chinese and Arabic in the window of the noodle place near his house. He'd played video games in Japanese. None of that had prepared him for the specific wrongness of looking at letters you know and having them say nothing.

He needed to get higher. There was a rise at the far end of the settlement and he went for it, past pigs in a pen that hit him with a smell so strong he held his breath, past a cart with wheels of solid wood, no rubber or metal on them anywhere, and he got to the top and stopped.

There was a city.

It was far off, sitting in the haze at the bottom of a wide valley and up over the hills beyond, and it was bigger than it had any right to be out here in the middle of all this, a long smear of pale stone with smoke standing up over it in a hundred thin gray lines. Danny felt a flood of relief so strong his legs went a little shaky with it. A city meant streets. Signs. A hospital. His mom. He started down the path toward it, already composing the speech he'd give to the first police officer he found.

Then he saw the building on the near edge of it and for a moment nothing happened in his head at all. It was round. Enormous, even from here. A pale ring of stone with rows of arches stacked up its side, and he counted the rows before he knew he was doing it, the way you count things when your brain needs something to grip. Four rows of arches. The morning sun was just catching the top of it and the stone was almost white. It was clean. It was whole. No cracks, no holes, no green stains running down the face. No missing sections, no scaffolding, no rebar where pieces had fallen. He had seen a picture of a building like that his whole life. On screens, in textbooks, in his eighth-grade history class that he'd mostly spent thinking about other things. On the side of a pizza box once. Everybody had. And in every single picture it was a ruin. That was the only way it ever was. Had been, for longer than the country he was from had existed.

And this one was whole.

Danny's legs stopped being reliable. He ended up crouched on the path with his arms around his knees and his breath coming wrong, fast and shallow, the edges of his vision going sparky, looking at the unbroken Colosseum sitting in the morning light at the bottom of the valley, and the bottom went out of everything.

No. He said that out loud, just the word, over and over, because it was the only one that fit. They built a fake one. They reconstructed it, people did that, there was a fake Eiffel Tower in Las Vegas. But the mud huts. But the woman's face. But the letters that weren't words and the square nails and the cart with the wooden wheels and the clothes and the birds and the air and the path with no tire tracks in the whole world he could see, not one, not a single straight line made by a machine except the lines on the screen in his hand.

He got the phone out. His hands were shaking for real now, the unmanaged kind. He opened the camera and pointed it down the valley and there was the building in the little rectangle of the screen, small and still and completely, impossibly real.

He took the picture. The fake shutter sound clicked.

He tried 911. The call sat there a second saying connecting and then the keypad came back, no ring, no operator, nothing. He tried his mom, because his thumb knew where her name was without looking. Same thing, calling, and then nothing, and then the keypad, and he made a sound he wasn't proud of and sat down hard in the dirt.

He sat there and looked at the building. He counted arches, stared at the rows and tried to estimate the scale of what he was seeing. He didn't get anywhere useful. His reference points were wrong. He had no idea how to convert what he was seeing into anything that meant something, except that it was large in a way a fake thing couldn't afford to be.

At some point in the last year, in some class he'd barely paid attention to, he'd learned when the Colosseum was built. First century AD. He'd probably learned it and immediately forgotten it because he was thinking about video games. He would have traded a lot, right now, for a better memory.

Two thousand years was not a number he could hold in his head. He tried and it slipped. He couldn't make it mean anything. He stared at the building and tried, and what kept coming back was the timeline poster in his eighth-grade history classroom, forty feet of it across the front wall, and how far off the edge of the frame the year 100 was. Almost off the beginning of the visible world.

The dog started barking first, somewhere back in the settlement. Then voices, more than one, coming up the path fast.

Danny got to his feet. His knees were not entirely his yet. He turned.

Four of them coming up the rise, men in the same dirt-colored shirts as everyone else but with leather over their chests on two of them. They had spears, long ones, real ones, the tips catching the new sun in four small bright points. They came at a walk with purpose in it, spread out to have him from more than one side, and the one in front had his arm out flat to hold the others at distance, watching Danny's hands and feet and the strange bright cloth of him, the way you watch something that might go off.

Danny put the phone in his pocket. He put his hands up, palms out.

It was the only word he had in any language they could possibly want.
