# Chapter 48: The Subtraction

The world I had built around myself began to come apart from the edges in. Like the cheap wool tunics: the hem first, a thread at a time, so that you go on calling it a tunic long after it has stopped being one.

Pamphilus went that spring, the spring of my three-and-sixtieth year. He had been the first man I ever bought free, with four thousand sesterces of my own money - the first money that was actually mine, the year after Caesar made me his freedman and I could at last spend what I had - and Hermes's cold chisel against the rivet of his collar. He had answered my question about where he would go with another question, *go where,* and stayed at the wharf forty years on real wages and outlived a wife and a child and most of his teeth, and he died in his bed in the room between the storerooms one night without troubling anyone. He was past seventy. There was nothing to say it was but the years. I paid for the burial out of my own purse and not the house's, because I had bought him once with the first money that was mine, and I wanted the last payment to come from the same place.

Eros went the next winter, the letterer, the man who had cut the first crooked Greek Homer into pearwood under my eye and then spent thirty years pulling sheets off the press until his right shoulder hung lower than his left. He had taught Vitalis the cutting before he died, which was the only labor I had asked of him at the end and the only labor he had cared about either, that the boy's lines were clean. So the press did not so much as cough when he stopped. It went on biting its sheets. I stood in the press-room the morning after his pyre and watched the new pressman lay a sheet and pull and lay another, and what I felt was cold: the machine carrying on across the gap where a man had stood as if the gap were something it had been designed to close.

There were others, that stretch of years, the small subtractions. An old carter, the woman who had sewn the first show-bag's seams a lifetime ago and whom I had thought already dead, a clerk of Onesimus's line. You reach an age where the names you have known the longest start going out, one and then a dark stretch and then another, and the room closes around each gap, and another gap opens.

---

Macer died that summer, in a chair, in his son's house, at ninety-six.

Men spoke of his age as a freakish matter, something between a record and an affront. He had outlived three emperors and the use of his own legs and the whole generation of contractors he had cheated and been cheated by. His son was a soft heavy man of sixty who still feared him. The priests of his guild had a word for a man that old that was not quite a compliment.

I had gone to see him in the last weeks of it, because Marcia said go and because a man does not refuse Marcia twice in the same season. The room smelled of leather even then. He had not sold a hide in fifteen years and the house was his son's, reeking of the son's perfume, and under it, faint, banked, the leather, as if the man had soaked in it so long the smell was releasing him slowly. He knew me that day. He looked at me down his ruined nose and said, *They told me you were sick in the chest.*

I said I had been, in the spring, and it had passed. He turned the ring on his wasted finger.

*A man learns to keep it standing in the corner like a creditor he is richer than. Thirty years I have had the creditor. You learn to let him wait.* And he laughed, or did what was left of laughing, a dry working of the chest with no sound to it, pleased with himself, pleased to be the one instructing the Thulean for once instead of the other way.

We did not say tender things. We had never once in forty-five years said a tender thing and we did not start with him dying, which would have shamed us both. He asked what the press cleared in a year now. I told him, roughly, in the new figures, and he made me say it twice and did the arithmetic against wages in his head, the head still doing that when the legs and the bladder and the rest of him had quit, and he said I was paying my pressmen too much, which was true, and that I always had.

Then I went home, and a month later his son sent word that he was dead.

I went and stood the correct distance at the pyre, a client at a patron's burning, which is what I legally was to the end. There were not many of us. The men who would have stood close were all ash themselves.

He left me the heavy gold ring. Nothing else, no coin, no share, no word of affection for a clerk to read out, just the ring, the one he had turned on his thumb when he was thinking for as long as I had known him. The clause in the will, drafted in his own brutal commercial Latin, ran that the ring should go *to the Thulean, who will know what it cost.* That was all. No name. He had known me forty-five years and named me in his will as the Thulean, the curiosity he had bought cheap, the one who had never once stopped being, to Macer, a barbarian from a place that probably did not exist.

He was the cage. He was also the only wall between me and worse cages for the years I needed a wall and could not build my own. He had owned me. He had bought me for less than a season's mules off a physician's tip, penned me in a guarded yard, let me use a peculium that was *not yours, I let you use it as your own,* and stood at the wall on the Caelian the day they tried me for my life and spent nothing to save me. He had told me to make myself small and that he would not burn for me. And he had never, in all the years he was afraid, given me up. He could have. There were seasons it would have cost him nothing and bought him a great deal. He let me stand in his atrium and told me to my face he would betray me slow, at an old man's memory, if they pressed him, and then they pressed him, twice that I knew of and likely more I never heard, and the memory stayed slow.

A man does not weep for the bars of his cell. The cell is where he learned the language and made the work and met the wife and lived, in the end, the only life he was going to get. The grief, if it was grief, had no clean edge to take hold of. It was like the leather smell: something leaving, slowly.

The ring would not go past the swell of my ruined knuckles. I could not wear it any more than he could at the end. I had Tyche put it in the box under the floorboard where the dead glass of my first country lies wrapped in oiled wool, two relics from two men who never met, one of whom owned me, mine now and no use to anyone, *who will know what it cost.*

---

I was writing two things that autumn, at two ends of the same table. On the right side, in Latin, the quarantine protocol document that Zoticus needed before winter: the rules for when a fever ran different from the usual fevers, hotter and faster and with the skin's wrong marks. *All troops returning from eastern campaigns to be held at designated quarantine points for fourteen days before entering populated areas. Separate water supply. Separate latrine pits, minimum forty paces from the main camp, downslope. Officers to inspect hands before any food handling.* Clinical, procedural, the dry accounting of what kills men in large numbers. Zoticus had been running the physician line for eleven years; he knew the protocols better than I did by now. The document was for the men he had trained, and the men they would train, and the physicians who would read it fifty years from now and wonder where the rules came from and find no name attached. That was correct. That was the arrangement.

On the left side, in English, in the cipher no one read but Tyche, the other accounting. A list of what the western ships had found and what Rome was now doing with what they found and what I had known on the day I drew the map for a mystified magistrate and what I had said and what I had not said. The western shore was inhabited. I had known it from the morning I got here, because I had learned it in school in a country that had not yet been founded, and I had said nothing, because saying it would have stopped the program before it started. The lie was twenty years old now and had produced twenty years of ships. The second organized crossing had departed the Garonne mouth in the spring. I had stood on the dock as it went, the same dock where I had watched the first one go more than thirty years prior, and I was the last man on the stone when the sails went small and the morning fog took them. I was not wondering if there was land. I knew there was land. I knew who lived on it. I knew what Rome would try to do with what the ship brought home. A man who stands on a dock watching a ship go into a fact he cannot change is not hoping. He is counting what he set in motion. I had written this down on the left side of the table, in English, in the cipher, where it would be honest and useless and preserved.

Zoticus came in while I was sitting there with both documents in front of me. He stopped at the doorway.

"The protocol draft," I said, pushing the Latin across. "Tell me what I have wrong."

He read it standing. He was fifty now, Heras's student and then his student's student, a careful man who had learned to say what he saw. He put the document down and tapped one clause about the water supply.

"Forty paces won't always hold on campaign," he said. "In the hill country, the slope goes the wrong way."

"What's your number."

"Sixty. And I'd add something about the sick tent being upwind."

"Add it," I said. "Your name on the revision."

He nodded, rolled the document under his arm, and left. The English page was still on the left side of the table. I looked at it a moment and then folded it and put it in the cipher-box.

---

That same autumn I made the argument again, this time to Antoninus Pius. I say "again" because I had made it to Hadrian twenty years before: steam and iron roads as the connective tissue of an empire, the thing that would let an eastern crisis reach a western answer in days instead of months. Hadrian had been interested in the way a man is interested in a clock he does not intend to buy. He had funded continued research, which was not the same as a program. Antoninus was different. He was a decent man - careful, unhurried, competent in the manner of a good estate manager - and he had no appetite for what he could not see. I sat across from his secretary's secretary in a room with the wrong smell and made the case with the models and the figures and the old argument about Trajan dying on the wrong side of a supply chain that couldn't move fast enough. The man listened. He was polite. He wrote nothing. He asked no questions that needed answering. I left knowing the argument had not landed and would not land, and I went home and wrote it into the encyclopedia in the section on civil administration, trusting it to whoever came after with better metallurgy and a different emperor.

The railway argument and the steam argument are the same argument. You cannot run a proper locomotive without steel that holds the load. The steel I can make will not hold the load. The encyclopedia explains both the argument and the gap, with the gap marked in the hooked stroke. Someone after me will close it.

---

After Macer the house was running well past me and I knew it.

I would come to the bench in the slack hour with some scheme half-formed, a better way to gum the binding so the codices did not split at the spine, and Vitalis, who was thirty now and ran the press as if it were a limb of his own body, would hear me out with the patient face a grown son gives an old father, and then tell me, kindly, that they had tried it two years back when I was abed with the winter cough, and it cracked worse, and the better way was already in use on every sheet in the room. Chloe kept the storeroom day-book in a hand so clean it shamed the clerks of my prime - Marcia had freed her by her own hand the autumn before any sickness took hold, against the day she said, and she had been right to - and she read the English cipher better than I could write it. Ulpia had the survey, the African coast laid to the last headland and held there, true and unworked, against a future hand. And Tyche held the whole of it, the cipher and the books and the figures and the thread of the enterprise gathered in her sixty years like reins in a driver's fist, and she did not need me to tell her how to drive.

The press ran card games now, too. Vitalis had taken up a contract with three Subura taverns for printed card runs, the same flat counting-sheets the Argiletum booksellers had been selling for games these fifteen years, and the day-tallies showed they outsold everything except the times-table sheets to the schoolmasters. I had taught Rome arithmetic and Rome had made a casino of it. I did not know what to do with that, so I did nothing.

I had become a passenger in the cart I built. People were climbing the ladder. I stood at the bottom of it with my hands too stiff to grip the rung and watched them go up.

---

Marcia I had thought would bury me.

She was younger by thirteen years and built to last, square and unhurried, and she had run her own warehouse and then ours through three reigns and a terror and never once been ill that I saw past a day in bed with a flux she beat by force of contempt. She had said it herself, years back in the storeroom, that a thing that is yours dies when you do, and she had meant me, plainly, that I was the perishable one, my head going and my hands following, and she had built the whole succession of the enterprise on the assumption that she would be there to hand it on after me. We both built on it. It was the floor we stood on.

It came on as an ache in the gut that summer, the summer I was five-and-sixty and she was four-and-seventy. A deep ache that woke her in the night, and then she could not keep her food, and then she went yellow at the eyes and under the nails, the slow rot inside that Heras would have named in a breath and shrugged at, because there was no door into it, the same wall I had stood against over Celer, no medicine in this whole world to make. Zoticus came and was careful and good and did what Heras had taught him, which was to tell it plainly. He told us both, in the storeroom, that it was in the liver, that it would not turn, that it would take some months and they would not be easy, and there were poppy preparations he could give for the worst of the pain and nothing for the rest.

Marcia heard him out. Then she asked him how many months, because she had a deed to finish. He said he could not say, before the cold, likely, and she said, "That will do," and sent him for the poppy, and went back to the tally-board.

She finished the deed. The transfer of trust that moved the warehouse and her own estate clear of Caepio's cousins forever and into the society and the children, the legal wall around the work drawn so tight no relative and no procurator and no emperor's reaching freedman could pry it loose. She sat over it with Ulpia reading her the small clauses she could no longer see for the pain and the poppy, and made Ulpia read each twice, and corrected the lawyer's man once on a wording that would have left a finger's-width gap a clever cousin could get a hand into, and closed it. The deed she had spent eight-and-thirty years teaching me to find. Then she let them put her to bed, which she had refused to do until the deed was sealed, and she did not get up again to any purpose.

I sat with her. She did not want talk. She had never wanted talk. We had been married eight-and-thirty years and most of what passed between us had passed in the medium of work, a tally slid across a table, a contract she would not let me sign, a figure I could not read that she read me in the flat assessing voice that took the world as it was and gave it back at its true weight with nothing added and nothing forgiven. In the bad hours, when the poppy was thinning and before the next dose, she talked a little, and it was never of love, which would have sounded like a man pricing something he was afraid of losing.

She told me which of the cousins would try, and how, and which clause would stop them. "The Ennii won't move on Ulpia," she said. "Not once the deed is hers. But they'll try on the cartage contracts first, because they're the ones you haven't sealed tight. Get Tyche to look at them before the month is out."

I said I would.

"And don't soften toward them over winter. You will want to. That's what they're counting on." I said I knew.

She was quiet a while. Then: "Tyche is to be obeyed in everything to do with the money. You are to be humored and ignored." And the ghost of the old dryness in it, because she knew exactly what I had become in my own house and loved me without one grain of the illusion that I still ran it.

She died before the cold, as Zoticus had said, on a gray morning with the river fog at the window. The breathing went long and shallow toward the end, the spaces between getting wider, and I sat counting them, the arithmetic that my hands and my head do when the rest of me cannot be trusted, and one space ran out and did not fill, and I went on counting it for a while into the quiet before I understood the count had no more numbers in it.

---

There is a side of the bed.

For eight-and-thirty years there had been a weight on the other side of the bed at night, a warmth, a particular shifting and settling I had stopped hearing the way you stop hearing the river, a breathing that was the floor under my own bad sleep. The bad sleep was the same after. The black hours and the rats in the warehouse and the river and the bad accounting were all the same. What was gone was the breathing. I would surface in the dark certain it was dawn, as I always had, and lie there with the old column of the dead running its figures behind my eyes, Sabinus and Geta's hand and Celer and the price of captives, and I would reach, with the worn animal habit of near forty years, for the sound of her breathing to set the floor back under it, and there was the river, and there was no floor.

On the third morning I got up before the light and went to the storeroom table and opened the day-book. Chloe had left it squared and closed with the stylus across it, as someone in this house had always done. I sat at the table and pulled the book toward me and opened it. The marks meant nothing to me in the dark. I had not been able to read small figures for twenty years, and these were smaller than most. I looked at the columns, the careful lines of Chloe's hand, and I picked up the stylus to mark something, anything, some entry that would say I had sat here and the house was still running. The stylus sat in my fist and I put it down. I looked at the open page for a long time and I could not find a number. Nothing came. The columns waited. I had counted my way through everything I had ever lost, every wall I had stood against, every body I had counted cold, and this morning the page was open and the columns were there and there was nothing in me for it. I closed the book. I put the stylus back across it, for Chloe, for the morning, and sat in the dark with my hands in my lap until the light came.

And there were the tallies she kept. She kept the day's loads on a board she would not let the clerks touch, and balanced it last thing and left it by her hand for the morning, and I had a habit, the years my eyes were going and after they were gone, of finding the board she had dropped asleep over and setting it where her hand would fall on it when she woke, because I could not read the figures but I knew the hand, knew the peculiar shape of her sevens that did not look like ones, and I would square the board under the dropped stylus by feel and leave it. There was no board now, no hand to drop it and none to find it. I went on, the first nights, half-reaching in the dark for a board that was not there, my fingers patting the empty table by the bed for a habit my body did not know yet how to stop, and I let them, because telling them to stop felt like a second burial, and they stopped on their own after a while, and that was worse again.

---

I am alone in the house now in the manner a man is alone in a house full of other people. Tyche comes most days and runs what I no longer run. Ulpia has the books and the deed and a household of her own forming around her, free of the Ennii. Chloe keeps the day-book. Vitalis runs the press. Zoticus looks in. The children of the children make noise on the storeroom floor. The maize from the western fields is in the market stalls now, and the vendor on the corner sells a flat bread made of it that is nothing like what grows in our latitudes, and the cook puts the soft red fruits from the same ships into the sauce with the pasta and charges an as more for it. The work goes on without me and the keepers climb the ladder.

I had thought, when I was young and frightened in this place, that the hardest part would be to be the only one who knew. I had not reckoned on this other: to be bought and freed and married and surrounded for forty years, and then to come out the far side of it with the work standing and the people gone, and the room around me as large and as cold as the field I woke in.

There is something I do now in the slack hour. I have Chloe set the storeroom day-book open on the table before me, and I put my two stiff hands flat on the leaf, on the figures I cannot read, the marks I taught this house and this city and a milestone on the Aurelian road, and I leave them there a while on the cool page. The page gives nothing back. It is only the work, going on, in a hand that is not Marcia's and not mine. After a time I take my hands off it and tell Chloe she may close it, and she closes it, and squares it on the table, and sets the stylus across it for the morning the way someone in this house has always done, and goes home to her own evening, and I sit in the chair until the light is gone and there is nothing left to look at that I could see anyway.
