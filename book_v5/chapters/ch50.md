# Chapter 50: Going

The body goes the way the press went when a bearing wore out, not all at once and not in the part you were watching. You oil the screw and the frame cracks. You guard the heart and it is the lungs that fill, slow, over a season, so that by the autumn I could climb the stair to the room over the loose board only if I stopped at the turn and held the wall and let the air come back in its own time, which it did, and then later did not, fully, ever again.

I had thought, when the wind first caught me on the wharf step three winters back, that I knew which door it would be. I was wrong about that, as I had been wrong about most of the timing in my life. The breath stopped me. The water came up under it. Zoticus, who was Heras's pupil's pupil and had Heras's flat refusal to lie to me, listened with his ear to my back as I had taught Heras's whole line to listen, and said the wet was in the bottom of both, and that it would rise, and that he could give me the poppy when I wanted it and could give me nothing for the rising itself, because no one could, because the rung was not built, was not anywhere near built, and would not be built for a length of time he could not have counted and I would not have told him.

So we did not pretend.

---

They moved my bed down to the storeroom in the last weeks, because I could not do the stair, and because I asked for it, though I did not say why I asked.

It was the driest room on the wharf. Marcia had chosen it for the pepper and the hides near five-and-forty years before, and finished her deed in it, and it smelled still of rope and old vellum and the river coming through the wall, and the table where she had drawn the trust stood against the far side with the day-book on it that I could no longer read. I wanted to be near the work. I told Ulpia that was the reason and it was a true reason and the rest of it I kept. The press ran on the other side of that wall. I could feel it through the floor when I lay still, the long shudder of the screw coming down and the lift of it coming up, fifty times a page and a page in the time it took me to take a few of my bad breaths, a thing in the world that did not need me to make it move and went on moving, and I lay with my hand flat on the boards to take it the way an old man takes the heat off a brick.

The mind held longer than the breath. That was the cruelty of the particular door I was given. For a while there were whole afternoons clear as the wharf in summer, where I could do the arithmetic of the quarter in my head while Tyche read me the figures, the head doing its one clean thing while the rest of me drowned by inches. And then there were the other afternoons, later, when the room went strange and the Latin would not come and I reached past it, in the dark behind my eyes, for the words of the other tongue, the first tongue, the one I had not spoken aloud to a living soul in fifty-seven years, and found that those were going too. I had a song once. I had a great many songs once. I went looking for one and found three words of it and a shape where the rest had been, and could not say whether the tune I put under the three words was the true tune or one I was making up on the spot to cover the hole, and there was, as there had been for a very long time, no one in the room or in the world I could ask.

---

In one of the clear afternoons I ate a tomato from the little pot Ulpia had set by the window, the last from the summer planting, still warm from whatever sun reached that ledge. I had been eating them since my late thirties. Ordinary. The skin split under my teeth and it tasted like the kitchen of a house no one had let me go back to.

---

The things that did not come went the same way as the song, in pieces, with the shape left where the thing had been. The heat made to turn a wheel without a horse or a slave hung on it. The little teeth that would have caught and let go and caught and let go and kept the hours true, that I drew a thousand ways and never once made stand still. The glass that should have shown the small living ones swimming and only ever showed a smeared rainbow and a lie. A wire with the lightning tamed and running quiet down the length of it into every house in a city, lighting the dark, which I could see whole and bright in the failing room and could not have so much as begun, not the first rung of it, not in five lifetimes of the body I had left. They came and stood at the foot of the bed, all of them, the unbuilt dead, and I had nothing to say to them either. I had moved what I could reach. The grain, the figures, the clean water, the press going through the wall. The ships I had sent west were gone and returned with crops in their holds, with strange rubber for our gaskets, with news of people on a shore that I had called empty when I drew it and was not, had never been, and I had not corrected that lie while I could and now I never would. That stood at the foot of the bed with the rest. The rest stood at the foot of the bed.

---

Ulpia was there most of the days. She had her mother's face by then and her mother's way of doing the necessary thing without making a scene of it, and she sat at Marcia's table with the books and did the work where I could hear her stylus, which I think she understood was a kindness, the sound of it, the small scratch of the figures going down.

She read to me from the upper rungs in the evenings, in the homeland tongue, in the cipher I could no longer see, the chemistry passage with the blank under it and the note across the blank, the long passage on the lines and the angles, the workings, the figures her grandfather's clerks had called shopkeeper's marks. She read it flat and clean and asked her questions after, flat and careful, and some of the questions I could still answer and some I could not, and on the bad evenings I could not tell which was which, and she did not let me know when I had answered the wrong thing. She only set it down and asked the next. She was keeping the honesty even there, in the dark, at the end. I had made the whole house promise to keep it. I had built that into her when she was a child and now it was being done back to me and it was the right thing and it was cold and I was glad of it.

One clear evening she looked up from the page and said, without warning, "Where did you learn the workings. The real ones. Not the cold north."

She asked it quietly, knowing I was going, giving me the door to put down something she could see I had carried. I lay there and looked at the shape of her against the lamp and wanted, with everything that was left, to give it to her. To say it once. To have one person in the world hold the true weight of it before the weight went into the ground with me.

"The cold north," I said. "The long way nobody came back along."

She heard the old answer. She knew it for the old answer. She let it lie, as her mother had, as Tyche had, all of them too good to make me say it and none of them ever told. She put her hand over my hand on the boards. Her hand was warm and the boards were warm under it where the press came through and I shut my eyes and let her think I slept.

---

Lucanus came on the rest-days from the basilica, in his good toga, three-and-forty and gray at the temples and stiff with me to the last, though the stiffness had gone soft, frayed rather than cut.

We had never warmed in an hour and we did not warm in these. But he sat the wrong way on the stool, as he had sat the wrong way on everything since he was a boy, and he told me the business of the courts in his fine careful Latin, the cases, the men, the long Roman game of obligation he played better than I ever had because he had been born holding the right end of it, born free where I was made, his whole clean life lived up a ladder I was shut off the bottom of. He brought me a thing once from a market stall without saying why he had brought it. It was a child's clay toy, a little smoking-stack cart on four wheels, a Thulean engine, the thing the contest had set Rome's children dreaming of these past fifty years, the cart that ran on its own fire and was never built and would not be built in his lifetime or his children's, and he set it on the table by the day-book and turned it once with one finger and watched the painted wheels go round and said nothing, and I said nothing, and it was the nearest the two of us ever came.

I had wanted other things for him once. I had wanted, for a while, when he was small, to make him the keeper, to put the script and the books into my own blood so that the one person who carried it all the way down would be mine. He had refused it as you refuse a coat that is the wrong shape for you, without cruelty, simply taking up his own life instead, the law and the standing and the free birth, and I had grieved that for years and then stopped, because the ladder I built was never going to ask a climber his father's name before it let him up, and it would have been a poor thing if it asked it first of my own son. So the books went down the workshop line and not the blood line, to a Felix not yet born who would one day stand in a portico off the Argiletum and read a strange treatise and want the next rung. My son sat the wrong way on a stool and turned a clay toy with one finger and we did not speak of any of it.

---

It was Tyche who was there at the night of it. Of course it was Tyche.

She was seventy and slower than she had been and her own eyes had begun to go, and she came in the deep of the night when Chloe sent the storeroom boy running, because Chloe had been sitting the watch and heard the breathing change. The breathing had been changing for days. This was a different change. The wet had risen to where I could not lie flat at all and they had me propped on the bolsters Marcia used to pile against the small of her back, and I was working at the air in long hard pulls, drawing it and getting half of it, and there was a sound in it I had heard before, once, on a frontier, on a green-willow basket coming down out of the sky in fire, a sound I would not write then and could not have stopped my own chest making now if I had wanted to.

Tyche sat where Ulpia sat. She took my hand off the boards and held it in both of hers, which were cold, both of ours cold, the two oldest things in the room. She did not tell me it would be well. She had never once in fifty years told me a thing that was not so to make me feel better, and she did not start at the end. She held my hand and she said the figures of the quarter, low, in the new numbers, as I had made her say them twice, and she did the arithmetic against the wages and the cloth out loud where I could follow it, because it was the one thing my mind could still take hold of, the one rope still strung end to end across the dark, and I followed it down, the columns, the carry, the remainder drawn as a small *r*, down to the bottom of the page where it came out clean.

She was paying the pressmen too much. I could not get the breath to tell her so. She knew I was thinking it. "You taught the whole house to overpay," she said against my ear. "Most expensive lesson you ever gave us. We keep it on purpose." She made the dry sound that had stood in for her laugh for fifty years, and I think I made a sound back. I meant it to be a sound back.

---

There was the room and the lamp and the wet sound and Tyche's two cold hands, and then the room was thinner, as the show bag went thin and pale when you held it up against the sun and saw the light come through the weave, and I was lighter, as the threadbare feed-sack went light on its string over a boy's head in a slaughter-lot, rising while the heat held, and I knew even then, the part of me that always knows, that the lightness is not staying up, the lightness is only the lift, the staying up is the fight and there is no more heat to feed it. So I was going down. I have always known the difference between rising and not falling.

The press is going through the floor. I can feel it in the hand she is holding, the long shudder down and the lift up, a page in the time of a few bad breaths, the screw Vitalis beds in the stone with the lead case patient underneath it, the marks folded in their hide in the dark, waiting, a thing that does not need me, that has not needed me in years, going on and on against my back. Tyche's thumb is moving on the back of my hand, smoothing it flat. She is saying the figures still. I have lost the figures. I have the sound of the figures.

There is grain. There is a field of cut grain in the early light and the stubble of it pale and sharp and the dew not yet off it, gold going gray going gold as the sun comes up the valley, and the cold of the ground coming up through the boots, the good boots I bought with my first paycheck, and far off down the long slope a great pale ring of new stone catching the first of the light, and the thing warm and smooth and heavy in my hand that will not call anyone, that has a picture in it of this very ring when it was new, and the four men coming up the rise with their spears low and the lead one watching my hands, and I am seventeen, and I am frightened, and there is no one, in the whole turning world, who knows where I am.
