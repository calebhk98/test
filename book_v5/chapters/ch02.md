# Chapter 2: The Cell

Nobody shot me, which is the bar I'd set for the morning and I cleared it.

The one in front kept his arm out toward the others and came at me sideways, slow. I kept my hands up. I kept them so far up my shoulders ached. He said something short and I said nothing, because I had nothing, and he said it again louder, like the volume was the problem, like I was a tourist and he could find the English under there if he shouted. I shook my head, which I think is close to universal, and his eyes went flat and careful in a way I did not like.

He reached out and took a fistful of my T-shirt at the shoulder, slow, feeling the cotton between his thumb and two fingers. Something in his face stalled. He looked back at one of the others and said a word, and that one came up and felt it too, and now there were two grown men with spears standing close enough to smell, woodsmoke and sweat and something sour under it, rubbing the hem of my sleeve and talking over my head about my shirt. The shirt was from a three-pack. It cost about six dollars. I wanted to tell them that and there was no version of me that could.

They tied my hands. Rough fiber that smelled of hay, wound twice around my wrists in front of me and knotted hard by the second man while the leader watched my face the whole time. I'd half braced for the cold of metal, because that was what my brain had filed under arrest, and the rope was worse, pinching in a thin line with no give in it. I remember thinking, with the clean idiot clarity of a person in shock, that I knew a better knot. I'd learned a trucker's hitch off a video to tie a kayak to the car. I knew a knot these men didn't, and it was the single most useless fact in the valley. Then they walked me down into the town.

I have tried a lot of times since to put the town in order and I can't, because I didn't go through it in order, I went through it like a wave, all of it at once and over my head. The path turned to a rutted lane and the lane filled up with people the way water fills a low spot. The smell came first and stayed: smoke and dung, the dung of more animals than I could name, and under it a thick rotten low note that turned out to be the tannery and a sharp one that turned out to be the fish sauce they put on everything. Heat came off the walls. The upper floors leaned in over the lane and there were faces in the dark doorways and faces in the windows above, and they came out to look, all of them, a kid with his hands tied being walked through by armed men. It was a normal enough thing that nobody was alarmed. They were only curious, and what they were curious about was me.

A woman pointed at my feet and said something to another woman, and they both looked at my boots. A boy ran alongside us at a careful distance until a man cuffed him off. An old man on a step, shelling something into his lap, watched me go by for a long flat second and then turned his head and spat.

After the old man I kept my eyes down. The ground was its own education. Mud, straw, a dropped fish head, a flattened dead thing that had been a rat, a gutter down the center of the lane that I stepped over without anyone telling me to, because some things you just know. My good steel-toes were already the color of the lane to the ankle.

The building they put me in was stone at the base and brick above, and the cell was a room off a yard, low, one window high up and barred with real iron, a door of heavy planks bound in iron. So they had iron. They had iron for the door and rope for me, and I sat on the dirt floor and worked out what that meant, and the best I got was that I didn't rate iron. I rated rope. That was either good news or it meant they hadn't decided yet what I was, and I went back and forth on which.

They left my hands tied. The leader stood in the doorway after the others had gone, watching me, and I tried. I said "Daniel." I tapped my chest with my bound hands as best I could and said it slow. "Dan-iel." He watched my mouth. Then he said something, four or five words, none of which I had, and waited, and I had to shake my head again, and whatever patience had been in his face packed up and left. He pulled the door shut. A bar dropped on the outside with a heavy wooden clunk I felt through the floor.

I shook for a while in the half-dark. When it leveled off into something I could think over I started counting, because counting was a thing my hands and my head could do together. The window was about a foot square. The room was eight feet by ten, give or take, paced heel to toe with my wrists still tied, which is a stupid-looking way to measure a cell. The light from the window had crawled a hand's width down the far wall since they'd put me in. I could tell time off that wall if I had to. I didn't have to. I had a clock.

I'd been so careful not to think about it. The whole walk down I'd felt it against my thigh through the denim, aware of it the entire time like a sunburn, and now I was alone behind a barred door and I got it out, which is harder than it sounds with your wrists lashed together, a whole two-handed contortion of it.

It woke up. Of course it woke up. Ninety-eight percent.

I almost called my mom. My thumb was already moving and I stopped my own hand, physically stopped it, because I'd watched the call die twice on the hill and it would die again, and I knew something now that I hadn't let myself know up there. The battery was the whole game. It was the only piece of home I had that still worked, and it was draining whether I used it or not, and every dead-end call to a tower that wouldn't exist for nineteen hundred years was a piece of it I'd never get back.

I turned the brightness all the way down. I turned off the radios, airplane mode, which was a joke, an actual joke I made to myself in the cell. Airplane mode in 98 AD. There was no one to tell it to, and I think that was the first time the size of it landed on me, that I had a joke and the joke died in my chest because there was nobody.

Then I opened Notes and started writing, fast, because that was scarier than the spears had been. I didn't organize it. I told myself I'd organize it later and later never really came. I wrote the date the phone thought it was, which was my world's date and wrong. I wrote ROME 98 AD?? with the question marks, because the only date I half trusted was the Colosseum and I trusted that about eighty percent.

Then I tried to write the things I was afraid of losing, and the thing that scared me was how fast that turned on me. I went for my mom's number. The area code came clean and the next three came clean and then the last four wouldn't line up. I tried them forward. I tried them backward. I got two versions that both felt almost right and wrote them both, with a question mark on the one I trusted less. I went for our address and got the house number and the street and the city and then stopped cold on the zip, five digits I had said out loud a thousand times mailing things, just gone, a blank where a number used to live. I wrote down Maya. I wrote Jess, and the meme she'd sent the night before that I never answered. I wrote Marcus, because this had his name all over it, the exact kind of stunt he'd spend two weeks on, except it wasn't, and I don't know why I wrote him down except that he was real and mine and nobody could take him off the glass.

I wrote down that the President was, and stopped, because who was I writing it for.

I was writing it for whoever found my body. That was the answer and I knew it the second I asked, and I had to put the phone face down on my knee for a minute.

Then I picked it back up, because there was one more thing. I held it out at arm's length, both hands, front camera, and there I was, this kid, hair stuck to my forehead, dirt on me, eyes too wide, the gray barred window a smear behind my head. I looked at myself looking at myself and I took the picture, and the fake shutter clicked in the stone room, and now there was a record that a kid named Daniel Mercer had been alive and seventeen and scared in a cell outside Rome on a day that hadn't happened yet. Two photos on the phone now. The Colosseum and my face.

I held the button and watched it go black, which took a real effort, like letting go of somebody's hand, and slid it back into my pocket and pressed my palm flat against the shape of it through the denim.

The door opened again before I'd gotten anywhere with the next problem. A crack of light, then one of the earlier guards, not the leader, the second one, the one who'd felt my shirt. He had a small clay lamp and no spear, and he left the door swung wide behind him, which told me what he thought of my chances of running.

He said something and I did nothing. He came over and tapped my shoulder with one finger, the shirt again, a question. He pinched the hem and stretched it to see the weave and made a sound between a grunt and a question, and looked at the seam and then the small hole near the collar, and I watched him fail to work out how it had been made.

Then his eyes went to my hip, to the shape in my pocket. He pointed, a sharp jab, twice, and whatever he said it meant that. Show me that.

I thought fast about what I had and the answer was nothing. He was between me and the open door, in a city I couldn't read in a language I didn't have. I took the phone out.

He flinched back half a step, then held. I laid it flat on both palms, the screen dark, holding it out the way you hand something to a man at a border. He came in close and looked at it without touching, then turned it over in my hands to see the back. He pressed the black glass with one finger. Nothing. He pressed harder, the press that's hunting for the lid, the opening, the catch. Nothing. He ran a thumbnail along the seam looking for a hinge, and found the volume buttons and pushed one, and nothing happened, because the phone was off and would stay off. Then he noticed the metal pull on my zipper, touched it, and when it slid an inch he yanked his hand back like it had moved on its own. He said something short to nobody, the sound that in any language means well, that's nothing, and handed the phone back to me. He pointed at the hole in my collar and said something that might have been what happened there, and I shrugged, and he made a sound like all right, barbarian, and left. He pulled the door most of the way shut, and a minute later somebody outside dropped the bar.

I sat still until I was sure nobody else was coming, then turned the phone on, ninety-six now, two percent gone on a photo and a panic, and added one line at the bottom, small: keep this inside the shirt, not the pocket. Then off again.

Because they were going to come back and want to know what I was. The leader had already asked, four or five words, and somebody over him was going to ask again, and the asking would stop being polite. I had until then to be worth something. Worth keeping. The other side of worth keeping was a salt mine or a field with a chain on it, or worse, and I'd walked through enough of the town to know those were real and close.

So I lay on my back with my tied hands on my chest and tried to think what I could give them. Something nobody here could do. Something that made me the kind of strange you keep instead of the kind you get rid of.

And it came apart in my hands, one piece at a time.

There was the phone in my pocket, and I couldn't have told you what was inside it past a lot of tiny switches flipping on and off very fast, and there was nothing in this whole world to make one out of and wouldn't be for two thousand years, so that was a thing I owned and couldn't explain and couldn't start to build. There was electricity, and I'd wrapped a nail in wire once in eighth grade and made it pick up paper clips, and I knew, sort of, that a magnet and a coil moving against each other gave you a current, and then what, a magnet from where, wire drawn how thin and by who, to light up what. There was gunpowder, which I knew was three things, sulfur and charcoal and the white one, the one that actually mattered, and the name of the white one would not come. I lay there in the dark digging for it and it stayed gone, and even if it came back it wouldn't matter, because I didn't know where you find it or how much of each or how to keep from blowing my own hand off the first time I guessed wrong. I'm bad at chemistry. I've always been bad at chemistry.

That was the trap. I knew real things. I knew the shape of a hundred things this world had never seen, and every single one of them needed a material I couldn't get, or a furnace I couldn't build, or a word I couldn't say, or ten years I didn't have tonight. It didn't make me a wizard. I was a kid who'd watched a lot of videos, holding a dead rectangle of glass, in a room with a dirt floor.

So I couldn't dazzle anybody, and I couldn't prove where I was from, and the truth was the worst card in my hand anyway. I kept coming back to it and kept putting it down. I'm from the future. There was no way to show it. Nothing on me showed it. The phone showed I had a strange object, and a strange object that lit up and was never wrong about the hour, in a place I already knew read the future out of bird flights and the insides of animals, did not say scholar. It said the other thing, the one with a fire or a stone behind it, the thing you feed to whatever lived in the round building. The truth was unprovable and it was mad and it was the kind of mad that got you killed by frightened people, and I had already frightened people just by walking down their lane.

Which left being from somewhere. Somewhere far enough that no one could check, far enough to cover the clothes and the silence and the wrongness of me, somewhere that made me odd instead of damned. A vagrant got thrown away. A man with some learning got kept and fed and asked questions. From the edge of the map, up north, past wherever north ran out for these people, a cold far place where the cloth is fine and strange and the men know their numbers. I didn't have the word Thule yet. I didn't lie there and build a clean cover story like somebody in a movie. I got as far as far away, and learned, and worth keeping alive, and the rest of it, the name of the cold country and how to sell it, came later, and it came hard, and it cost me. The spine of it I had that first night on the floor. Never the future. Never the truth. Only the far country.

I'd just gotten that far when the hunger arrived, and it arrived as its own event, a fist closing slowly under my ribs, and I understood I hadn't eaten since a night in a world that didn't exist anymore. The hunger and the thirst and the needing to pee all came up around the same time, the body keeping its own schedule, not caring that everything else was over. There was a clay pot in the corner I'd been ignoring, and I worked out, with great dignity, what the clay pot was for.

The light on the wall went orange, then gray, then it was only the window, a paler square in the dark, and I sat against the cold stone with my knees up and my tied hands hanging off them.

The bar lifted near full dark. A man came in with a clay lamp and a bowl. He was younger than the others, or used harder in a different way, built like a soldier and economical like one, and he had a strip of something dark on his shoulder, leather or braid, that the spear-carriers hadn't had, which I took to mean he ranked them, and I was right. He set the lamp in a niche and crouched in front of me on the balls of his feet and considered my wrists and then my face, the look you give a horse somebody's told you to judge. He held out the bowl.

It was warm. Grain mush, gray, the sour sharp reek of the fish sauce cut through it, and in that moment it was the most beautiful object anybody had ever handed me. I went to take it and couldn't, my hands tied, and made a fumbling useless grab, and he caught the bowl before it spilled, fast, no fuss. He looked at my wrists. He looked at my face. He worked something out.

He took a short knife off his belt. I went very still. He turned it and put the edge to the rope between my wrists, not my skin, and sawed through it in two strokes, and the rope dropped away and my hands came apart and the blood went back into them in a hot pins-and-needles rush that made me hiss.

He put the bowl in my hands. He stood, picked up the lamp, and at the door he stopped and pointed at his own chest with two fingers and said a word, slow and clear, and said it again. Two syllables, the second one falling. Then he tipped his head at me and waited, and I understood, and I tapped my chest with a hand that didn't fully work yet and said, "Daniel," and he tried it back, mangled, the middle of it gone soft and wrong, and something in his mouth almost moved toward a smile and didn't get there.

He took the lamp with him. The bar dropped. I sat in the dark with the warm bowl in both my freed hands and ate the worst food of my life with my fingers, fast, scraping the clay, and I licked the bowl after.
