# Chapter 2: The Cell

Nobody shot me, which is the bar I'd set for the morning and I cleared it.

The one in front kept his arm out toward the others and came at me sideways, slow. I kept my hands up. I kept them so far up my shoulders ached. He said something short and I said nothing, because I had nothing, and he said it again louder, like the volume was the problem, like I was a tourist and he could find the English under there if he shouted. I shook my head, which I think is close to universal, and his eyes went flat and careful in a way I did not like.

He reached out and took a fistful of my T-shirt at the shoulder, slow, feeling the cotton between his thumb and two fingers. Something in his face stalled. He looked back at one of the others and said a word, and that one came up and felt it too, and now there were two grown men with spears standing close enough to smell, woodsmoke and sweat and something sour under it, rubbing the hem of my sleeve and talking over my head about my shirt. The shirt was from a three-pack. It cost about six dollars. I wanted to tell them that and there was no version of me that could.

They tied my hands. Rough fiber that smelled of hay, wound twice around my wrists in front of me and knotted hard by the second man while the leader watched my face the whole time. I'd half braced for the cold of metal, because that was what my brain had filed under arrest, and the rope was worse, pinching in a thin line with no give in it. And the dumbest thing went through my head right then, that I knew a better knot than this one. I'd learned a trucker's hitch off a video to tie a kayak to the car. I knew a knot these men didn't, and it was the single most useless fact in the valley. Then they walked me down into the town.

The town didn't come at me in order. It came like a wave, all of it at once and over my head. The path turned into a rutted lane and the lane filled up with people the further in we went. The smell hit first and never let up, smoke and dung, more kinds of dung than I could sort out, a thick rotten thing underneath all of it, and over that something sharp and sour and fishy, like fish sauce left out in the sun. Heat came off the walls. The upper floors leaned in over the lane and there were faces in the dark doorways and faces in the windows above, and they came out to look, all of them, a kid with his hands tied being walked through by armed men. It was a normal enough thing that nobody was alarmed. They were only curious, and what they were curious about was me.

A woman pointed at my feet and said something to another woman, and they both stared at my boots. An old man on a step, shelling something into his lap, watched me go by for a long flat second and then turned his head and spat.

After the old man I kept my eyes down. The ground was its own education. Mud, straw, a dropped fish head, a flattened dead thing that had been a rat, a gutter down the center of the lane that I stepped over without anyone telling me to, because some things you just know. My good steel-toes were already the color of the lane to the ankle.

The building they put me in was stone at the base and brick above, and the cell was a room off a yard, low, one window high up and barred with real iron, a door of heavy planks bound in iron. So they had iron. They had iron for the door and rope for me, and I sat on the dirt floor and worked out what that meant, and the best I got was that I didn't rate iron. I rated rope. That was either good news or it meant they hadn't decided yet what I was, and I went back and forth on which.

They left my hands tied. The leader stood in the doorway after the others had gone, watching me, and I tried. I said "Daniel." I tapped my chest with my bound hands as best I could and said it slow. "Dan-iel." He watched my mouth. Then he said something, four or five words, none of which I had, and waited, and I had to shake my head again, and whatever patience had been in his face packed up and left. He pulled the door shut. A bar dropped on the outside with a heavy wooden clunk I felt through the floor.

I shook for a while in the half-dark. When it leveled off into something I could think over, I started counting. The window was about a foot square. The room was eight feet by ten, give or take, paced heel to toe with my wrists still tied, which is a stupid-looking way to measure a cell. The light from the window had crawled a hand's width down the far wall since they'd put me in. I could tell time off that wall if I had to. I didn't have to. I had a clock.

I'd been so careful not to think about it. The whole walk down I'd felt it against my thigh through the denim, aware of it the entire time like a sunburn, and now I was alone behind a barred door and I got it out, which is harder than it sounds with your wrists tied together, both hands working at it just to get it free of my pocket.

It woke up. Of course it woke up. Ninety-eight percent.

I almost called my mom. My thumb was already moving and I stopped my own hand, physically stopped it, because I'd watched the call die twice on the hill and it would die again, and I knew something now that I hadn't let myself know up there. The battery was the whole game. It was the only piece of home I had that still worked, and every dead-end call to a tower that wouldn't get built for nineteen hundred years burned a piece of it I'd never get back.

I turned the brightness all the way down. I turned off the radios. Airplane mode, in 98 AD. It should have been funny, and there was nobody to tell it to, and that was the first time the whole size of it landed on me, the joke just dying in my chest because there was no one there.

Then I opened Notes and started writing, fast, because that was scarier than the spears had been. I didn't organize it. I told myself I'd organize it later and later never really came. I wrote the date the phone thought it was, which was my world's date and wrong. I wrote ROME 98 AD?? with the question marks, because the only date I half trusted was the Colosseum and I trusted that about eighty percent.

Then I tried to write the things I was afraid of losing, and the thing that scared me was how fast that turned on me. I went for my mom's number. The area code came clean and the next three came clean and then the last four wouldn't line up. I tried them forward. I tried them backward. I got two versions that both felt almost right and wrote them both, with a question mark on the one I trusted less. I went for our address and got the house number and the street and the city and then stopped cold on the zip, five digits I had said out loud a thousand times mailing things, just gone, a blank where a number used to live. I wrote down Maya. I wrote Jess, and the meme she'd sent the night before that I never answered. I wrote Marcus, because this had his name all over it, the exact kind of stunt he'd spend two weeks on, except it wasn't, and I don't know why I wrote him down except that he was real and mine and nobody could take him off the glass.

I wrote down that the President was, and stopped, because who was I writing it for.

I was writing it for whoever found my body. I had to put the phone face down on my knee for a minute.

Then I picked it back up, because there was one more thing. I held it out at arm's length, both hands, front camera, and there I was, this kid, hair stuck to my forehead, dirt on me, eyes too wide, the gray barred window a smear behind my head. I looked at myself looking at myself and I took the picture, and the fake shutter clicked in the stone room, and now there was a record that a kid named Daniel Mercer had been alive and seventeen and scared in a cell outside Rome on a day that hadn't happened yet. Two photos on the phone now. The Colosseum and my face.

I held the button and watched it go black, which took a real effort, like letting go of somebody's hand, and slid it back into my pocket and pressed my palm flat against the shape of it through the denim.

The door opened again before I'd gotten anywhere with the next problem. A crack of light, then one of the earlier guards, not the leader, the second one, the one who'd felt my shirt. He had a small clay lamp and no spear, and he left the door swung wide behind him, which told me what he thought of my chances of running.

He said something and I did nothing. He came over and tapped my shoulder with one finger, the shirt again, a question. He pinched the hem and stretched it to see the weave and made a sound between a grunt and a question, and looked at the seam and then the small hole near the collar, and I watched him fail to work out how it had been made.

Then his eyes went to my hip, to the shape in my pocket. He pointed, a sharp jab, twice, and whatever he said it meant that. Show me that.

I thought fast about what I had and the answer was nothing. He was between me and the open door, in a city I couldn't read in a language I didn't have. I took the phone out.

He flinched back half a step, then held. I laid it flat on both palms, the screen dark, holding it out the way you hand something to a man at a border. He came in close and studied it without touching, then turned it over in my hands to see the back. He pressed the black glass with one finger. Nothing. He pressed harder, the press that's hunting for the lid, the opening, the catch. Nothing. He ran a thumbnail along the seam looking for a hinge, and found the volume buttons and pushed one, and nothing happened, because the phone was off and would stay off. Then he found the little metal pull on my jeans and touched it, and when the zipper slid down an inch he pulled his hand back and stared at it. He said something short to nobody, the sound that in any language means well, that's nothing, and handed the phone back to me. He pointed at the hole in my collar and said something that might have been what happened there, and I shrugged, and he made a sound like all right, barbarian, and left. He pulled the door most of the way shut, and a minute later somebody outside dropped the bar.

I sat still until I was sure nobody else was coming, then turned the phone on, ninety-six now, two percent gone while I'd sat there with the screen lit, and added one line at the bottom, small: keep this inside the shirt, not the pocket. Then off again.

They were going to come back. Whatever the leader had been asking me, somebody over him would ask it again, and the next time the asking probably wouldn't be polite. I had until then to turn into something worth keeping. I kept seeing the people from the walk down, the ones bent under loads, the ones with marks on them that nobody gave a second look, and that was the other side of worth keeping, and it was sitting right outside the door.

So what did I have. I lay on my back with my tied hands on my chest and went down the list, and the list kept coming apart in my hands. The phone, except I couldn't have told you what was inside it past a lot of tiny switches going on and off, and there was nothing in this world to build another out of, not for two thousand years. Electricity, except all I really had was a nail I'd wound with wire in eighth grade to pick up paper clips, and then a magnet from where, and wire from where, to light up what. Gunpowder, except it was three things and I could only name two, sulfur and charcoal and the white one, the one that mattered. My mouth had gone dry back on the march and it was past dry now, thick and cottony, and it kept dragging my head off the thought, so I'd lose the white one and grab for it and lose it again. Even if I had it, I didn't know how much, or where you dig it up, or how to keep from blowing my own hand off the first time I got it wrong. I'm bad at chemistry. I've always been bad at chemistry.

That was the whole problem, right there. I knew real things. I knew how a hundred things worked that nobody here had ever seen, and every one of them needed something I couldn't get, a material, a furnace, a word I couldn't say, ten years I didn't have tonight. None of it made me a wizard. I was just a kid who'd watched a lot of videos, holding a dead piece of glass on a dirt floor.

Then there were feet in the yard, more than one set, and voices, coming across the dirt toward my door, and they stopped. I was up against the wall before I knew I'd moved, my tied hands shoved over the lump of the phone, trying to make myself small and harmless. The bar scraped. My heart went so hard I could feel it in my teeth. Then somebody laughed and the feet moved off, and it hadn't been for me at all, and it landed on me that the next time they stopped outside my door it might be the real thing, and I still didn't have anything to be.

I went back to it with my hands still going, because I had to have something to be before those feet came back for real. What I actually had was smaller than all of that, small enough that I almost waved it off. I could write. Real writing, rows of English, and not one of them would read a word of it, and I honestly couldn't tell whether that helped me or buried me, because for all I knew it just looked like a kid dragging a stick through dirt, and a stranger who makes marks and stares and can't talk is a stranger you decide is simple, or a spy, or something you don't keep around. But it might read as letters to somebody who knew what letters were, and that would at least say I'd been taught. I could do numbers, fast, in my head, further than anybody was going to expect off a kid who fell out of a field. I could scratch a map of the whole world in the dirt, the parts they had and the parts they'd never heard of, and even if it came out nothing like the maps they drew, it was still a map, and a person who could draw the world had to have come from somewhere worth coming from. And I could point at my own chest and say my name, slow, until somebody said it back. None of it was magic. None of it stopped a spear. But it might be enough to make somebody hold off and keep me alive until they worked out what I was.

Somewhere. That was the other half of it, and it mattered more than the numbers did. Not the truth, ever. The truth was the one thing I couldn't say and couldn't show. I'm from the future. There was no way to prove it and every way to die for it. To these people, who I was pretty sure read tomorrow out of birds and the insides of animals, a thing in my pocket that lit up and always knew the hour was witchcraft, plain and simple, the kind of thing they burned people for, or fed to whatever lived in the round building. So I'd be from far off instead. Up north, past where their maps gave out, some cold place full of men in strange cloth who knew their letters and their numbers. I didn't have the name of the place, or half of how I'd sell it. I had the spine of it and not much else. Never the truth. Never the future. A man from far away, worth keeping alive.

By the time I got that far, my body had quit caring about any of it. The thirst was a real ache now, and the hunger had come up under it like a fist closing slow, and I hadn't eaten since a night in a world that didn't exist anymore, and the needing-to-pee turned up on top of all of it, my body going about its business like nothing was wrong with the rest of it. There was a clay pot in the corner I'd been ignoring, and I worked out, with great dignity, what the clay pot was for.

The light on the wall went orange, then gray, then it was only the window, a paler square in the dark, and I sat against the cold stone with my knees up and my tied hands hanging off them.

The bar lifted near full dark. A man came in with a clay lamp and a bowl. He was younger than the others, or used harder in a different way, built like a soldier, no wasted motion in him, and he had a strip of something dark on his shoulder, leather or braid, that the spear-carriers hadn't had, which I figured meant he was over them somehow. He set the lamp in a niche and crouched in front of me on the balls of his feet and considered my wrists and then my face, the look you give a horse somebody's told you to judge. He held out the bowl.

It was warm. Grain mush, gray, the sour sharp reek of the fish sauce cut through it, and in that moment it was the most beautiful object anybody had ever handed me. I went to take it and couldn't, my hands tied, and made a fumbling useless grab, and he caught the bowl before it spilled, fast, no fuss. He looked at my wrists, then at my face, and worked something out.

He took a short knife off his belt. I went very still. He turned it and put the edge to the rope between my wrists, not my skin, and sawed through it in two strokes, and the rope dropped away and my hands came apart and the blood went back into them in a hot pins-and-needles rush that made me hiss.

He put the bowl in my hands. He stood, picked up the lamp, and at the door he stopped and pointed at his own chest with two fingers and said a word, slow and clear, and said it again. Two syllables, the second one falling. Then he tipped his head at me and waited, and I understood, and I tapped my chest with a hand that didn't fully work yet and said, "Daniel," and he tried it back, mangled, the middle of it gone soft and wrong, and something in his mouth almost moved toward a smile and didn't get there.

He took the lamp with him. The bar dropped. I sat in the dark with the warm bowl in both my freed hands and ate the worst food of my life with my fingers, fast, scraping the clay, and I licked the bowl after.
