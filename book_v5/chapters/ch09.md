# Chapter 9: Macer

They walked me across the city with the bag still folded under my arm.

The two broad men kept exactly one pace behind me and a half-pace to either side, so I walked at the point of a little triangle pretending not to be one. Heras walked at my shoulder and said nothing, which from Heras was a whole speech. The steward went ahead, and I followed the back of his neck up out of the poor lanes along a street that climbed, where the fronts got quieter and the doors got heavier and the gutters stopped stinking quite so personally.

The door was oak, studded. A slave opened it before the steward touched it - had been standing behind it all morning with his ear to the wood, waiting. In this house people stood behind doors so other people would never have to wait at them.

Inside it was cool and dim and smelled of beeswax and lamp soot and leather. A lot of leather - the smell of the place a saddle comes from. A floor of small black and white stones, a square of noon light falling through the roof into a shallow basin of water, and then the steward stopped and put his eyes on me with a meaning I could read in any language. Stand here. Do not speak first.

The room was full of business. I had pictured, on the walk, some kind of throne arrangement, a man who had shaped his day around my arrival. There was no such arrangement. Along one side two clerks sat on stools with tablets on their knees, styluses going, a man in a traveling cloak reciting numbers that another man wrote down, a slave crossing the bright square of floor with a tray and crossing back without it, all of it moving around one chair.

The man in the chair was not looking at me. He was looking at the tablet the slave had brought him, holding it out at arm's length, squinting a little, the posture of a man whose eyes have started to go and will die before he admits it. He was big through the shoulders and going soft over it, gray cropped close, a face that had been out in weather for a lot of years and had the broken veins to show it. He wore a plain tunic, good wool, no stripe, because an equestrian wears a narrow stripe and this one could not be bothered. A ring on his hand, gold, heavy, a smear of dried something on his thumb that might have been ink or blood, and either way he had not washed it off.

He read the tablet. He did not hurry. He let me stand there with my burned hands and my folded ruined balloon while the clerks scratched, and I understood that this was the first lesson, before a word: his time was worth money and mine was worth whatever he decided to pay for it, and that gap was the entire room.

Then he handed the tablet back without looking at the slave who took it, and turned his attention to me. Professional. The same appraisal the steward had given the bag in the lot - older at it, wider. He moved from the cloth under my arm to my boots, machine-stitched, soled in a black stuff no cobbler could make, and his eyes stopped on them. They always stopped on them. Then my face. Then he said something to the steward sideways, not bothering to lower his voice because I was furniture, and the only word I caught was a number. The steward answered low. The man in the chair grunted.

"You," he said, and now it was to me, and his Latin came slow and loud and a little too clearly, with very careful spacing, for a child or a dog or a foreigner he suspected of being stupid. "You are the one who burned down a quarter of the Subura this morning."

I knew enough to know what he was doing and not enough to do anything clever back. "No one burned," I said. My Latin came out badly, as it always did under pressure, the cases sliding, the v turning to w in my mouth before I could stop it. "One tunic. One roof. No man."

"One roof." He turned a hand over. The ring caught the light. "Do you know what a roof costs, in the Subura, when a foreigner sets fire to it in front of four hundred witnesses and a priest?"

"No."

"No." Something moved at the corner of his mouth, a man tasting a sum he was going to enjoy. "Neither do they. By tomorrow the roof will have been a whole house, and the day after that it will have been three, and a child in it, and the foreigner will have called the fire down with a word. You have made me famous before noon. Two men were at my door this morning wanting to know what I keep in my workshop, and one of them was a senator's freedman, which is worse than a senator." He shifted his weight. The chair complained. "I bought you for less than I pay for a season's mules. Did you know that?"

"No," I said again, because it was true.

He set the tablet aside and picked up instead a wooden disc - heavy, flat, a craftsman's gauge for checking a wheel's roundness, sitting there in the table's clutter. He turned it against his palm, inspecting the rim, while he looked at a water-stain on the far wall. He was not done.

"A scholar from the edge of the world," he said, still working the disc. "Draws maps. Knows the new figures. Heras told my steward there was a madman in a country jail who could divide numbers in his head, and for the price of a mule I could find out whether he was a marvel or a lunatic, and either way it would be cheap." He glanced at Heras, money owed in both directions. "He did not mention you would cost me a roof."

Heras spoke for the first time, dry. "He did not mention it because it had not happened. I sold you the sum: a strange foreigner, cheap, possibly worth nothing, possibly worth a great deal, and a year to find out which. The year is up. You found out which this morning, in front of four hundred witnesses and a priest."

The clerks had stopped scratching. I understood, with the back of my neck, that the room had been arranged to hear this.

The man in the chair set the wooden disc back on the table. A silence.

"Sit him down," he said, and a stool appeared under me, pushed by a girl I had not seen come in, and I sat because my knees were glad of it. "Not you," he said to Heras. "You I trust as far as I can throw a Greek, which is exactly as far as you have been useful. Stand there and be useful." Heras inclined his head a fraction, accepting a compliment that had a knife folded inside it.

The steward stepped forward and said, formally: "Titus Flavius Macer. Equestrian. Principal contractor, leather and timber, to the legions in Dacia, among others. You are in his house."

Macer's expression said nothing about that.

"Here is where we are, Thulean." The first time he used it. He liked it - I could hear that he liked it. "This morning you stopped being a thing I forgot I owned. That is bad for you. When you were a madman who drew maps, you cost me a mule and nobody knew your name, and that was the safest you will ever be in your life, and it is over. Now you are the foreigner who throws fire at the sky in front of a crowd and a priest reads it. A senator's man was at my door. Do you know what happens to a cheap oddity that a senator wants and an equestrian owns?"

I did not know, and he could see I did not know. "Nothing good," he said, "for the oddity." And he gave one short bark of genuine amusement, and it was the first human sound anyone in that room had made all day, and I hated that some animal part of me, after a year of being a sum, was glad to be laughed at like a person.

Then he got to the point. The point was a knife dressed as a gift.

"So." He leaned forward, forearms on his knees, and the chair eased. "You are going to stop costing me roofs and start making me money, and I am going to keep you alive while you do it, which this morning became a thing that requires keeping. You follow?"

"I follow some," I said. "Good," he said. "Then follow this." He picked up the disc again. With one hand he turned it, rim to rim, inspecting it while the other hand went up and began folding fingers one at a time, a contractor counting a tally. His eyes stayed on the disc.

"You want cloth. You will have cloth, by the bolt, the close-woven, as much as you ruin, and you ruin a great deal." First finger down; he tilted the disc and ran his thumb along its edge. "You want hands. Two slaves, mine, to fetch and stitch and not to run, and if you teach them anything that costs me money I will sell the cleverer of them." Second finger, the disc set flat now on his knee, picked up again. The floor tilted very slightly under me and I kept my face still because Heras was watching me keep my face still.

Macer glanced up from the disc. "Still with me." I said yes, and he went back to the rim. "You want a place. There is a yard I hold off the timber wharf, a real yard, with a wall and a gate I can lock, where you can burn what you like and the wall is between you and the next senator's freedman." Third finger. He set the disc flat on his knee, picked it up again. "You want metal, wood, a forge - it all goes on a tablet to him" - the steward - "and he decides if you are lying about the price, and you will be, everyone lies about the price, I built a fortune on it." Fourth finger.

He stopped there and tapped the disc's center once with a knuckle.

"And you want money." He set the disc down on the arm of his chair, laid his hands flat on his knees, and looked at me directly for the first time since I had sat. "There will be a fund. A peculium." He said the word slowly so I would have it. "It is not yours. A clever master lets a clever slave keep a little jar because a man guarding his own jar guards the master's whole house and never notices he is doing it. I am telling you this to your face." He leaned back. The disc slid off the chair arm and he caught it without looking, one-handed. "You will take it anyway, because the other thing on the table is a senator and a priest and a crowd that this morning said the word witch out loud, and you have, Thulean, no other door in this whole city but mine."

He had said it plainly, measuring out the hole's dimensions so I would stop thrashing. I sat there with the bag in my lap and did the only thing I had, which was think. A sum has two sides. He had spent the morning telling me what I cost him - the mule, the roof, the senator - and the hardware-store part of me had been keeping tally on the other side. Men at his door before noon. A senator's freedman. He had not walked me here under guard for something he planned to throw away. You do not put a wall around a mule.

I played the one card I had. "The wall," I said. My Latin came slow and I let it. "The wall is good. The hands are good." I looked at the folded gray cloth in my lap. "The flight this morning - it went up. You saw. Everyone saw. It came down because I can't - because the heat. I can't keep the heat in." I stopped there. Started over. "I know why it came down. I've known for a year." I made myself stop again, in a pause I had learned from them. "No one else in Rome knows why."

The room was very quiet, and something shifted in Macer's face. Sharper now. A man who has just heard the mule talk and is recalculating the mule.

"There it is," he said softly, almost to himself. Then, to the steward - not bothering that I could hear: "He knows what he is. That is the dangerous kind." Back to me, louder: "So that is your little knife. The only one who knows how. You think I have not bought a hundred men who were the only ones who knew how? The only one who knows how dies of a fever and takes the how with him, Thulean, that is what the only one who knows how does." He let that land, cold. "Which is exactly why I am going to give you the hands. So that one fever does not cost me my balloon." He said it like it was already in a ledger column with his name at the top of it.

It went through me anyway - the first time anyone but me had put a name to it, in his mean mouth, as a possession.

He reached back, picked up the wooden disc, looked at it one more time. Then he put it down on the table with a click, done with it, and folded both hands on his knees.

"You will teach the hands," he said. "Some of it. Enough that a fever does not ruin me, not so much that I do not need you, and you will guess wrong about which is which and lose either way. You keep me needing you. I keep you alive. The day you teach too little, your balloon dies with you, and I do not forgive a roof." He sat back. "I find I like you. Do not let that comfort you. I have liked mules."

I did not understand all the words. The logic was plain enough.

"Free," I said. I should not have. It came out of the part of me that was still eighteen and stupid and a year homesick. "If - if it makes you money. If I teach them and the thing flies and you - yeah. Free. That's all I'm saying."

The steward's stylus stopped. Heras, beside me, went very still - a man watching another man step toward an edge.

Macer looked at me for a long moment the way you look at a door with no handle. Then he let out a single hard breath through his nose - not quite a laugh, not quite a curse, somewhere between them, low and blunt and final. "Jupiter's balls," he said, very quietly, at no one in particular. Then his face settled back into something that might have been patience if patience had teeth.

"Free," he said, and tasted it, and let it go. He looked at Heras. "You taught him the word?" Heras said nothing. Macer turned the heavy ring on his thumb, slow, once around. "It is not mine to give. Freedom worth anything in this city is a citizen's freedom, and citizens are made by men a great deal higher than me. I can keep you. I can feed you and wall you and put a man on your gate." He let the ring settle. "I cannot make you a Roman. We are both reaching past our hands this morning. The difference is I know exactly how far mine reach. Learn that and you might live a while."

A beat, watching me - it had gone in. "Now get him out of my floor," he said to the steward, already reaching for the next tablet, the room closing over the place where I had been. "And feed him. He looks like something I bought cheap."

The girl led me out.

She had been at the edge of the room the whole time - the one who had pushed the stool under me - and I had not looked at her because you learn fast in that house not to look at the furniture. She was small, fourteen maybe, in a plain undyed tunic, her hair cut short and uneven, and she moved as the slave behind the door had moved - quiet, ready, gone before you could thank her - except that her eyes were not quiet at all. She had stood there fetching stools and taken in every move on the board: the mule, the roof, the wall, the little jar, free, and filed all of it behind a face that gave nothing back. When she caught me seeing her see it, she dropped her eyes fast, automatic, a year of practice in it.

"Tyche," the steward said, in the flat register of naming a tool. "Yours. Fetch and carry. She does not run." He said it to me, but he was looking at her when he said the last part, and she was looking at the floor, and I understood the sentence was a leash with two ends and I was holding one of them now whether I had asked for it or not.

She brought me water in the corridor, in a plain cup, and bread, because the master had said feed him and in this house what the master said became true at once in the body of a girl. My hands were shaking. She watched them shake with the same attention she had given everything, and said nothing. Two pieces of this man's property in a cool corridor that smelled of leather. I drank, watered wine, faintly sour, the best thing I had tasted in a year, and I hated that too.

"Thank you," I said. In Latin. It came out wrong, the case wrong, always wrong.

She looked at me then, full on - the same measuring look the master had given me, the look this whole city gave a thing to find out what it was worth. Except she was fourteen and a slave and had no money to back it with. Just the looking, naked, the only thing she owned. Whatever she decided she kept. She took the cup back before I dropped it, turned it once to check the rim, and tucked it against her side.

"The yard is by the wharf," she said. Her Latin was quick and low and clean, a city child's, and she did not slow it down for me as Macer had, which I took, stupidly, as the only respect I had been paid all day. "Long walk. You should eat the bread while you walk. He won't give you time to eat it there."

Then she went ahead of me toward the oak door, carrying the cup that was not hers in a house that was not hers, and I followed her out into the noon light with the bread in one hand and my burned balloon folded under the other arm.
