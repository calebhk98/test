# Chapter 32: Proclaimed at Antioch

I heard about it from a man selling fish.

It was the cold end of winter, the wharf greasy with a thaw that had no warmth in it, and I had gone down for no better reason than that the press had run out of ink-stone and I wanted to walk while it was fetched. There is a fish-seller near the Aemilian bridge who keeps his prices on a slate in the new figures, has done for years, one of the first men in Rome to do it without my asking him to, and I stop there sometimes to look at his slate. He was not minding it that morning. He was telling the man beside him, across a tray of greying mullet, that the new one was giving it all back.

"All of it," he said. "Armenia. The river country. The lot. Pulling the legions home off the far side of everything Caesar took, and the men out there are to march back and pretend they were never there."

The other man said something about the cost of getting there, the men buried getting there, and the fish-seller said the new one had a different idea of what a campaign was worth, and went back to his fish. I stood with my hands on the cold edge of his stall and could not for a moment make my fingers let go of it.

I had heard a great many things since I came home. The city ran on talk that winter, and most of it turned the wheel and ground nothing. A fish-seller is a fish-seller, not a dispatch. But he had the names in the right order, and the right scorn, and a man at a fish-stall does not invent Armenia.

Then from somewhere behind me, down along the dock-pilings where two soldiers were watching a grain barge warp in, one of them said it low, in the clipped way men state the uncomfortable calculation: that Plotina had signed the adoption papers after the old man was cold, that Attianus had been convenient with the seal, that the new one's claim had a woman's hand on it before it had anyone else's. The other soldier did not answer. They watched the barge. I bought a fish I did not want and carried it back up to the yard and did not taste it.

---

What the fish-seller had I got better the next day, and worse, from men who knew. It came in pieces all that spring, arriving through the mouths of soldiers and the letters of frightened men, and the pieces did not flatter the man who had ordered it. He was abandoning the east. He was giving it back - a city forgives a young emperor a bad year, the gods send misfortune, but giving was different from losing. The conquests the old man had spent his last strength taking, the cities, the rivers with the unpronounceable names, the new province they had cut their coins to celebrate before the ink on the dispatches was dry, the boy was handing back across the line to the Parthians as a man hands back a tool he has decided he never needed. The border was to come home to the river it had always been before the old man pushed it. The order was to be done quietly and quickly and as if it were obvious, which was the part that turned the conquest men purple, because to do a thing quietly is to say it was never worth shouting about, and they had shouted about it for four years.

I went down to the Forum to feel the size of it.

You could feel it. The men who had made their careers carrying Caesar east stood about in their good wool with the particular stillness of men who have been told the road they walked is to be unbuilt behind them. A triumph had been voted, was still owed, would still be held, for a war whose ground was at that moment being given away, and nobody could decide whether to celebrate the taking or mourn the leaving, so they did something in between that looked like indigestion. I heard a senator's freedman, a sleek one I half knew, say to another that it was sound generalship, that the old man had reached past his arm and the new one was only drawing the arm back to the body where it could be defended, and the words were reasonable and the man saying them had the face of someone reciting a line he had been handed that morning and did not yet dare disbelieve. The reasonable men said it was reasonable. The army men said nothing in the Forum and a great deal in the wine shops, where I did not go, because a freedman with a Trajan name drinking in an army wine shop the spring the army was being told its war was a mistake is a man writing his own line in a clerk's roll.

I had said it myself. That was the weight I could not put down, like the cold edge of the fish-stall. Two winters back, sitting in a tent behind Antioch with a friend's fever-smell still in my clothes, I had thought, cold and clear, that somebody was going to have to let the conquered ground go, that the east was a dead hand closing on sand, that no emperor with sense would keep paying men into that country to hold cities that would not stay knelt. I had thought it with a shame I could not account for, because thinking a true thing should not shame you, and yet. And now a man I had never seen, with no idea I existed, sat down at the start of his reign and did the exact arithmetic I had done in the dark and reached the exact total and acted on it with a stroke of a pen. He was right, and I knew he was right, and I wanted to be sick.

Because Celer had died on a road in that country. He had taken a thrust below the ribs holding a supply road open to a province, and the wound had gone bad on the way to me, and I had cleaned it with my own hands and packed it and posca'd it and dressed it and he had died anyway, before dawn, with no last word, while I held the cup. He had held a road to a place. And the place was now to be given back, the road abandoned, the province struck off, as if the line had never been drawn there, as if a man at a desk had rubbed it out with his thumb. He was right to do it. The road went nowhere worth a man's life. I had known that road went nowhere before the boy was ever told he was emperor. None of that put one breath back in Celer, and all of it made his death a loss the empire had now officially decided it had not needed.

I wore the strap at my shoulder, where my hand found it. I worked the buckle with my thumb, the same motion Celer had used on his own kit when he was thinking something out, and I did not say anything to anyone, and I went home.

---

Heras I went to because he was Greek, and the new emperor, by every account that reached the wharf, loved Greek things with a fervor the old man had reserved for roads. He was past sixty now, sixty-four or five, dried down as men dry rather than soften, his hands as clean as they had been the day he first cut my rope of an argument out from under me in a cell. He had a fire going small and a cup of something heated, and he let me sit a long time before he made me talk, which is a courtesy he extends to no one he does not love and would die before admitting.

"So," he said at last. "You have heard. The barbarian gives the east away, and the Greek-lover sits in the city your old man built, and you do not know which way to stand. You have the face of a man doing sums that will not come out even."

"Tell me about him."

"I have never met him." He turned the cup. "I am from Pergamon, Daniel, not from his bedchamber. What I have is what every Greek in this city has, which is gossip and pride, and we Greeks dress our gossip in better cloth than your Romans do, but it is the same washing under the cloth." He drank. "Still. You want it, you may have it. He is a reader. The old man read what a contract required and no more, and was honest about it, and built things you could walk on. This one reads for the pleasure of it. He composes. He has opinions about poets dead three hundred years and he will tell you the wrong opinion is an offense against him personally. They say he speaks Greek better than half the men in Athens and is prouder of it than any man in Athens would be, because a man born to a thing wears it loose and a man who climbed to it wears it buttoned to the throat."

"That sounds like a small concern."

"You did not let me finish." He held up a clean finger. "The poet is the part the dinner tables love. Under it there is a different animal. He has soldiered, hard, on the cold rivers, and the men who soldiered with him do not call him soft, they call him exact. He misses nothing and he forgives the man who made him notice the miss least of all. You are thinking, a philosopher, a man of books, this is a man I can talk to. Daniel. The most dangerous master a clever man ever serves is the one clever enough to know exactly how clever the servant is being." He set the cup down. "The old soldier could be flattered, because a flattered general thinks the flatterer a fool he can use. This one cannot be flattered, because he has read every flattery ever written and graded them. You will have to be plainer with him than you have ever been with anyone, and plain is the one thing you have never once been since I have known you."

I let that go by. There was no profit in answering it.

"He gave the east away," I said. "You think that's sense or weakness."

"I think," Heras said, "that he looked at a map and saw what it cost to hold and what it returned, and decided to keep the part that pays. Which is sense. And I think a Roman of the old conquering kind cannot tell the difference between sense and weakness, because to him the holding is the point and the cost is glory, and a man who counts the cost out loud has insulted the dead." He paused, and when he continued his voice shifted slightly, not warmer, but different - the way a physician shifts when he stops talking about a case and starts talking about a body. "There is one other thing. He is meticulous about his own health. He has read the medical texts. He arrives at a physician's door having already decided what is wrong with him and what the treatment should be, and the physician's job becomes confirming the diagnosis he has already reached." He picked up the cup again. "I mention it because a man who will not be corrected by his physician will not be corrected by his engineers either. He will listen to everything. He will act on his own reading. When you show him the work, make sure the work says what you need it to say before he decides what it says."

He looked at me, and there was the old cut in it, the skeptic's blade he had never let dull. "It is what you have been telling this city for near twenty years, you understand. Count the cost. Do the thing that pays. You taught a fish-seller to keep his prices in marks that cannot be cheated and you taught a quartermaster to weigh men against water. And now there is an emperor who counts the cost of an empire the same way he counts a supply wagon, and you sit in my room looking as though the gods had played a joke on you. Perhaps they have. It is the joke they specialize in. They give you the thing you asked for and they wait to see your face."

He was not wrong, and I had not come to be told he was not wrong. But there was a use in it, sharp under the salt. A man who counts. A man who will keep the part that pays and let the rest go without a tear for the men who took it.

I sat with that a while, turning Celer's buckle, and Heras let me.

"He won't want to expand," I said, mostly to myself, and Heras answered immediately. "He has just told the world he won't, at the cost of looking like a coward to every general the old man left him. A man does not pay that price to lie. No. He wants to pull the arms in to the body and lock the doors and walk the rooms of what he has and put them in order. He is a man, I think, who would rather have a small house perfectly kept than a great one half built and leaking." Heras picked the cup up again. "Why does that frighten you more than the giving-away did? You have gone a color."

I had not meant to go a color. I got up and put a log on his small fire that did not need a log, to have my back to him a moment.

Everything I had built and shown and sold and frightened men with was a tool for the great half-built leaking house. The eye in the sky was for scouting an enemy you meant to march on. The bursting tube was an argument for a weapon you meant to point at a wall you meant to take. The figures that fed a quartermaster fed an army that was going somewhere. Every skill I had made myself into in this city was a man who made the reaching out cheaper and surer and more terrible. I had sold Rome a better way to push.

And the man on the road did not want to push. He wanted to pull in. Near twenty years, and I had built myself for the wrong emperor.

"Daniel," Heras said behind me. "The fire is lit. Sit down before you set the roof on it, as is your custom."

---

I did not say any of that to Heras. I said it, in the end, to Marcia, because Marcia is the only person I have ever been able to say the cold things to without dressing them, she having no patience for cloth of any kind. She heard me out in the tally-room with the lying clock dripping, and when I had finished, she did not waste a breath comforting me.

"Then half of what you are is worth nothing to him," she said. "Worse than nothing. It's a smell." She had her tablet out and wrote a short column on it, working the problem by making it a list. "Say it plain. Who is Marcus Ulpius Danihel. He is the dead emperor's marvel. He wears the dead emperor's name. He flew the dead emperor's wars and fed the dead emperor's wars and made the dead emperor a weapon that burst and threw a stone across a river for the dead emperor's officers to clap at. Every line of it has the dead man's name on it. You came to this city as the old man's wonder and you have spent your favor being useful to the kind of empire this new one has just told the world he does not want."

I said yes, and she moved on without pausing. "A new prefect drawing a line through the rolls, looking for the old regime's men, will not have to look hard for you. You're at the top of the page in large letters." She set the stylus down and touched the wall, the old habit, as if checking the plaster held. "So. We are not going to sell him a balloon."

It was the first sentence anyone had said in a month with a forward in it instead of a behind, and I straightened up at it. She did not wait for me to answer. "Four things. Clean water in a city is worth more to a man who means to stay home and govern it than a flying basket is to a man who never means to march again. The figures that cannot be cheated are worth more to a treasury settling thirty years of war debt than to a quartermaster on the move. The press - a man who reads, who composes, who would rather have a small house perfectly kept. Daniel. You have spent five years making it cheaper to put a true thing on a page fifty times over and you have been selling it to schoolmasters for an as. And the fourth: we make your name worth keeping for what it does for his house, not for what it did for the dead one's wars." She set the stylus against the tablet's edge. "We have a year, near enough, before he walks through the gate. A year is enough."

"He owes me nothing," I said. "The old man owed me. He'd seen me drink foul water to prove a filter. This one has never heard my name except as a mark in a roll he wants to be rid of."

"Then we start from that." She did not look up when she added the next part, which was her mercy. "And we stop sitting on a roof being sad about a soldier, which is what you have been doing for five weeks. You've worn that buckle to a shine."

I looked at her list. Four lines. The first map of any of this I had been given, drawn cold by a woman who had never been east of Ostia, and it was righter than anything I had managed since the sea.

There was also news from the Hispanian works - a letter from the overseer, the first service-fee figure on a pump running without me - and I folded it and did not show it to anyone. The bronze tube work had paid off in a fixed emplacement out past the quarry flats, a short thick-walled piece that broke a test wall cleanly and held together under the shot, which was the thing the bursting tube had argued was possible and the thing near twenty years of boring prizes had been working toward. Something ran through me when I read it, cold and sharp, the feel of a thing you have been walking toward in the dark finally putting up a wall under your hand. The thing I had been building toward for eleven years was real, and what it meant for walls and sieges had changed, and the man now reading dispatches in the east would eventually hear of it, and when he did I wanted to already be the man he found useful rather than the man he found suspicious.

There was one matter I did not raise with Marcia. I had carried it home from the supply camps, half-formed and recoiled from, the two badly-drawn continents I had hung across a blank ocean on a magistrate's wax near twenty years gone. There was a version of me that wanted to take the empire's whole bottomless reaching and turn it the one direction it had never reached, out west, across the water, away from the people it ground up in the east. I had told it to no one. I had nearly told it to Celer and the chance had died with him. Sitting in the tally-room with Marcia's four cold lines in front of me, I turned it over once more and set it down again, because her argument and Heras's animal both pointed at a man who wanted to pull his arms in to his body. You do not sell a man locking his doors a map to a country a year's sail past the edge of the sea. Wrong gift, wrong emperor. It fit nothing he had told the world he was. I put it back where I kept it, under the floor of my head with the dead glass and the ratios, a direction I had no door for yet.

"What's the year for," I said.

"To make you the kind of man," Marcia said, "that a careful emperor keeps because dropping him would cost more than feeding him. We have a press, an enemy who's been waiting for the lock to change, a senator who doesn't know whose man he is, and a college that holds a door into everything we own. And we have an emperor coming who counts. So we are going to do the one thing this house has always done. We are going to be the cheapest way to keep something that the man with the money has already decided he wants to keep." She stood. "Eat. The boy asked for you twice today. He's decided you're his because you let him hold the strap. Don't undecide it."

---

Lucanus had the buckle when I came out, sitting on the floor of the press room turning it, learning my one nervous trick off me without either of us teaching it. He held it up when he saw me, not to give it back, to show me he had it, to make me part of having it. He was seven now, or near enough, and had not recognized me when I came back from the east, and had been finding his way back to me since, one strap and one question at a time.

"Whose was it," he said. He had learned the question off the grown people that month. It was the question the whole city was asking at every door.

"A soldier's," I said. "A friend of mine."

He thought about that with Marcia's square weighing face. "Is he coming," he said. Meaning, I think, the friend.

I sat down on the floor of the press room beside him, in the ink smell, and I told him no, the soldier was not coming, but somebody was, and I did not yet know what to make for the one who was. He was seven and turning a dead man's buckle in the lamplight, asking when.
