# Chapter 53: The Far Whisper

They had been out of sight of any known land for thirty-one days. The boy whose work it was to read the box read it now in the gray before sunrise, on his knees on the heaving deck with the lamp shielded in the crook of his arm.

The box was bolted to a cradle that swung in two rings, so that the deck could pitch and roll and the thing inside stayed level, or near enough. Inside it a coiled brass spring, no bigger than a closed fist, drove the works through a gate, and behind the gate a bar swung in its own slow time and let the wheel down a tooth at a stroke. A small dry knock, and a wait, and a small dry knock. He had been taught that the bar did not care that the sea heaved, that this was the whole secret and the cost, the device men had drowned for the want of for longer than the shipwrights could say. He could not have explained why it was true. He only had to read it. He read the count of strokes against the wheel of hours behind the little window and set it down on a slate in the figures every counting-house in the world now used, the figures with the round mark for nothing, and carried the slate up to the pilot.

The pilot took the sun later, when there was a sun, with a graded brass quarter-circle and a smoked glass, and read the height of it as her teacher had, and his before him. That gave her how far north. The box gave her how far west, by the hour the sun stood high here set against the hour it would have stood high over the yard they had sailed from, which the box still kept, patient, all these thirty-one days, gaining a little on the cold mornings and losing it back in the wet, so little that across a month it came to almost nothing, which was the work of lifetimes. She marked the two together on the chart and put her finger on the place where they crossed, in the white part, where no coast was drawn.

There was a coast under her finger by noon.

It came up first as a smell, before the lookout called it: leaf-rot and wet earth and woodsmoke, a green smell off the water that the sea had never offered them. Then a low blue line that hardened into hills, dark with forest down to a pale margin of beach. The pilot put the lead over the side herself, the tallowed lead on its knotted line, and called the soundings down, twenty, then fifteen, then nine, the bottom shoaling, gray sand coming up in the tallow. She brought the ship round to stand off in deep water and held her there, sails backed, the whole crew at the rail and not one of them speaking, looking at the land they had crossed a year of dead reckoning and one patient knocking box to reach.

On the beach there was a fire.

A made fire, banked and tended, a thread of smoke standing up straight in the still air, and near it boats drawn up on the sand, long dark boats, and figures who had stopped whatever they were doing and stood now at the water's edge and looked back at the ship the way the ship looked at them. One of them lifted an arm. Whether to call others or to warn them off or to wave, no one aboard could say. The hills behind them had more smoke in three more places, far up the green, the smoke of people who had been living there a long time.

The pilot lowered the lead and did not give the order to make for the beach. She held the ship off in the deep, in the gray sand soundings, and looked at the fire, and at the boats, and at the figure with its arm up. She had sailed a year to a far edge of a world with its own fires lit, its own boats, its own silence looking back at her.

Below, the log was a printed book with blank leaves bound in, the kind the presses turned out by the thousand now. In the front of it, as in all these logs, were the old tables a pilot needed, the star-heights and the tide-reckonings and the rule of the box, copied and recopied through so many hands that the language had worn smooth. At the foot of the rule of the box there was a name, or the wear of one, the place where a name had stood at the bottom of the oldest copy and been carried down half-legible into every copy since. No one read it as a man. It was just the words that ended the page, the way *finis* ends a page, a sound with no person behind it. The pilot went down and her eye went over it without stopping, as it had a thousand times, and she dipped her pen and turned to the white leaf at the back and began to write down where they were.

She wrote the soundings, the bearing, the height of the sun. She wrote the fire on the beach and the boats and the figures and the three more smokes up the green hills, plainly, because it was true and the next ship would need to know it. She did not write what it meant. She did not know what it meant.

When she had set it all down she went back up into the light. The crew were still at the rail. The figures on the beach were still there, more of them now, and the fire still stood its thread of smoke into the windless afternoon, and beyond the beach the unknown country went up green and dark and enormous into a sky she would have to learn new stars in to cross again.

She stood at the rail among the others and looked west at the shore, the way you look at a thing you have only ever drawn, and behind her, bolted level in its swinging rings below the deck, the box kept its small dry knock against the silence, a step and a wait and a step, even and even, telling the hour of a yard a year behind them to a coast that had never had an hour told to it before.
