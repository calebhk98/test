## SECTION 1: The Premise and Why It Matters

Daniel has been performing a careful, limited version of himself for everyone else for his
entire life in Rome. Heras guessed at the depth of his knowledge and accepted not-knowing
as the price of the friendship. Macer never cared about origins, only results. Tyche knows
about the English cipher and has learned it as a keeper's tool — but she knows the *shape*
of what Daniel carries, not the full substance. Marcia knows he is unusual in ways she has
chosen not to examine directly. None of them know the complete thing: where he is from,
what the world he came from was like, how different the future is from everything they
imagine.

His children are different. Both of them. He gives them everything.

This is not a decision Daniel makes explicitly. It is what happens when a man who thinks
in English raises children. He talks to them in English without deciding to. He reads to
them in English because the stories in his head are in English. He tells them what comes
up — what he's thinking about, what he's working on, what he half-remembers from a world
they will never see. By the time either of them is old enough to ask "Father, where did
you learn that?", the answer is already half-obvious. They have been accumulating evidence
for years before he says it directly.

**The critical correction this document makes:** Both children get the full truth, the full
English education, the full catalog of what Daniel knows. Lucanus is not kept in the dark.
Daniel raises both of them as much like twenty-first-century children as he can manage.
Lucanus ends up choosing law. That is his own path, made with full information and with
everything Daniel had to give him. The fact that he walks away from the workshop and the
encyclopedia is a statement about who Lucanus is — not about what Daniel withheld.

This is also Daniel's one act of pure generosity with knowledge. The cipher's two-at-a-time
pacing, the tech program's careful sequencing, the encyclopedia's hooked marks distinguishing
known from guessed — all of it is measured, all of it is for later. With his children, he
gives everything. Not because he thinks they can use it or that it's safe. Because they are
his children and the loneliness is real and they are the only people in the world he can be
himself with.

This is not a subplot. It is the most intimate set of relationships in the book.

The arc does not produce a plot event. Neither child changes Rome using what they know.
Ulpia keeps the secret, holds the encyclopedia, and becomes his interlocutor and
collaborator. Lucanus carries what Daniel gave him into the courts, where legal arguments
with Daniel's fingerprints on them — concepts like presumption of innocence, evidence
standards, proportionality — circulate without anyone knowing their origin. The arc's
payoff is quieter than event: a father and his children, in a garden or a study, talking
in a private language about the shape of the universe. Most of what they say to each
other never makes it to the page.

## SECTION 2: Phase Timeline — What He Teaches and When

### Lucanus, Age 0–5 (~108–113 AD): The Language, the Early Years

English from birth, simultaneously with Latin. Daniel is ~27 when Lucanus arrives. He is
not yet rich, not yet settled, still finding his footing, and he talks to the baby in English
because that is the language his thoughts come in. Marcia watches this without comment. She
has decided, in the way she decides things — by laying out all the sides and choosing —
that Daniel's eccentricities are not her business to manage.

Lucanus learns English the way he learns Latin: by hearing it constantly, by having it be
the language one parent uses. He does not know it is unusual. He knows it is private —
they do not speak English in front of slaves or guests — but he thinks of this as a family
custom, like a particular way of marking a feast day.

He is a physical child more than a verbal one in these years. He wants to know how things
work in the immediate sense — why a pot makes that noise, what happens if you drop this.
Daniel finds this familiar. He was not so different.

---

### Lucanus, Age 5–12 (~113–120 AD): The Playground, the Early Stories

**The playground (~111–115 AD, when Lucanus is 3–7):**

Daniel builds something in the garden that Romans don't build: a dedicated play structure.
He builds it when Lucanus is old enough to use it and Ulpia is not yet born or barely a
toddler. He builds it because he knows what these things feel like. He builds it because
he wants his children to have it.

What he builds:

- **Swing:** Rope and a wooden seat. Romans had *oscillum* — swings with a religious
  dimension, also recreational, hung from trees and frames. He builds a proper swing
  on a wooden frame. A neighbor or visitor who asks can be told it's an *oscillum*
  frame. This is not quite a lie.

- **Slide:** A smooth inclined board with raised sides, sanded and oiled for smoothness.
  Pure carpentry. He sands the surface until it is slick, oils it, builds the raised
  sides so a child doesn't roll off. Pamphilus builds it in an afternoon from Daniel's
  description. Pamphilus does not ask what it is for. He builds what he's told to build.

- **Monkey bars:** Horizontal bars at height between two upright supports. Also pure
  carpentry. Two posts, crossbars at intervals, notched and pegged in. Nothing technically
  complex. He knows what monkey bars are; he can explain them to a carpenter; they can
  be built. He builds them because he remembers hanging from them.

- **Sandbox:** A shallow enclosed area with sand. Romans don't do this as a dedicated
  play space, but there is nothing difficult about it. He does it because he remembers
  sandboxes.

The play structure is noticed by neighbors and by workshop visitors who come to the house.
It is found slightly odd — not offensive, not alarming, just unusual. A child of equestrian
or better family is expected to play games, not on a wooden structure in the garden. Daniel
explains it briefly if asked ("children play on it"), does not explain the reasoning, and
the topic drops.

He built it because he knows what a swing feels like. Because he wanted their childhoods
to have something. He did not build it for Rome. He built it for them.

**What this says about Daniel:** He is recreating something from his own childhood for
his children without being able to explain why it matters. He cannot say "this is what
playgrounds look like where I come from." He builds it instead.

**Early stories for Lucanus:**

Daniel tells Lucanus the same stories he will later tell Ulpia — not as planned lessons
but because these are the stories in his head and the child is there and asking. Shakespeare
plots simplified to their bones. Jules Verne premises told as adventure. Science fiction
as things that happen in stories from his world.

Lucanus's engagement with stories is different from what Ulpia's will be. He wants to know
who wins. He wants to know if the plan worked. He is less interested in the machinery of
the submarine and more interested in whether they escaped. When Daniel tells him about
*Around the World in Eighty Days*, Lucanus wants to know if Fogg made the bet. Daniel says
yes. Lucanus says: "Then he was right to try." Daniel notes this and does not make too
much of it. (A child can say something true by accident. He notes it; he does not canonize
it as the day's deepest utterance.)

**Early science for Lucanus:**

- The earth is round; the earth goes around the sun. Lucanus accepts this and asks if
  you'd fall off the bottom. Daniel explains gravity. Lucanus thinks about this for two
  days and then asks if the people at the bottom are also right-side up. He is seven.
  Daniel says yes. Lucanus accepts this with the satisfaction of a problem resolved.

- Things too small to see can make you sick. He passes this on to his friends as a fact.
  Daniel is informed of this by a neighbor. He does not correct the behavior.

- Dinosaurs: Lucanus is young enough that this is pure wonder before it becomes
  intellectual. He wants them to be real. He finds it slightly disappointing that they
  are not available. He asks if there are any left. Daniel says no, and tells him about
  the asteroid, and tells him that birds are descended from dinosaurs. Lucanus stares
  at a pigeon for an uncomfortable length of time.

### Ulpia, Age 0–5 (~118–123 AD): The Language

English from birth, simultaneously with Latin. Daniel is ~37 now — older, more settled,
no less likely to slip into English when he's thinking out loud. Lucanus is ten when Ulpia
arrives; he already has English. She learns a private family language from two sources.

She is a different child from Lucanus in these years. Where Lucanus wanted to know who
won, she sits and watches things for a long time before she asks about them. She is slower
to speak and then suddenly all there. Daniel will eventually understand that she is
building up a question and waiting until it is exactly right.

**Songs:** Daniel sings to both of them. He knows a lot of songs, or knows the shape of
them. The problem is the holes. He knows the opening verse of a dozen songs and then the
words blur. He knows the chorus but not the bridge. He knows the melody but cannot find
the second verse. He fills in what he can, hums through what he cannot. Neither child knows
the difference — they have nothing to compare it to. But he knows. Singing Ulpia to sleep
with the second verse missing from a song he hasn't thought of in twenty years is a quiet
private grief that is entirely his. She sleeps. He sits with it.

**What Ulpia learns in this phase:** English as a natural language, not a studied one.
By age five she switches between Latin and English the way she switches between speaking
to her nurse and speaking to her father. She doesn't know that this is unusual. She thinks
of it as a family custom, the same way Lucanus thought of it at her age.

### Both Children, Age 5–12: The Playground and Early Lessons

By the time Ulpia is old enough for the playground (~123 AD), Lucanus is fifteen and
has long since outgrown it. The slide and the monkey bars have weathered; the swing still
hangs. Ulpia uses it the same way Lucanus did. The sandbox has been replenished with
fresh sand. A neighbor who visits remarks on the structure. Daniel says "my children
play on it." This is the whole explanation he offers.

**For both children, early in the education:**

He draws maps. The maps he draws are different from Roman maps — familiar Mediterranean
outlines, but also Africa extending farther south than anyone has confirmed, and large
shapes to the west that Roman maps don't have. He tells them both: "There are people
there. Rome's ships will find them eventually." He tells Lucanus first (~112–115 AD),
and Lucanus asks if Rome will conquer them. Daniel says: "Probably, if Rome gets there
first. That's what Rome does." Lucanus considers this. He does not find it troubling, which
Daniel notices and files away.

When he tells Ulpia (~123–124 AD), she asks: "Do they know about Rome?" He says no.
She asks: "Do we know about them?" He says: "A little. I know a little." She asks: "Then
who knows more?" He says: "Neither side, yet." She thinks about this for a week and
comes back to ask: "What happens when they find out about each other?" He tells her, in
broad strokes, what happens. It is not a cheerful answer. She receives it the way she
receives most hard things: flat, focused, asking the next question.

**Arithmetic extensions:** Both children learn fractions and the logic of long division
from Daniel. He explains it as "finding out how many times one number fits inside another."
Lucanus uses this for the rest of his life. Ulpia uses it and also asks what you do when
it doesn't fit evenly. He teaches her remainders and then, later, decimals.

**Basic astronomy, given to both:** The earth goes around the sun. He says this
matter-of-factly. Neither child argues it. They ask why they can't feel the movement.
He gives them the ship-on-a-calm-sea analogy. Both accept it and neither repeats it to
their tutors, for different reasons: Lucanus doesn't see why it matters; Ulpia has already
learned to keep private what is private.

**Germ theory, given to both:** Things too small to see can make you sick. He tells
them this more specifically in private than he does in public — viruses and bacteria as
distinct things, how the immune system works, what a vaccine is (the concept, not a
formula he can produce). Lucanus deploys this information to avoid being sick. Ulpia asks
what bacteria look like. Daniel describes them as best he can. She asks how anyone knows
what they look like if they're too small to see. He explains microscopes. She asks if he
can build one. He says the glass is the problem. She asks what the glass needs to be like.
The conversation goes on for an hour.

### Lucanus, Age 12–20 (~120–128 AD): The Serious Years

By the time Lucanus is twelve, his path is forming. He is interested in law — in argument,
in how rules work, in what happens when two rules conflict. Daniel finds this genuinely
interesting to engage with. He knows enough about law from his own world to have useful
things to say, and the conversations become real.

**What Daniel gives Lucanus in this phase:**

- *The full modern knowledge catalog* — not in any organized sequence, but as it comes
  up. Lucanus asks what a car is after Daniel mentions it offhand. Daniel explains the
  internal combustion engine. Lucanus wants to know how fast. Daniel tells him: faster
  than any horse. Lucanus asks: "Faster than a messenger relay?" Daniel says: "Yes,
  and you don't have to change the horse." Lucanus thinks about what this would do to
  military communication and never says it out loud, but Daniel sees him thinking it.

- *Newton's laws as principles* — demonstrated with objects. Things in motion stay in
  motion. Force equals mass times acceleration. Every action has an equal and opposite
  reaction. Lucanus gets these faster than Daniel expects and immediately asks what they
  mean for siege engines. Daniel explains. This is a conversation that would concern
  him more if Lucanus weren't so clearly headed for the courts.

- *History — his history* — given carefully, as with Ulpia later, with attention to
  what will be useful and what will be paralyzing. He tells Lucanus what happens to
  Rome. He tells him about the western empire's fall, about what comes after, about
  the shape of things over the next fifteen hundred years. Lucanus's response to this
  is different from what Ulpia's will be. He doesn't ask what it was like. He asks:
  "What do the lawyers do?" Daniel is not entirely sure what he means. Lucanus
  clarifies: when things are falling apart, when the institutions are failing, what
  do the people who work inside the institutions do? Daniel tells him the truth — some
  prop them up, some abandon them, some adapt. Lucanus files this.

- *The full stories* — Shakespeare, Verne, science fiction, fairy tales. He gets
  all of it, at an older age than Ulpia so he processes it differently. When Daniel
  tells him about Star Wars — space, wars, a mystical force, a father revealed —
  Lucanus wants to discuss the politics of the rebellion. He is a Roman; factions
  make sense to him. He and Daniel have a long argument about whether the rebels were
  right to destroy the Death Star without a trial. Daniel finds this conversation
  simultaneously amusing and clarifying about who Lucanus is.

**What Lucanus does with what he knows:**

He uses it. Not loudly. He doesn't announce where his ideas come from. But when he argues
— first in practice with Daniel, then later in the courts — the arguments have a particular
shape. Presumption of innocence as a principle, not just a practice. Evidence standards as
something that should be stated explicitly, not assumed. Due process as a concept that needs
a name before it can be defended. These are arguments with Daniel's fingerprints on them.
Lucanus applies them to Roman law without attribution, without explanation, without ever
saying where they came from.

Daniel sees this happening and says nothing. It is the best possible outcome and also a
quiet reminder that his children will use what he gives them in ways he cannot predict or
control.

### Ulpia, Age 5–9 (~123–127 AD): Early Lessons

She is being educated by household tutors in the standard way — Latin letters, basic
figures, appropriate behavior for a girl of her class. Daniel supplements this in their
private sessions.

- **Reading and writing in English:** She can already read and write in Latin; English
  follows the same literacy, applied to a different script. She is faster at this than
  Daniel expects. She asks why the letters look different. He tells her it is an older
  script from his family's homeland. This is as close to true as anything he tells her
  at this stage.

- **Arithmetic extensions:** She knows addition and basic multiplication from her Latin
  education. Daniel teaches her fractions and long division. He explains it as "finding
  out how many times one number fits inside another." She immediately asks what happens
  when it doesn't fit evenly. He teaches her remainders. She asks why remainders exist —
  why the universe doesn't arrange itself to divide evenly. He does not have a good answer
  for this. She notes the gap.

- **Stories:** He tells her Shakespeare plots — simplified, without the blank verse,
  but with the shape intact. She has strong opinions. She thinks Hamlet took too long
  and is not interested in his reasons. She thinks Romeo and Juliet were both being
  stupid and she says so, waiting for Daniel to disagree. He mostly doesn't disagree,
  which she finds satisfying. She thinks Titania was manipulated and that Oberon was
  wrong and that the play presents this as comedy when it shouldn't. Daniel is not sure
  she's wrong about this.

  He tells her Jules Verne plots — the submarine, the balloon, the journey to the
  interior of the earth. She is fascinated by the submarine and asks how it is
  propelled. He explains the principle: sealed hull, ballast tanks, screw propulsion,
  mechanical engine. She says: "So it needs fire?" He says yes, essentially. She
  thinks about this and asks how the fire gets air. He explains the sealed-tank
  approach. She asks if he could build one. He says he doesn't have the materials.
  She asks if he could figure out the materials. He says that's a different question.

  He tells her science fiction premises — the ones where people go to other planets, or
  build machines that think, or travel through time. He doesn't describe these as fiction
  at first; he describes them as things that happen in stories from his world. Later, when
  she is old enough to distinguish categories, she asks which of these are real. He tells
  her: "People have been to the moon. Machines that think are something people were
  working on when I left." She asks: "Were they good machines?" He says: "Some of them
  were useful. Nobody knew yet if they were good."

### Ulpia, Age 9–14 (~127–132 AD): The Serious Schooling

By now she reads English fluently — faster than Daniel reads Latin, which is a running
private joke between them. He writes materials for her: textbook-style notes in his cipher,
covering things her Roman tutors don't know.

She is also, by this point, the only person outside the cipher circle who reads English.
She doesn't know she is reading a cipher. She thinks she is reading in her family's private
script. She will understand what she is reading when she is told the full truth.

**Mathematics:**

- *Algebra:* He can do this. He teaches it as "finding the unknown." She takes to it
  immediately. She has a habit of reducing problems to their simplest form before doing
  anything else, which is good algebraic instinct and also characteristic of her in every
  other domain.

- *Geometry:* Rome already knows substantial geometry (Euclid is in circulation). Daniel
  extends it into applications she hasn't seen: calculating the area of an irregular shape
  by breaking it into triangles, estimating the height of a building from shadow angles,
  solid geometry. She is particularly interested in the practical applications — how do
  you actually cut stone to make an arch? He explains the keystone principle. She asks how
  Apollodorus did it for the bridge at Drobeta. He tells her what he knows, which is
  actually quite a bit.

- *Calculus (the idea of it):* He cannot fully reconstruct calculus. He was in precalc
  when he left. What he can give her is the concept: rates of change, the idea of finding
  area under a curve by slicing it thin and summing, the notion of instantaneous velocity.
  He describes these as "ways of handling things that are changing while you look at them."
  She finds this interesting but can see the gap where the formal machinery would be. She
  notes the gap. He acknowledges it. She writes "UNFINISHED — Father does not know the
  full method" in the margin of her notes, in English. He sees this and does not correct it.

**Physics:**

He teaches her Newton's laws as principles, demonstrated with objects around the room:
things in motion stay in motion; force equals mass times acceleration; every push has an
equal push in the other direction. He tells her about gravity as a force between all things
with mass, not just between heavy things and the earth. She asks if the moon has gravity.
He says yes, and that it pulls on the earth's water, which is why the tides move. She has
noticed the tides. She says: "So the moon is pulling the sea." He says: "Yes." She says:
"Then why isn't the sea on top of us?" He explains. She thinks about this for several days
and comes back with three more questions he is not fully equipped to answer. He answers
what he can and marks the rest.

**Chemistry:**

He knows the periodic table imperfectly. He can reconstruct hydrogen and helium, lithium
through neon, and the common elements — carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, sodium, phosphorus,
sulfur, chlorine, iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold, mercury, lead. Past that, his memory
becomes unreliable and he knows it. He tells her:

"There is a table of all the basic substances things are made of. Each one has a number
and a weight. I can tell you the first twenty or so with confidence. After that I'm
guessing."

He tells her atoms exist, that everything is made of them, that they are too small to see
but their behavior can be predicted. She finds this interesting as a framework and
immediately asks what the small things that make people sick are made of. He tells her
they are also made of atoms, that they are small living things with their own structure,
that they reproduce. She asks how they know to make more of themselves. He says that is
one of the questions his world was still working on when he left. She writes: "UNFINISHED
— biology of reproduction at the small level — unresolved even in Father's time."

**History — his history:**

He tells her future history, dosed with attention to what will be useful and what will be
paralyzing. He has already told Lucanus this material; telling Ulpia feels different because
she processes it differently. Lucanus asked what happens to the institutions. Ulpia asks
what the people were like.

He tells her about the medieval period, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment — not in
detail, but in shape. She asks: "Did people in your world know this was coming?" He says:
"They knew the history. They didn't know if the pattern would repeat." She thinks about
this for a week and asks: "Do you know if it will repeat?" He says: "No. I know it did
once. I don't know what I've changed."

He tells her about democracy as a system — the modern form: elected representatives,
written constitutions, government deriving its legitimacy from the governed. He tells her
about the American Revolution — what it was about, what it produced. She asks: "Was it
better?" He says: "For a lot of people, yes. For some people it took a long time to be
included." She understands this immediately as a description of a familiar problem. She
does not need it explained.

**Technology concepts:**

He explains principles:
- *Internal combustion:* Controlled explosions pushing a piston, which turns a shaft.
- *Flight:* The shape of a wing and why it produces lift (faster air over the curved top,
  slower air under the flat bottom). She asks if he's been in a flying machine. He says
  yes, several times. She asks what it was like. He says: "Boring, mostly. The inside
  looked like a room. You couldn't see the speed."
- *Radio:* Information carried on waves in the air — not visible, but real; they can be
  generated and detected. He cannot build a radio. He can explain that it works.
- *Cameras:* Light on a surface that reacts to it — a chemical reaction fixed in place.
  He explains the pinhole-camera principle. The photographic chemistry he cannot
  reconstruct in useful detail, but she understands the concept.

She accepts all of this as things that are technically possible. She does not ask him to
build them. She understands that he is describing a world she will not see.

**Ethics:**

He does not lecture. He has conversations. He tells her, over years, about things that
were treated as normal in Rome that he cannot treat as normal: slavery, what women are
allowed to own and decide, the assumption that birth determines worth. He is careful not
to make her feel her world is uniquely evil — he tells her about the ways his world
continued to do harm. But he is honest:

"There are things here I can't make myself believe are right. Your mother knows this about
me without my saying it. You're old enough to know it directly."

She takes this seriously. She does not perform enlightenment about it or become a crusader.
She is a Roman woman of her class in most of her public behavior. But she thinks about
what he says. He can see her thinking.

### Both Children: The Full Knowledge Catalog

Daniel does not have a curriculum. He tells them things as they come up. Ulpia asks about
a caterpillar and he tells her about metamorphosis and then about evolution and then about
dinosaurs. The conversations sprawl. Lucanus asks about war and Daniel tells him about
WW2 and then has to explain what a world war is and then has to explain what an atomic
bomb is and then has to explain nuclear fission, and then Lucanus sits with this for
three days and asks: "Did the people who built it know what they were making?" Daniel
says yes. Lucanus asks: "Then what's the right thing to call them?" Daniel doesn't have
a good answer. He tells Lucanus he doesn't have a good answer. Lucanus notes this as
data.

The following is organized by category. Everything on this list gets told to both
children. Their responses are noted where they differ.

---

#### Science and Nature

**Dinosaurs:** Lived 65-plus million years ago. Wiped out by an asteroid — he explains
what an asteroid is (a large rock moving through space; when it hits a planet, it releases
more energy than every bomb ever made). Some dinosaurs were enormous; some were small.
Birds are descendants of dinosaurs. This lands strangely every time he says it.

*Lucanus (age 7 or 8):* Stares at a pigeon for too long. Asks if the pigeon knows.
Daniel says no. Lucanus seems vaguely disappointed in the pigeon.

*Ulpia (age 5 or 6):* Asks what they ate. Daniel tells her some ate plants, some ate
other dinosaurs. She asks which ones ate other dinosaurs. He tells her about Tyrannosaurus
rex — enormous, upright, small forearms. She asks why the arms are small. He says he
doesn't know; they think it might be evolutionary — the arms didn't help enough to be
worth the cost of growing them. She asks: "Can things get worse over time?" He says yes,
if what gets worse doesn't matter to survival. She sits with this in a way that suggests
she is applying it to something other than dinosaurs.

**Black holes:** Stars that collapse under their own gravity. Gravity so strong not even
light can escape. At the edge — the event horizon — you could fall in without knowing
where the boundary was, because nothing comes back from past it to tell you. He knows the
concept, not the math. He tells them what he knows and stops where his knowledge stops.

*Lucanus:* Asks if anyone has been to one. Daniel says no — they're too far away and too
lethal. Lucanus asks: "Has anyone tried?" Daniel says people have thought about it, but
the nearest ones are so far away that the travel time is longer than the history of Rome
by a factor of thousands. Lucanus works through what this means and then goes quiet.

*Ulpia:* Asks what's inside. Daniel says nobody knows — the physics of what happens past
the event horizon is genuinely uncertain in his time. She asks: "So there are things even
your world doesn't know?" He says yes. She asks what else. This is the question that opens
the longest conversations they ever have.

**How suns work:** Nuclear fusion — hydrogen atoms combining into helium, releasing
enormous energy. That is what fire is at the largest scale. The sun will eventually run
out of fuel, but this is billions of years away; he tells them this with the caveat that
they should not be concerned about it in any practical sense.

*Both children:* Neither is concerned. Lucanus asks who discovered this. Daniel says
scientists in his world worked it out over centuries. Lucanus asks if anyone got credit
for it. Daniel says several people did, in different parts. Lucanus says that seems like
an argument waiting to happen. Daniel agrees.

**Eclipses:** Solar eclipses happen because the moon passes exactly between earth and sun;
the geometry is precise enough that the moon almost perfectly covers the disc. Lunar
eclipses happen because the earth's shadow falls on the moon. He can explain both from
geometry. He can predict them in theory; in practice his eclipse calendar memory is unreliable.
He knows they are predictable and occasionally gets one right.

**Evolution:** Life changed slowly over billions of years. Humans share common ancestors
with apes. The mechanism is random variation plus survival advantage — traits that help you
survive get passed on; traits that don't, eventually don't.

This is the hardest one to explain without Roman conceptual scaffolding. He tries anyway.

*Lucanus:* Asks whether this means there is no plan. Daniel says in the strict evolutionary
sense, no — variation is random, selection is environmental, there's no direction aimed at
a particular outcome. Lucanus finds this unsatisfying and says so. Daniel says he found it
unsatisfying too, at first. Lucanus says: "What changed?" Daniel says: "I got more
interested in what it means than in whether I liked it." Lucanus keeps this. He uses a
version of it in court, years later, about a law he finds unjust.

*Ulpia:* Asks how long until humans and apes can't be told apart anymore. Daniel says that's
not how it works — they diverged from a common ancestor long ago; they are not turning into
each other. She asks: "Then we're cousins?" He says yes, essentially. She thinks about this.
He can see her thinking about what obligations cousins have.

**Metamorphosis:** A caterpillar builds a cocoon and inside it dissolves almost entirely
into biological soup — most of the structures break down — and then reassembles into a
butterfly with entirely different anatomy. He shows them caterpillars when he finds them
and explains what is happening inside. Neither child entirely believes this at first.

*Ulpia:* Goes and finds a cocoon and checks on it every day for the length of its cycle.
Reports back when the butterfly emerges. Asks: "Is it the same animal?" Daniel says it has
the same DNA — he has to explain what DNA is, which takes a long time — but the form is
completely different. She asks: "Is it the same animal or not?" He says it depends what
you mean by "same." She decides: "I think it's the same. It remembers what it was." He
doesn't know if she's right. He marks this as UNRESOLVED.

**Plate tectonics:** The continents move, slowly — Africa and South America were once
joined. He draws this roughly, showing the way the coastlines fit together like puzzle
pieces. Earthquakes happen at the edges where plates collide. Mountains are pushed up by
the pressure of plates meeting.

*Both children:* The coastline-fit is the convincing part. Lucanus says it looks like
someone tore them apart. Daniel says essentially, yes. Ulpia asks what's in between the
plates. Daniel explains the mantle, liquid rock under enormous pressure. She asks if that
comes out anywhere. He tells her about volcanoes and how they relate to plate boundaries.
This leads to a long conversation about Vesuvius and what actually happened.

**The Mariana Trench:** The deepest point in the ocean — miles straight down, pressure so
intense that a person without protection would be crushed instantly, temperature near
freezing, no light whatsoever. Strange creatures live there. In his time, no one had been
to the bottom by direct observation; they had only mapped it indirectly.

*Ulpia:* Asks what the strange creatures look like. He describes what he knows — bioluminescent
organisms, creatures with adapted pressure tolerance, things that look like no animal in
Roman natural history. She draws, from his description, something that is not quite right
but is trying. She asks if there are any in the Mediterranean. He says no; the Mediterranean
is too shallow and too warm.

**Mount Everest:** The tallest mountain; so high the air is too thin to breathe without
assistance. In his time people climbed it, with equipment — breathing apparatus, insulated
clothing, ropes and camps established up the slope. He tells them the date of the first
ascent (1953; he is fairly confident in this).

*Lucanus:* Asks why. Daniel says because it was there. Lucanus looks at him. Daniel says:
"That's actually why. Or the closest thing to why." Lucanus says: "That's a terrible reason."
Daniel says: "I know. People did it anyway." Lucanus files this as data about what people
will do when they want to say they've done something.

**Weather:** How clouds form (water vapor cooling and condensing around particles). What
lightning actually is — an electrical discharge between regions of different charge, usually
cloud-to-cloud or cloud-to-earth. How rainbows work — light refracting through water
droplets, different wavelengths bending at slightly different angles, spreading the colors.
He has explained electricity to both of them in basic terms before he gets to lightning,
so lightning is confirmatory rather than a new idea.

*Ulpia:* Asks if he can make a small lightning. He says he has made a small version of the
electrical effect — static electricity, which she can feel when she pulls wool apart on a
dry day. He demonstrates. She asks if lightning is that but bigger. He says yes. She asks
if you could use it for anything. He explains the answer and they end up talking about
electrical power for the rest of the afternoon.

**Germs and viruses (extended):** He tells his children more specifically than he tells
anyone in public. Viruses and bacteria as distinct things. How the immune system works —
white blood cells, the body's recognition and response system, the memory the immune
system builds after fighting off an infection. What a vaccine is: a weakened or dead
version of a pathogen, introduced to train the immune system without causing full disease.
He cannot formulate vaccines; he explains the principle.

*Lucanus:* Asks whether the physicians know about any of this. Daniel says Heras knows
the framework — things too small to see, the germ approach — but not the specifics. Lucanus
asks: "Should you tell them the specifics?" Daniel says he has given Heras what Heras can
use. Lucanus says: "That's not the same thing." This is an early version of an argument
Lucanus will make in different forms for the rest of his life: what you can use and what
is true are different questions. Daniel acknowledges he's right. He doesn't change what
he does with Heras. Lucanus notes this too.

*Ulpia:* Asks what happens to the bacteria when you kill them. Daniel explains. She asks
what happens to bacteria when the host dies. He explains that too. She goes quiet and then
says: "So disease doesn't care." He says: "No. It doesn't care." She receives this and
applies it, silently, to several things at once.

#### Technology (Concepts, Not Blueprints)

Daniel explains these as principles. He cannot build most of them. That is not the point.

**Solar panels:** Sunlight directly converted to electricity through a material that releases
electrons when hit by photons. He has to explain what a photon is (a particle of light — or
a wave; it's both, which is confusing, and he admits it's confusing). The children find the
idea of something that makes electricity from sunlight comprehensible as a principle even
if the material is beyond Roman fabrication.

*Ulpia:* Asks whether everything Daniel is explaining eventually comes back to electricity.
He considers this and says: a lot of it does. She asks: "Then if you could make electricity
easily, what else would work?" He thinks about what depends on electrical power. The
conversation goes on for two hours.

**Refrigerators and air conditioning:** Moving heat from inside a space to outside it, using
a fluid that absorbs heat when it expands and releases it when it compresses. That is the
principle. That is how cold preservation works. That is also how you cool a room.

*Lucanus:* Immediately asks what this would do to food preservation and supply chains.
Daniel explains the implications — food that would spoil in days kept safe for weeks, ice
in any climate, cold storage for medicines. Lucanus works out some of what this would mean
for the army's logistics. He does not say this out loud; Daniel can see him thinking it.

*Ulpia:* Asks if you could make ice using this principle. Daniel says yes, that is how
mechanical ice is made in his time. She asks what it would cost to make cold in Rome,
if he could build the machine. He works through it with her. They conclude: not impossible
in principle; impossible with current materials for the heat-transfer fluid and the
compression mechanism.

**Cars:** An internal combustion engine — gasoline (refined from petroleum, which he
explains is ancient biological matter compressed underground over millions of years) burns
in controlled explosions that push pistons that turn wheels. A car can go faster than
any horse, for longer, without tiring. He tells them what it sounds like, what it smells
like, what it feels like to travel in one.

*Both children:* The speed is the part that gets them. Lucanus asks about the army's
use of something like this. Daniel says his world had vehicles like this for armies —
armored, weaponized — and that they changed warfare completely. He gives them a brief
account of tanks. Lucanus sits with this and then says: "So a legionary can be killed
by a machine before he can see the man who killed him." Daniel says yes. Lucanus says:
"That changes what courage means." Daniel says: "Yes. People argued about that." Lucanus
says he doesn't think it's an argument. He thinks it just means something different now.

**Alarm clocks:** A mechanical device that wakes you at a specific time — he is working
on mechanical versions and can explain the principle. He tells them later versions use
electricity. The concept: a mechanism that counts elapsed time and triggers an action at
the right moment. This connects to the clock problem he's been working on for years.

**Telephones:** Voice converted to an electrical signal, transmitted over wire, converted
back to voice at the far end. He cannot build this. He explains what it does: a person in
Rome could speak and a person in Alexandria would hear it instantly, without a messenger,
without delay. He tells them about radio — same principle but wireless, no wire required.

*Ulpia:* Asks what happened to messengers when this existed. Daniel says messengers were
still used for some things but the profession changed completely. She asks: "What did they
do instead?" He says they did other things. She asks: "Was that decided or did it just
happen?" He says it mostly just happened. She notes this as a pattern — things becoming
unnecessary without anyone deciding to make them unnecessary.

**Satellites:** Objects placed in orbit around the earth, moving so fast they fall around
the earth rather than into it — the same principle as any projectile, but at orbital
velocity the curve of the earth falls away at the same rate as the falling object, so it
circles indefinitely. He explains this carefully because it requires understanding orbit.

*Lucanus:* Works out the geometry from what Daniel has told him about gravity and
projectiles. Asks: "So it's falling, but sideways so fast it misses?" Daniel says yes.
Lucanus says that's actually elegant. This is one of the few times he says something is
elegant. Daniel notes it.

*Ulpia:* Asks what you could see from orbit. He tells her — the shape of the continents,
weather systems, the curvature of the earth, things moving on the surface if you're
close enough. She asks if anyone has looked. He says yes, extensively. She asks if anyone
took pictures. He says yes. She asks what the earth looks like from space. He describes
the Pale Blue Dot — a distant image, the earth a point of light in an enormous darkness.
She sits with this for a long time.

**Washing machines:** Mechanical agitation of water and cloth. The physical principle
isn't complex; what's complex is the motor. He explains what the machine does if not
how it does it: you put clothes in with water and something to help cleaning, and the
machine moves them repeatedly until clean. Both children understand immediately that the
labor this replaces is enormous. Ulpia asks who did the washing before there were machines.
Daniel says: servants, slaves, women. She asks: "In your world too?" He says: "Yes, until
the machines." She asks: "Then who freed them?" He says: "The machine did, mostly."

**Planes:** He has explained the wing and lift already. He extends to the experience.
Thirty thousand feet up — he has to explain what feet are, then translate to something
meaningful — hundreds of people in a metal enclosure, a meal served, arriving across an
ocean in hours. He tells them what it looks like out the window: clouds from above, cities
at night as grids of light, the Atlantic as a featureless dark expanse.

*Lucanus:* Asks whether Rome could have conquered more, faster, with planes. Daniel says
yes, and more than that — strategic warfare changes completely when you can move people
and materiel by air. He gives them a compressed account of air warfare from his world.
Lucanus files this with the same silence he gives to information that has implications he's
not ready to state.

**Rockets:** Reaction engines — expelling gas at high speed in one direction pushes the
rocket in the other direction. That is how you get to space. He explains what space is
first (the region beyond the atmosphere, where there is no air, where the silence is
total, where objects float because there is no net gravitational pull to fall toward in
the absence of a nearby body). He explains that rockets powerful enough to escape earth's
gravity require an enormous amount of fuel and that most of the rocket is the fuel.

*Both:* The concept of escaping gravity is the hard part. He explains escape velocity.
Ulpia works out the implication: "So if you go fast enough in any direction, the earth
can't bring you back?" He says yes. She asks: "Has anyone gone that far?" He says yes —
spacecraft have left the solar system. She asks: "Are they still going?" He says yes,
still moving, still sending signals back when he left. She asks how long it takes to
get a signal back. He tells her. She sits with the time scale.

**Cameras:** A surface that reacts to light, preserving the image. He has seen photographs
and describes them: an exact record of what something looked like at a specific moment,
preserved indefinitely. He describes portrait photographs — someone's face, accurate
as no painter ever achieved. He describes photographs of places he knew — streets, buildings,
landscapes — that he can no longer visit.

*Ulpia:* Asks if he has any photographs. He says no — he arrived with nothing. She asks
what he would photograph if he could. He names several things without hesitating. She
notes that he answers immediately, which means the list was already in his head. She does
not say this. She notes it.

**Computers and the internet:** Machines that do arithmetic very fast — millions, then
billions of operations per second. Eventually able to store and retrieve information.
Eventually able to simulate processes too complex for any human mind. He explains the
internet as all the computers connected to each other: you could ask a question from
anywhere and get an answer from anywhere in seconds.

This conversation goes long, both times he has it.

*Lucanus:* Asks what people argued about on the internet. Daniel tells him. Lucanus asks:
"The same things people argue about here?" Daniel says mostly yes. Lucanus says: "Then
the technology doesn't change what people are." Daniel says: "It changes how fast and how
far." Lucanus says: "But not what." Daniel agrees.

*Ulpia:* Asks whether the encyclopedia could be put on a computer. Daniel says yes,
something very like it — and far more, built by thousands of people, available to anyone.
She asks what happens to it if the machine breaks. He tells her about redundancy, copies,
server farms — she asks what a server is, and they go down that road. She eventually asks:
"Is this better or worse than what we're doing?" He says: "Better in almost every way."
She asks: "But?" He says: "You can lose it all at once if the wrong thing breaks. We're
doing it on stone and vellum. That takes longer to destroy." She writes this down.

**Space travel:** He tells them humans walked on the moon. He tells them the date — July
1969. He tells them the name of the first man to step on the surface. He tells them what
the footprints look like: still there, probably, because the moon has no weather.

*Both children:* This is the one that takes the longest to be believed. He tells them he
knows people who watched it happen, live, on a screen in their home, from thousands of
miles away. Lucanus says: "On a screen." Daniel says: "Television. Moving pictures with
sound, on a flat surface. People watched events from anywhere in the world as they
happened." He has to then explain television, which he was going to do separately anyway.
Ulpia asks: "Was the man who walked on the moon famous?" He says enormously famous.
She asks: "What did he do afterward?" Daniel tells her — retired, mostly; stayed on earth.
She finds this notable. The most famous act of a lifetime, and then you just continue.

#### Food (What He Misses; What They Could Eventually Have)

These conversations happen over years, often during meals, when something Roman tastes
wrong to him or when he's describing something he's not eating.

**Pizza:** Bread with tomato sauce and melted cheese. He describes it in enough detail
that they could probably make it — the issue is tomatoes don't exist in Rome yet, and
he knows this. He tells them about tomatoes. They have something to look forward to,
hundreds of years from now.

*Lucanus:* Says this sounds like flatbread with cheese. Daniel says it is flatbread with
cheese plus the tomato sauce, which makes it different. Lucanus says: "How different?"
Daniel says: "Completely different." Lucanus is not convinced but does not pursue it.

**Ice cream:** Cold dairy, sugar, and flavoring, churned while frozen. Impossible in
Rome without refrigeration. He tells them about it anyway. It becomes one of the things
he promises to find a way to approximate. He has not approximated it yet.

*Ulpia:* Asks what it feels like to eat something cold. He explains. She has had cold
water in winter. He says it's like that but in food form, and sweet. She considers this
and says: "That sounds painful." He says: "It can be. You eat it too fast and your head
hurts." She says: "Then why do people eat it fast?" He says: "Because it's also very good."
She files this as a data point about human behavior.

**Popcorn:** Corn kernels and high heat. The kernel explodes when the water inside
becomes steam fast enough. He explains that they don't have corn yet — it grows in the
western lands he's shown them on the map. He tells them when they have corn, this is one
of the first things they should try.

**Hamburgers:** Ground beef, formed into a patty, cooked on a flat surface, served on a
round bread. Both children find this deeply strange. Lucanus asks why you would grind the
meat. Daniel explains: more consistent cook, easier to mass-produce, fits the bread better.
Lucanus says: "And this is popular where you're from?" Daniel says enormously popular.
Lucanus says he doesn't understand and moves on.

**Chocolate (modern form):** He has already told them about cacao, which exists in Roman
trade in very limited form. He tells them what his world did with it: solid bars of
chocolate, chocolate mixed into cakes, hot cocoa with sugar dissolved in. He describes the
taste in detail. Ulpia asks him to describe it several more times, over the years, and he
does, each time slightly differently because he can feel the exact memory getting fuzzier.

**Carbonated water:** Water with dissolved carbon dioxide — bubbles, slight sharp taste,
different from still water. He thinks about whether he could produce this. He tells them
what soda tastes like — sweet, bubbled, cold. He cannot make soda. He thinks he might be
able to make the carbonated water component if he pressurizes CO2 into water. He has not
tried yet.

*Ulpia:* This is the one she pushes him on practically. She asks if the principle is
achievable. He says probably. She says they should try. They try. They get a version of
it — it bubbles, which is interesting, and it tastes faintly acidic, which is not quite
right — and call it a partial success. This is the note on which the food conversation
often ends: partial success, mark it, move forward.

**Cereal:** Grain processed into small pieces, eaten cold in milk. Both children find this
inexplicable. Ulpia asks why you would eat grain cold. Daniel says it's quick — you don't
have to cook anything. She asks why that matters. He explains that in his world people
were often busy in the mornings. She says: "Busy doing what?" He says: "Going to work."
She asks: "But the kitchen slaves could—" He says: "Many people didn't have kitchen
slaves." She processes this. "Then who cooked?" He says: "Usually the person eating."
This opens a long conversation about what Roman assumptions she didn't know she was making.

#### Sports and Games

**Football (American):** He tries to explain the rules. It's hard without equipment. He
draws the field, explains the down system, the touchdown, the field goal. The stop-start
nature baffles both children. Lucanus asks why play stops after every action. Daniel says
because each play is a unit — teams reset, confer, then execute. Lucanus says this seems
more like a negotiation than a game. Daniel says there's an element of that. Lucanus
says he likes it better now.

**Soccer:** He doesn't have to explain this for long. Ball, goal at each end, don't touch
with hands except the keeper. Both children understand immediately because Romans have
ball games. He tells them it's the most popular sport in his world, by far. Lucanus asks
why. Daniel says it's simple to learn and you can play it anywhere with almost no equipment.
Lucanus says: "That's also why bread is popular." Daniel says yes, essentially.

**Formula 1 racing:** Cars going very fast around a circuit — fastest lap times, pit stop
strategy, tire management. He explains that the engineering is as much of the competition
as the driving. Lucanus is more interested in this than he expects. Lucanus says: "So the
team that builds the better machine has an advantage over the team with the better driver?"
Daniel says yes, within limits. Lucanus says: "That seems like it would eventually just be
an engineering competition." Daniel says: "Sometimes it is." Lucanus says: "And people
watch this?" Daniel says: "Millions." Lucanus thinks about what millions of people watching
an engineering competition means. He does not say what he concludes.

**Baseball:** Bat, ball, bases, innings. He explains this with some nostalgia that he is
aware of as nostalgia. He tells them the names of teams he followed. He tells them about
specific games he watched. Both children are politely interested but Ulpia notices that
he is not explaining the game anymore; he is remembering something. She lets him go on
for a while. She does not interrupt.

**The Olympics:** He tells them about the modern Olympics — the same games but worldwide,
every country, every four years. The torch relay. The opening ceremony with all the nations
marching in. The scale of it — the largest athletic event in his world.

*Lucanus:* Immediately asks about the politics. Who decides who competes? What happens
when countries at war send athletes? Daniel tells him about the Olympic truce concept in
the modern games, about the boycotts, about times when politics ended a nation's
participation. Lucanus says: "So it's still Rome, with different names." Daniel says:
"Some things are."

*Ulpia:* Asks whether women competed. He says yes, fully in the modern games — the same
events, the same fields. She asks since when. He tells her the history of women's inclusion
in the modern Olympics. She notes that it took a while. He says: "Yes. It did." She
receives this without comment, which is its own comment.

#### History (His History)

**What happens to Rome:**

He tells both children. Not as prophecy — as history he knows, which he acknowledges may
have changed because of him. The western empire falls in ~476 AD. The eastern empire
continues until 1453 AD — more than a thousand years. He tells them about the sack of
Rome, about what comes before it, about the pressures that accumulate over centuries —
the Germanic migrations, the overextension, the succession crises, the economic strain.

*Lucanus:* Asks what the lawyers were doing in the final years. Daniel says the legal
tradition persisted well past the fall — Roman law influenced everything that came after
it, for centuries, including legal systems in Daniel's own world. Lucanus sits with this
for several days. He comes back and says: "So the law outlasted the empire." Daniel says:
"Yes." Lucanus says: "Then the empire wasn't the most important thing." This is as close
as Lucanus gets to a mission statement.

**The medieval period:** After the western empire falls, a long period when fewer people
read and write and the knowledge is held mostly by monasteries. Then a gradual recovery.
He tells them about the feudal system — lords and serfs, everything organized around land
and obligation, war as the dominant organizing principle.

**The Renaissance:** Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Galileo. A period when people
rediscover learning, build on it, and the rate of discovery accelerates. He tells both
children about Leonardo — the notebooks, the observations, the flying machines drawn
centuries before anyone built them. He tells Ulpia: "He kept notebooks the way you keep
notes. Trying to hold everything." She asks: "Did it work?" He says: "His notebooks
survived five hundred years and we could still read them." She says: "That's good." He
agrees. That's good.

**The Enlightenment:** Reason as the basis for knowledge. Locke, Newton (whose laws
Daniel has already taught), Voltaire. The idea that you could build knowledge systematically
and that tradition was not sufficient justification for anything.

*Lucanus:* Takes to the Enlightenment more than any other period. He asks Daniel to
explain it several times. He uses versions of Enlightenment arguments — without naming
them as such, without knowing they're called that — in law. Daniel hears about this
indirectly and does not know whether to be pleased or concerned. He decides pleased.

**World War One:** He explains what a world war is first — the entire world in the same
conflict. Twenty million dead. Trenches dug across hundreds of miles, soldiers living in
mud for years, dying in small increments for small gains. Poison gas. Artillery. The
absolute waste of it.

*Both children:* This is the first history that makes them go quiet in a way that isn't
processing. Lucanus asks who started it. Daniel says historians still argued about this
in his time. Lucanus says that seems important to know. Daniel says: "They tried." Lucanus
says: "But they argued about it." Daniel says: "Yes." Lucanus says: "Then the law wouldn't
have helped much." Daniel says: "The law wasn't really there yet, at the international
level." Lucanus says: "Then that's the next thing that needs building." He says this in
the same tone he uses for everything — concrete, unhurried — and then moves on.

**World War Two:** Bigger. Seventy to eighty million dead. The Holocaust — he tells them
about this carefully, because it requires telling about systematic organized killing on
an industrial scale, and there is no way to make this comprehensible or comfortable. He
tells them because they need to know that this is possible; that civilization is not
protection against it; that it happened in a world more advanced in almost every technical
measure than Rome. He also tells them the Allies won.

The atomic bomb. He explains nuclear fission — the same basic physics as fusion but in
reverse, splitting atoms rather than combining them, and the energy released is enormous.
He tells them that two bombs were dropped on two cities in Japan. He tells them the
casualty figures. He tells them the war ended shortly after.

*Lucanus (on the atom bomb):* He asked before — what do you call the people who built it
knowing what it was? Daniel still doesn't have a good answer. Lucanus says: "I've been
thinking about it. I don't think there is one answer. I think it depends what they thought
the alternative was." Daniel says: "That's the argument they made." Lucanus says: "Was
it true?" Daniel says: "Probably. Not entirely." Lucanus says: "Then that's the answer.
Some of them were right. Some of them were wrong. And they were the same people."

*Ulpia (on the Holocaust):* She asks: "How did ordinary people do this?" Daniel says:
"People have asked that question in my world for a hundred years. The answers are
complicated." She asks: "What are the answers?" He tells her what he knows — the
psychology of dehumanization, the structure of obedience, the way ordinary people can be
part of a system they don't have to see the whole of. She listens to all of this and then
says: "And Rome does none of this?" He doesn't answer immediately. She says: "I know. I'm
asking anyway." He says: "Rome does versions of this to people it has decided are not
quite people." She says: "Yes." She knows. She was asking to make sure he'd say it.

**The Cold War:** Two superpowers with enough nuclear weapons to end all human life, not
fighting directly but competing through proxy wars, intelligence operations, and the
space race. He explains what mutually assured destruction means — that neither side could
use the weapons without being destroyed in return, which meant neither used them. He
tells them this arrangement held for decades, which he still finds remarkable.

*Lucanus:* Says this is the strangest legal arrangement he's ever heard of. Daniel says
it wasn't exactly legal — there was no law that enforced it. Lucanus asks: "Then what
did?" Daniel says: "Fear. And the math." Lucanus says: "Fear enforced by math." He notes
this. He will use something like this argument later in a different context.

**The space race:** Two countries competing to be first in space and on the moon.
Americans won. July 20, 1969.

He has told them about the moon landing separately. He tells them now about the race —
the Soviets first in orbit, the Americans committing publicly to the moon, the engineering
effort, the failures along the way, the Saturn V rocket and what it took to build it.

*Ulpia:* Asks whether the Soviet cosmonauts were upset that they didn't get there first.
Daniel says the first Soviet cosmonaut who would have gone to the moon — Komarov — died
in a spacecraft accident before the race ended, and several others did too. She asks
whether they knew the risk. He says yes. She asks why they went. He says: "I think some
of them believed in what they were doing. Some were ordered. Some wanted to go." She asks:
"Which did you believe?" He says: "Mostly the first." She says: "That's a charitable read."
He says: "Yes."

**Future countries and geography:**

He draws a rough map of what the world looks like in 2000 AD. Both children compare it
to Roman maps. The differences are enormous — the Americas, Australia, the Arctic and
Antarctic mapped. He explains which areas have large populations, which are largely empty,
where the major cities are. He explains what a nation-state is — a political unit defined
by a border, a government, a shared (roughly) identity — and how different this is from
the Roman model of a city and its territories.

#### Stories and Fantasy

**Fairy tales:** The full European tradition — Cinderella, Snow White, Sleeping Beauty,
Rapunzel, Rumpelstiltskin, Hansel and Gretel, the real version. He tells these with their
original dark edges, not sanitized. The stepmother who dances in red-hot shoes. The wolf
who eats grandmother. Both children find the darkness familiar — Roman stories are not
gentle — but the texture is different from what they know.

*Ulpia:* Asks why so many fairy tales involve stepmothers as the villain. Daniel says he's
never had a good answer for that; scholars argued about it. She asks: "What did the
scholars say?" He tells her. She says: "That seems like projection." He says: "Scholars
said that too." She marks this as UNRESOLVED but notable.

**Shakespeare's plots:** Both children get these, at different ages. Ulpia's opinions
(Hamlet too slow; Romeo and Juliet both foolish; Titania manipulated, Oberon wrong) are
recorded above. Lucanus, older when he first gets them:

*Lucanus on Measure for Measure:* He becomes interested in this one specifically — a
judge who condemns a man for a crime the judge has also committed. He asks Daniel to tell
it again. He works out the legal argument from both sides. He says: "The play doesn't
resolve it properly." Daniel says: "No. That's intentional." Lucanus says: "Then the
playwright thought the law was inadequate here." Daniel says: "I think so." Lucanus says:
"He was right." He returns to Measure for Measure several times over the years.

**Jules Verne:** Around the World in Eighty Days, Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea,
Journey to the Center of the Earth. He tells these as adventure stories. Both children are
fascinated by the submarine. Ulpia asks how it's propelled; Lucanus asks where they're
going and whether they make it.

**Science fiction:** He tells Star Wars as a story — space, wars, a mystical force, a
father revealed, a small band against an empire. He doesn't mention he saw it in a
movie theater. He tells ET — a creature from another world, small, afraid, trying to get
home. He tells Jurassic Park — scientists who bring back dinosaurs using preserved genetic
material and then the dinosaurs escape. This lands especially hard after the dinosaur
conversations.

*Ulpia (on Jurassic Park):* Says: "So they made the thing and didn't think about what
would happen next." He says: "In the story, essentially." She says: "Is this a common
pattern?" He says: "Yes." She says: "Including with things you've built." He says: "Yes."
She says: "Have you thought about what happens next?" He says: "I think about it all the
time." She says: "Good." She does not ask what his conclusions are. He notes that she has
enough information to guess.

He tells them The Martian — an astronaut stranded on Mars, keeping alive by improvising.
He tells them 2001: A Space Odyssey — space travel, an alien artifact, a computer that
turns dangerous. He explains the genre of science fiction: stories that start from "what
if this were possible" and follow the logic. Both children understand this as a mode of
thought they already use. It has a name; that's new.

**Fairies:** The full European tradition — small winged creatures, magic, the Fae,
changelings, the deal you should never take. He tells them this tradition is different from
Roman mythology — it comes from the north of Europe, from cultures Rome has mostly
conquered or pushed back. He shows them how different cultures imagine similar things:
spirits of wild places, beings between human and divine, the danger of their attention.

*Ulpia:* Asks whether he believes in fairies. He says no. She asks whether the people who
told these stories believed in them. He says: "Some did. Some were using them to talk
about something else — the danger of the forest, strangers who look like neighbors, deals
you don't understand until too late." She says: "Then they're useful even if they're not
real." He says: "Yes." She says: "Like some of Roman religion." He says: "Yes." She says:
"I won't say that to anyone else." He says: "I know."

**Santa Claus:** He explains this carefully because it's strange to explain. A figure
associated with winter who brings gifts to children — based loosely on a historical bishop
named Nicholas, but the version children believed in is not historical. He describes the
costume, the reindeer, the chimney, the list of who has been good. He also has to explain
Christmas.

*Both children:* Find this one genuinely baffling. Lucanus says: "And children believed
this was real?" Daniel says: "Completely, until they didn't." Lucanus asks: "What happened
when they found out?" Daniel says: "Varies. Some children were very upset. Most were fine."
Ulpia asks: "Did you believe it?" He says: "For a while. Then I didn't." She asks: "Were
you upset?" He says: "No. By then I already understood that stories can be worth something
even when they're not true." She says: "Like the encyclopedia." He says: "The encyclopedia
is true." She says: "I know. I meant the other stories." He acknowledges the distinction.

**Dragons:** The European tradition — large fire-breathing reptiles, usually a danger to
be defeated. He compares this to Chinese dragons, which are different in character —
auspicious, water-associated, not automatically a threat. He shows them how different
cultures imagine the same type of creature and what that says about what the cultures
value and fear. He compares both to Roman serpents and monsters.

*Ulpia:* Asks whether any of them were real. He says large predators sometimes inspired
these stories — crocodiles, large snakes, possibly dinosaur fossils interpreted by people
who didn't know what fossils were. She asks: "Could you tell the story of what actually
happened to something — say, the fossil — and have it be as interesting as the dragon
story?" He says: "I think so. I think the true story is stranger." She says: "Then you
should tell it that way."

#### Daily Life in His Time

**What a city looks like in 2010 AD:** Skyscrapers — buildings thirty, fifty, a hundred
stories high, steel-framed and glass-faced, rising above the scale of anything Rome has.
Electric lights at night that make cities visible from far away, never going completely
dark. The noise — cars, machines, a background level of mechanical sound that never stops.
The density.

*Lucanus:* Asks how people orient themselves in a building of a hundred stories. Daniel
explains elevators — mechanical boxes that move vertically. Lucanus asks how. Daniel
explains the cable and counterweight system, later the electric motor. Lucanus works this
out and says: "So the height of a building stops being a practical limit." Daniel says:
"Yes, when you have the materials." Lucanus says: "And Rome doesn't." Daniel says: "Not
yet."

**Grocery stores and supermarkets:** Everything in one building, all year round, from all
over the world. You push a cart and choose. Priced, labeled, shelved. He compares this to
the Forum market but enclosed, climate-controlled, and staggeringly larger in variety.

*Ulpia:* Asks who decides the price. He explains: usually the seller, within what the
market will bear, within regulations. She asks who regulates. He gives her a partial
account of food safety regulation in his world. She asks: "Did it work?" He says:
"Better than nothing. Not perfectly." She says: "That's honest." He says: "I try."

**Hospitals:** Buildings dedicated entirely to medicine, with trained staff available at
all hours, equipment for diagnosis and surgery, wards for different conditions. He
explains what modern surgery involves — he cannot perform it, but he can describe what
it looks like and what it accomplishes. He tells them that in his world, operations
that would be fatal in Rome were routine survivable procedures.

*Ulpia:* Asks about surgery without anesthesia. He explains what anesthesia is —
rendering someone unconscious safely before cutting them. She asks how. He explains
the basic principle of general anesthesia without the specific drugs. She says: "This
is one of the things you could change, if you remembered more." He says: "I know the
principle. I don't know the compounds." She writes: "ANESTHESIA — Father knows concept,
not formula. HIGH PRIORITY when chemistry is further along."

**Schools:** Everyone goes, through approximately age eighteen. Taught by specialists
in each subject — mathematics by a mathematics teacher, history by a history teacher,
each domain separately trained. This is different from the Roman model of a single
tutor for everything.

*Lucanus:* Asks what happens to children who don't attend. In his time, Daniel explains,
attendance is compulsory — legally required. Lucanus asks who enforces this. Daniel explains.
Lucanus asks: "And the families accept this?" Daniel says mostly yes, because the
alternative — not being able to read — has become a serious disadvantage. Lucanus works
out the logic: "So the state makes education mandatory because uneducated citizens are
a problem for the state, not primarily for the citizen." Daniel says: "That's the
mechanism, yes. The justification given is usually that it helps the citizens." Lucanus
says: "Both can be true." Daniel says: "Yes. Both can be true."

**Television:** Moving pictures with sound, on a screen. You can watch events happening
on the other side of the world as they happen. He has tried to explain this before but
it always requires establishing electricity, transmission, screens. By the time they're
old enough for the full account they have enough background to follow it. He describes
watching the moon landing on television — sitting in a room, watching a screen, seeing
an image transmitted from the moon, in real time.

*Ulpia:* Asks what he watched when it wasn't the moon landing. He tells her. He mentions
specific shows; some of the titles come easily, some are blurry. She notices which come
easily and which blur. She does not say anything about this yet.

**What night looks like with electric light:** You can see as well as day. Cities never
go fully dark. He describes walking through a city at midnight and seeing clearly —
storefronts lit, streets bright, people going about their business. He describes what the
adjustment is like: that darkness, real darkness, is something people in his world rarely
experienced except deliberately.

*Lucanus:* Points out this would eliminate certain kinds of crime. Daniel says that's
been argued. It doesn't eliminate crime; it changes where crime happens. Lucanus notes
this as a pattern: "New tools change the shape of things. Not whether they happen."
Daniel says: "Yes." Lucanus says: "I've noticed that about what you build, too." He
says this without accusation. Daniel receives it without defense. They sit with it.

## SECTION 2b: Daniel's Lossy Memory

This is a thread, not a scene. It runs through everything.

Daniel's memory is not a recording. It is a human memory, twenty years or more distant
from the life it's trying to recall. Things that mattered get blurry. Things that didn't
matter get surprisingly clear — he can remember the exact color of his bedroom wall, but
not the second verse of a dozen songs. He can remember the plot of a movie but not the
name of the actor in it. He knows there was a song at a friend's birthday party that he
can almost hear, but only the melody, never the words.

**Specific examples of what he can't fully reconstruct:**

- There was a movie with a speech near the end, a character standing somewhere significant
  and saying something about what you leave behind. He knows the shape of the speech. He
  can almost quote the first sentence. The exact words won't come. He has written the shape
  of it in the encyclopedia with a note: "Something like this. Not quite right."

- A song he sang to his children — the second verse is simply gone. He knows where it should
  be. He hums through it every time and the words don't arrive. He has now been humming
  through the same gap for twenty years.

- The exact date of a specific historical event he is fairly certain of but not certain
  enough to be unqualified. He writes it down with a note: "~1453; I believe this is right
  but I would not bet heavily on the year."

- A word in English — a specific technical term, something to do with a mechanical process —
  that he uses regularly in his own thinking but cannot produce when he's trying to write
  it down. He writes around it every time. He has a placeholder in the encyclopedia:
  "the process by which [TERM I CANNOT RECALL]." The placeholder has been there for
  fifteen years.

- He knows a piece of music — orchestral, something his mother played — well enough to
  hum eight bars with confidence. After eight bars, he diverges. He doesn't know if he's
  inventing the rest or misremembering it. He has never committed the missing bars to
  paper because he can't tell which they are.

**Ulpia's response to the lossy memory:**

She notices this before she says anything about it. She starts keeping a list, in English,
of things he half-remembers — classified as: things he knows solidly, things he knows with
a flag, things he knows the shape of but not the content, and things that are simply gone.
She has been doing this for two years before she mentions it to him.

When she mentions it, she shows him the list. It is longer than he expected. He looks at
the "simply gone" category. He says: "I thought I remembered that one." She says: "You
told me a version of it three times. The versions don't match." He says: "Which version
is right?" She says: "I don't know. None of them might be. I flagged all three as
uncertain." He looks at the list for a while. He says: "This is useful. Keep doing this."
She says: "I already decided to." He says: "I know. I'm saying I want you to." She
acknowledges this. She keeps doing it.

By the time she is his collaborator on the encyclopedia, the lossy-memory tracking is part
of her editorial method. Everything he says in their sessions gets cross-referenced against
previous versions of the same claim. When they don't match, she notes both. When he can't
resolve the discrepancy, she marks it: "DISCREPANCY — Father gave two versions; he cannot
now determine which is correct; record both; treat neither as definitive."

This is her contribution to the encyclopedia's method. It is also, quietly, its most
important feature — the only version of what Daniel knows that is honest about the
difference between what Daniel actually remembers and what Daniel is guessing.

### Age 14–16, The Truth-Telling (~122–124 AD for Lucanus; ~132–134 AD for Ulpia)

**Lucanus first:**

Lucanus is told the full truth sometime in his middle teens — approximately 122–124 AD,
when he is 14–16. He has already been given everything by this point. The disclosure
is not a revelation of new information; it is a revelation of context. He knows about
the future, about Daniel's world, about everything on the knowledge catalog. He just
didn't know the full framing: that Daniel is not from elsewhere in the Roman world but
from a different time entirely.

**How the conversation goes with Lucanus:**

Daniel tells him in English, in the study. He tells the facts: when he was born, how he
arrived, that he has told no one the complete thing. He tells Lucanus that he has given
him everything — everything on that list — and Lucanus can do with it what he decides.

Lucanus listens to all of this. He is quiet for a long time. Then he asks:

"What does this mean you owe Rome?"

Not: *what was the world like?* Not: *do you want to go back?* He asks what Daniel owes.

Daniel says: "What I've given it. What I'm giving it."

Lucanus says: "Is that enough?"

Daniel says he doesn't know. He thinks it might be all there is.

Lucanus says: "I think you owe it more. But I don't know what that looks like from where
you are, so I'm not going to tell you." Then he asks: "What do you owe me?" Daniel says:
"Everything I've told you. And to keep telling you what I know." Lucanus says: "Then
we're fine." He means it. He goes back to whatever he was working on before the
conversation.

Daniel sits with this for days. It is the most Lucanus thing possible: obligations
assessed, decision made, conversation complete.

**Ulpia, told later (~132–134 AD):**

See Section 3 for the full structural note on the truth-telling scene with Ulpia. The
scene is different from Lucanus's. She asks different questions. She processes differently.
Where Lucanus asked what Daniel owes, Ulpia asks what the world was like.

The two disclosures are parallel, separated by a decade, and they illuminate the difference
between the children: Lucanus hears the truth and immediately asks what it requires;
Ulpia hears the truth and immediately asks what it meant.

### Age 14–16 (~132–134 AD): The Truth — Ulpia

This is the scene the arc has been building toward. See Section 3 for the full structural
note.

The teaching continues through and after the truth-telling. The disclosure changes its
register: she is no longer a student receiving what her father decides to share. She is
a person who knows what she is working with. The sessions become more like editing
sessions than lessons.

She begins keeping her own notes in English. Not just receiving what he writes for her,
but writing down questions, marking gaps, noting where his knowledge becomes uncertain.
She has his habit of the hooked mark (she invents it independently, or inherits it; he
cannot tell). She uses it to flag things he has told her that he has also flagged as
uncertain.

### Age 16–20 (~134–138 AD): Peer

By the time she is sixteen, their sessions look different. She brings him questions he
cannot answer. She identifies gaps in the encyclopedia's structure that he hadn't noticed.
She pushes him on things he has stated confidently that she suspects are imprecise.

"You told me the periodic table has over a hundred elements. You can give me about thirty.
What happened to the other seventy?"

"I don't remember them. I knew them once. They're gone."

"Should I note that the table is incomplete in what you've written?"

"Yes. Note it."

She notes it. She adds her own note: "Father believes approximately one hundred basic
substances exist; has given us approximately thirty; believes the remainder are
retrievable from first principles in a better-equipped laboratory. This should not be
taken as a complete enumeration."

This is her voice. Not his. She has internalized the method — acknowledge what you know,
mark what you don't, don't let the gaps be mistaken for completeness — and is applying
it to his own work.

Daniel finds this almost unbearably useful and also slightly humbling. She is more
systematic than he is about the limits of his own knowledge. He was trained in the habit
of marking uncertainty; she applies it more rigorously than he does.

### Lucanus: Growing Away Without Growing Apart

Lucanus's distance from the workshop and the enterprise is gradual and not unfriendly.
He chooses law — first rhetoric, then practice, then standing as an advocate. He is good
at it in the way Daniel expects: the arguments have structure, the principles are stated
explicitly, the reasoning is followed to its conclusion. Other lawyers find him demanding
to argue against because he states his premises first and then holds everyone, himself
included, to the implications.

His relationship with Daniel is warm but different from Daniel's relationship with Ulpia.
He comes home for meals, asks what Daniel is working on, listens with genuine interest,
and does not offer to help. He is not cold; he is not distant. He has simply chosen a
different room in the house. Daniel understands this. He would not have wanted Lucanus
to choose otherwise.

The one thing Lucanus never lets lapse: he checks in on the catalog of what Daniel knows
and doesn't know. Not to add to it himself; to make sure someone is managing it. When
Ulpia takes this over, Lucanus asks her once if she has it in hand. She says yes. He
says: "Good." He does not ask again. He trusts that if she says yes, she means it, which
is a form of respect that she notices.

He knows as much as Ulpia about the future and modern knowledge. He simply does different
things with it. Ulpia becomes an encyclopedia keeper. Lucanus becomes a Roman lawyer with
arguments that have a particular shape. Both of these are Daniel's children, doing what
Daniel gave them, in the only way they could.

---

### Age 20–35+ (~138–153 AD): Keeper

After Hadrian dies (~138 AD) and the ocean program loses its champion, Daniel begins the
explicit work of securing the encyclopedia against his own death. Ulpia is the primary
keeper of the English-language version. She edits it. She adds to it. She knows what he
knows and what he does not know, and she keeps the distinction with more precision than
he would have kept it himself.

Their conversations in this phase are the most equal they have ever been. She has read
everything he has written. She has asked him to reconstruct, from memory, things he
thought were gone, and recovered more than he expected. She has also found things in his
encyclopedia that she can update from her own observation — medical practices she has seen
Zoticus apply that have diverged from what Daniel originally described; engineering
developments in the workshop that have advanced past his initial framing.

Some of their best conversations are in English, about things he cannot say in Latin
because Latin does not have the words. Concepts in mathematics that he can only express
using English notation. Principles from physics that require a vocabulary Rome has not
developed. The private language is not just personal now — it is technically necessary.
Some of what he knows can only be said in the words he learned it in.

In his last years, when his eyes fail and his hands are less reliable, she reads his notes
back to him. She reads the encyclopedia. She reads his own handwriting to him when he
cannot make it out. He corrects errors in her reading. She corrects errors in his original
text. They are, by this point, collaborating on a document neither of them could have
produced alone.

## SECTION 3: The Truth-Telling Scene — Ulpia

**Structural placement:** ch43 or ch44 territory (~132–136 AD, Ulpia ~14–16)

**Setting:** His private study, or the garden in the evening when the household is quieter.
In English. This is important: the full truth gets said in their private language, not in
Latin. Latin is the language of Rome, of public life, of everything he performs. English
is for this.

**Tone:** The scene is NOT sentimental and NOT dramatic. He has been building toward it
for years and he is still not sure how to say it. He says the facts. She asks questions.
The questions are good. He answers them.

Note on the parallel: Lucanus has already been told. Whether this disclosure happens in
the same chapter or elsewhere, it is worth noting that Daniel told Lucanus first, by a
decade, and that Lucanus's question was *what do you owe?* This context shapes how Daniel
tells Ulpia — he is not doing this for the first time. He knows something about how it
goes.

**What he tells her:**

He tells her the year he was born — approximately 1993 AD (he has to translate this; he
uses the Julian calendar to work back and tells her "roughly nineteen hundred years from
now, by the calendar Romans will eventually adopt; I can show you the calculation"). He
tells her what the world was like — briefly, because she already has pieces of this from
years of stories. He tells her how he arrived: he doesn't know. He tells her that he has
told no one the full truth. He names who knows partial truths.

"Heras guessed at the depth of what I know. Tyche has learned English and knows the
cipher is real. Your mother knows I am not what I seem. None of them know this. You are
the only person I have told the complete thing." He pauses. "And Lucanus, who you should
know I told first. He asked what I owed. He has handled it exactly as you'd expect."

She considers this. She asks: "What did you tell him you owed?" He tells her. She asks:
"Do you agree with him that it's not enough?" He says: "Sometimes." She says: "Good."
She doesn't elaborate. She asks her next question.

He tells her she does not have to believe him. He tells her she can check. Everything he
has told her about what is coming — he has been right about the parts that have already
happened in her lifetime. The rest will happen or it won't. She can watch.

**Her response:**

She does not weep. She does not exclaim. She processes.

Then she asks specific questions:

*How does he know he didn't imagine it?* He shows her things — English notes he made in
the first weeks, his own handwriting in a script she can read, notes so specific and
internally consistent over the first days that confabulation seems less likely than the
alternative. He describes his phone — a small black device that held more information
than the largest library in Rome, that he exhausted in a matter of weeks, that is now
buried with him when he dies. He describes it with enough specificity — the exact things
he searched for, the sequences of what he found, the way the battery depleted — that she
cannot easily construct an alternative explanation.

*What happened to the people he knew?* He tells her about his family: parents, a sister,
the neighborhood he grew up in, the school he attended, the specific small irrelevant
things he remembers — a friend's name, a teacher who was kind, a dog he walked twice a
week. These are not strategic. They are just true, and telling them to her is the closest
he comes in the scene to something that isn't information.

*Do you want to go back?* He gives her an honest answer. He says: "I wanted to for a
long time. Now — I have a life here. You are here. Lucanus is here. I don't know anymore
what it would mean to want to go back. I don't know if it would be possible even if I
knew how. What I know is that I am not going back." This is not a triumphant resolution.
It is the honest state of a question he has stopped asking.

*Why are you telling me this now?* He says: "Because you are old enough to hold it, and
because I am not going to live forever, and because what I know should not die with me
in a form no one can use."

*What was the world like?* This is the question Lucanus didn't ask. She asks it. He
answers it. They are there for a long time.

**After the scene:**

The education shifts register. She is no longer a student receiving what he decides to
share. She begins asking questions he has been avoiding — not because she is cruel but
because she is thorough and the gaps she can see are real.

"You've never written down the full sequence for soap-making at scale. Why not?"

"I thought Heras had it."

"Heras is dead. Zoticus has a version. Is Zoticus's version correct?"

He tests it against his memory. He tells her what is right and what is approximate.
She writes it down. This is the new mode.

## SECTION 4: What She Does and Doesn't Tell Others

**She tells no one the full truth.** This is not a decision she agonizes over. It is a
decision she makes once and holds without revisiting. The full truth is not useful to
anyone who couldn't already use it, and the people who could use it — the cipher keepers,
the people building the ladder — already have what they need from the encyclopedia. The
story of where Daniel came from would become a distraction, a legend, something to argue
about. She doesn't want it to become a legend. She wants the work to survive.

**Lucanus knows and keeps it the same way.** He has never told anyone. He does not plan
to. He considers it, as he considers everything, from the angle of what it would produce.
He decides it would produce the wrong things and so he doesn't. He and Ulpia do not
explicitly discuss this with each other; they both simply don't tell anyone, and when
they're in the same room both not-telling, they can see that the other isn't either.
This is a form of solidarity that requires no words.

**She tells Marcia** that Daniel taught her things he doesn't discuss with everyone, that
their private sessions covered more than Latin letters and household management. Marcia
already suspected this. She does not push. There is an unspoken acknowledgment between
them that this is Daniel's corner of the household and Marcia's interest in it is limited
to its not causing trouble.

**She tells Tyche very little.** Their relationship is one of mutual respect and mutual
wariness. They both know they are the two women who will carry Daniel's work forward.
They are not rivals, but they are not close. Tyche knows English; Ulpia knows the full
truth of Daniel's origins. Neither woman tells the other what she knows. This is a
deliberate, unspoken arrangement that neither of them acknowledges.

**The private language in public:** She and Daniel sometimes use English in public settings —
a word, a phrase, a reference that means something only they know. This is not coded
conversation about the encyclopedia or about the future. It is just: they have a private
language and sometimes they use it. It is visible as nothing more than a father and
daughter with an unusual bond. Anyone who hears it assumes it is Daniel's "homeland
dialect," a Thulean peculiarity inherited by his daughter. Nobody thinks much about it.

**One specific example that can appear on the page:** She addresses a question to him at
a workshop gathering, in English, about something technical. He answers in English. Everyone
present assumes they are using a private dialect and carries on with the conversation.
What they have actually done is hold a technical consultation about something in the
encyclopedia, in front of a room full of people, with no one the wiser. This is one of
the very few moments in Daniel's life in Rome where he is not performing.

## SECTION 5: The Playground as Character Signal

**Placement:** Part V, early — the structure is built ~111–115 AD, primarily for Lucanus;
by the time Ulpia is old enough to use it, it is simply there. The signal beat probably
fits in ch35–36 area (~121–122 AD), when a visitor notices it.

**The beat:**

A workshop visitor or neighbor comes to the house on business. On the way through the
garden or past the garden wall, they see the structure — the swing, the slide, the monkey
bars, the sandbox. It is not enormous. It is not alarming. It is simply unusual: wooden,
deliberate, not a game set up in the moment but something built to be there, and slightly
worn from use.

The visitor asks what it is. Daniel says: "My children play on it." The visitor looks at
it again — at the seat on the swing, the inclined board with its oiled surface, the
horizontal bars between the two uprights — and asks nothing further, or asks "where did
you get the design?" Daniel says something brief and noncommittal. The conversation moves
on.

He built it because he knows what a swing feels like. Because he wanted their childhoods
to have something. Because these things exist where he comes from and they can be built
from wood and a carpenter doesn't need to be told what the pieces are for. He did not
build it for Rome. He built it for them.

**One paragraph. One observation. One small exchange. Then it is just there.**

Do not expand this. Do not make it a scene about Daniel's grief for his own childhood.
The playground is not a grief scene. It is a character-signal scene: this is a man who
builds slides and monkey bars for his children in a world that doesn't have them, without
explaining why. Pamphilus built the slide without asking what it was. That is also correct.

## SECTION 7: What NOT to Do

**Do not make Ulpia preternaturally wise.** She is smart, relentless, and direct. She is
also a child until she isn't, and then a young Roman woman with Roman assumptions she has
not entirely shed. She will defer to authority in social situations even while privately
disagreeing. She will accept things that Daniel has marked uncertain without being able
to resolve them. She will ask good questions but not always the right ones. Her specific
voice from the character file: "the relentless questioner — states things flat, the way
she says a sum; pushes at a problem certain there is a true figure." That is her gift.
It is not omniscience.

**Do not have Lucanus be the wiser sibling.** He is perceptive and his questions are real,
but he cannot out-philosophize Daniel either. He says things that are true in a child-
accidental way sometimes. Daniel can notice it. Daniel must not canonize it as the day's
deepest utterance. Rule 5 from the character file applies to both children.

**Do not present Lucanus's choice of law as a loss.** He knows everything. He chose law.
That is a complete statement about who he is. The document that explains he "doesn't carry
the ladder" is descriptively accurate but should not carry a tone of tragedy. He is
carrying something. It's just a different thing.

**Do not have the truth-telling scene become a weeping reunion.** It is two people having
a factual conversation about an impossible thing. He tells her facts. She asks questions.
The questions are good. He answers honestly. She does not perform amazement. She processes
and then works. If there is any emotion in the scene, it belongs to Daniel, briefly, not
to her — and even then it should be implied, not stated.

**Do not have either child go around correcting Romans with future knowledge.** Both of
them keep the secret. Ulpia uses what she knows to maintain the encyclopedia. Lucanus
uses what he knows in the shape of his arguments. Neither announces where the ideas came
from. Neither is a prophet.

**Do not make Daniel's lessons explicit school scenes with him lecturing.** They are
conversations. Ulpia asks questions he doesn't expect. He answers what he can and marks
what he can't. She pushes on the marks. With Lucanus, the conversations are more lateral —
he asks what things imply rather than what they are, and Daniel follows the implication
rather than leading the lesson. Neither child learns from a curriculum.

**Do not make the playground a major set piece.** One paragraph. One note from a visitor
or neighbor. Then the swing and the slide and the monkey bars are just there, in the
garden, being used.

**Do not have Daniel know everything.** He was in high school when he left. He knows
algebra but not advanced mathematics. He knows Newton's laws but not general relativity.
He knows the first two rows of the periodic table with confidence and becomes increasingly
uncertain after that. He knows the plots of books he's read but not the exact text of
most of them. He has a movie with a speech he can almost quote. He has a second verse
missing from a song. His memory is lossy. Both children know this about him. Ulpia tracks
it. Lucanus accepts it as a constraint and works within it.

**Do not let the Naming Note cause confusion.** Tyche's freed name is "Ulpia Tyche" — a
nomen she refuses and is never called on the page. Ulpia Severa is Daniel's daughter.
They are different people. The friction belongs to Tyche's nomen only.

## A NOTE ON VOICE IN ULPIA SCENES

She speaks more than Tyche and less than Marcia. She does not have Tyche's habit of
stating the damning fact and stopping. She does not have Marcia's habit of building a
long structural argument with every side laid out. She asks the question that needs to
be asked, waits for the answer, and asks the next question. She is, in Daniel's words
to no one, the only person he's ever talked to who makes him feel like the conversation
is going somewhere.

The private language between them is a register shift for Daniel. When he speaks English
with her, he sounds slightly more like himself than he does in Latin — looser, less
performed, more likely to reach for a modern reference and actually be understood. She
catches the references. She does not always understand them, but she notes them and asks
later. She keeps a list, in English, of words and phrases Daniel has used that she cannot
find in anything he has written for her. She intends to ask him about all of them. She
does not always get to.

**A note on Lucanus's voice in English:** He speaks English competently and without the
warmth that Ulpia finds in it. He is more likely to use it precisely than naturally —
he thinks in Latin and translates. Ulpia thinks in both. This is audible to Daniel when
they speak: Lucanus's English is more careful, more formal, slightly lagged. He doesn't
mind this. He learned the language at six, not from birth. The difference is small but
Daniel can hear it.

The last thing Daniel says to Ulpia in the narrative is technical. That is correct.

The last thing he says to Lucanus is warmer and shorter and not about the work. That is
also correct.
