# Reviewer context for the chapter

This file is for reviewers. It exists so you don't have to explore a
repository to evaluate the chapter. Read this and the chapter file only.

## What you are reading

A single chapter from a long YA-adjacent novel. The target reader is a
9th grader (roughly 14 years old). Plain readable prose is the goal, not
literary fireworks.

## Setup, briefly

The narrator, Daniel "Danny" Mercer, is an ordinary American
seventeen-year-old (high-school junior, hardware-store job, ~3.0 GPA,
good at math, average at everything else) who is somehow transported
back in time to Rome in 98 AD. He is not a prodigy. He is not a secret
genius. The premise of the book is that an ordinary modern teenager
with a standard high-school education could move history if he survived
long enough and had the nerve, the credibility, the language, and the
money. The bottleneck is never IQ.

Daniel narrates this in close, immediate first-person past tense. He
is the same teenager telling it from a small remove of days or weeks,
not decades. There is no old-man-looking-back vantage. The narration
must NOT read as a polished memoir composed in old age. The narration
voice is plain, immediate, slightly more ordered than his in-scene
panic — but still the kid's voice, not a mature stylist's. No
"years later," no "I would come to learn," no "I know now."

Spoken dialogue should be even rougher than narration: hedged,
contraction-heavy, incomplete, panicky.

## Where in the story this chapter falls

This is roughly chapter six. By this point:

- Daniel has been in Rome about a year.
- He is still effectively enslaved by a Roman household; he was given
  to a workshop loft after he proved useful (he drew the world map and
  showed Arabic numerals to a Greek physician named Heras).
- He has learned passable conversational Latin by humiliation and
  immersion. He is not fluent.
- His smartphone died at the end of chapter 4. He has no notes.
- He has tried two things and had them shrug-rejected: Arabic numerals
  (educated Romans squinted at them and went back to tally marks), and
  germ theory (Heras called it dangerous folk-superstition).
- At the end of the previous chapter, lying in his loft above the
  household fire, he felt warm air rising through the floor and
  realized he could build a hot-air balloon out of scrap cloth.

## This chapter's beat

Daniel tries to build the balloon. He has no money, no workshop, no
materials of his own, and no precision tools. He attempts four
versions, fails three, and partially flies one. He burns himself. He
burns up a length of cloth a friend funded. He still hasn't really
flown anything anyone outside the slaughter-lot has seen.

## The two other named people in this chapter

- **Stichus**: a ten-year-old water-boy / errand-runner attached to
  the household. Brings Danny rag scraps. Holds the balloon string.
- **Heras**: the educated Greek physician (~mid 40s), Danny's nearest
  thing to a friend. Dry, ironic, well-spoken Greek-into-Latin voice.
  Funds one round of materials out of curiosity.
