### MINOR

**[MACER — Ch33]**
Scene: Macer refuses to help Daniel during the Hadrian succession crisis.
- Macer drops his voice — "Macer does not drop his voice, Macer says the crude thing at the volume of a man selling timber." The narrative explicitly flags this as out of character, then allows it.
- The absence of the "boy" and "Thulean" nicknames is described as "the nearest thing to fondness he let himself."
- His parting use of "boy" is described as "not unkind" — assigning affective content to a word Macer uses instrumentally throughout.
- His actions remain entirely self-interested (he gives Daniel nothing, shuts the door). The drift is in the explanatory framing, not the behavior. Daniel's retrospective note ("He had warned me") re-establishes the moral complexity without sentimentality, keeping the scene within the arc.
- **Fix threshold:** Low-impact; worth tightening "nearest thing to fondness he let himself" and "not unkind" to preserve ambiguity. The scene does not need to be rewritten — only the interpretive gloss narrowed.

**[TYCHE — Ch20 / Ch41 — Nomen friction underdelivered]**
Bible note: "by law her freed name is 'Ulpia Tyche' but she REFUSES 'Ulpia' and is only ever 'Tyche' on the page; the imposed nomen is a lasting friction."
- The text correctly never uses "Ulpia Tyche" for her. The refusal is implicit.
- Ch20: "your bought name on me" — she calls the freed name Daniel's possession, not hers. The actual nomen "Ulpia" is not spoken by either party.
- Ch41: "freed by a man whose name the law had stapled to her against her will and who signed nothing with it" — narrative description only, not scene.
- In 53 chapters, no one calls her "Ulpia" to her face and is corrected or met with silence.
- Secondary structural note (not a voice drift): Daniel's daughter is named "Ulpia Severa" (ch38 onward), which means "Ulpia" appears frequently in late chapters as an unproblematic name, inadvertently softening the association with Tyche's imposed and refused nomen.
- **Fix threshold:** One brief scene — someone using the full freed name, Tyche responding with a single syllable or silence — would fulfill the bible's "lasting friction" without disrupting the arc.

### BORDERLINE (NOTED, NOT FLAGGED)

**[HERAS — Ch24, late scene]**
The "tube that bursts" conscience speech runs slightly longer than Heras's usual mode and its function as the book's skeptical conscience becomes visible as a function. The *manner* (mild, looking at the river, not polemical) holds register. Not a drift; worth awareness on any future revision pass.

**[HERAS — Ch38, irony-drop]**
The text explicitly marks this as exceptional ("the thing he did rarely"), which preserves the register by naming the absence. The speech is close to inspirational — not Heras's mode — but is ended by the habitual dismissive warmth ("Now go home, it is late, and I am too old to comfort a barbarian past the second cup"). Held.

**[TYCHE — Ch22, wedding gift line]**
"He's careless with money but he's not a liar, watch the first and trust the second" — said to Marcia, not Daniel; operational rather than sentimental; the utility-first voice is preserved. Borderline warmth only.

## 3. Cross-Character Patterns

**A. Interior softness narrated too explicitly (the "visible subtext" problem)**
The one genuine drift (Macer, ch33) and the two borderline flags (Heras, ch38; Tyche, ch22) all share the same mechanism: a character whose emotional register is defined by suppression or obliqueness has their interior made slightly too readable by the narrating voice. Macer's "nearest thing to fondness," Heras's irony-drop, Tyche's wedding gift — in each case the suppression is what makes the character work, and the narrator stepping in to name it reduces the load-bearing ambiguity. The pattern to watch: when Daniel's narration explains what a secondary character is *really* feeling, it risks doing for the reader something the text has otherwise trained the reader to do for themselves.

**B. Conscience-speech verging on authorial mouthpiece**
Heras's ch24 high-ground speech and Apollodorus's ch37 "you fold your true things smaller" speech are both moments where a secondary character delivers the novel's central thematic argument in concentrated form. In Apollodorus's case it is entirely earned by twenty years of accumulated character logic. In Heras's case it is mostly earned but the scene's function is slightly too visible. Neither requires a fix, but both belong to the same authorial tendency: when the book needs its argument stated, it reaches for the secondary character with the sharpest tongue and the least personal stake. The risk is that these characters become occasion rather than agent. Both are currently on the right side of the line.

**C. Bible-mandated friction handled obliquely**
Two bible-specified frictions are present in the text as narrative description rather than dramatized scene: Tyche's refusal of the "Ulpia" nomen (flagged above) and — to a lesser degree — her ongoing anger at Daniel for the years she read the cipher as a slave who could be sold for knowing it (ch47 handles this, but it surfaces once rather than recurring as a friction). The pattern is consistent: the author is more comfortable carrying the mandated friction as a note in narrative consciousness than as direct dramatic conflict. This produces clean prose and risks underdelivering on what the bible calls "lasting." One scene each — not a revision of the whole — would close the gap.
