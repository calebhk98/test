# *The Long Way Home* — Daniel's Food Arc

**Read this alongside:** `V2_MASTER_CONTEXT.md`, `V2_DANIEL_FINANCES.md`, `V2_NEW_WORLD_CONTACT.md`

**Purpose:** Reference document for the food thread across all 53 chapters. Food is
not a subplot. It is character texture — a recurring private register that tracks
Daniel's psychological state, his relationship to normalcy, and his practical
engagement with Roman material life. The chapter assignments in Section 7 specify
where food beats belong. Everything else is reference.

---

## SECTION 1: What Rome Has in 98 AD

### Grains

**Wheat:** The staple. Romans distinguish hard wheat (durum, used for porridge/puls
and bread) from soft wheat. The best bread wheat is expensive; the urban poor eat
more puls than bread. Durum wheat is critical: it has a higher gluten content than
soft wheat and — though Romans do not know this — it is exactly what makes pasta
work. The prerequisite is there from day one.

**Barley:** Lower-status grain, associated with slaves and soldiers' punishment rations.
Also used for brewing a rough fermented drink (posca-adjacent).

**Emmer (farro):** Ancient grain, old-fashioned by 98 AD but still used, especially
for puls. Nutty flavor. Daniel will eventually recognize emmer flour in some recipes.

**Spelt:** More common than soft wheat in northern provinces; hearty.

**Millet:** Used. Less prestigious.

**Summary:** Rome has everything needed for pasta, bread, porridge, and grain-based
cooking. The gluten infrastructure is present.

---

### Fats and Sauces

**Olive oil:** Abundant and high-quality, especially from the Spanish and North African
provinces. The best grades are used for cooking, cheaper grades for lamp fuel. Romans
cook in olive oil the way modern Americans cook with butter or vegetable oil. It is
everywhere and inexpensive in Rome. Daniel will never lack it.

**Garum/liquamen:** Fermented fish sauce. Made from fish guts salted in large vessels
and left to ferment for months; the liquid that drains off is garum. Its smell is
extraordinary and not initially pleasant to a modern nose. It is added to nearly every
savory Roman dish in place of or alongside salt — functioning as both seasoning and
umami amplifier. There are quality grades from cheap to premium (the best garum from
Spain and Carthage was expensive and prized). Romans also have muria (lighter brine
runoff) and allec (the paste remaining after garum production).

**For Daniel:** Garum is his single most important psychological food hurdle. It smells
wrong, it looks wrong, and it is on everything. Once he gets past that, he will recognize
it as functionally identical to Southeast Asian fish sauce, which is to say: a powerful
umami foundation for almost any savory cooking. The evolution from "this is disgusting"
to "okay this is fish sauce" to "I can work with this" is one of the food arc's core beats.

---

### Proteins

**Pork:** The Roman favorite. Every part used — salt-cured pork, sausages (botellus,
lucanica), smoked and cured hams, offal. Roman charcuterie is sophisticated and Daniel
has nothing to add to it. He will not improve Roman pork preparations; he can only enjoy
them or not.

**Lamb and goat:** Common, especially in the east and outside the city. Cheaper than pork.

**Chicken:** Common and affordable. Hens kept in urban households. Eggs widely available
as a result.

**Beef:** Less common than pork; cattle were more valuable as draft animals. Available but
not the default meat choice.

**Fish:** Rome is a maritime culture. Fresh fish from the coast, preserved fish (salted,
dried, smoked) available inland. Oysters were a luxury. The garum production means fish
are processed at scale.

**Game:** Venison, hare, boar, various birds. Available at market. Daniel will encounter
roasted game birds and find them recognizable.

**Eggs:** Widely available because chickens are common. Romans used eggs in sauces, in
baking, in binding, but less often as the main event of a meal. A fried egg or an
omelette as the centerpiece of a dish would be unusual.

---

### Legumes

Lentils, chickpeas (cicer), fava beans (the oldest cultivated legume in the Mediterranean),
and various dried peas. Lower-status food in general, but Roman cooking uses them
extensively in soups, purees, and as protein fillers.

---

### Vegetables

**Available:** Cabbage (very common; often boiled to extinction), leeks, onions, garlic,
turnips, carrots (yes — Romans had carrots, though they were often purple or white rather
than orange), cucumbers, lettuce, celery (a flavoring herb as much as a vegetable),
asparagus (a luxury), beets, radishes.

**Absent:** Tomatoes, potatoes, sweet potatoes, peppers (all New World — none of these
exist in the Roman world). Aubergine/eggplant barely present at the Empire's eastern
edge. No corn on the cob, no green beans as known today.

**For Daniel:** The vegetable situation is the most disorienting aspect of Roman food.
The absence of tomatoes means no tomato sauce, no pizza (as he knows it), no ketchup.
The absence of potatoes means no fries, no potato soup, no mashed potatoes. He will feel
this as a persistent low-level wrongness for years.

---

### Fruit

Grapes (fresh and dried as raisins, and as wine), figs (fresh and dried), apples, pears,
plums, pomegranates, dates, quinces, cherries, mulberries. Citrus exists at the
Empire's edges (lemons and citrons in the Near East and North Africa) but is not common
in Rome itself.

---

### Dairy

**Cheese:** Many types, varying by region. Soft fresh cheeses (similar to ricotta), aged
harder cheeses, smoked varieties. Cheese is common and affordable.

**Milk:** Fresh milk is rare and culturally suspect in Rome. Romans considered drinking
fresh milk a barbarian practice (it was associated with pastoral peoples on the
periphery). Cheese and butter appear, but a glass of fresh milk is not Roman.

**For Daniel:** He will not be offered fresh milk. If he wants dairy, it is cheese.

---

### Spices and Herbs

**Pepper:** Black pepper, imported from India via the spice trade. Expensive but
available in Rome — a standard luxury spice in upper-class cooking. The amount used
in Roman recipes is striking to modern taste; some elite Roman dishes call for pepper
the way Americans call for salt.

**Cumin, coriander, fennel, dill:** All widely used. Fennel is practically Roman national
seasoning.

**Lovage:** A celery-like herb used constantly in Roman cooking; most prominent in
Apicius. Strong, distinctive flavor.

**Rue:** Bitter herb used as seasoning; modern cooks rarely use it. Romans did.

**Silphium:** Extinct by the late first century. Asafoetida (hing) is the approximate
substitute; available in eastern markets.

**Mint, oregano, thyme, savory, marjoram:** All present and widely used.

**Anise, caraway:** Used in bread and sauces.

**Ginger:** Known, available as an import. More common in medical use than culinary.

**What's absent:** Chili peppers (New World). Vanilla (New World). Many of the sweet
spices that entered Europe later via the spice trade (nutmeg and mace were barely
known in 98 AD Rome; cinnamon was rare and expensive; cloves even rarer).

---

### Sweeteners

**Honey:** The Roman sweetener. There is no cane sugar in the Roman world; beet sugar
had not been developed. Everything sweet is sweetened with honey or with grape must
reduced to various concentrations (defrutum = heavy reduction; sapa = lighter). Carob
was known. Date syrup available in the east.

**For Daniel:** He will miss sugar acutely. Sweetness is available but the palette is
narrower — everything sweet tastes like honey, which is not a bad thing, but it is not
the same. Desserts are possible; they are just honey-based.

---

### Wine and Drink

Wine is everywhere. Romans drink it watered (water-to-wine ratios varying by occasion
and class; drinking undiluted wine was considered barbarian). Posca (vinegary wine
mixed with water) is a common everyday drink. Beer exists in the northern provinces
but is not Roman in character.

Water, if boiled and filtered (Daniel will insist on this from the start), is available.

---

### What Is Absent — The Complete New World List

Items Daniel cannot have until the Atlantic expeditions return (~115+ AD, with
agricultural establishment adding 1–2 seasons of lead time after arrival):

- **Tomatoes** — no tomato sauce, no ketchup, no pizza as he knows it
- **Potatoes** — no fries, no potato soup, no mashed potatoes (Andean origin; may not
  arrive until second or third expedition wave, if at all in Daniel's lifetime)
- **Sweet potatoes** — absent until first expedition returns (available Caribbean ~115 AD)
- **Corn/maize** — absent until first expedition returns; arrives as grain, not fresh corn
- **Chili peppers** — no hot sauce, no spicy food as he knows it
- **Chocolate/cacao** — no chocolate; no hot cocoa (possible from second expedition,
  if Mesoamerican contact achieved; may arrive early 120s AD)
- **Turkey** — no Thanksgiving bird; chickens are available
- **Vanilla** — no vanilla extract
- **Squash (New World varieties)** — no butternut squash, no pumpkin (some Old World
  gourds exist, but not the species he's used to)
- **Beans (most common varieties)** — green beans, kidney beans, black beans are New World;
  fava beans and chickpeas are available

This list is not trivial. Roughly a third of what Daniel grew up eating, and most of the
flavor profiles that felt most American to him, are gone.

## SECTION 2: What a Normal 2020s American Teenager Actually Knows How to Cook

Daniel is not a chef. He is not a foodie. He is a 17-year-old from a household that
cooked basic things, ate widely, ordered out, and had a working kitchen. His food
knowledge is:

- Empirical (he knows what works from having made it)
- Principle-based (he understands the logic of why certain things work)
- Fragmentary (he knows complete dishes but has gaps; he can make pasta but
  cannot tell you the chemistry of gluten development)

### What He Can Do Reliably

**Eggs:** All basic preparations — scrambled, fried, hard-boiled, soft-boiled, omelette.
He can make an omelette that works: eggs beaten, herbs or fillings folded in, cooked
without flipping. This is a technique Romans don't use. Rome has eggs and uses them in
dishes; it does not really serve an omelette as a standalone item.

**Pasta:** He knows the principle — flour plus eggs plus salt, combined into a dough,
rested, rolled thin, cut into shapes. He does not need a recipe card; he understands
that pasta dough needs to be developed (not just mixed) and that it improves with rest.
He knows pasta needs to be cooked in actively boiling salted water. He knows the
difference between properly cooked pasta (with some give) and overcooked pasta
(mushy). He knows pasta needs sauce — not just to be boiled and served dry. He does not
know the exact water-to-flour ratios from memory, but he can work them out
iteratively. Rome has durum wheat, eggs, salt, and boiling water. He can make pasta.

**Stir-fry logic:** He understands high heat, oil, fast cooking. He knows you add
ingredients in order of how long they take. He knows not to crowd the pan. He knows
that the goal is seared rather than steamed. This is technique, not ingredients, and
Rome has pans (sartago) and oil.

**Simple bread and flatbreads:** He knows yeast makes bread rise and that flatbreads
(no yeast or quick-rise) are different from leavened bread. He can make a rough
flatbread on a hot surface. He cannot necessarily bake a perfect loaf, but he knows
the principle. Rome already has better bakers than he is; this is not his contribution.

**Basic sauces — reduction logic:** He understands that boiling a liquid down
concentrates it. He understands that fat, acid, and something to balance them makes
a sauce work. He knows you can build a pan sauce from the fond (browned bits) left
in a pan. He may not use the word "fond" but he does the thing.

**Vinegar pickling:** He has watched someone make pickles. He knows vinegar plus salt
plus optional sugar plus water is the basic brine. He knows to sterilize the jar (he
knows this because germ awareness, if nothing else). Rome already pickles things with
vinegar; he has nothing to add conceptually, but he uses the technique.

**Basic sweet things — honey plus flour plus eggs logic:** He has made cookies. He
knows the logic of fat plus sugar plus flour plus eggs makes baked goods. He can
substitute honey for sugar and adjust for liquid content. The results will be
different from a chocolate chip cookie but they will be recognizable as a baked
sweet thing.

**Spice intuition:** He has cooked with enough different spices to have basic flavor
intuition — he knows that cumin pairs with warmth and earthiness, that fennel pairs
with pork, that acid (lemon, vinegar) brightens things. He does not have a systematic
understanding of flavor science, but he has working intuition from years of
eating widely.

**Soups and stews:** He can make a basic soup — aromatics (onion, garlic) in fat, then
liquid, then protein and vegetables. He knows to season in layers. He knows that long
cooking breaks down tougher cuts of meat (braising logic).

**Grilling:** He has cooked over a fire or a grill. He knows you wait for the coals
(not flames) for even heat. He knows to rest meat after cooking.

### What He Cannot Do

**Pastry (serious):** He can make a rough cookie or simple cake. He cannot make
croissants, puff pastry, or anything requiring precision butter technique.

**Charcuterie:** He cannot make better cured meats than Rome already makes. He will
not try. This is an area where he is simply a consumer.

**Wine or beer:** He does not know how to make wine from scratch or brew beer. He can
drink what Rome produces and will not improve it.

**High-precision anything:** He is not a pastry chef. He works by feel and adjustment,
not by gram-precise measurements.

**Anything requiring New World ingredients:** He cannot make tomato sauce before
tomatoes arrive. He cannot make a potato soup before potatoes arrive. He knows
this and does not pretend otherwise.

### The Key Principle: Technique Over Ingredients

Most of what Daniel contributes to Roman food is not new ingredients. It is
applying techniques to existing ingredients in combinations that Rome does not do.
Romans have all the components of a frittata; they do not make frittatas. Romans
have pasta-ancestor doughs; they bake them, they do not boil-then-sauce them.
Romans have pans and oil and high heat; they do not consistently use the stir-fry
principle. The ingredients are there. The combinations and techniques are not.

## SECTION 3: What He Introduces — Phase by Phase

### Phase A (98–103 AD): Survival and Basics

Daniel is poor, dependent, and eating what he is given. This is not an experimental
phase for food; it is an adjustment phase.

**The garum evolution:** This is the food arc's foundational beat. First encounter
with garum is genuine disgust — the smell is overwhelming and the automatic
response from someone raised on mild American flavors is rejection. He eats
around it. He avoids it. Then at some point (probably within the first year)
he tastes something that uses garum well and the flavor clicks: it is umami,
deep and savory, the same thing that makes a good ramen broth or a properly
seasoned stir-fry work. The Roman version is just... more aggressive. More fishy.
But the principle is the same as fish sauce, which appears in every Southeast Asian
dish he ever liked. He stops avoiding garum and starts using it. The evolution
from disgust to acceptance to working familiarity is shown across chapters 03–05,
compressed into small moments, not announced.

**The first private experiment:** An omelette, made for himself alone. He has eggs
and herbs (dill or lovage, whatever is at hand) and a pan and oil. He makes an
omelette. It is not a special moment in Rome's culinary history. It is a normal
moment in Daniel's private life — a small act of being himself in an alien world.
Someone in the household tries it. Their response is something like "that is how
barbarians eat, apparently," which is both dismissive and not untrue. The omelette
is not Roman. But it was eaten.

**Key constraint:** Daniel eats what he is served for most of this phase. His food
experiments are private, small, done with whatever happens to be in the kitchen.
No business, no supply chain, no ability to source unusual ingredients. He is
learning Roman food from the inside.

**What he notices:** The heaviness of Roman flavor combinations. Garum plus cumin
plus reduced wine in the same dish can be a lot. He starts to understand that Roman
cooking is built for a different palate than his — one trained to heavier, more
complex, more layered combinations. He does not try to change it; he tries to
understand it.

---

### Phase B (103–110 AD): Workshop Wealth, First Food Business Seeds

Daniel has a kitchen, income, and time to experiment. This is where his food
contributions begin in earnest.

**Pasta introduced:** This is Phase B's significant food contribution. Romans have
laganum — a flat, baked pasta-ancestor used in layered dishes (the ancestor of
lasagna). They do not have boiled pasta served with sauce as a standalone dish.
Daniel makes it. He makes dough from durum flour and eggs, rests it, rolls it thin,
cuts it into shapes (strips first, because that's easiest without a die), boils it
in salted water, and serves it with oil, garum, and whatever herbs are at hand.
The result is not exactly al dente by his standards (he has to explain the
al dente concept through trial and error rather than verbal instruction), but it
is pasta. Romans who try it find it: edible, unusual, slightly strange in
texture compared to their baked equivalent.

He does not make a speech about pasta. He makes pasta, serves it, and watches
people eat it. Some like it. Some find the texture wrong. The workshop helps
develop muscle memory for the rolling.

**The tavern-keeper arrangement:** Somewhere in Phase B, Daniel finds a tavern-keeper
near the workshop whose establishment he trusts — call him Gnaeus, or similar —
and makes a quiet arrangement. He will supply certain recipes or techniques in
exchange for a standing bowl of something decent and a small cut of any dish
that uses his approach. This is not a business. It is a verbal arrangement between
two people who understand each other. The tavern-keeper gets novelty; Daniel gets
a kitchen he doesn't have to run and an outlet for food ideas he wants to test
at scale. The tavern-keeper is a minor recurring character — present in the
workshop neighborhood, mentioned in passing, occasionally useful as a social node.

**Egg-forward dishes:** He introduces the frittata principle. Eggs plus whatever
is at hand (chopped leeks, crumbled cheese, a bit of leftover meat) plus heat equals
a frittata, whether you call it that or not. Rome has all the components. Daniel
makes it, serves it at workshop gatherings, and it becomes one of the things that
makes people want to come to those gatherings.

**The fig-and-vinegar reduction:** He wants ketchup and cannot have it. No tomatoes.
He makes what he can: figs or plums reduced with vinegar, honey, a bit of cumin or
coriander. It is a condiment, not ketchup. It is sweet-sour-savory and it works
with roasted pork and with cheese. He knows it is not what he wanted. He uses it
anyway.

**Food as social lubricant:** Workshop gatherings have better food than comparable
Roman social events at his level because Daniel cooks things people haven't had
before. This is not calculated. He cooks because he wants to eat something decent,
and the workshop is the place where he can. The food is one of the things that makes
the workshop feel like a community.

---

### Phase C (110–120 AD): Contest Wealth, Small Food Enterprise

Daniel has enough money and standing to make the food arrangement more formal.

**The tavern operation formalizes:** The verbal arrangement with Gnaeus becomes a
more explicit partnership. A few dishes from Daniel's repertoire — the pasta
preparation, the egg-forward dishes, a couple of sauce preparations — are permanent
items on the tavern's rotation. Daniel gets a small cut. He does not manage the
place. He supplies the concept; Gnaeus supplies the labor, the kitchen, and the
customers. This is the correct model for Daniel: he is not running a restaurant,
he is being Rome's informal recipe consultant for one specific tavern. Nobody calls
it that.

**The limited menu approach:** Daniel understands that doing a few things very well
beats doing many things adequately. He pushes Gnaeus toward a rotating menu with
perhaps four or five items done well, rather than the standard thermopolium approach
of "everything we can make." Gnaeus resists this on business grounds (customers want
choice). They compromise. The result is a thermopolium that is slightly better than
average, not a culinary revolution.

**Sauce philosophy:** Daniel's contribution to Roman sauce-making is the concept
of restraint — not heavy garum plus heavy spice on everything, but finding what
makes each dish taste more like itself. He cannot explain this in Roman culinary
vocabulary because Roman culinary vocabulary does not have a word for what he
means. He shows it instead, in specific dishes where the sauce complements rather
than overwhelms. Some people notice the difference; most do not.

**Pre-Atlantic limitations acknowledged:** He still has no tomatoes, no potatoes,
no corn. He works around this with what exists. He is not waiting passively for
the Atlantic expedition to return; he is cooking with Rome's ingredients, which
are good. He just knows something is missing from his flavor memory.

---

### Phase D (120–138 AD): Post-Atlantic First Crops Arrive

The Atlantic expedition returns with the first New World samples, probably around
115–120 AD. Establishing viable crops from those samples takes 1–2 growing seasons.
So Daniel has working New World ingredients somewhere in the 117–122 AD range for
the earliest crops.

**First wave returns (maize, sweet potato, possibly tobacco):**

*Maize:* He receives the first seeds or cobs and immediately recognizes corn. He knows
corn is a grain. He does not know masa harina or nixtamalization (the alkaline
treatment that makes corn fully nutritious and changes its texture — that knowledge
is not widely held even in his era, and he doesn't know it). He does know you can
grind corn into meal and cook it as a porridge. He makes a rough polenta within the
first season: corn meal cooked slowly in water with salt. It is not exactly the
polenta he knows, but it is the same thing. He serves it with olive oil and something
sharp (hard cheese, garum). People find it dense and unusual but filling. It is
food. He knows it will become something.

*Sweet potato:* He knows to roast it. He has roasted sweet potatoes before — whole,
in a fire or hot oven, until they are soft all the way through and the skin is
charred in spots. He does this within the first week of receiving tubers. He tastes
one. It is sweet and filling and it needs nothing added to it, though salt helps.
He serves them to the workshop. They are an immediate hit because they are
genuinely delicious and unlike anything Rome eats.

**Cacao:** If the second or third expedition reaches Mesoamerican contact zones,
cacao beans may arrive in the early 120s AD. Daniel knows chocolate exists but
does not know how to process it from raw cacao. He knows cacao beans need to be
fermented (already done before they're dried for trade), roasted, and ground. He
knows the result is a bitter paste that you mix with liquid and sweeten. He figures
this out over one or two failed attempts (making the paste too coarse, getting the
roast wrong, mixing the ratios incorrectly). When he gets it right, he makes hot
chocolate: ground cacao paste, boiled water or watered-down wine, honey to sweeten.
It is nothing like a modern Hershey bar. It is dark, slightly gritty, bitter
underneath the honey, and it is chocolate. He does not explain what it is. He
makes a cup, hands it to Heras (if he is still alive) or Marcia or Tyche, and
watches their face. He says nothing.

**Tomatoes:** Tomatoes arrive as seeds. He grows them. His first season, he
gets fruit but it's not enough — the plants need to be established, the soil needs
adjustment, and he likely makes mistakes. In the second season he gets a real
harvest.

**The tomato moment:** This is earned. He makes a basic tomato sauce — tomatoes
crushed and reduced with oil and garlic and salt and a bit of garum for depth — and
he puts it on pasta. The pasta he has been making for years. The sauce he has
been unable to make for years. He eats it. He puts down the bowl. He says nothing.
This is not a triumph speech. This is a man who has been eating around an absence
for twenty years finally eating the thing. It is a private moment. If anyone else
is present, they notice he has gone quiet. Nobody asks why.

**Ketchup:** Within weeks of establishing the tomato sauce, he makes ketchup. It
is tomatoes, vinegar, onion, spices, reduced to a thick condiment. It is not Heinz.
It is recognizable. He puts it on roasted pork and on bread and uses it as a dipping
sauce. He gives some to Gnaeus the tavern-keeper without explanation. Gnaeus
puts it on the menu. His customers like it.

**Potatoes:** If the expedition reaches the South American coast (Andean origin),
potatoes may arrive in a later wave. If they arrive, Daniel roasts them whole and
makes rough potato preparations. But potatoes are not guaranteed in his lifetime
given the expedition's geographic limitations. Their arrival is conditional; the
story should not depend on them.

---

### Phase E (138–155 AD): Late Life, Legacy

**Diffusion without attribution:** Some of what Daniel introduced has spread
without his name attached. A thermopolium somewhere in the Subura serves what the
sign calls "barbarian pasta" (pasta boiled and served with a sauce, not baked).
It is popular. Daniel eats there once, out of curiosity, and the pasta is
overcooked and the sauce is more garum-forward than he would choose. But it is
pasta. He says nothing about it.

**The tomato in late life:** He grows tomatoes in his garden. In his last years,
he eats a tomato from that garden. One sentence, no speech. The act is private
and complete.

## SECTION 4: The Food Business Model

**What it is not:** A restaurant empire. Daniel does not want to manage a kitchen.
He does not want to be in the food business in any serious sense. He has enough
going on.

**What it actually is:** One trusted tavern-keeper (Gnaeus) near the workshop who
carries Daniel's recipes in exchange for a small ongoing arrangement. Daniel
develops a technique or a dish, shows Gnaeus how to make it, and takes a very
small cut or a standing credit — meals, occasionally cash, a social node in the
neighborhood. This is not one of Daniel's significant revenue streams. It is
something between a hobby and a favor-network.

**The value:** Social. The workshop neighborhood knows that Gnaeus's place has
interesting food that came from Daniel's kitchen. This builds the workshop's
reputation as a place where things get done differently. It provides Daniel with
a kitchen to test ideas that is not his own. It provides a recurring social
gathering point where information moves.

**Gnaeus as minor character:** He should appear 3–5 times across the book, always
in the workshop neighborhood, always briefly. He is not developed at length. He is
present. His voice is the voice of a working Roman small businessman — practical,
mildly curious, not invested in Daniel's larger projects. He thinks of Daniel as
"the barbarian who has interesting ideas about food and is good for business."
This is the correct relationship.

**What Daniel never does:** He never makes speeches to Gnaeus about food philosophy.
He shows up, demonstrates, tastes the result, adjusts, leaves. Gnaeus does the
rest.

## SECTION 5: Food as Character Texture

Food beats are some of the book's best quiet moments because they are private in
a way that most of Daniel's other activities are not. His tech work is always
for someone. His financial moves are always strategic. His food moments are for
himself.

### The Garum Evolution (Ch03–Ch05 area)

The full arc: disgust → avoidance → reluctant use → working familiarity → actual
appreciation. The evolution should happen in background texture, not as a named
character arc. Daniel never announces "I have made peace with garum." He just stops
complaining about it and starts using it correctly, and the reader notices the
shift by its absence.

The insight that should appear on-page, briefly, in his interiority: *He recognized
it as fish sauce. Once he recognized it as fish sauce everything else followed.*

### The First Private Omelette (Ch03 or Ch05)

He makes this for himself alone, early on. Not for the household. Not as a
demonstration. He finds eggs and a pan and oil and an herb and he makes himself
an omelette because he wanted an omelette and he knew how to make one. The act is
a small statement of personal normalcy in an abnormal situation. It is the first
food moment that belongs entirely to him.

### The Frittata at Workshop Gatherings (Phase B)

These appear in passing as texture — people come to workshop gatherings partly
because the food is good. This should never be the chapter's main beat; it should
be mentioned in one or two sentences as part of setting.

### The Fig-and-Vinegar Reduction (Phase B)

A small private joke. He makes the best tomato-substitute he can from what is
available, he knows it is not tomatoes, and he uses it anyway. A sentence or two,
no pathos.

### The First Time He Eats Something That Surprises Him Into Emotion (Phase B–C)

Not necessarily a specific dish Daniel introduced. It might be something Roman —
a particular preparation of fresh figs with aged cheese, or a lamb braise with
fennel and cumin, or a good plate of fresh pasta the second time he makes it —
that causes an unexpected moment of pleasure that catches him off guard. He was
not expecting to enjoy himself. He did. The emotion is not happiness about Rome;
it is simpler: good food made him feel, briefly, like a person with a normal life.
This beat works best if he is alone or nearly alone. No witnesses to what
it means.

### The Polenta Beat (First crop season, ~118–120 AD)

He makes polenta from the first maize he has access to. It is rough and dense and
needs work but it is corn meal porridge and he knows what it is. The reaction is
not triumph — it is recognition. *There it is.*

### The Sweet Potato Beat (~118–120 AD)

He roasts a sweet potato. He eats it plain. It is good. He does not explain to
anyone what it is.

### The Cacao Beat (Ch43 — the scene is specified)

He makes hot chocolate. He serves it to Marcia (or Tyche, or whoever is present).
He gives the name wrong — he half-remembers the word from school and it is not
right and he knows it is not right. He does not correct himself. This is one of
the book's small private jokes: the man who spent 40 years being meticulously
careful about what he claimed to know gives a wrong name to one of the most
important crops of the New World and simply lives with it.

**Per ch43 brief:** This scene is assigned to ch43 (Family chapter, ~135–138 AD).
Execute as specified there — brief, one exchange, no speech.

### The Tomato Moment (Post-harvest, ~120–125 AD)

This is the food arc's emotional payoff. He makes tomato sauce and puts it on pasta.

## SECTION 6: What NOT to Do

**No restaurant empire subplot.** Daniel does not build a food business. He has one
tavern-keeper partnership. That is the ceiling.

**He doesn't give speeches about food.** He does not lecture Romans about the New
World's culinary gifts. He makes things. He serves them. If someone asks what
something is, he gives a brief functional answer. He does not explain the agricultural
history of tomatoes.

**Food innovations spread casually.** "Barbarian pasta" at a Subura thermopolium
is not Daniel's doing; it is diffusion, and diffusion looks like someone vaguely
remembering they once ate something somewhere and making their own version of it.
The spread is messy and imprecise and mostly unattributed. This is correct.

**He doesn't "invent" Italian food.** He applies techniques to existing ingredients.
He introduces a few things from his own time into Rome's ingredient base. The result
is not Italian food; it is Roman food with some additional techniques and eventually
some additional ingredients. The difference matters.

**Timing is strict.** He cannot make tomato sauce before he has tomatoes. He cannot
make chocolate before he has cacao. He cannot reference sweet potato before the
expedition returns. The ingredient wall is real and the story must respect it.

**No magic kitchen.** His cooking is constrained by what he actually has access to:
a workshop kitchen in early phases, a more equipped kitchen in later phases, the
tavern partnership as his testing ground. He does not have modern equipment. He
does not have a pantry full of unusual spices. He has what Rome has, plus eventually
what the Atlantic expeditions return with.

**The garum evolution is not a joke.** It would be easy to play Daniel's initial
garum disgust for laughs and then drop it. The arc should complete — he not only
tolerates garum, he uses it well. By Phase C, garum is a standard tool in his
cooking and he no longer thinks about the smell.

## A NOTE ON VOICE IN FOOD SCENES

Daniel's food moments should have the same dry register as everything else he does.
He does not wax lyrical about Roman ingredients. He does not give food a spiritual
weight it doesn't have in his personality. He likes eating. He misses certain things.
He makes the best of what he has. When something is good, he notes it briefly. When
something is bad, he eats it anyway or avoids it. The garum evolution matters because
it shows his capacity to adapt and recognize — not because food is his deepest concern.

The tomato moment is the exception because it has been earned by twenty years of
absence. Even then: he eats it, he puts the bowl down, he says nothing. The reader
provides the significance.

That is the correct register for Daniel and food throughout.
