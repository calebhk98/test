# Characters

Writers: use these as canon. Keep voices distinct. Minor walk-ons can be named
from the stock in `07_glossary.md`. Ages are given for 98 AD and you can compute
later ages from the timeline (Daniel is 17 in 98).

**V2 IMPERATIVE — read before writing a single line of dialogue:**
See `CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md` for voice-range rules, crude speech guidance,
class-marking grammar, and the "muting" problem. The core failure of V1: everyone
sounds too smart and eloquent. V2 must fix this by showing class through grammar
and vocabulary — not just through content and deference — and by quoting rough/
inarticulate/crude characters instead of silencing them.

---

## DANIEL "DANNY" MERCER — protagonist, narrator
- 17 in 98 AD. American high-school junior from a midsize suburb. Part-time job
  at a hardware store (so: knows his way around tools, fasteners, a tape measure,
  basic building; has watched enough to fake competence, not engineer it).
- Strong at math (through precalc, a little self-taught physics), shaky on
  chemistry and biology, scraps of history from class and YouTube, a steady diet
  of sci-fi/fantasy novels. NOT a prodigy. This is the whole point.
- Voice: dry, self-deprecating, undercuts his own big moments, swears, reaches
  for modern references no one will get. Fakes confidence in public; privately
  terrified, homesick, and increasingly lonely as the one person who knows.
- Core wound/engine: terror of being a nobody (which here means dead or enslaved)
  and terror of dying with everything still in his head. Over decades, the second
  becomes the larger fear.
- Arc: frightened teenager -> the man who flew -> a workshop owner with enemies
  -> a power player who has to choose how far to push -> an institution-builder
  racing his own mortality. He gets things wrong constantly and learns the hard
  way. He never stops being a transplant who doesn't fully belong.
- DON'T: make him smoothly competent, make him remember things a normal teen
  wouldn't, let him win without cost.

## HERAS — Greek physician, the skeptic and the conscience
- ~45 in 98. A freedman physician from Pergamon, Greek-speaking, also fluent
  Latin. First sent to examine the strange foreigner (is he mad? is the clothing
  witchcraft?), stays out of curiosity, becomes Daniel's first real friend.
- Function: intellectual sparring partner who refuses to be impressed. He demands
  Daniel prove claims, punctures the germ pitch, and keeps the science honest. He
  is the reader's skeptic on the page. Also the partner on medicine, sanitation,
  the "invisible creatures" framing, distillation/antiseptics.
- Voice: dry, learned, ironic, allergic to enthusiasm. Quotes Hippocrates and
  Epicurus. Treats Daniel as a gifted, infuriating student. Speaks in compressed
  aphorism. Mock-formal teasing: "The Thulean remembers the road to my house."
  Never laughs aloud — only "the dry uptick of the mouth." Disagrees by
  dismantling you patiently, then keeping the part that works without admitting
  you were right.
- V2 DISTINCTION from the blunt-men cluster: Heras's irony must be warmer and
  more elliptical than Macer's, Celer's, or Apollodorus's. He teases; Macer
  prices; Celer books consequences; Apollodorus delivers verdicts. In V1, Heras
  occasionally blurs with the others when read without attribution tags — his
  manner can collapse into generic "blunt Roman authority." Fix: keep the warmth
  (however suppressed) present, and keep the Greek intellectual's habit of
  framing a challenge as a question rather than a statement.
- Arc: skeptic -> reluctant believer in method (not magic) -> the man who
  carries the medical work forward and helps protect the encyclopedia. Older than
  Daniel; will likely die before him (a real loss to land mid-late book).
- DON'T: issue inspirational speeches unless the text explicitly frames it as
  exceptional. "He did the thing he did rarely, which was to drop the irony
  entirely, and it always landed harder." Reserve that moment; don't repeat it.

## TITUS FLAVIUS MACER — the patron (middle-book engine)
- ~50 in 98. Equestrian. A hard, shrewd army-contractor: leather, timber, grain
  for the legions, with real frontier connections. Owns slaves without a flicker
  of thought.
- CANON (see coordinator decision in canon log): Macer is the equestrian who has
  HELD Daniel as de-facto property since the early chapters (the unnamed "owner"
  who dealt only through a steward and authorized the public demo). Daniel does
  NOT meet him face-to-face until ch09, where he is first named. Macer took Daniel
  on cheaply as a speculative oddity.
- Function: after the balloon proves real at the public demo (his gamble), he
  takes a real look at the asset he owns, pours money into a proper workshop, and
  the owner-relationship becomes a patron/partner one over time. He is protection
  AND a cage AND, at first, literally Daniel's owner. Genuinely fond, genuinely
  exploitative, genuinely dangerous when Daniel grows too valuable to control. He
  does NOT free Daniel; Daniel's freedom comes from the emperor (ch17), which
  reshapes their balance. The relationship that defines the rise.
- Voice: blunt, transactional, funny in a mean way, respects results and money,
  contemptuous of philosophers. Calls Daniel "the Thulean" or "boy" long past
  when it fits. NEVER warm directly. The gold ring turned on the thumb is his
  nervous/calculating gesture. His "short ugly bark" is the closest he gets to a
  laugh. States cost and moves on — does not argue. He is "the man who says the
  crude thing at the volume of a man selling timber."
- V2 FIX — crude oath: give Macer ONE genuine crude Roman oath, once, when he is
  genuinely angry about money or a lost contract. A single occasion where the
  volume and the language match his established character. It deepens him; it does
  not break him. After that, keep the rest of his roughness as statement and
  pricing. Not every scene — just once.
- V1 drift note (ch33): "nearest thing to fondness he let himself" and "not
  unkind" applied to his parting use of "boy" — narration makes the affection too
  legible. V2: tighten these phrases; preserve ambiguity. Macer's actions stay
  self-interested; the narration should not explain what he feels.
- Arc: investor -> partner -> rival of a kind -> a man Daniel must outgrow
  without destroying. His casual slaveholding is a recurring friction with Daniel.
- DON'T: make him sentimental, make him warm on purpose, let him say a tender
  thing even once — "We did not say tender things. We had never once in forty-five
  years said a tender thing and we did not start with him dying."

## TYCHE — slave, then freedwoman; the thread to the epilogue
- ~14 in 99 (born ~85 AD; canon log anchor). A kitchen/errand slave in Macer's household, assigned to fetch and
  carry for Daniel. Quick, watchful, frighteningly good with numbers once shown.
- Function: Daniel teaches her to read, write, and calculate, then later English
  as a "homeland script." She becomes his most trusted apprentice and a keeper/
  copyist of the encyclopedia. The bond is sibling/found-family, deliberately NOT
  romance. Roman assumptions about a man and his female slave are a tension Daniel
  has to navigate, and that discomfort is part of the book's quiet war.
- Voice: starts terse and guarded (a slave's survival caution), grows sharp,
  funny, fierce about the work. Eventually freer-tongued than is safe. Answers
  in fragments. States the damning fact and stops. "The corner of her mouth did
  the thing that is the closest she comes to a smile." Flat city Latin — no
  slowing it for the foreigner. Disagrees by stating a true number; concedes to
  a correct figure instantly; otherwise immovable.
- V2 DISTINCTION from Marcia: Tyche answers in fragments and stops; Marcia builds
  long structured arguments. Tyche states the number; Marcia lays out the whole
  case, every side, having pre-decided. Both are transactional and sharp; keep
  them distinct without relying on attribution tags. See CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md.
- Legal name note: by law her freed name is "Ulpia Tyche" but she REFUSES "Ulpia"
  and is only ever "Tyche" on the page; the imposed nomen is a lasting friction.
  V2 FIX: add one dramatized scene where someone uses the full freed name to her
  face and she responds with a single syllable or silence. The refusal must be
  shown, not just carried as narrative description.
- NAMING NOTE: "Ulpia" also appears as Daniel's daughter's name (Ulpia Severa).
  Different people. The friction belongs to Tyche's nomen only. Do not let the
  daughter's unproblematic "Ulpia" dilute Tyche's anger at the same syllable.
- Arc: property -> apprentice -> freedwoman -> partner in the enterprise ->
  survivor who outlives Daniel and carries the ladder forward (epilogue link).
- DON'T: romanticize her enslavement or make her gratitude saccharine. "I'm not
  thanking you" is not gratitude withheld — it is a structural refusal. She has
  her own anger and her own agenda.

## GAIUS JULIUS HERMES — freedman blacksmith; the metallurgy thread
- ~40 in 98. Freedman ironworker, proud of his craft, scornful of theory.
- Function: Daniel's partner on plows, then steel, then the cannon-that-bursts.
  He embodies the steel chokepoint: skilled hands, no way to measure or hold
  temperature, gorgeous results one batch and brittle garbage the next.
- Voice: terse, physical, proud, dismissive until proven wrong, then loyal.
  Speaks between hammer blows — fragmentary by physical necessity. Demands a
  number. Reduces problems to "how much / how hot / how long." City Latin, fast,
  consonants bitten off short. Flat workman's anger, no flourish. No humor.
  Disagrees with: "Then you've told me nothing."
- V2 DISTINCTION from Macer: both demand numbers, but Hermes demands a replicable
  production variable ("one I can do twice"); Macer prices in coin. Give Hermes
  a physical anchor — hammer rhythm, apron sparks, the fire — so he does not blur
  with Macer on the page when lines appear without tags.
- Arc: skeptic craftsman -> partner -> the man who keeps chasing repeatable steel
  and mostly fails, which is honest and bitter.

## QUINTUS MARCIUS CELER — army officer; the war/frontier thread
- ~35 in 98. Equestrian, a serving tribune/prefect, practical and ambitious.
- Function: the man who first sees the balloon's military value and carries it (and
  Daniel) toward the army and the Dacian wars. Daniel's window into the legions
  and into what his inventions do when they reach a battlefield. A friendship with
  a soldier's bluntness.
- Voice: clipped, competent, gallows-humored, no patience for court games.
  "Frontier Latin, no music in it." Imperatives. Asks the one practical question.
  States ownership of consequences as bookkeeping. Tenderness only ever appears
  as a clipped order.
- V2 FIX — quote the soldier's oath: "He said something low and soldier's-rough I
  didn't catch" recurs in V1. In V2, quote it once. Let the reader hear what a
  career frontier officer says under stress. Not theatrical profanity — one short
  oath, specific to the situation. After that scene, the muting-via-narration can
  resume. But once: the reader must hear it. See CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md.
- Arc: champion of the balloon -> battlefield friend -> a cost the book can spend.
  He dies ch29 of sepsis from a wound Daniel fails to save — where Daniel first
  sees his gifts kill someone he cared about.

## LUCIUS VETTIUS CRISPUS — grammarian/philosopher; intellectual antagonist
- ~40 in 98. Well-connected Roman man of letters, vain, a darling of literary
  salons. First patronizes Daniel as a curiosity, then resents him as a rival for
  attention and patronage.
- Function: the social/intellectual threat. He probes Daniel's contradictions,
  spreads the charge of fraud, and can escalate "magic" or "impiety" into real
  danger. He is clever and not always wrong, which makes him dangerous.
- Voice: elegant, allusive, cutting, performs erudition. Greek tags, Strabo
  citations. He is a smart, controlled, strategic deceiver — never blundering.

## AULUS VIBENIUS — senior haruspex; the religion friction

- Function: the ambiguous religious figure. Vibenius can bless Daniel's work as

## PUBLIUS CORNELIUS SCAEVA — ambitious senator; political danger
- ~45 in 98. Old-family senator hungry for military glory and influence. Wants
  Daniel's weapons and devices for his own advancement; a rival pull against Macer.
- Function: the high-stakes political game. Being coveted by a senator is a trap.
- Voice: smooth, gracious, lethal underneath; speaks in favors and obligations.
  The book's primary example of a brilliant, controlled, strategic deceiver. Never
  miscalculates stupidly. Always already several moves ahead.

## MARCIA — freedwoman; Daniel's eventual partner/wife (mid-late book)
- ~25 in 98 (age-appropriate as the years pass). A freedwoman, widow of a
  merchant, runs accounts and the business side of an enterprise with more nerve
  than most men in the room.
- Function: a genuine partner, not a prize. She grounds Daniel, manages what he
  can't, and has her own agenda for the enterprise and any children. Their union
  (informal *contubernium* until Daniel gains citizenship, then marriage) is a
  mid-book development. Keep it unsentimental and real.
- Voice: blunt and transactional like Macer, BUT argumentative and architectural —
  she builds long, structured, persuasive paragraphs. Lines the edges of a tablet
  instead of raising her voice. Tests people. Refuses sentiment, then leaves a
  hand near (not on) yours. Dry, deflationary humor. Disagrees by laying out the
  whole case, every side, having pre-decided.
- V2 DISTINCTION from Tyche: Marcia argues; Tyche states and stops. Both are
  transactional and sharp. Keep them distinct without relying on tags. See
  CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md for contrast examples.
- NOTE: introduce only when the timeline reaches it; do not foreshadow heavily.

## ULPIA SEVERA — Daniel's daughter; cipher/encyclopedia thread (Parts VI–VII)
- Born ~118 AD to Daniel and Marcia. First appears ch38; the name "Ulpia" appears
  ~50x in late chapters.
- Function: Daniel's blood daughter. Fierce at figures, cipher-keeper candidate.
  Reads Daniel's failing work aloud to him in his last years. Holds the books and
  the African survey after Marcia dies. She is the direct link from Daniel to the
  next-generation carrier chain.
- CRITICAL NAMING NOTE — Ulpia ≠ Procula: Ulpia Severa is Daniel's blood daughter
  (b. ~118). Procula is the ch52 epilogue climber, ~20 in ~190 AD, two generations
  later, explicitly NOT a blood descendant. The V1 timeline (`03_timeline.md`)
  wrongly conflates these two characters. The prose itself keeps them correctly
  distinct; the V2 bible must maintain this. The African survey handoff belongs to
  Ulpia. The escapement belongs to Procula. They are different people.
- Voice: the "relentless questioner" as a child — states things flat, the way she
  says a sum; pushes at a problem certain there is a true figure. Answers Macer
  sharply when he makes the quartermaster remark (ch39). As an adult: more words
  than Tyche, same habit of reducing questions to their factual core.
- V2 NOTE: Ulpia as a child must not get the chapter's thematic mic-drop line.
  See the Lucanus entry below.

## MARCUS ULPIUS LUCANUS — Daniel's son (ch26+)
- Born ~112 AD to Daniel and Marcia. 34+ appearances. Law-track child who
  ultimately rejects the workshop in favor of the courts.
- Function: the child who chooses his own path away from Daniel's enterprise.
  He does not carry the ladder — which is honest and a loss.
- Voice: a child, then a young man. Concrete vocabulary. Confidence that outstrips
  its basis. Incomplete thoughts. Should sound like a child, not a small adult.
- V2 CRITICAL FIX — the Lucanus problem: In V1 ch37, Lucanus at ~10 years old
  delivers "Then he did all right," and Daniel explicitly canonizes it as "the only
  thing said in the house all that day that I could not fold any smaller." A
  10-year-old out-philosophizes the narrator and lands the chapter's thematic
  capstone. THIS MUST BE TEMPERED IN V2. Options: give the line to an adult;
  let Lucanus say something true in a child-accidental way that Daniel notices
  without enshrining; or cut the narrator's explicit endorsement ("I could not
  fold any smaller") so the line is observed but not canonized. The pattern of
  side characters out-perceiving the protagonist must be resisted everywhere, but
  a 10-year-old doing it is the most egregious instance.

## PAMPHILUS — collared slave, recurring; the class-voice vehicle (ch10+)
- Collared porter. First appears ch10 when Daniel unsuccessfully tries to remove
  his collar. Bought and freed by Daniel (ch18). Remains at the yard as a freedman
  through ch48. 71+ appearances.
- Function: thematically central to the slavery thread. Also — critically for V2
  — he is the book's primary vehicle for class-marked uneducated dialogue. He
  fills the gap V1 created by muting every inarticulate character.
- V1 PROBLEM: "Pamphilus did not say anything" recurs (ch13, ch20). The book kept
  him silent so the clean register of everyone who speaks was never broken.
- V2 FIX: Give Pamphilus at least 4 actual spoken lines across the book.
  - Voice: very short (2-6 words per sentence). Ungrammatical. Concrete nouns
    only. Gets specific things wrong. Swears tiredly at objects, not people.
    No subordinate clauses. No balanced syntax.
  - RIGHT: "That wall. Three times I done it. Don't hold."
  - RIGHT (corrected): "Boss said stack here. I stacked." [To wall, after being
    told he stacked wrong:] "Gods-cursed thing."
  - WRONG: "I have repaired this section three times and it continues to be
    structurally unsound." — This is V1's register. Do not give it to Pamphilus.
  - He can understand a situation correctly without articulating it correctly.
    Daniel can note that Pamphilus said what was wrong in six words, none quite
    right, but clear enough.
  - He is NOT comic relief. His limited speech is texture, not mockery.
- Minimum V2 line count: 4 direct spoken lines, distributed across his appearances.
- DON'T: continue "Pamphilus did not say anything." Give the inarticulate a voice.

## FELIX — freedman/assistant; prize-tale teller (ch19, ch39, ch44, ch52 backstory)
- A freedman under Daniel's umbrella. 26+ appearances. Connected to the
  failure-endowment (ch39) and epilogue-climber thread (his craft failures
  eventually feed Procula's solution, ch52).
- Function: tries the hard technical problems and fails publicly. His failures are
  evidence that Daniel's constraints are real, not just social ones. Also a
  prize-contest participant and storyteller — the living version of the contest's
  purpose.
- Voice: warmer than Hermes, more talkative. Not uneducated but not polished —
  a craftsman with opinions. Gets enthusiastic; Daniel or Marcia has to bring him
  back to the practical question. Does not have epigrams.
- NOTE: Felix's failures must be genuine. He is not secretly a genius held back
  by circumstance. He is a competent craftsman who cannot crack the steel problem
  or the escapement — and that is honest, not a diminishment.

## NASO — recruit; voice of confident-stupid (ch11, recurs through ch29)
- Young recruit, burned in the balloon accident ch11. 23+ appearances.
- V1 problem: narrated as "stupid with the particular fearlessness of a boy who
  has not yet been hurt," but his only near-quote is reported, not given directly.
- V2 FIX: Give Naso one crude, cocky, foolish line BEFORE the disaster. Let the

## ONESIMUS — senior abacus clerk; the confidently-wrong figure (ch10–20)
- Senior accountant who resists the new numeral system. 14+ appearances.
- V1 status: technically excellent (out-computes Daniel on the board), stubborn,
  but not truly wrong in his reasoning — only conservative and inflexible.
- V2 FIX: Promote Onesimus to the book's primary "wrong and stays wrong" figure.
  Give him a scene where he argues FOR the abacus, gets the REASONING wrong (not
  just the preference), cannot be moved, and is plainly the lesser mind for it.
  He misunderstands the new figures' advantage — argues about speed (the abacus
  is faster for simple sums, which is true) while missing the point (written-down
  calculations enable what you cannot hold in one head). He argues the wrong point
  with confidence and loses the argument by being factually wrong, not just
  conservative.
- Voice: dignified in bearing; argument is weak. "It is a settled method that
  has served Rome for three hundred years." Does not understand what he is arguing
  against. Stays wrong to the end.

## ADDITIONAL NAMED CHARACTERS (identified in prose; profiled V2)

These recur or carry an arc beat but had no bible entry. Added from a full-manuscript
character census. Keep brief; expand only if a chapter needs it.

### EROS — freedman letterer/pressman; the print-shop spine (ch16+, ~15 chapters)
- Greek-trained letter-cutter. Cuts the first wooden blocks (the Homer line), then runs
  the press for decades; trains the apprentice Vitalis; eyes failing by the late books;
  witnesses the collegium endowment deed; dies after passing the craft on (ch48 area).
- Function: the human continuity of the printing enterprise — the press is not abstract,
  it is Eros's hands. The keeper-chain's craft side, parallel to Tyche's cipher side.
- Voice: a craftsman with ink to the elbow; concrete, work-focused, not eloquent; no
  epigrams. Speaks of blocks, lines, and lamp-black, not ideas.

### STICHUS — water-boy then yard worker; recurring household presence (ch05+, ~7 chapters)
- Starts ~10 as the workshop water-carrier who teaches Daniel his first word ("aqua") and
  dislikes him; grows "into his shoulders" and works the wharf yard for the enterprise.
- Function: a measure of time passing in the household; a minor walk-on who recurs.
- Voice: child's bluntness early; later an unremarkable working register. Not a philosopher.

### DEMETRIOS — Greek glass-cutter/lens-grinder (ch23, ch26, ch38)
- The craftsman who grinds lens discs for the spyglass/telescope work, reluctantly and
  with frustration; later sells spyglasses; the glass keeps defeating him (seeds, waves,
  bad pours). Embodies the optics chokepoint the way Hermes embodies the steel one.
- Voice: impatient, proud of his craft, sceptical of Daniel's tolerances. Demands of glass.

### DRUSUS — Macer's guild/materials contact and intermediary (ch05, ch07, ch15)
- The connection through whom Daniel posts early problem-specs (the glass-clarity prize)
  to the craft networks; spreads word of prizes among craftsmen.
- Voice: functional, transactional; a fixer. Brief.

### METTIUS — ambitious equestrian; salon host (ch10, ch16, ch25)
- Wealthy striver who hosts the literary salons where Daniel is displayed and where Crispus
  attacks him; lends his house for set-pieces.
- Voice: socially eager, status-conscious; a host, not a thinker.

### GLABRIO — bronzesmith; the recurring prize near-miss (ch19, ch26)
- A craftsman who shows up repeatedly in the prize records attempting tolerance specs
  (the cylinder bore, alloy work) and getting close, never quite winning; Daniel knows his
  name though they rarely speak. Embodies the prize model distributing risk to people who
  can least afford the near-miss. [Note: the outlines reference further Glabrio appearances
  (ch21/ch29) and a later retainer — the written prose currently has him in ch19 and ch26.]
- Voice: terse craftsman; mostly seen on the page through his submissions, not dialogue.

### BITUITUS — Gaulish captain/yard-master; the Atlantic program (ch36, ch44, ch46)
- The leather-faced Gaulish captain Daniel quietly funds years before the Hadrian audience;
  pilots/organizes the western probes out of the Tagus and later the Gaulish yards; builds
  the deep-hulled, double-planked ocean ships and is proud of them.
- Function: the hands that carry out the crossing program; its continuity after Daniel.
- Voice: blunt seaman/builder; practical; proud of a hull the way Hermes is of a heat.

### ILLA — woman from the western shore; the contact arc's moral center (ch36+)
- Came back with Bituitus's second probe; somewhere past thirty; sits in the tally-room
  and learns the new figures faster than any Roman clerk Daniel has tested. Her own name is
  one Daniel "could not hold in his Latin mouth"; she accepts, without enthusiasm, being
  called "Illa" ("her" — a placeholder, not her name). She has two words for her homeland:
  one meaning roughly "the people's land," the other her kin-group's name, which Daniel does
  not repeat because it is theirs.
- Function: the living rebuke to the "uninhabited" lie; the person from the world that
  existed before Daniel arrived, who has no idea what that means. Handle with restraint and
  dignity; she is not a symbol to be explained, and her interiority is not Daniel's to narrate.
- Voice: minimal on the page; watchful; competent at figures. Do not give her speeches or
  make her gratitude/forgiveness legible.

### GNAEUS — tavern-keeper; the food-arc partner (ch34; relationship spans Part II onward)
- Runs a thermopolium near the Aemilian bridge. Daniel gives him recipes (the pasta
  preparation, later the proto-ketchup) years earlier; Gnaeus works them into his counter
  rotation without ceremony or attribution and stops thinking of them as Daniel's. "The
  barbarian who has interesting ideas about food and is good for business."
- Voice: a working tavern-keeper; unsentimental, commercial; food is business, not art.

### MARCELLUS — contest winner; the protégé who goes wrong (ch38)
- A freedman's son from the Transtiberim; won a prize three years earlier (a water-lifting
  screw for mine shafts). Took Daniel's introductions to two mine-operators, posed as
  Daniel's agent with authority he never had, and extracted ~8,000 sesterces in advance for
  improvements he couldn't and didn't make. Daniel repaid the operators with interest and
  cut him off; Marcellus's reply was "more aggrieved than apologetic."
- Function: the system Daniel built being gamed; the prize model's failure mode embodied.
  Not a cartoon villain — a man who convinced himself none of it was wrong.

### STATIUS — farrier; the unexpected prize winner (ch24)
- A horseshoe-maker who submits a decades-deep iron heat-colour/temperature table (built by
  his father and grandfather, calibrated against thousands of real horseshoes) — cruder than
  Daniel's own data and more useful. The prize model surfacing craft knowledge Daniel could
  not have reached. (Sends it via his nephew.)
- Voice: not on the page much; the table speaks for him.

### SEXTUS PEDANIUS — glassmaker of Brundisium; the first prize payoff (ch07)
- Wins the first glass-clarity prize with a bone-ash decolourant technique his father
  developed for decorative vessels — a method Daniel did not know existed, bought for ~200
  sesterces. The beat that clarifies the prize model: he is buying disclosure of existing
  Roman craft knowledge invisible to him, not inventing.
- Voice: a working glassmaker; brief.

### SATURNINUS — optio; field-sanitation/flux (ch12, ch14)
- A campaign optio (distinct from the unnamed ch02–04 optio). Explains flux mortality in
  camp; moves Daniel away from Sabinus's body after the fall; helps keep the maimed Naso on
  the army's strength.
- Voice: frontier soldier; matter-of-fact about death; short.

## REAL HISTORICAL FIGURES (use carefully; mostly offstage)
- **TRAJAN** (emperor from 98, in Rome from 99, d. 117): soldierly, practical,
  affable, a builder who genuinely likes engineers. Personal audiences are major
  set-pieces; keep him mostly a distant force. He is not cruel but he is the
  state, and the state is lethal. Voice: the cold affable-then-transactional
  soldier. "Affable weather" over a "cold sum." Not performing wit.
- **APOLLODORUS OF DAMASCUS**: Trajan's master engineer/architect. The one real
  mind in Rome who can meet Daniel as a peer. Voice: Greek-accented, fast,
  magisterial. Proud "the way a wall is plumb." Contempt "total and serene."
  Single verdict held forever ("a clever toy that does one thing"). He
  historically clashed with Hadrian (who later had him killed/exiled) — a usable
  late thread.
- **HADRIAN** (Trajan's ward; emperor 117-138): around the court as a younger man,
  intellectually voracious, prickly, dangerous. The succession resets every
  relationship. Voice: Greek-and-Latin, quick, lacquered, predatory-playful. Sets
  verbal traps and watches which you step in. "You have until I am bored."
- **PLOTINA** (Trajan's wife) and **ATTIANUS** (Praetorian prefect): the engine of
  the contested succession of 117. Offstage engines; Plotina appears once in passing.
- **PLINY THE YOUNGER** — NOTE: this cameo was never written in V1. The ch51
  letter interlude used invented correspondents (Norbanus Rufus / Caecilius
  Priscus), not Pliny. In V2, either use him as originally planned or note
  explicitly that the letter belongs to invented figures. CONFIRMED: the written
  ch51 letter is headed "GAIUS NORBANUS RUFUS TO HIS FRIEND SEXTUS CAECILIUS
  PRISCUS" — both are invented figures, a generation after Daniel's death; Rufus is
  the snobbish narrator-of-record who half-credits the Thulean. Keep them as the
  ch51 voices; do not swap in Pliny unless deliberately revising.
- **DECEBALUS** — King of Dacia; the offstage antagonist of the Dacian wars (ch12–18).
  Encamped behind "the Loaf"; negotiates, fights, and finally cuts his own throat rather
  than be taken, his head carried south as proof. A historical force, not a voice character.
- **ANTONINUS PIUS** — Hadrian's successor (ch46+). A grave, decent, unhurried senator
  who will "die in his own bed, having gone nowhere and refused every adventure" — no
  appetite for the ocean program, which is why its imperial prestige-funding lapses under
  him (while the institutional/commercial crossings continue). Voice: even, careful,
  unspectacular; the anti-Trajan, anti-Hadrian.
- **THE FOUR CONSULARS** (Palma, Celsus, Nigrinus, Lusius Quietus) — executed without
  trial at the start of Hadrian's reign (ch33); the purge that poisons the Senate's trust
  and frightens Daniel's circle. **Lusius Quietus** (the Moorish cavalry commander, also
  ch29) is the one with a face in the prose; the others are mostly names — deliberately, as
  Daniel "cannot even hold" them. Historical; offstage.

## RELATIONSHIP NOTES
- Macer "owns" Daniel's situation early; Heras owns his mind; Tyche owns his
  conscience; Celer owns his fear of consequences; Crispus/Scaeva/Vibenius are the
  three faces of danger (intellectual, political, religious).
- Daniel's deepest, least sayable problem: none of them can ever fully understand
  where he is from or what he carries. Show the loneliness; never have him
  monologue it.
- Ulpia Severa (daughter) and Tyche are the two women who carry the ladder after
  Marcia dies. Mutual respect; mutual wariness; neither is the other's subordinate.

## VOICE RANGE RULES (apply to ALL characters)

These rules exist because V1 committed a systematic failure: everyone who speaks
sounds too smart, too eloquent, and too perceptive. V2 must expand the range
downward as well as honoring the established upper register.

### RULE 1: Class is marked in grammar and vocabulary, not just content.
Lower-class characters speak in incomplete sentences, wrong word usage, thin
vocabulary, and limited syntax. A slave or dock worker does not produce balanced
antithetical clauses. The difference between Macer and Pamphilus is not just
attitude — it is sentence structure and word choice. See CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md
for specific grammar rules by social register.

### RULE 2: Muting is not a solution.
If a character would have to speak roughly or inarticulately, the answer is NOT
to give them no dialogue. Give them dialogue that is rough and inarticulate.
"Shouted something I didn't catch," "Pamphilus did not say anything," and "said
something soldier's-rough I didn't hear" must be replaced — in at least some
instances — with the actual quoted speech. The reader can handle it.

### RULE 3: Crude oaths are permitted and required for specific characters.
Roman soldiers, dockers, and craftsmen swore. The Pompeii graffiti and Catullus
prove this. The complete absence of crude speech from all quoted dialogue is an
anachronism of politeness. At minimum: Macer once, Celer once, Pamphilus
occasionally (tired swearing at objects, not people). Not every scene — once per
character is enough to crack the seal. See CHARACTER_VOICE_GUIDE.md.

### RULE 4: Wrong characters must exist and stay wrong.
V1 has no character who argues poorly, gets the reasoning wrong, and cannot be
moved. Every antagonist is a brilliant strategist; every walk-on is perceptive.
V2 needs: Onesimus (confidently wrong about the abacus reasoning), Naso (dim and
fearless before the accident), at least one crowd heckler who says something
genuinely stupid. These characters are real, not mocked.

### RULE 5: Children do not get the scene's thematic capstone line.
The Lucanus problem (ch37) is the clearest instance: a ten-year-old must not
out-philosophize the narrator. Child characters in this book have a V1 tendency to
say the truest thing in the chapter. Resist it. A child can say something true by
accident; Daniel can notice it; but Daniel must not canonize it as the day's
deepest utterance.

### RULE 6: Crowd hecklers are quoted, not reported.
"Shouted something I didn't catch" is a device for avoiding rough register. Quote
2-3 actual crowd lines in at least one scene: crude, superstitious, mean, dumb.
Pompeii-graffiti level. "Thulean witch!" "Give us the flying man, the real kind!"
A dirty joke at Daniel's expense. This is not degrading the crowd — it is treating
them as real people with real speech.

### RULE 7: Not every character gets a quotable epigram.
V1 distributes polished antithetical aphorisms too widely. The cadence becomes a
house style. In V2: polished philosophical epigrams belong to Heras, Apollodorus,
and Hadrian. Other characters can have sharp lines, but in their own register.
Macer's sharpness is a mean joke. Tyche's sharpness is a flat fact. Hermes's
sharpness is a demand. Only the three named above get the chiastic closure-cadence.
