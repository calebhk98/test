# Premise, Theme, and the Argument of the Book

## Logline
A seventeen-year-old wakes in the dirt outside Rome in 98 AD with a dying phone
and a high-school education, and learns that ordinary modern knowledge, applied
with nerve and patience, is enough to bend the arc of an empire.

## Working title
*The Long Way Home* (placeholder; alternatives: *Ultima Thule*, *The Ladder*,
*All Tracks Lead to Rome*).

## The central argument
The book exists to make one case: an ordinary teenager, armed only with a
standard modern education and the general shape of how things work, could change
history. The bottleneck is not genius. It is courage, credibility, language,
money, and the patience to do things in the right order. Daniel is deliberately
NOT a prodigy. He is average-to-good. That is the point.

## Themes
- **Knowledge as a ladder, not a shortcut.** Daniel can't hand Rome a computer.
  He can only move the bottom rungs and trust others to climb. The encyclopedia
  he leaves is explicitly a ladder built from the simplest arithmetic upward.
- **The quiet war.** Daniel hates slavery and illiteracy. He has no power to
  argue Rome out of either. So he wages the fight through inventions that make
  slaves less economical and literacy more useful. The attack on slavery is
  never accidental, and almost never spoken aloud.
- **The cost of being the only one who knows.** Loneliness, the impossibility of
  ever being fully understood, the temptation of god-like leverage, the fear of
  dying with everything still in his head.
- **Is changing history hubris or duty?** The book does not resolve this. Daniel
  argues both sides with himself across decades.

## Tone
Grounded, wry, intelligent. Real failures and humiliations. Period texture that
feels lived-in. Hope earned through labor, not handed out. The reader is treated
as an adult who can connect dots.

## Hard constraints (the honesty rules)
- Daniel only knows what a bright-but-normal 17-year-old in the 2020s would. He
  is allowed to be WRONG in the way such a teenager would be wrong.
- No deus ex machina. The phone dies within days (see world rules -- the phone
  dies around day three to four). After that, everything is human memory, fallible.
- Every technology obeys the material reality of 98 AD. Things fail for real
  reasons: bad steel, no precision tooling, no chemistry, social resistance.
- The "impossible bait" (computers, rockets, floating trains) is deliberately
  seeded as imagination fuel, and the book is honest that Daniel will never see
  most of it built.

## The shape of the whole (one paragraph)
He arrives, panics, is arrested, survives on a drawn map and a refusal to be a
nobody. He learns Latin under survival pressure and builds a hot-air balloon
that makes him the man who flew. Money and protection follow, then a workshop,
then enemies. He pushes harder - steel, the telescope, gunpowder demonstrated by
a cannon he knows will fail, rail carts, crop rotation, the germ framing dressed
as invisible divine creatures, a printing press, an annual story contest to make
a whole civilization start dreaming in technology. He navigates Trajan's reign,
the Dacian and Parthian wars, the lethal politics of being too valuable and too
powerful. Trajan dies; Daniel must rebuild everything with Hadrian. He redirects
Roman ambition toward the ocean with a true-and-false map. He spends his last
decades on institutions meant to outlive him and on the encyclopedia - written
first in English, partly translated - a ladder for whoever comes after. He dies
far from home, having moved the bottom rungs. An epilogue, centuries later,
shows someone beginning to climb.
