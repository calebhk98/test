# Chapter 7: Iterations

The seam was always where it died.

I figured that much out before the spring rains stopped, standing in the slaughter-lot with a flap of scorched linen in my good hand and the smell of old blood baked into the dirt under me. Every bag I made wanted to fly. Heat went in, the cloth bellied out, the whole stupid thing tugged at the string like it had somewhere to be. Then a seam would let go with a sound like someone tearing a sheet, and my afternoon was on the ground.

So I went after the seams. That was the spring.

A normal person sews two edges together and trusts the thread. I had done that, and the thread held fine. The cloth around the thread did not. Hot air found the little gaps the needle punched, every stitch a tiny door, and leaked out through a thousand doors faster than I could heat it. I needed the edge itself tighter than the field of the cloth, which should have been obvious and was not, to me, for about three weeks.

What got me there was watching an old man patch a grain sack down past the midden. He folded the mouth of the sack over on itself twice before he ran his cord through, so the strain pulled on a triple thickness and not on one. I stood there long enough that he looked up at me with the flat patient hate of a man who would like the foreigner to go be strange somewhere else.

I folded my seams after that. Edge over, edge over again, sew through all three, then lay the mutton grease down the inside of the fold so the cloth on either side of the holes was sealed before air could reach the thread. It was slow. My fingers cramped. The needle went under the nail of my left thumb once and I bled all over a quarter-libra of linen I could not afford to waste and used it anyway, a brown smear up one panel like the bag had its own old wound.

The folded seam held. A thing that had given me ten breaths in the autumn gave me, on a gray morning in what I think was April, something past a hundred. I counted them out loud like an idiot, in Latin, because counting was the one thing my Latin did cleanly.

Heras came out near the end of it, picking his way around the drying blood with the hem of his tunic pinched up in two fingers.

"You are talking to it," he said.

"I'm counting."

"You are counting at it. There is a difference." He stopped a careful distance from the brazier — since the autumn he had stopped coming close to the fire, after watching me burn my arm putting one out. He did not trust the project not to do that again. He was right not to.

The bag sat fat above the green-wood cone, straining, lit yellow from underneath. A hundred and forty breaths. It was beautiful, and it was going to come down the second I let the fire die, and we both knew it, and the heat was the whole next problem, which I had no idea how to solve and had been not-thinking about for a month.

"How long can a man hold a fire under it," Heras said. He had a way of saying the thing I was avoiding as though he had just thought of it himself.

"As long as there's a man and a fire."

"And the man stands in the smoke holding a cone of green wood, and the cloth above him is grease, and the fire is open and below all of it." He let that sit. "You burned your arm putting one of these out. You have, I think, two arms."

I let the fire die. The bag came down over the cone like a slow gray ghost lying down to sleep, and I caught it before it touched the coals.

---

The heat was the second war, and it was worse than the seams, because a seam I could see.

There is no dial on fire. I kept reaching, in my head, for the knob on my mom's gas range, the little click and the blue ring you could turn down to a whisper or up to a roar, and there was nothing under my hand but coals and air and the choice between more and less in the crudest possible terms. More meant flame, and flame meant the open mouth of a greased linen bag hanging directly over it, which is a sentence that should end with somebody getting hurt and usually did.

What I wanted was to put the heat inside something. Carry the fire in a box, so the box could be hot and the bag above it could be merely warm. I built three versions of that box and I am not proud of any of them.

The first was a cracked cooking pot Stichus smuggled out with the bottom already gone, that I packed with coals and held up into the throat of the bag on a forked stick. It worked, a little, for about as long as it took the bottom of the bag to char where the pot kissed it. A contained fire is still a fire, and clay throws its heat in every direction including up into the cloth eight inches above it. I scorched a brown ring into a panel I had folded and greased for two days, and said a word in English Heras asked me to translate, and I told him it meant the same thing in Thule that it did here.

The second version had a lid, a tin of pierced bronze that an old soldier sold me for half the coppers I had, which were Heras's coppers, which I will get to. The holes let the heat up and were supposed to keep the sparks down. They did neither well. A spark is small and a hole is a hole, and one evening a single ember went up through the bronze and into the throat of the bag and I watched a glowing orange dot crawl up the inside of the cloth like it was reading the seam I had sewn, looking for the way in. I did not put that fire out in time.

A small fire, as fires go. The bag went fast — a fat gray belly turned to a falling sheet of flame in the time it takes to stand up. I had been working closer to the shed that evening because the wind off the river was bad and I wanted the wall to break it, and a sheet of flame does not care about your plans for it. It came down across the lean-to where the women stacked the cut faggots for the fleece fires, the dry brushwood took, and for about thirty seconds I thought I was going to burn down a piece of the household I was a prisoner in.

I beat at it with the wet fleece I had soaking for exactly this and screamed for Stichus and Stichus screamed for everyone, and it was the kitchen woman who put it out, arriving at a dead run with a tub of fleece-water and throwing it in one motion like she had been waiting all spring for the excuse. The brushwood hissed and went black. A corner of the lean-to roof was gone. The back of my right hand had a fresh burn where I had grabbed at something I should not have, two fingers' worth, blistered up white by morning, and I cleaned it with cooled boiled water out of pure stubborn habit while it throbbed.

The kitchen woman stood over me in the wet dark. She looked at the black corner of the roof, and then at me. "Thulean." She pointed at the roof, then at me, then made a short chopping motion at the ground. "Next time — I don't put it out." Her Latin was fast and the rest of it ran past me, but the tone and the gesture were plain enough that I stood up and carried my fire gear to the far edge of the slaughter-lot while she watched.

I believed her. I built the fire on bare dirt inside a ring of river stones like a kid at camp, and did not work when the wind was up. The third firepot, the one I am least ashamed of, was a double arrangement — a small bronze cup of coals nested inside a larger clay pot with a hand's width of air between them, so the clay stayed cool enough to hold and the heat came off the top in a column instead of a cloud. It still could not lift a bag from the ground, too heavy to hang and too weak to fill. But I learned something carrying it around the lot in the evenings, watching the column of shimmer rise off it dead straight in still air and lean and break up the second a breath of wind crossed it.

Filling the bag on the ground, full and fat and hot off a fire I could make as big and as ugly as I wanted because nothing hung over it, then sealing it and getting it away before it cooled: that was the trick. Fill, then release. It would not stay up long. It did not have to. It had to go up high, in front of people, on its own, with no man and no fire under it, so nobody could say there were strings.

I tested it small first, with the threadbare see-through sack from the autumn that Heras had called very pretty. I filled it off a stupid big greedy fire I built in the stone ring with no cone, the open mouth of the sack held down over the flames on two green sticks until it fought me, until it was a hot round thing trying to leave. Then I pinched the throat shut with a twist of wet cord and let go.

It went up. No string. It went up over the rail and over the midden and a good way into the gray evening sky, turning slowly, the bottom still glowing faint where the air inside was hottest, and it kept going up for a while after I was sure it should have stopped. Then it slowed and leveled and began, gently, to come down a long way off, sinking into someone else's field beyond the lot where I never found it.

I had not breathed in a while. I started again.

Heras was next to me. I had not heard him come out. He was looking at the empty sky where the bag had been with an expression I had never seen on him, and he is a man who works hard at having no expressions.

"There were no strings," he said quietly.

"No."

"I watched for them. There were none." He was telling himself what he had just seen, out loud, because he did not yet believe his own eyes. He had spent a year refusing to be impressed by me. He had taken apart the numbers and the invisible creatures and the maps, found the seam in each one and pulled. There was no seam in this. A thing had risen into the sky with nobody holding it.

He pulled his eyes down at last and put the dry irony back on like a coat. "It does not stay up."

"No."

"It goes up beautifully and then it wanders off and falls in a field and a peasant beats out a fire in his barley and curses your gods." He had recovered all the way now. "You have made a thing that flies away from you. Congratulations. It is the most useless miracle I have ever seen."

"It's a year of work," I said, "to throw a sack at the sky."

"Yes." He looked at me sideways. "And men have paid more for less." He turned back toward the buildings and I walked with him. "You think you have failed to make a thing that stays."

"I have."

"You have made a thing no one in this city has ever seen. That needs no explaining. Cannot be argued with. Any drunk in the Subura would walk a mile and pay a copper to watch it." He stepped around a puddle. "The trouble is you cannot show it to anyone, because you belong to a household that would prefer you stop setting it on fire."

---

That spring I had a few other ideas I needed somewhere to put.

A man near the estate had a cart that used three people to move his tools from one end of a long building to the other and back again, all day, because the load was too awkward to carry in the hands and too wide to pull easily. I had been noticing this for weeks the way I noticed the lead pipes and the lack of soap — quietly, in passing, not ready to do anything yet. Then one morning I sketched a tray mounted over a single wheel and asked one of the household carpenters, through Stichus's translation, whether such a thing could be built. He looked at the sketch for a long time. He said it could. Two days and a fistful of iron nails, and the cart man moved his load with one hand. I do not know what happened after that; I did not go back to watch. It was the kind of thing that either spreads because it is useful or doesn't because someone has already thought of it, and I could not afford to be attached to the result either way.

I also talked to a man who managed a piece of land for whoever owned the household's estates. He was moving his fallow field in a three-year pattern: crop, crop, rest. I asked what he planted in the resting field. He looked at me as if I had asked whether the sky was a roof. Nothing, he said. That was the point of fallow. I asked him what he knew about legumes — beans, specifically, broad beans — and whether he had noticed that fields that had grown beans gave better crops the year after. He said his grandfather had believed something like that, which was an old man's thinking and not his. I told him I had seen the same thing in Thule, which was the most reliable lie I had available, and asked him to try one field. One field. He did not argue further, but he did not hide how little he cared for the idea. I could not explain why it worked because I could not remember enough biology to explain it to myself, let alone to a man who had been farming longer than I had been alive. One field. I filed the rest under things that would either prove themselves or not. The mechanism was somewhere in the soil, in the roots, in some accounting between plant and earth that I had a half-remembered sense of but could not reconstruct. I let it go. He never asked me again, which meant either it was working or he had already given up and was too polite to say so.

And the kites. This was Stichus's fault, or credit, depending. He had found a scrap of linen too torn to sew into anything and a length of stripped bamboo from somewhere and had made himself a flying toy that he flew from the roof of the shed in the afternoons when he thought no one was watching. I watched it for three days before I came and sat with him on the roof. The kite was a disaster, wrong-balanced, wouldn't hold an angle. I took it apart and rebuilt it in about an hour while he watched with the expression of someone who has had a thing improved past the point where it was still theirs. It flew beautifully. I made two more from scraps over the following week, slightly larger, and Stichus took them to the market near the fountain and came back with some coins and a tale of six children standing in a lane staring at the sky. The coins were negligible. The children in the lane were the point. Every child who went home talking about a thing in the sky was one more person who thought the sky was possible. I could not explain why this felt important at the time. It just did.

---

The household I belonged to had an owner I had still never properly met. I knew there was a man. Coins came down through the kitchen woman to Stichus to me in amounts that made it clear someone above her had decided I was worth feeding and not worth much more. Heras dealt with him, or with a steward of his, in rooms I never saw. The linen I sewed and the grease I burned and the half-libra I bled on had all been bought on credit against the idea that the strange northern man in the loft would one day be worth the outlay, and I had spent a year proving only that he could turn good cloth into ash.

That spring the city changed, and even out past the midden I could feel it.

The cart traffic that ran all night grew until I could not sleep for the groan of axles, and in the mornings the foot traffic ran the other way, toward Rome, whole families in clean tunics with the look of people walking toward a thing they did not want to miss. The emperor was coming, Stichus told me. He had won his wars on some river at the edge of the world and was coming home at last, and the city was going up the road to see him.

I had been in this world a year and never once been inside Rome. I had seen it from the field the first morning, the whole vast pale sprawl down the valley with the Colosseum gleaming new on its edge, and then I had been marched the other way, into a town, into a cell, into a loft, and never gone down.

Heras took me in. He said it was so I would stop asking. I think he wanted to watch my face.

We went on foot with the crowd, down out of the hills and along the river road, and the city came up around us all at once. I had done the math from the field — a million people, the largest thing humans had ever built — and been quietly proud of the math. The math did not survive the gate.

It was the smell first, a wall of it, smoke and bread and shit and fish and ten thousand cooking fires and something sweet and rotten under all of it, and then the sound, which had no bottom to it, a roar made of a hundred thousand smaller roars that never resolved into anything. The buildings went up five and six floors of brick and wood, leaning over the lanes until the sky was a crooked river of light overhead, laundry strung between them, faces in every window. People packed and shoving and loud, slaves with litters shouting a path, a chained line of other men walked somewhere with placards hung on them and nobody looking twice, a fountain roaring in a square with a crowd six deep around it. I had thought I understood this place. I had drawn it. I had a number for it. I stood inside it and the number meant nothing, and for the first time since the field I felt the full weight of how far down at the bottom I was — one foreigner with burned hands in the largest city that had ever existed, and nobody in it knew my name or my mother's name or the word for the thing I had thrown at the sky.

We passed a pair of men crouched in the shadow of an arch, one with a cup and three bone dice, the other watching him roll with the focused attention of someone deciding whether to be angry or pleased. Coins on the pavement between them. Three other men half-watching from nearby, two of them fidgeting. I stopped without quite meaning to. The one with the dice took the other's coins. Rolled again. Took them again. I stood there counting the rolls in my head, adding up what each side of the cube must be doing, and somewhere in that arithmetic a small clear thought clicked into place: the man rolling the dice knew which face came up most often. The other men did not. A man who knew the numbers and played in enough volume could make money on those dice as surely as he could from any trade. I walked on. I filed it under "lead pipes" and "no soap" — things to look at again when I had time and standing for it.

The emperor came in modestly, which is the thing everyone could not stop saying, modestly, modestly, passed down the crowd like the best joke. I expected a god on a chariot. I got a big plain soldier in a clean toga walking on his own two feet through the gate like any man, stopping to take the hands of the senators who came to meet him as though they were equals and he was glad to see them. The crowd around me went up in a noise I felt in my chest more than heard. A man near me was weeping. I did not understand what it cost a city to have an emperor who walked, after whatever the last one had been, and I did not need to understand it to feel the want in the crowd, the enormous shared hunger to be ruled by someone who came in on foot.

And in the press near a temple front, where men in togas were doing something slow and formal over a low altar, I saw the one who saw me.

He was an old man, gray, with the look of someone important standing very still while younger men did the work, and he had something dark and wet laid open on a board in front of him, an animal's insides, and he was reading them the way Heras read a wound. A diviner. His back was half to the crowd and his attention down on the board, and then for no reason at all he lifted his head and looked across two hundred people directly at me, at my burned hands, or my barbarian clothes that I still wore under a borrowed cloak because I had nothing else. He looked at me the way you look at something that has appeared in the entrails and should not be in the entrails. He did not smile. He did not frown. He held it for a count of three, and then a younger priest stepped between us and the old man's head went back down to his board.

"Who is that," I asked Heras. My voice came out wrong.

Heras followed my eyes and his face shut like a door. "A haruspex. A reader of the liver. An old and powerful one." He took my elbow and turned me into the crowd, away. "Aulus Vibenius. Do not point at him. Do not let him point at you." We went four streets before he said anything else, and when he did it was lighter, on purpose. "You wished to see Rome. You have seen it. It is loud and it stinks and a great many people have come to watch one man walk through a gate. Are you satisfied."

I did not answer. I walked.

---

It was Heras who said it on the road home, in the dusk, with the crowd thinning behind us and the river going copper.

"The whole city is in the streets," he said, "for the next month at least. Every man in Rome wants a thing to talk about over his wine that no other man saw first. Games, processions, a hundred fools swallowing fire in every square."

"It doesn't stay up," I said, because I had to be the one to say it now.

"It does not have to." He stopped walking for a moment, looking at nothing in particular on the far bank. "It has to go up, once, high, where a crowd can see it and a man with money can stand near the man who owns it and say, that one, that one is mine." He started walking again. "I have spoken to him about you. The man whose roof you sleep under. I told him you waste his cloth and burn his sheds and that he should sell you to the mines."

"Thanks."

"And then I told him what I watched go up over the midden this evening with no string on it." He paused at a puddle and stepped around it carefully. "A Greek physician of Pergamon, before any man he named — I would swear to it." I could hear in the dark that he was pleased with himself, and under the pleasure something more careful. "He is not a clever man. But he can smell when a device is worth money before he understands why. He wants to see it himself."

"And if he sees it."

"Then he will want every man of standing in Rome to see it standing next to him."

So: a year of burned cloth and two burned hands and a bag that flew away and fell in a field, and now a city full of people with nothing to do but be impressed, and a man I had never met who owned me and was about to decide that the Thulean and his sky-sack might be the most valuable thing he had ever bought on credit.

I did not sleep that night. I lay in the loft with my burned hand throbbing and the dead phone, the cold useless glass of it, in my fist where I keep it when I cannot sleep. One bag. One fill. One throw at the sky with the whole of Rome watching and no string and no second try. If it went up, I was worth something. If it caught on the fire I had to fill it from, in front of all of them, I was a barbarian who had set himself alight for their amusement, and the man who owned me would remember the cloth and the sheds and the mines.

I got up before light and went out past the midden to the cold fire ring and started sewing the best bag I had ever made, folding every edge twice, by feel in the dark, because the demonstration was coming whether the bag was ready or not, and I wanted it to be the seams that held this time.
