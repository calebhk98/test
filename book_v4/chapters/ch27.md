# Chapter 27: The Accusation

Vibenius came for the reason on a market day in early spring, choosing a morning when the wharf was loud enough that no one would notice an old man walking through it, and quiet enough at my gate that I would have no one to hide behind.

I was at the bench with a bad casting of Hermes's in front of me, a hinge that had cooled with a flaw down the middle of it, and I was turning it over and feeling sorry for the iron when Stichus came and said the priest was here. Not the priest's name. The priest. The whole yard had learned that word over the winter without my teaching it to them. It moved through the household the way a disease moves from the next street, before anyone names it.

He stood inside the gate in the same place he had stood before, in the reach of the watchman's brazier though the morning did not need it, and he did not look at the yard this time. He looked at me, and he said, "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," I said, as if I knew what was tomorrow.

"They convene tomorrow. In the house of Manius Aemilius Scaurus, who has lent it because his is a name that leans nowhere and so can hold a thing that has not yet decided which way to fall. There will be three of the college, and there will be your accuser, and there will be such of the great as care to come and watch a foreigner answer for the gods." He said all this in his flat old Latin, even-voiced as a man telling you the tide. "It is a room. Bring nothing you cannot carry in your two hands and your mouth. And the reason." He paused. "You have it ready."

I had not had it ready. I had spent the winter not having it ready, putting off the thought of it the way a man does not pack for a death he has been told is coming, because the packing makes it true. "I have it," I said.

"Say it to me now."

And there it was, the thing I had dreaded more than the room, that he would make me try it on him first, the man who read livers, the man whose whole art was knowing on which days he was allowed not to know what a thing meant. I opened my mouth and had nothing in it. A winter of nothing.

"Tomorrow," I said, "you'll hear it tomorrow."

He looked at me a long moment with the milk-edged eye and the clear one, and I could not tell, then or ever, whether what crossed the folded face was contempt or patience or simply the next thought. "No," he said. "Tomorrow I hear what you say to the room. The room wants a performance and you will give it one and it will mean nothing to me, because I have watched men perform for forty years and the gods are not in it. I want the other thing. The thing you would say if you believed the door behind the room opened onto the actual seat of an actual god and he was leaning forward to hear you. Say that to me. Now. Or do not, and I will go to the house tomorrow with nothing of yours in my hand, and I will read whatever I read, and you will take your chances with three priests and a senator and a man who hates you, on the merits, as a foreigner and a freedman, with no chances at all."

So I gave it to him. I will set down what I said, because it cost me, and because a man should keep an account of the prices he has paid, even the ones he paid in counterfeit.

---

"The knowing isn't mine," I said.

He waited.

"That's the reason. The whole of it. A man can't steal what was never his to begin with, and he can't climb to a god's house on a ladder the god didn't first set down for him to find. Where I'm from we have a way of saying it. That the mind is a gift, and the things the mind builds are the gift opening its hand. I didn't make the air hold up a hot bag. I found that it does, because someone made it do that before there was anyone to find it. I didn't make the figures count true. They counted true before I was born and will after I'm dead. Every rung I've shown this city was already there in the world, set down, waiting, and I only had the eyes to see it was a rung and not a stone. A man who builds a staircase out of what the gods left lying isn't reaching past them. He's saying thank you in the only language that doesn't lie, which is work. The fire I bring is theirs. I'm carrying it back to them and asking them to take the offering."

I ran out. It had come out of me in a rush, half of it lines I'd turned over at night and half invented with his eyes on me, and the worst part was that it was good. It would work in the room. It had the arrangement of the thing they wanted, the god put back at the foot of the ladder, the cleverness made into reverence, and not one word of it did I believe. I do not believe the air was made for me to find. I believe the air is air. I had taken the truest thing I owned, that there is a real world and it can be read by anyone patient enough to look, the one thing I had carried two thousand years still holding, and I had dressed it in robes and handed it to a religion I think is a beautiful empty house, and called the dressing-up piety, because the alternative had a courtroom at the end of its slope.

Vibenius did not tell me whether it was good. He did not tell me whether it was true, or whether he believed I believed it, or whether any of that was a thing he weighed at all. He stood and let it settle in him, the way he let a liver settle, and then he said, "It will do for the room." He drew his cloak and turned and the milk eye caught the brazier light and then he was gone out the gate on foot, and I stood holding a sentence I had built to save my life and could not say to my own face in a mirror.

---

The house of Aemilius Scaurus was on the Caelian, the kind of old house that has stopped trying to look like money because it has been money so long it has forgotten the want of it. They put me in an anteroom first, which I understood was so that everyone who mattered could arrive and settle and be a wall I would walk in to face rather than men I would meet as they came. Marcia had wanted to come. I had said no, and meant it for once. She had spent the night before with Tyche instead, going through every name we knew would be in the room, their debts and feuds and wives, building me a map of the field, methodical as she mapped a new account, and at the end of it she set down the stylus and said, "It's not the kind of figures I can add," which from Marcia was as near to fear as I had heard her come.

When they brought me in, the room had the great in it.

I read it badly. A man reads a language he learned late and under threat and gets the nouns but misses the music. There was a long room with the spring light coming in high and cold, and a sort of arc of chairs, and in the chairs the men of the priestly college, three of them, in their bordered togas with the apex caps that make even a fool look like a judgment. Vibenius was the rightmost of the three and he did not look at me. Behind and around, on benches and standing, the watchers, a dozen, twenty, men I half-knew and men I had never seen, here because a foreigner answering for heaven is the best free entertainment a Roman of standing can buy on a spring morning. I found Macer among them with my eyes, fast, the way your tongue finds the sore tooth. He was near the back, in his good wool with no stripe, and he gave me nothing. He looked at me the way a man looks at a load coming off a ship he has not yet decided to buy.

And Crispus stood to speak.

He had dressed for it simply, which was clever, the man of letters with no need of purple, and he began the way the good ones begin, with the gods and not with me. He spoke of the rites his fathers had kept, the colleges that had carried Rome since before there were marble temples, and how a city is a contract with heaven, paid each year in blood and grain and the right words in the right order, and how the fortune of Rome rested on that contract being kept by men who knew they were below the gods and grateful to be.

Then he turned to me, and he was kind, which is how I knew the knives were out.

"I bear this man no malice," he said, and the room understood, as it was meant to, that he bore me a great deal. "I have admired his cleverness. We all have. The flying bag, the figures the shopkeepers love, the water you may drink without dying, the tube that throws a stone across the river. Cleverness. Caesar himself has been pleased to be amused by it, and who am I to weigh my judgment against Caesar's." A small bow toward the absent emperor, a fence neatly stepped over. "But the gods are not amused by cleverness. The gods made the world and gave it to us in trust, ordered, each thing in its place, the farmer below the soldier and the soldier below the magistrate and all of them below heaven, and a man's first piety is to keep his place in the order and teach his children to keep theirs."

One of the two younger priests shifted forward in his chair, leaning, chin up. Vibenius did not move.

Crispus continued. "This man teaches Rome's children to leave their place. I do not accuse him of the flying bag, which is a toy, or the figures, which are a convenience, or even the water, which I am told saves lives, and I am glad of saved lives. I accuse him of the dream he sells them at his yearly festival, on Minerva's own day, under the goddess of true and honest craft, in printed sheets a child buys for an as and learns by heart. He gives them prizes, with his own coin and Caesar's favor and a great house's name on it, for stories. And the stories all say one thing. That there is a ladder. That a man may climb it, rung by rung, by his own works, his sons after him, higher and higher, the cart that needs no horse, the bag that holds an army aloft, the glass that shows the creatures too small for the eye, and at the top of the ladder, the child wrote it herself, the child he crowned, at the top of the ladder is heaven. The seats of the gods. To be reached by works. By cleverness. With no rite at the foot of it and no priest on the road and no offering at the door."

He let that sit. Then: "He calls it a prayer." Crispus had not forgotten my line at the baths. He had been saving it. "He told me so himself, that his ladder to heaven is the longest prayer there is. A prayer is a man on his knees with his hands open, asking. This is a man on a staircase with a hammer, taking. And in the matter of the invisible creatures he says the gods send into the body, which he teaches the physicians to fight with boiling and washing as if a man might hold off the will of heaven with a clean rag, I ask whether this is the reverence of a man who knows his place, or the impiety of a man who has taught a whole city to dream of standing where the gods stand, and made them love him for it. The Greeks had a long word. We have a short one, and a knife to go with it. I do not ask for the knife. I ask only that the gods be consulted, through their proper readers, as to whether they are mocked. And I will say plainly what I believe those readers will find: this enterprise of figures and printed stories and yearly prizes is not the act of a man within the Roman order. It is a foreign system wearing Roman clothes. And I will say plainly this as well: the invisible creatures, if they are the gods' sent thing, cannot be fought with a rag, and if the rag appears to work, appearances are what Rome should fear. I predict this plainly before the gods and this company, so that what follows may be measured against it. If they are content, I will be the first to embrace him. If they are not, then no favor of any living man can stand between him and what is owed."

He sat. It was, I have to say it, a fine piece of work. He had built me a gallows out of true planks. Every board of it was a thing I had actually done. The contest was mine. The ladder was mine. The invisible creatures were mine, and the clean rag against the will of heaven, that one I felt go in like a splinter, because it was the realest thing he said and I had been saying it for fifteen years. I noted, somewhere under the cold, his one falsifiable move: he had committed, in front of witnesses, to the creatures being the gods' undefeatable sent thing. He had no way to know what the fever sheds were already showing.

---

Then it was mine to answer.

The speech Marcia and I had built went out of my head the moment I opened my mouth. I stood in that cold high room with the great watching and heard my own accent thick in my ears, the v gone to w, a foreigner's mouth, and knew before I'd said six words that I could not win this on words. Crispus had Latin in his bones from the nursery. I had it from terror and drill. He could make the room laugh and weep in the same sentence. I could ask for bread and be understood most days. Against his rhetoric I had a wax tablet. I did not answer the ladder. I answered the rag.

"The man is right that I teach the physicians to wash," I said. "I'll not pretend I don't. I taught it because children were dying of a thing that gets into a cut, and the washing makes fewer of them die, and I have watched it make fewer of them die with my own eyes, in this city, in the fever sheds by the river where your honors do not go." My voice was shaking and I let it shake; it was the truest thing in the room and I had nothing else. "He says it's impiety to fight the will of heaven with a clean rag. I'd ask him this. When a man's house is afire, and he carries water to it, is he fighting the will of the gods, or is he using the water the gods put in the well? When the flux is in a camp and a tribune boils the water and loses ten men instead of a hundred, has he insulted the gods, or has he buried ninety fewer sons?" I had found the floor under me now, the only floor I had. "I never told anyone the creatures aren't sent. I've always said they're sent. I say the gods send them, and I say the gods also showed us the boiling and the clean rag, a father who sets a hard task leaving the tool beside it. To use the tool isn't to spit on the father. It's to say I see what you left me, and I thank you, and I'll not let your gift sit in the dirt while children die for the want of me bending down to pick it up."

It was the reason. I had not meant to use Vibenius's reason in the room, I had meant to save it for him alone, but I was drowning and it was the nearest plank and I grabbed it and it held. I heard it hold. The room went the particular still that a room goes when a thing has been said that the listeners would like to be able to disagree with and find they cannot. I had given them the gods at the foot of the ladder. I had said thank you in the language that doesn't lie. And I had done it about dead children in the fever sheds, which is a thing no Roman of standing wants to be heard arguing the other side of.

Crispus rose at once to take it back. "Charmingly put," he began, "but the question is not the rag, the question is the ladder, the question is whether-"

And Scaeva, who had not yet spoken, who had been sitting in the best of the watchers' seats in his broad purple with his long lined face arranged into pleasant attention, said, into the gap before Crispus could close it, in that low oiled voice that filled the room without seeming to rise, "The question is whether Rome is better with this man's fire or without it. And I have served on three frontiers and buried more of my own men to bad water than to the Dacians, and I find I am not minded to call it impiety that a freedman of Caesar's house has handed the legions a way to keep their bowels and bring their children home. Minerva is the goddess of craft. I had always understood that craft pleased her."

He did not stand. A senator of his years does not stand for a freedman. He simply spoke from his seat, and the speaking from his seat was itself the message, that he was so much larger than the matter that he need not rise to settle it, and the room read it, all of them, faster than I did. The great houses had been waiting to see which way the great houses leaned, and one of the great houses had just leaned, openly, on a spring morning, in front of witnesses, with the emperor's name and his own purple behind it, and the lean of one such man at the right instant is worth more than an hour of any foreigner's drowning eloquence.

I watched Macer at the back while it happened. I could not help it. He had not moved through all of Crispus's gallows-building, and he did not move now, except that something in his face eased, the ease of a man who sees that the thing is going to be decided by someone else and that he will not, after all, have to spend himself on it. He had brought me into Rome on a gamble of mules. He had made his fortune turn on me. And in that room, with a charge over me that could have ended in a knife and a crowd taught a lesson, he had hung back at the wall and let a richer man take the weight, and kept his own safety dry, and I do not think he ever once considered doing otherwise, because Macer thinks in seasons, and a season is a thing you survive. I felt the cold of it more than I felt the relief, and filed it where I filed the rest of what that old man was, next to the day he first called me a useful mule and meant it kindly.

---

The college conferred. That is to say the three of them put their heads together and murmured, and Vibenius's was the head the other two leaned toward, the old one, the one who would not lean, leaned to by the two who would. And then they did not give a verdict in words, because that is not how it is done, the gods do not file opinions. They called for a sacrifice.

It would be public. It would be at the Quinquatrus, Minerva's own festival, the same days I held my contest, and that was Vibenius's hand in it, I am sure of that much and nothing more, the neatness of it, my offense and my expiation folded into the same five days of the same goddess so that the city would see the wound and the healing in one motion and remember only the healing. A white heifer to Minerva, paid for out of my purse, led by my own hand to the altar before the temple on the Aventine. And the dedication. The dedication had teeth. I would dedicate the work, the contest and the press and the figures and the enterprise of it, formally, with the college's words, to Minerva, goddess of craft, so that all of it stood henceforth under her protection and within her rite, which meant within theirs.

I knelt on the Aventine steps eight days later in front of more of Rome than I had ever stood before, more than the demo, more than the cannon, the crowd packed down the slope and up onto the rooftops, and held the rope of a white heifer with gilded horns while a priest of Minerva's college made the offering and Vibenius stood by in his apex cap and watched. They put the wine on the beast's head and the meal between its horns, and the man with the hammer stunned it and the man with the knife opened its throat, and the blood came down the stone runnel cut there for exactly this, ancient and practical, a gutter for god's portion, and the smell of it went up with the incense, copper and smoke and sweet resin. A younger haruspex took the liver out steaming and read it and held it up and pronounced it sound, full, the lobes all present, the gods content. The crowd made the sound a crowd makes. And then the words were said over my work, the press and the figures and the ladder, all of it taken into the keeping of the goddess of true craft, consecrated, made Roman, made theirs.

I knelt through it. I made the responses I had been taught, the right words in the right order, the contract paid. I bowed to the smoking liver of an animal I had bought, in the name of a goddess I do not think is there, in front of a city that loved me for it, and somewhere in the crowd I knew Crispus was watching the gods declare, in blood, in public, on the goddess's own day, for the barbarian, which was the worst thing that could have happened to him and the thing I had handed him to ensure it never happened to me. I had taken the secret center of my whole life, the ladder, the real true ladder I am building in a script Tyche keeps under a floorboard, the rungs for whoever comes after, and I had let them bury it alive under incense and a knife and call it piety, and I knelt there and let them.

The blood ran down the runnel. The crowd loved me. I knelt through it and made my responses and finished.

---

It bought my life and it cost me three things, and I knew the price of all three before the heifer had stopped twitching.

The colleges had their hook in now, in the press and the contest both, the right to be consulted, the rite said over every Quinquatrus by men in apex caps before the winners could be crowned. The thing I had built to teach a city to dream past its priests now belonged, on the books of heaven, to its priests, and they would never let go of it. I had handed them the leash to the one device of mine that mattered and they did not even know what they were holding.

Scaeva had me. The lean had been seen. Every man in that room and every man they told had watched a senator hungry for the field spend his weight to save me, and Scaeva did not lend at small interest. I had walked into that house Caesar's Thulean and walked out, in the eyes of Rome, Scaeva's. The tube would hold one day and the powder was already loose, and a man who has saved your life on a spring morning is owed, when the asking comes, an answer he will like. Scaeva had spoken from his seat, in the open, and the openness of it told me what the spending was for: he was building a record, making it visible he had spent the weight, for whoever would need to see it later. I thought about that on the walk home, what a man who builds a public record of his gifts is arranging for himself when the calling comes.

And Vibenius. Vibenius had read me sound. He had stood by while the younger man held up the good liver, and he had let it be sound, and I have never known whether he believed my reason, or was bought by Scaeva's lean, or made a cold dry calculation that an old priest who would not lean had, for once, found it cheaper to bless than to brand. He never told me. He never once showed his hand. That uncertainty is itself the rope he hung me on, finer than Crispus's, and I carried it. What I knew, walking down off the Aventine through a crowd that wanted to touch my cloak, was that the old man had spent something of his own to make my liver sound, whatever his reason, and that a thing spent on you by Vibenius was not a thing you paid back in coin or in sulfur or in a customs man at the gate. He held a debt I could not see the figure of, and he had told me himself, that night at my gate, that what he did with my answer would not be in my hands. He had done it. It had saved me.

Somewhere while I knelt at that altar, a ship called the Corva had cleared the Tagus mouth on the morning tide, Bituitus at the stern with the sealed route specifications I had spent two years writing out in the cipher no one else could read and then rewritten in plain navigation terms so a Gaulish captain could follow them without knowing what they were. I had given him the wind bands marked in striped lines and told him to verify as he went. There was no ceremony. There was no one from me at the dock. I was on a hilltop arguing about gods while the most important departure of my working life went out on a morning tide without me, which is a thing I have never stopped thinking about.

I walked home down the slope with the smell of the blood and the incense still in my clothes, and Marcia met me at the gate, and did not ask how it went, because she could read it off me as she would a ledger.

"They have the contest," I said.

"I know," she said. She had known before I said it; she had been working out, all the hours I knelt, what it would cost the enterprise to have the priests' hand in it, and how to price around them, because that was her craft and her craft did not stop for the gods. "We'll work inside it. A hook in a thing is a thing two can pull on." She stood aside to let me in. Then, as I passed, she put her hand flat against my chest, over the stink of the offering, and held it there a moment, and took it away.

Inside, the baby was awake in his basket making his small blind sounds at the ceiling. I stood over him with the blood-smell on me and did not pick him up, because I did not want it on him. He had his father's fresh enemy and a god he would be taught to fear who had just, on the books, adopted the family trade. He gripped the air. I let him.

Out past the gate the wharf went about its loud business in the failing light, the carts, the porters, the river taking everything down to the sea. I picked up the bad hinge from the bench where I had left it that morning, the one with the flaw cooled into the middle of it, where the iron had gone wrong all the way through and only looked sound from the outside.
