# Chapter 25: Three Faces

The boy was eight days old when the senator's litter stopped at my gate.

We had named him nothing yet. That is the Roman way and I had not fought it; you wait, you let the early days do their winnowing, and on the ninth day you give the name, before which a child is not quite a person you are allowed to have. Marcia ran the books from her bed with the boy at her breast and a tablet on her knee, and worked sums one-handed while he fed, and once, in the dark, I came in and found her looking at him with an expression I had no word for in either language, something between accountancy and terror. She caught me looking. "He eats and he sleeps and he does the other," she said. "A sound investment so far." She put her hand flat on his back to steady him, and did not move it for a long time. So that was the house the litter stopped at: eight days into a boy's life, the yard still smelling of the oil Heras had used, Marcia's women upstairs sorting offerings for the naming.

---

It came with eight bearers, which I learned later was the precise number, the senatorial number, the number that says I could afford ten and have chosen restraint. The man who climbed out of it had the unhurried face of a man who had been dangerous once and had learned patience was better.

Publius Cornelius Scaeva was tall, and going soft in the shoulders under good living without quite losing the frame of them. Fifty-seven, fifty-eight. A long face, deeply lined down from the nose, the lines of a man who smiles often and means it about a third of the time. His hair was iron and brushed forward in the old fashion, and he wore the broad purple stripe without the smallest fuss about it, as Macer wore no stripe at all - two men telling you the same thing from opposite ends. His voice, when it came, was low and unhurried and beautifully placed, an orator's instrument kept oiled, and he used it certain it would be listened into.

"The Thulean," he said, and took both my hands in both of his, which a senator does not do, which is itself a gift, a deliberate spending of dignity. His hands were dry and warm. "I have wanted to stand in this yard for two years and have been too much a coward to come. A man hates to be one more face wanting something from the man everyone wants something from." "You honor the yard," I said, which was the safe Latin, the toga-Latin I had drilled into myself, and he heard the drill in it and smiled wider, forgiving me it.

He would not sit in the tally-room. He wanted to see the press. He stood over Eros's shoulder while a sheet came off, the times-tables of all the dull things, and watched the gray digits bloom on the wet paper. Then something happened that I had not expected: he leaned down and read the whole sheet, all of it, all the way across, and his eyes moved the way a man's eyes move when the numbers are actually interesting to him, not when he is performing interest for a guest. He straightened. "There it is," he said, softly, as if to himself. "There is the engine they don't understand." He turned to me. "They think you are about flying. Or fire. The Senate, when your name comes up, and it comes up, thinks fire." He touched the edge of the drying sheet with one finger, not smearing it, a man who knew where ink was. "But this. A boy in Lugdunum and a boy in Antioch can be made to think the same number on the same day. That is an army that needs no feeding. I have read your contest tales, all of them. My guests thought I'd gone simple." The smile again, the third-of-the-time one. "I had not."

He had read the tales. He had found the one true thing in my whole strange enterprise - the figures, the steady work under the spectacle - and named it back to me in my own yard. I felt the warmth of being seen go up my spine before my head could catch it and call it what it was: a man who had done his reading on me, down to the bone of what I cared about, and was holding it out to show he understood the cost of what it took to build. I have been flattered by experts. Romans flatter as they cook, richly and at length and with a clear sense of who is paying. This was something else. It was the reading itself that should have worried me.

Then he showed me the bit. We walked. He talked, and the talk was a country in itself, broad and pleasant and watered everywhere with names, this consul who was a cousin, that legate who owed him, and woven through it, never once landing, the soft repeating word: useful. How few men were useful. How a useful man in Rome was a coin every great house wanted in its purse, and how a coin in too many purses got worn thin and clipped, and how a wise man chose the one purse. He did not say be in my purse. He said it for an hour without saying it, and at the end of the hour I understood that I had been proposed to, and had not been able to find the sentence I could have used to say no, because there had been no sentence, only an hour of warm water you could not push back against any single part of.

At the gate he took my hands again. "Macer is a fine man," he said, as you'd say a dog was a fine dog. "A contractor. He thinks in seasons, leather and grain, this year's army. I think in"-and here he paused, a craftsman's pause, not quite stopping-"longer things. You think in longer things. I knew it from the staircase tale, the child's, the one your enemy calls impiety." He watched me when he said the word, lightly, to see it land. "We should be friends, you and I. Friends do small things for each other, and the small things make the large ones possible. I will start. Tell me a small thing you need."

I should have said nothing. A slave's no and a freedman's no are the same animal wearing different collars, I knew that, I had known it on a hill in Dacia watching a man burn for the lack of my no being a thing that happened. But I had a household now and a boy eight days unnamed and a yard full of people on my wages whose safety was the emperor's favor and nothing else, and a senator with eight bearers was at my gate offering to do me a service, and you do not refuse a great man's gift, because the refusal is the only message it carries and it is a loud one.

So I maneuvered - lied a little with the truth. "There's a customs man at the river gate," I said, "who holds my sulfur three days every load and takes his bite each day. It costs me a denarius and a week's temper."

It was true and it was nothing and it was, I thought, cheap, the kind of favor that obligates a man only by a denarius. A flick of pleasure crossed Scaeva's face, quickly housed. I had not understood, standing there, that by naming the customs man I had told him I import sulfur, how much, and through which gate, and that I had a temper that could be reached through my purse. I had thought I was buying time with a coin. I had handed him a map of where my walls were thin and called it small talk.

"Consider it done," he said. "There. We are friends." He stepped up into the litter, and the curtain was half across when he leaned back out, easy, an afterthought that was not one. "The powder," he said. "When the steel comes. Hermes will solve the steel, your great men are pouring enough into his fire. And when there is a tube that holds, the man who has the tube and the powder together is going to be asked, by someone, to point it somewhere." His eyes were very kind. "Better it be a friend who does the asking. Macer would sell it to the highest bidder and not lose a night. I would use it well, and remember who gave it."

He pulled the curtain back another inch and added, without any change of tone, "The one who lost his hand at the wrist - is he settled with you, or still looking? Good workers leave when they are courted well, and I would not want your best men inconvenienced just when the steel comes right."

He described the man precisely enough that I knew he could have named him, and had chosen not to, which was worse. A senator's notice does not reach into a forge-man's kitchen. It reaches there only when someone has been asking questions of the right neighbors for a long time and writing the answers down. He was showing me the depth of his reading without showing me the reading.

The curtain came across. "Kiss your son for me. Choose him a strong name."

The bearers took the weight up onto their shoulders all together, a small grunt and a lurch, and the box of him went off down the wharf.

I stood at my gate until the bearers turned the corner, and then I went back inside. Behind me, the river traffic moved on the water as if nothing had been arranged.

---

Crispus I met by accident, which is how Crispus liked best to meet me, so that it would look like the gods arranging his triumphs.

It was at the baths, the good ones, where I went now because a man of my standing was expected to be seen sweating among his betters and where I never once relaxed. He was in the warm room holding a small court, four or five younger men of letters who laughed where the laughs went, and I saw him see me and saw him decide, and there was no avoiding it without making the avoidance the story, so I came over and we did the dance.

He had aged into himself well; vanity keeps a man, I'll give it that. Fifty-two and oiled and lean, the gray at his temples arranged. He greeted me with enormous warmth, which is how you know with Crispus that the knives are out, and asked after my son with the tender solicitude of a man who would have been delighted to hear the child had died in the night.

"And the work," he said. "Always the work. They tell me Caesar's Thulean has a senator courting him now. Cornelius Scaeva. A great honor." He let the younger men murmur at the name. "Though one does wonder. Scaeva is a man of, shall we say, appetite for the field. The triumph he was not given. One imagines what he sees when he looks at your little tubes." He smiled at me, the warm knife. "But that is politics, and I am only a poor reader of old books. I concern myself with truer things."

Here was the change, and I felt it before I could name it, a stair that wasn't where my foot expected it. The old Crispus would have gone for the lie of Thule, for Strabo and Pytheas and the geography of my fraud, and we'd have fenced over coastlines and I'd have won the room by being charming about my own dubious north. He had spent years there. He had worn that ground bare.

He had stopped going there, and the change sat in the air between us like a door he was holding open.

"The staircase," he said. "Your prize this year. To the stars." He said it to his young men, performing it for them, but his eyes stayed on me. "I was unkind about it in the moment, and a friend chid me, and I have reconsidered, and I find I was not unkind enough." A few of them laughed. He raised a hand, mock-modest. "No, hear me. A ladder of knowing, the child wrote, each son building a step. Charming. And what is at the top of the ladder?" He spread his hands at the warm wet air. "Heaven. The seats of the gods. The Thulean teaches Rome's children that a man may build himself to the gods' own doorstep, step by step, by his own cleverness, with no rite, no priest, no offering. The barbarian's heaven by ladder." The phrase landed soft and sure; he had thrown it a hundred times now and it flew straight. "I called it impiety as a jest. I find I meant it. There is an old word for a man who would scale the heavens by his own works and put no god at the foot of it. The Greeks had a long one. We have a shorter."

The warm room was very quiet under its dripping. One of the young men had stopped smiling and was watching me carefully, watching to see how I took a wound, which told me the wound had been aimed and the young men had been brought to witness it.

Crispus had always lacked teeth. He had wit and bile and the geography of my lie, and none of it could touch me while the emperor's name sat on mine, because a man of letters who proves the emperor's wonder a fraud has merely embarrassed the emperor, and emperors do not love men who embarrass them, and Crispus knew it and it had caged him for years. Impiety was a different animal. A charge, with a courtroom at the end of it and priests who could be consulted and a crowd that could be turned, and it touched the gods, who outranked the emperor, who was careful with the gods and would not spend a denarius of his own favor shielding a foreigner the priestly colleges had decided was an offense to heaven. Crispus had stopped trying to prove I was a liar. He had found he did not need me to be one. He only needed me to be a man who had taught a city to dream of climbing past the gods, which I had, on the page, in front of witnesses, and which was true.

"It's a child's drawing of a staircase, Crispus," I said, and made it light, and made it land, because the room was still mine if I was fast enough. "Her torch was bigger than her head. If the gods are troubled by a wool-factor's daughter, they're more nervous than I was raised to think them." A laugh, a small one, mine for the moment. "Where I come from we build steps because the gods put the mountain there. A man climbing toward heaven praises the one who made the mountain worth climbing. Call it the longest prayer there is."

It was good. I had gotten good. He saw it work the room and let it, gracious in the small loss, because he was no longer playing for the room. He had said the word in front of witnesses and watched me answer it, watched me admit in the answering that I knew exactly what charge I was answering.

"A prayer," he said, pleased, tasting it. "I shall use that. The Thulean calls his ladder to heaven a prayer." He pressed my shoulder in parting, warm and dry and brief. "We must dine. You are far better company now that you understand what we are talking about." And he went off to the cold water.

I sat in the heat I no longer felt.

---

I did not seek Vibenius. You do not seek a haruspex; a visit the colleges remark on, a man going to the priests with a question on his face. He came to me, and he came on the worst possible afternoon, and whether that was chance I could never decide, the same as I could never decide anything about that old man except that he saw further than I did and let me know it without once raising his voice.

It was the day before the naming. The house was full of the small frenzy of it, Marcia's women and the offerings to lay out and the bulla to hang round the boy's neck, the little gold case with the charm in it against exactly the kind of attention I had been collecting all winter. I had stepped into the yard to be away from it for a breath, and the watchman came to say a man was asking for me, an old man, no litter, on foot, and would give no name but said I would know the face from a ceremony in the year the present Caesar walked into Rome.

I knew the face. I had carried it eleven years. He had locked eyes with me across the crowd the year I first saw the city, as if he already had a question filed against my name, and he had stood at the bottom of the Subura lot the day my balloon burned in the street and called it an omen with two edges, and he had sat silent at Mettius's salon weighing me while Crispus circled. Each time he had looked and not spoken. Now he was at my gate, on foot, an old priest of sixty-seven with his face folded down into itself like something kept too long in a drawer, and the timing of it, the day before I named my freeborn son, sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

He would not come in past the gate. He stood just inside it where the watchman's brazier could reach him, and he looked at the yard, the press-shed, the well, the washing line, Stichus crossing with a load, the whole small kingdom, for a long time, reading it.

"You have a son tomorrow," he said. No question.

"Tomorrow, gods willing."

"Gods willing." He turned the phrase over as if checking it for false coin. His Latin was old and slow and very clear. "Men say that the way they say the weather. You said it as if you had thought about whether they were willing." He looked at me then, full on, the first time we had ever stood close enough for me to see that one of his eyes had gone milk at the edge. "Most men do not wonder whether the gods will a thing. They assume it, or they fear it, but they do not wonder. You wonder. I have watched you wonder for eleven years, from the day the fire went up over the Subura and the gods let it rise and then put it back down on the roofs. I told them then I did not know what it meant. I am old, and I have read a great many livers, and I will tell you the secret of my craft, which is that most days I do not know what it means either, and the art is in knowing which days that is allowed and which days it is not."

I said nothing. I had learned, with Heras, what silence buys you, and Vibenius was an older and deeper silence than Heras, and I held still in front of it.

He drew his cloak closer. The wind off the river had teeth that evening.

"A man came to the college last month," he said, "of standing, with a complaint dressed as a question. About a foreigner who teaches Roman children to climb to heaven by works, and whether the gods are mocked by it." He looked at the brazier. "We have not answered him. We answer slowly, and we wait to see which way the great houses lean before we lean." A pause. "Scaeva's house leans toward you this winter. That is known. A man wonders why a senator with old battles still in him leans toward a maker of fire. Your enemy with the phrases wonders it loudest of all, because if your protector is a man hungry for war, then the ladder to heaven is also a ladder to the field, and impiety becomes something with an army behind it, and that is no longer a question for slow priests. That is a question for the courts."

He had laid it out flat, all four of them - Scaeva's hand on my shoulder, Crispus's word in the colleges, the colleges waiting on Scaeva, and somewhere at the end of the slope a courtroom. He had seen the whole field from the gate, in an afternoon, what it had taken me a winter to half-feel as a coolness on my neck.

"And you," I said, because I had to say something and that was the only position in the field I did not understand. "Where does your house lean."

Something moved in the folded face that might once, fifty years before, have been a smile. "My house does not lean. That is the only power an old priest has left, that he can decline to lean while the great houses fall over each other. The day I lean is the day I am no longer worth consulting." He turned to go, and then stopped, half out of the brazier's light, and did not look back. "When they ask me whether the gods favor the fire you bring or are offended by it, I shall have to say something. I have not decided what. I have been not deciding it for eleven years, which is a luxury, and luxuries end." A pause. "It would help me, when the day comes, to know that the man who brings the fire knew it was a fire and brought it anyway, for a reason he could say to a god's face. Have that reason ready. I may come and ask you for it on a day not of your choosing, and what I do with the answer will not be in your hands. Name your son well."

And he went out through the gate on foot, an old man on the river stones in the cold, no litter, no name given, leaving me holding a debt with no figure on it, an obligation I could not pay down by a denarius, a question filed against my name eleven years ago and not yet answered, and now, by his own telling, soon to be called.

---

I went back into the house, where the bulla lay out on a cloth, the little gold case, gold because we could afford gold now, with the charm sealed inside it against the evil that comes for a child the world has noticed. Marcia had the boy.

I picked up the bulla and held it. The small weight of it sat in my palm.

Marcia watched me. "You're going to drop it," she said. "It's gold."

I gave it to her. She fitted it round the boy's neck to see it sit, took it off again, set it back on the cloth for the morning, all without setting down her tablet, and the boy made the small blind sounds a new thing makes when it does not yet know what is in the room with it, and I stood in the warm lamplight of the house I had built on the high ground and listened, under the women's voices, for feet on the slope.
