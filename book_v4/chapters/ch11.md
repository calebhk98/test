# Chapter 11: The Army's Eye

The soldier came on a dry blue morning when I had finally gotten a bag to behave.

I had the show bag full and tethered on three lines over the slaughter-lot, fat and trembling, and Pamphilus was feeding the fire under the mouth-hoop with the slow steady patience of a man who had learned that I would yell if he hurried, and Tyche stood at the line counting breaths under her breath the way I'd taught her to keep time, and for about a hundred of those breaths everything in my life was working at once. I remember thinking, with the specific stupidity of a teenager, that maybe I had this. Maybe the worst was the seams and I had beaten the seams.

Then there were two men at the gate, and the guard let them in without saying a name at them, which the guard never did, and I knew before I turned around that whoever this was, he was over Macer's head.

The first man through was an aide, you could tell by the careful way he held a leather case. The second man came in behind him and stopped just inside the wall and looked at my balloon for the length of about ten breaths, and he did not say anything during those ten breaths, which is how I knew he was the dangerous one.

He was maybe thirty-five. Not tall, not short. Built like something you'd have trouble moving off a doorway. He had on a plain military tunic, undyed wool gone the color of dust, with a wide belt and good boots that had walked a lot of road, and a face that had been out in weather for fifteen years and had stopped pretending otherwise. Cropped hair going gray at the sides. A nose that had been broken and set by someone in a hurry. He stood with his weight even on both feet like a man who expected the ground to do something.

He watched the bag. He watched the lines. He watched Pamphilus feed the fire, and then he tracked the heat up off the fire to the mouth of the bag, and I watched him do the arithmetic that everyone did when they saw it for the first time, the hot-air-rises arithmetic, and arrive at the bottom of it faster than anyone I'd shown.

"It's the fire that holds it up," he said. Not to me. To the air, confirming something he'd already decided. His Latin was clipped flat, frontier Latin, no music in it. "Stop the fire, it comes down."

"Yes," I said.

He looked at me for the first time. Took in the accent, the bare neck, the whole of me, in about a second and a half, and filed it.

"How far down the river could that be seen," he said.

Everyone else who saw the balloon asked how high it went, or how it worked, or whether it was magic. This man asked how far it could be seen, a question you ask about a watchtower. I felt something cold go down my spine.

---

Macer arrived before I'd finished answering, which meant Macer had known the soldier was coming and had wanted to make an entrance, which meant Macer had set the whole thing up. He came through the gate turning his gold ring with his thumb and wearing his good wool and his most expensive smell, and he was happy. I had not seen Macer happy before. It was worse than his other moods.

"Tribune," Macer said, and clasped the soldier's arm, and the soldier let his arm be clasped without enthusiasm. "You see I did not waste your morning."

"Not yet," the soldier said.

"This is the Thulean." Macer said it to the tribune as if pointing out a horse with a good record. "Quintus Marcius Celer. You will be polite to him, boy, he matters."

So that was the name. Quintus Marcius Celer. He gave me a short nod, the kind you give an instrument you intend to use, and turned straight back to the bag, and I understood the social order of the morning in that one motion. Macer wanted the contract. Celer wanted the bag. I was the bag.

"Bring it down," Celer said. "I want to see how long it takes a man to do it and how many hands it needs and what it weighs in the basket."

"There's no basket," I said.

He turned.

"It doesn't carry anything," I said. "It can't. It lifts the cloth and the air inside it and that's the whole budget. Add a coal-pot to keep the fire going and it won't lift the coal-pot. That one" - I pointed up at the bag, swaying fat on its lines - "would carry maybe the weight of a full water jar before it stopped rising, and it can't carry its own fire, so once it's up, it's cooling, and once it's cooling, it's coming down."

I was telling him the truth because the truth was my friend here. The truth was the wall between me and what I could feel coming.

Celer listened to all of it with his head tipped slightly, and at the end of it he said, "So make it bigger."

"And how does a man get a message down," he said, before I could answer the first part. "From up there. Flags? Fire signal?" He said it fast and practical, a second item on a list he was already running. "How does what he sees reach the ground."

That one stopped me cold. I had been so occupied thinking about how to get a man up there alive that I had not spent a day thinking about what the man did once he arrived. Celer had already done the next part of my problem.

"Flags," I said finally. "Coded flags. Agreed patterns before he goes up. Or a line running down to a man on the ground."

"A line to the ground," Celer said. "And if the line gets cut. And if the wind takes the flags. What then."

I did not have an answer. He moved on.

---

The money conversation happened two days later, in the tally-room, Macer at one end of the plank table and Celer at the other with his aide making notes.

Macer opened at a daily rate, the kind you pay a craftsman. Celer countered by asking what Daniel thought a battlefield observation was worth. I heard myself say twelve thousand sesterces for the contract term, which was too low, and I knew it was too low the moment I said it. Celer's aide didn't look up from his tablet.

"I was thinking forty," Celer said.

"For the contract term," I said. "With a ten-thousand holding fee while I build to spec."

Celer looked at Macer. Macer turned his ring once on his thumb.

"Eighty thousand," Macer said. "And the Thulean's share is his to negotiate."

I held that number in my mouth for a moment. I knew, in a rough way, that observation from height was worth more to an army than any fixed fortification. I knew I was the only person in the Roman world who had made a thing rise and come back down in one piece. I did not know how to say this in a room with two men who had killed people and would not flinch at killing me.

"Eighty thousand to Macer," I said. "And forty to me, direct, deposited with a factor of my choosing. Non-returnable if the project ends early."

Celer nodded. "Agreed."

Macer was not happy. But he was happy enough.

---

That night I lay on the pallet in the tally-room and did the math I did not want to do.

You can do it on your fingers if you're scared enough. Hot air lifts because it's lighter than the cold air it pushes out of the way, and the difference is small, a fraction of the weight of the air, and the air doesn't weigh much to begin with. To lift a grown man - call it a hundred and fifty pounds of Roman with his sandals on, plus the cloth, plus some kind of basket, plus a fire and the fuel to feed it - you need a bag so big I could barely hold the number in my head. The volume goes up with the cube, so to double your lift you don't double the bag, you build something that swallows the slaughter-lot. And the cloth to make it weighs more the bigger it gets, and the bigger it gets the more seam there is to leak, and the leaking was the part I had never beaten and was starting to understand I might never beat, because the cloth was linen and linen is a sieve.

And the fire. To keep a man up you don't light it and release it like the show bag. You carry the fire with him. You put an open flame in a basket under a person, under a bag made of greased cloth, and feed it the whole time he's up there. I had watched greased linen eat itself in the time it takes to shout. I had burned my own arm putting out a smaller version of this. I knew what I was building.

I lay there in the dark and thought about what I was building.

---

Celer came back often, which I learned was how the man worked. He didn't summon me to some office and have things explained. He came down to the wharf in the dry middle of the day, sent his aide off, and stood in my yard with his arms folded and made me show him things and made me tell him why they didn't work. Somewhere around the third visit I stopped being afraid of him in the ordinary way. I started being afraid of him differently - the kind of fear you develop for someone you've started to like.

Because he was good. That was the trap of him. He listened. When I told him something was impossible he didn't tell me I was lying. He'd ask one hard practical question, and then another, and the questions were good questions, and somewhere in the third I'd realize he wasn't trying to flatter the problem into working, he was trying to find the exact edge of what was real so he could use that edge and not an inch more.

"You're afraid of it," he said once. We were watching Pamphilus and two of the wharf-women fold a bag along its panels. He said it flat, as a note.

"Yes," I said.

"Good. The men I bury are the ones who weren't." He picked a thread off the folded cloth and rubbed it between his fingers, feeling the grease, the weight, the give of it. "I've watched a siege tower go up in the morning and be a smear of charcoal and screaming by midday because the man who built it loved it. Don't love it." He let the thread drop. "Tell me again why it can't carry its own fire."

There was a cavalry unit exercising in the field past the timber rafts that afternoon - a dozen riders working their horses through a sharp turn, and I watched one of them mount and land in the saddle wrong, his feet scrambling for purchase at the horse's flanks, and I thought of half a memory from some YouTube channel I'd watched one afternoon in Ohio about medieval cavalry. The foothold. The metal loop. The thing that didn't exist yet in any Roman stable.

I grabbed a scrap of linen from the pile and a stick of charcoal from the brazier and sketched it: two loops of wide leather strap, one each side, hung from the saddle tree at the right length to take a rider's weight. Simple as a belt buckle.

"What's that," Celer said.

"A way to keep a man on his horse." I handed him the sketch. "He mounts with both feet in the loops. He stays seated in a turn. He can fight without using one hand to grip." I watched him look at it. "How many replacement riders do you spend on mount injuries every season."

Celer looked at the sketch for another ten breaths. "More than I'd like," he said.

"The same principle as the thing you already know." I pointed at his own boots. "A foot brace, made of leather. A smith could make twenty in a day."

"I'll have Hermes look at it," he said, and tucked the scrap of linen inside his tunic. He handed the charcoal back to me.

The next day, when his aide brought equipment to inspect, I saw one of the soldiers carrying an arcuballista - the heavy two-armed cross-bow - braced against his hip and pulling with both hands against the string. He was strong. He got it back maybe six times in ten minutes, gasping between.

I drew another sketch that night: an L-shaped hook of iron, bolted to the bow's stock. Foot in the hook, pull the string back with both hands and a bent knee, string locks in the notch. A weak man spans a weapon built for a strong one. Any man, trained in a morning. No archers needed; just men who can be shown once.

I gave it to Celer the next morning. "How many of your replacements arrive knowing how to pull one of those back."

He looked at the sketch. "None of them."

"With this, any of them can."

He said nothing for a moment. Then: "Hermes again."

"Hermes again," I agreed.

"I heard you," Celer said. "Build it tethered. We'll see how wrong."

---

I made the basket out of green willow soaked in the river so it wouldn't catch easy. I built the fire-bowl as a clay-lined bowl sitting on a wet plank instead of an open pot, so that if it tipped it wouldn't just spill coals into a man's lap, and I made Pamphilus and the wharf-women run the lines so that three people could haul the bag down fast from three directions. I made them practice the haul-down empty until they hated me. I had a man stand by with a soaked hide to throw, and a barrel of river water, and I made the rule, which I shouted until I was hoarse, that the first sight of flame anywhere it shouldn't be, you cut the fire and haul, you don't wait, you don't look at me, you haul.

And I made the bag too big and too heavy on purpose, which sounds backward, but a bag that can barely lift a man rises slow, and slow was the only friend I had. I wanted a thing that grudged its way up to the end of a short tether and hung there like a sulking ox while a soldier looked at the river and then we hauled it down.

---

They didn't even use one of the wharf-women, who could at least swim and who weighed less than a soldier and who I'd half-built the thing around. Celer brought his own man, because the army doesn't borrow a slave girl when it has its own.

The man's name was Naso. Titus Aelius Naso. He was younger than me, maybe eighteen, a recruit Celer was breaking in, lean and grinning and stupid with the particular fearlessness of a boy who has not yet been hurt by anything. He climbed into the green willow basket on the morning of the trial with the whole wharf watching from the wall and the timber rafts, and Macer up on the wall with his arms folded looking like a man counting money he hadn't been paid yet, and Celer at the head line, and me beside the fire-bowl with the soaked hide under my hands.

I checked the lines myself. I checked them twice. I had the soaked hide laid over the lip of the basket so the willow nearest the fire-bowl wouldn't dry and crack. I told Naso the rule: anything goes wrong, you don't try to fix it, you sit down in the basket and put your arms over your head and we bring you down.

Naso looked at the three haul lines, at the wet plank, at the soaked hide, at the barrel of river water, and at me standing there with my jaw tight, and he grinned. "Any man can hold a rope up there," he said. "What's the sky going to do, fall on us? Thulean's more of a mother than my mother." His mate on the wall laughed. Naso waved a hand and turned to face out over the river like he was already sightseeing.

We lit the fire.

It went the way I built it to go, at first. It went up slow. It grudged. It hated lifting Naso and told us so, swaying and sulking, and the fire-bowl threw heat up into the mouth and the bag filled and stiffened and Naso came up off the ground a hand's breadth, then a forearm, then a man's height, with the three lines paying out hand over hand and singing tight, and the wharf made the noise crowds make, that low rising aaah, and Naso whooped and stood up in the basket to wave.

He should not have stood up.

When he stood the basket swung and his weight came off center and the bag heeled over, just a little, just enough, and the mouth tipped and the flame in the bowl leaned with it and licked sideways up out of the bowl toward the cloth, and I was already shouting haul before my mind had finished the thought, and the three of them hauled, and the bag came heeling down fast and crooked, and the leaning flame found the grease.

It is so fast. There is no moment where you might still put it out. There is a seam, and then there is fire along the whole seam, a brown line opening into a bright one, and the bag that was lifting a man is suddenly a bag pouring fire down onto the man it was lifting.

Naso did the worst possible thing, which was the human thing, which was that he tried to climb out of the basket while it was still a man's height up and falling. He got a leg over the willow with fire coming down over his shoulders and his tunic catching and he half-jumped, half-fell, and the basket came down on top of him with the burning cloth folding down over all of it like a wave, and then there were six people in the smoke including me and the soaked hide was on him and the river water was going over him in barrels and I had my own hands in it beating at the cloth on his back and I did not feel my arms at all.

We got him out. We got the fire off him. The bag burned itself to a rag of ash on the wet ground in about the time it takes to tell it, and stank of mutton grease and scorched hair, a smell I will not get out of the back of my throat as long as I live.

Naso was alive. He was screaming, which I had to tell myself was good, because the ones who go quiet are worse. His back and the backs of his arms and one whole side of his neck were burned down past the red into the gray-white that Heras would later, very quietly, tell me was the bad kind, the kind that doesn't hurt at the center because the part that feels is already gone. His left hand had taken the fire-bowl when he fell on it and would never be a hand again, not as it had been when he climbed in grinning an hour before. His ankle was broken from the fall. His grin was gone and would be gone for months and some of it would never come back.

But he was alive. I said it that day too, out loud, to nobody, while Heras worked on him in the shade of the shed with the cleanest hands in Rome and a face that did not look at me, and I stood there holding the words alive, he's alive in front of me like the soaked hide, like something I could put between myself and what was on the ground.

---

Macer came down off the wall.

I thought he was going to be angry. That a burned soldier was a ruined contract, a dead deal, a thing gone wrong in front of an army officer he'd been courting. I almost wanted it. I wanted somebody to be as horrified as I was so I wouldn't have to carry the whole weight of it alone.

Macer looked at Naso on the ground, and at the rag of ash that had been four months of my work, and at me, and he turned his gold ring once with his thumb.

"Smaller fire next time," he said. "Or a longer basket. Work it out."

And Celer, who had hauled the line until his palms bled and who had been the first one into the smoke after me, crouched by his recruit and said quietly, "Hercules' balls, boy. Stop it." Naso stopped screaming for a full breath and lay there panting and shaking. Then Celer stood up and wiped his bleeding hands on his dust-colored tunic and looked at the burning rag and the broken willow and the boy, and I saw him doing it again, the arithmetic, the cold clean adding-up, and I saw him arrive at the bottom of it.

"He got up to a man's height," Celer said. "I saw the far bank over the wall. I saw the rafts on the far bank, Thulean, I counted them." There was no cruelty in how he said it, which was the unbearable part. "It works. It works and it's a butcher and we'll do it again with a smaller fire and a longer basket like the man says." He glanced down at Naso, and something moved in his face that he didn't let stay. "And next time the fool sits down when he's told."

He walked back up to the gate to send his aide for a litter.

I stood in the wet ash with the smell of it still in the air and watched Heras's clean hands move over what was left of Naso's back. I picked up the broken end of one of the willow lines, the line I had checked twice that morning.

I held it for a long time.
