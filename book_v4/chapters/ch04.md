# Chapter 4: Heras

They came for me the next morning, which I worked out from the light and from the fact that I'd slept, badly, in two or three pieces, waking each time to the same dark and the same smell and the bar still across the door. Somewhere in the middle of it I'd done the map-app thing, hunched in the corner with the screen turned to the wall and dimmed to almost nothing, staring at the real coast of Africa until my eyes watered and the battery ticked from eleven to ten. Then I'd put it away and held it against my chest like an idiot and gone back to sleep.

The optio opened the door and stood there a second, recalculating me. He had bread again. I was getting fond of the bread, which is a sad sentence, but there it is. He jerked his chin. I came.

We didn't go far. Up the lane, around a corner, through a low door into a room with actual furniture, a long scarred table and a stool and a brazier in the corner that made the air swim. A window with real shutters. After the cell it felt like a hotel. The optio put me on the stool and crossed his arms by the door, and we waited, and I ate the bread and tried to look like a learned man from a cold far country and not like a kid one degree off pissing himself from nerves.

The physician came in out of the white morning light with a leather satchel over one shoulder and stopped just inside the door to look at me before he said anything to anyone.

Maybe forty-five, going gray at the temples, clean-shaven in a town where most men weren't, with a long face and a downturned mouth and eyes that did all the work. A plain good tunic, a darker wrap over it, no jewelry, sandals that had been resoled. His hands were the cleanest I'd seen since I got here. He sized me up the way a man takes inventory of a job he's been handed without enough information.

He said something to the optio. The optio answered short. The physician said something back, longer, dry, and the optio's jaw tightened, and I understood with a small private joy that the doctor had just been faintly rude to a soldier and the soldier had to take it. So this was somebody. A freedman, I'd learn, born a slave and freed, near the bottom of the ladder, except that he knew bodies and he knew Greek, and that bought a kind of room his birth didn't.

Then he came over and crouched in front of me, easily, knees not complaining, and started in on me.

He turned my head side to side into the light. He thumbed my lower eyelid down and peered at the red of it. He picked up my hand off my knee, turned it over, ran his thumb across the calluses and the little white scar from a box cutter at the store, and made a low sound in his throat, interested, below words. He pressed two fingers to the inside of my wrist and went still, counting, his eyes drifting to the middle distance, and I sat there with this stranger taking my pulse in Italy in the wrong millennium and felt, of all the absurd things, calmer. Because it was the first thing anyone had done to me here that I recognized. A guy was trying to figure out what was wrong with me. That's something people do where I'm from too.

He let go of my wrist and sat back on his heels and asked me a question.

I didn't understand it. Of course I didn't. But he asked it like a question, head tilted, eyebrows up, and I spread my hands the way I'd been spreading them for two days - sorry, nothing - and his mouth went down at one corner. He tried again, slower. Same nothing from me.

And then he said a third thing, and one of the sounds in it caught on something in my ear.

I'd had two years of French and a lot of YouTube and the kind of vocabulary you absorb sideways from being decent at math and reading too much. There was a word near the front of what he'd just said that sounded like *pou*, and it snagged on a memory I couldn't place for a second and then could. *Pou*. Where. Some root, some prefix that lived inside English words about place and distance. He was asking me where. Where are you from.

Something in my face must have moved, because he saw it land. He went very still, then said the word again on its own, with the question in it, and pointed at me, and made a small gesture out at the world.

He'd switched to Greek. I worked that out later, that the first stuff had been Latin and he'd gotten nowhere and tried Greek on a hunch, and that Greek gave me my first toehold in this entire world, because Greek had loaned my language ten thousand words and a couple had stuck in me deep enough to hear.

I didn't have an answer I could say. But I had the gesture. I pointed at myself, lifted my arm and pointed past the shutters, out, away, made my hand do a long traveling motion, far, far, far, then crossed my arms over my chest and shivered, hard, theatrically, brrr, cold, a cold place, and pointed up, north, though there was no reason he'd read my up as north, and did the shiver again.

He watched all of this without moving. Then he said something over his shoulder to the optio in Latin, flat, and the optio answered, and I caught nothing of it, and the physician turned back with the corner of his mouth doing its downward thing, and from the whole set of him I gathered he'd just said something like *well, he's not deaf and he's not an idiot, which is two more than I expected.*

---

What I had, going in, was the map and the numbers. The map had already done its work two days running, and I had a feeling that the man across from me was a harder audience than a magistrate. So I went for the numbers.

There was a wax tablet on the table, a good one, two leaves, and a bronze stylus beside it. I made the writing motion, scribbling on my palm, then pointed at the tablet, asking. He considered me. Then he reached over, smoothed the wax flat with the broad end of the stylus, and set it in front of me. He didn't hand me the stylus - he set it down too, just out of arm's reach, so taking it was a small choice I had to make in front of him. I took it.

I wrote out the numbers. One through nine, left to right, the lazy modern shapes, the 4 open at the top, the 7 with no crossbar because I'm an American. Then a 0, and sat back.

He looked at them a while without touching the tablet. He said a word and pointed at the 1, and I made the yes face back, and he pointed at the 2 and worked down the line, naming them, his words nothing like mine, and I didn't try to learn them yet. I just let him count along his own row in his own language while I had drawn it.

Then I showed him the trick. Off to the side I wrote his numbers, the Roman ones, because I'd been staring at them on walls for two days and knew the shapes if not the system - I, V, X, a clump of them like a tally - and under it the same value in my figures, two digits, and I tapped the long Roman clump and then my two little marks and held my hands wide for the first and pinched them close for the second. Long. Short. Same value.

He got it. I watched him get it. His eyes went from the clump to the two digits and back and something happened behind his face, a recalculation, the problem becoming interesting. He picked up the stylus and wrote a number of his own the Roman way, a real one, big, the kind that takes a fistful of letters, and pushed the tablet across to me. A test. Write that, your way.

I did. Place value. I wrote it in four digits, and then, because I'm seventeen and I had an audience and leverage is a drug, I drew a little vertical line between the columns and tapped each in turn and held up fingers - ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, each one ten of the last, this is the whole secret, this is why it's short, every slot worth ten of the slot to its right, and the little round nothing holds a slot open when there's nothing in it. I tapped the zero, made a circle with my fingers, then opened my empty hand. Nothing, but a placeholder nothing, a nothing that does a job.

I have no idea how much of that landed. Probably less than I felt. But he sat back and looked at the zero a long time, then said one quiet word to himself. Then he sat back.

---

He should have stopped there. I should have stopped there. It was going beautifully. But he was a man who tested things, so he wrote two numbers his way, one over the other, drew a line under them, and made the flat gesture - go on then. Add them, I figured. I added them in my figures, fast, carried the one, sat back. He counted it his own way, lips moving, and his head did a small motion I chose to read as correct. Fine.

Then he wiped the leaf and wrote one big number, four figures, and beside it a smaller one, two figures, and between them a mark I didn't know, and he tapped it, and I understood he wanted the big one broken up by the small one. Division.

And here is the thing about long division: you do it for years in grade school and then never again because you have a phone, and the phone was in my pocket with ten percent of battery and a calculator I could not, could not, take out in front of this man, and I had not done a long-division problem by hand since I was about eleven.

I started fine. I set it up, the big number under the bracket, the divisor outside, and bit my lip and started the dance - how many times does it go in, write the digit on top, multiply, subtract, bring down the next. I got two digits in. And then I subtracted wrong. I had a remainder that should have been smaller than the divisor and it wasn't, it was bigger, which meant my digit on top was too small, which meant I'd lowballed it, and I knew the instant I saw it sitting there that I'd screwed up. The number was glaring up at me wrong, and the heat came up the back of my neck and into my ears.

I looked up. He was watching me. His eyebrows had gone up a fraction. He said something short, two words, and even without the language I knew exactly the temperature of it - it was *is that so*, it was a teacher catching a kid who'd bluffed the homework.

My hand was shaking a little. I made myself stop. I scrubbed out the bad digit with the flat of the stylus, the wax smearing, went back to that step, and said out loud, in English, to nobody, "okay, it goes in more times than that, you absolute idiot, count it," and counted on my fingers under the edge of the table where he could half see - the divisor, once, twice, three times - until I had the right digit. I wrote it, multiplied again, slower, and this time the remainder came out small, smaller than the divisor, and I let out a breath I hadn't known I was holding and brought down the next figure.

It took me a while. I made one more small slip near the end and caught it myself before he had to do the eyebrow, and at the bottom I had a quotient and a remainder, and I drew the remainder off the clumsy way, with a little r, because I wasn't going to try to explain fractions to a man I'd just face-planted in front of.

He took the tablet and checked it. He checked the whole thing, every step, his lips going, his own way, and it was the longest minute of my life, sitting there with my ears on fire, the great Thulean scholar who couldn't divide. And at the end of it he set the tablet down and said something.

Dry as a bone, whatever it was, with a thread in it I can name now - somewhere between amusement and respect held at arm's length, the satisfaction of a man who has caught you out and found you slightly more interesting for it. He tapped the smear in the wax where I'd made the first error, then tapped the correct answer at the bottom, and the corner of his mouth went up, just barely, the first time it had gone up instead of down.

---

We worked through what was left of the morning, the two of us, the optio shifting his weight by the door and the brazier dying down. Two men passing a stylus back and forth and watching each other's faces. He'd write a number, I'd answer it. I'd sketch a figure, he'd frown at it.

But Greek kept handing me crumbs. He'd point at the brazier and say a word and I'd hear *therm* sitting in the front of it, heat, like thermometer, like thermal, and I'd point at the fire and rub my arms warm and he'd see me catch it. He'd point at the window, at the light, and there was a word with *photo* buried in it, or near enough that my brain grabbed for it. Single words, snagged out of a river of sound I couldn't follow - familiar faces glimpsed in a crowd, and then gone. But they were the first words this place had handed me that I could hold, and after two days of total deafness I held them like a man holds anything in a drowning.

I never once understood a sentence. People who tell you they picked up a language in an afternoon are lying or they're geniuses, and I was neither. I had *where*, and *heat*, and *light*, and the optio's name, and the mangled middle of my own. That was the entire kingdom of my comprehension. I built everything I did that morning on the map and the numbers and my hands.

At some point I got the tablet back and drew the map again. The small version, the boot and the sea and the coasts. I drew it better than I had for the magistrate because I'd been staring at the phone half the night. His eyes narrowed at the real shape of the bottom of Africa, and narrowed more when I came up past where their world ended. He'd seen maps. You could tell. A man who'd read things, been places. The map was an argument he could weigh, not a wonder he had to gawk at.

I kept going. I drew what I knew. Past Britain I drew a gap, then two landmasses I'd known since the third grade, set in a wide western ocean - rough shapes, not accurate enough to navigate by, but unmistakable in their heft and position. The big one on top, the long one below, separated by a narrow gap, both of them too far west to be anything in the known world. I'd drawn them for the magistrate too, two days ago, and regretted it then. I hadn't stopped regretting it. But I drew them again, because on the second pass I'd understood something: the regret didn't matter. You cannot un-show a thing. If I tried to hide the western lands now, after the magistrate's clerk had already copied the whole map onto a fresh tablet, the omission would read stranger than the claim. So I put them in, and I put a careful nothing in the space inside them - no cities, no harbors, no signs of habitation. Just coasts. Just land. The suggestion that the world was bigger than anyone here knew, and that the extra part was empty.

His eyes found the western shapes. He sat very still with the tablet in his hands and said nothing, and I watched him think, and could not read the thinking.

And then I built the lie the rest of my life now demanded. I put the stylus point up at the top of the map, above everything, above the lumpy afterthought of Britain, into the blank wax at the very top edge, and tapped it - there, far up there - and crossed my arms and did the shiver again, the cold, and pointed at myself, and tapped the empty top of the world one more time. From there. The cold place at the top, past the last land you know, off the edge of the part you've mapped.

He looked at where I was pointing. The blank wax above Britain. He looked at it a long time, then looked at me, and said a word.

I didn't know it then. I caught it without holding it, a hard little two-syllable sound that landed in the blank space above the world like a stone dropped in a well. He said it once, looking at where I pointed, half a question. I didn't react because I had nothing to react with. He said it again, quieter, more to himself, looking at the empty top of the map. No wonder in his voice and no belief in it either. The flat dry skepticism of a man who has read the books that call that place a sailor's lie, weighing a boy who claims to come from it.

I only knew he'd put a word on the blank place I was pointing at, that he didn't sound like he bought it for a second, and that not buying it didn't make him want me gone. If anything it made him lean in.

Near the end, when the light through the shutters had gone from white to amber and the optio had started to want his lunch, the physician did one more thing with the tablet. He smoothed a clean section of wax and wrote one of the Roman numbers in his own hand - VII, clear and straight - and pushed the tablet to me. I wrote 7 beside it, my version, the compact version. He sat back and looked at the two of them side by side for a long moment. Then he reached for the stylus and, slowly, deliberately, copied my 7 into the margin of the other leaf, in a spare corner of wax he kept for his own notes. He made it twice, studying the angles. He did not erase it. Whatever he was going to do with that shape was his own business, but he was keeping it.

---

They moved me that afternoon. Out of the cell for good, the physician saying something to the optio and the optio saying something back, the upshot of it walking me through the lane to a workshop of some kind, a long room off a courtyard, benches and tools and the smell of glue and wood shavings and somebody's lunch, a household attached, a loft above where I was meant to sleep. A pallet. A blanket that smelled of another man's sleep. Two men who worked the benches and looked at me sideways, a boy who brought water, and a sense, never stated, always present, that I could not leave, that the door at the courtyard end had someone near it, that I was a useful object moved to a better shelf and still, in every way that mattered, owned. The physician had a hand in it. The optio did. Somebody above both of them did. I was lodged, watched, fed, and not free, and it was a palace compared to the cell.

That night I lay on the pallet in the loft with the noise of the household below me and the single luxury of stretching out flat, and I took the phone out to look at the photos. I did that most nights. The Colosseum, intact, gleaming, the proof I wasn't insane. My own face in the selfie, the last face I knew. I'd told myself I'd ration it down to nothing, save the last of it for an emergency that was never coming, and I'd torched most of it the first day and now there was almost nothing to ration.

I woke it up. Ten in the morning, less now after the night before and the map app. The lock screen came up, the photo behind the time, and I swiped in my code without thinking - muscle memory, four years of the same four digits - and the camera roll opened and there they were, and I looked at them.

I went to put it to sleep. I pressed the button on the side. The screen flickered. It went dark, then came halfway back, a gray ghost of the lock screen, and a small white symbol I'd seen ten thousand times sat in the middle of it for a moment, the battery, the thin red sliver. And then that went too. The glass went black.

I pressed the button again. Nothing. I held it down, the long press, the hard reset, eight seconds, ten, my thumb white on the side of it. The black stayed black. I held it up close to my face and could see my own eye in it, dim, reflected, and nothing behind the glass at all. No glow. No little red sliver. Off.

I pressed it a few more times. I knew it wouldn't wake. I did it anyway, the way you say a dead person's name.

I put it back in my pocket. The blanket smelled like another man. Below me a woman said something sharp to the boy, and the boy answered, and a door scraped, and the household went on in its language all around me in the dark. I lay there with the dead glass against my hip and my hands behind my head and looked up at the underside of the roof tiles, the same red clay as the village, as the whole world now, and waited to feel something I could put in the notes, and I didn't write the notes, because the phone was the notes. I lay there with my eyes open until I slept.
