# Chapter 39: Institutions

A man cannot leave money to a press. I learned this from Marcia in the storeroom, the way I learned most law, by proposing something stupid and watching her decide whether it was worth the breath to tell me why.

I had said I would put the press in my will, leave it Eros's wages and a sum to cut new blocks, and let it run. She asked who received the bequest. The press, I said. She said a press is a thing, like a hammer, and you do not leave a hammer money, you leave it to a man, and the man sells it when his son wants the timber for a boat. A will moves things to people. It does not make a thing that holds itself.

"Then how does a thing hold itself," I said.

"It doesn't." She pulled a fresh tablet toward her and turned it in her hands, thinking. "You want a thing that goes on when the people who care about it are dead, and the law was not written by men who wanted that. It was written to move a dead man's property to his living kin without a fight." She set the tablet down. "So we do not give the law a thing it hates. We give it one it knows. A burial club."

I did not follow, and said so. "A man dies," she said, "and his trade fellows want him buried decent and his name said once a year over wine." She uncapped the stylus, not writing yet, not done. "So they make a *collegium*, and the *collegium* holds the strongbox and the meeting room, and you can leave your estate to a *collegium* because the law knows what a *collegium* is and it does not fear it." She set the stylus flat on the tablet - explanation done, terms beginning. "The press becomes the thing the society keeps. It draws wages from the rents. It pays apprentices. It keeps going after you are in the ground."

"And I'm the corpse they toast." She had already turned to tally the rents on the tablet. "You're the corpse who left them a quarry's worth of pearwood," she said, "so they'll toast you sincerely a while."

---

It took the better part of a year, and most of it was not mine to make. I stood beside the thing while Marcia made it, because the work had stopped being mine and I had not stopped reaching for the tools. She found the form in an old guild of letter-copyers, a sleepy society of a dozen freedmen who met four times a year, drank bad wine, and copied wills and petitions for the courts at fixed rates. They had a charter, a strongbox, two rooms behind a fuller's, and a god, which mattered. Marcia bought their goodwill with a hall of their own and a promise that the new craft would not undercut their court work, and folded the press into them as a new branch, the cutters and pressmen and inkers entering as members who paid their monthly coin and drew their wages from the society's rents and the sale of sheets.

I endowed it. That dry word covered an hour in a notary's room while a man read out in formula-Latin that Marcus Ulpius Danihel conveyed to the society a half-share in the rents of two wharf-front shops, in perpetuity, the income applied to the wages of the printing-branch, the cutting of blocks, and the teaching of apprentices, and that I reserved no power to revoke it.

I signed it. Eros witnessed it, because the law wanted citizen witnesses and he had been one since before I was, and when the notary finished he said, dry, that he had cut blocks for twenty years for a man who could be sold, and now he was a brick in a building, and he wasn't sure which of us had come up in the world. Then he asked, because Eros never let a sentiment stand without putting a tool in its hand, who would teach the boy.

Someone had hung a small wooden board above the door of the press hall that winter, cut clean with the society's name in two lines. I had not ordered it. Eros had not mentioned it. I did not ask.

---

There was no boy. That was the hole in the whole structure, the one Marcia had named in the storeroom.

Eros cut blocks. He had cut every block we ever printed from, and he cut them deliberately, his eyes worse than mine from twenty years of pearwood by lamplight, holding the gouge at the length of his arm now and feeling the line as much as seeing it. The second pressman could pull sheets. He could not cut. Cutting a block in reverse, a whole page of letters backward and clean so they print forward, is something you learn in years or not at all, and we had no one learning it. I had never made it anyone's livelihood to spend three years cutting spoiled blocks before he cut a good one. I had needed Eros and I had had Eros, and the gap was already years wide by the time I saw it.

We took an apprentice the autumn the charter was sealed. His name was Vitalis, and he was twelve, a wood-carver's son from the Subura, freeborn, his father dead of the autumn fever, his mother glad of a place that fed him. He came with his own small knives wrapped in a rag and a habit of holding wood up to the light to read the grain before he set the edge to it, which his father had taught him, and which Eros watched once and grunted at, and that grunt was the boy hired.

The bargain the society made with him was the arrangement I had not understood I was building until I watched it signed. Three years bound, fed and housed and taught, no wage; then journeyman's wages and a member's share; and a clause that he would in his turn teach two before he drew his pension from the funeral fund. He was bound to the society, which would outlast me, feed him when his eyes went the way Eros's had gone and mine were going, and which he would feed in turn. I had spent twenty years being the only man who knew how to do a thing so no one could afford to kill me. I sat in the fuller's back room and watched a freeborn boy bind himself to a club so the knowing would be in more than one pair of hands, and the cleverness I had lived by all that time turned over in my chest and lay the other way up.

The first month he ruined eleven blocks. Eros made him cut the same line of the times-table backward until the seven came clean, and the boy cut it eighty times, and on the eighty-first Eros pulled a sheet and laid it under the boy's nose and said nothing, and the boy looked at his own seven printed forward and true. I heard it from Eros that evening, secondhand, from the man who did it, the way I now heard most of what the press did.

---

The contest was harder, because the contest had a god in it, and the god had a tax man.

It ran, still, as it had run for twenty years. I funded it from my own coin, Macer took the credit and gave the reading in his domus, and I stood as the tame barbarian judge. It was held every spring on the Quinquatrus, Minerva's festival, chosen long ago as cover, a thing crowned on the goddess of true craft so no haruspex could call it impious. And since the year they nearly broke me on the Caelian, since the white heifer and the dedication I had led with my own hand, the priestly colleges held a hook in it. A right to be consulted. A priest the colleges sent each spring, who watched the reading, took an offering, said his words over the winners before they were crowned, and was given a sum everyone called incense and everyone knew was for the colleges.

To outlive me the contest needed its own coin - an endowment, the same structure we had built for the press. And the moment it had its own coin it became a property with an income, and a property the priestly colleges held a standing right in was a thing they could, on a bad day, with the right enemy, claim to administer for the gods. Endow it as we had the press, Marcia said, and we would have built the colleges a permanent revenue and handed them, in the same act, the argument for taking it.

"So we don't endow it," I said.

"So we endow it carefully," she said, "and we feed the priest before he is hungry."

What she built I would not have thought of in ten years, and a Roman woman who had fought her husband's cousins for her own warehouse thought of in an afternoon. She did not endow the contest. She endowed Minerva. A sum was settled, through the same society dressed for the purpose, as a perpetual gift to the goddess for the keeping of her spring rite, the cleaning of a small shrine of hers on the Aventine, the incense, the priest's portion. And bound into the deed, as the founder's wish for how the gift be administered, was the contest: the prize said yearly on her festival, the winners blessed by her priest, the whole machine of it folded inside a pious endowment as the works a temple does with a temple's money. The colleges got their revenue named and fixed and theirs, which made them the contest's protectors against any later priest who might meddle, because you do not let another man tax what is already taxed to you.

The contest got walls. It became a rite, and a rite is harder to abolish in Rome than a funeral.

I said it cost more than funding it from my purse ever had, that we had taken a thing I paid for out of pocket and built it a temple's overhead, a priest's stipend, incense by the pound forever. "You paid for it out of pocket," she said, "and your pocket dies. Now Minerva pays for it, and she is very old and shows no sign of dying. She is expensive and she does nothing and she will outlast every man who ever tried to break you. For a freedman with enemies and a barbarian's name, she is the cheapest senator you will ever keep."

Vibenius was four years dead by then. He had called his debt in once, at the worst hour of my life, and I had paid it in a prayer I did not believe, dressed in law I did not understand, and now I had used that same prayer to build the contest a permanent priest. He would have seen the joke; I was fairly sure of that. The incense burned. The shrine got swept. The priest said his words in the spring, and the winners were crowned under Minerva's roof, which was more shelter than they had had before.

---

The patronage fund was Marcia's whole skill and almost nothing of mine. The idea I had carried for years. No one man can afford to fail at steel for ten years, which is precisely why no man has the steel. Hermes had chased it since before I was free, gorgeous one batch and brittle garbage the next, and every wasted batch was bread out of his own mouth, so he chased it only in the slack hours, between the plowshares and gate-bands that paid. So build a fund that keeps a craftsman at one hard problem for ten years, fed, supplied, free to ruin a hundred crucibles, asked for nothing but the trying and the writing-down of what failed.

Saying it was the easy part. Making it a structure the law would hold and a man could not pocket and walk away from took Marcia three drafts and a quarrel with a notary. A foundation in trust, the income of a settled capital tied to a purpose, administered by the same society that held the press, with a board of three who chose the craftsmen, judged the writing-down, and paid the next year's coin only against the last year's labor honestly recorded, win or fail. The capital could not be touched, only its income spent. A craftsman drew a living wage, enough to keep him at the bench and not enough to make the bench worth abandoning. And he held his post against his record of attempts, not his successes, which made the whole thing strange and made it work, because the moment you pay a man for results he stops telling you about his failures, and his failures are the rungs.

I put Hermes on it first, which was no decision, only a debt paid. He was past sixty and his shoulder had gone bad and he took the steel commission in the manner an old soldier takes a pension that lets him keep marching, with no thanks and much complaint about the board's record-keeping. He would chase repeatable steel until he died, and fail, and how he failed, batch by batch, the iron and the charcoal and the color of the heat his eye could no longer trust, went into a book the society kept and Tyche kept a copy of in the cipher.

---

I had not seen Felix in years when he came to the wharf about the fund. He had been a boy when he won the contest, a counting-house slave nineteen or twenty, sharp and starved-looking, who had written a tale of a ship that fed its own fire and drove itself across the sea on a metal that did not yet exist, and who had said, at the year's end, that he thought he could fix the pot. I had laundered his prize toward his peculium because the law would not let me simply give a slave a hundred denarii, and had not thought hard about him since.

He was forty now, near enough, broad through the chest where the boy had been all elbows, a free man six years and a bronze-caster with a furnace of his own behind the cattle market. He had bought himself out at thirty-four with the peculium the prize had helped fatten, and carried his freedom lightly, without the wound the freed-by-favor carry. He had heard, as the cattle-market end of the river hears everything, that the Thulean's wife was paying men to fail at hard things, and walked over to ask whether the pot counted.

I asked if he still had the tale. He had burned it years ago, he said, it embarrassed him, a boy's drawing of a ship with no oars and no sail and a stack belching smoke like a kitchen. What he had not burned was the principle, that fire makes water into a wind, and a wind can push a thing, and if you could catch the wind from a boiling pot and make it shove and shove again instead of just lifting a lid, you would have a horse that ate wood and never tired. He had built a dozen pots in the years between. He showed me his hands while he said it, the old burns layered under the new, like mine had once been. The pots all tore, or the wind got out the wrong way, or the bronze went soft at the seam where the heat lived, and that last was the wall, the same wall the old smith was at. You needed a metal that held its strength when it glowed, and there was none in Rome.

"There isn't," I said. "Hermes has been failing to make it for thirty years. We're paying him to keep failing at it now."

"Then I will fail next to him," Felix said, "and use whatever he gets."

I gave him a wage, a term of years, and a furnace allowance. I told him the board judged him by the writing and not the working. He heard all of it, and then he asked the only question that mattered to him, how many pots a year the allowance bought. I told him. He did the sum faster than I could now, holding nothing at arm's length, his eyes still good, and said it bought enough to fail honestly, which was all he had ever wanted. He had spent six years failing dishonestly in the slack hours, lying to himself the next pot was the one, and a man paid to say the pot tore was richer than a man who had to pretend it hadn't. Then he went off to talk to Hermes about charcoal, two men who would fail at the same wall from opposite sides for the rest of their lives, on my coin, with my blessing. I envied them, watching them go, both still able to read their work close.

---

Lucanus did not want the workshop. I let myself know it the spring of the apprentice, when he was seventeen and came to the press once because I asked him to. He stood in the ink-smell with his hands behind his back, as a guest stands in a temple he does not worship in. He was a good boy, freeborn, citizen-clear where I would always carry the freedman's stain, and he wanted the things the freeborn want, the law and the rolls and a seat in the basilica, to be a man who decides cases rather than one who cuts blocks. He had Marcia's flat way of pricing a room and none of her hunger for the warehouse. I did not push him. I had a daughter who could read close.

Ulpia was eleven and did the sums Lucanus would not. She came to the yard when Marcia let her and stood at Tyche's elbow at the tally-bench, and Tyche, who suffered no one gladly and the child least of all at first, had begun to hand her real columns, the wharf's own, because the girl was faster than the second clerk and made fewer errors. There was no place for her in any of it. A freedman's daughter does not keep a counting-house. She marries where her mother can place her and brings her figures to her own kitchen accounts. Marcia said this without heat, flat as a figure, that the girl was wasted and would stay wasted and I should not fill her head with a future the law had bolted shut. I said the law had bolted a great many doors I had walked through anyway. Marcia said yes, and I had walked through them as a man, and the girl was not one, and would I please not teach my daughter to break herself against a wall I could see and she could not. I had no answer. I taught her the nine-times course and then the elevens, and did not teach her the cipher, not yet, though Tyche had begun to look at the child as a hand she might one day need.

---

The machine in the shed behind the wharf needed three afternoons of actual trial after months of slow assembly. The Wimshurst discs had been spinning for two years, the spark reliable. The copper wire was fine-wound on an iron core. The crystal was the piece that cost me the most time: lead ore from the silver-workings, rough and grey, and a cat's-whisker of copper wire that had to be walked across its surface until it locked onto the right face. You felt it more than saw it. You knew the face when the wire found it.

When it locked, the earphone clicked, and I had strung the antenna wire from the shed's roof-beam to a pole on the wharf twenty paces off, with a sheet of the dot-dash code from the semaphore work laid flat on the bench. I keyed the first letters. Three short. Three long. Three short.

Across the yard, in the second shed where I had set the receiver that morning, a boy Tyche had positioned with an earphone pressed to his head sat up straight.

I watched him through the open door. He looked at the earphone. He looked at me. Then he shouted across the yard: "It's clicking. It's doing it by itself."

I was doing it, with a spark and a wire and a slab of lead ore. But I understood what he meant. The sound had crossed the open air with nothing visible carrying it. No wire between the sheds. No string. No man. Just the click, arriving.

I keyed it again. Three short. Three long. Three short. Then a word in the dot-dash: FELIX.

The boy started yelling that it was doing letters. Nearly off his stool. I put the key down and wrote in the bench-log: *first transmission across open air, no wire between, nine paces, signal clear.* Then I crossed out nine and wrote twenty. I had miscounted.

Tyche came in at the end of it and read the log over my shoulder. She said nothing for a moment, then: "Is that the one with no wire." I said yes. "Then you should write it down properly," she said, "not in that scrawl. A man who cannot read your hand will not know where the paces end and the notes begin."

She meant the cipher. She was right, so I wrote it again in the cipher, with the full account of the crystal and the whisker and the code, because it needed to be a rung. The boy who had been in the second shed came in while I was writing and asked if he could try. I told him it was a tool, and told him to sit, and showed him the code.

---

I came to the press one evening late, the autumn the charter was a year old, to find Eros gone home and the lamp left burning over the cutting-bench, against every rule of the shed, fire and oil and a hundred dry blocks. The apprentice was asleep over his work, his head on his folded arm, a half-cut block under his slack hand and the gouge fallen on the boards. He had been cutting a prize-sheet for the spring, a tale of a cart that ran on a road of iron, and had gotten the wheels and one cart and was at the smoking stack when sleep took him.

I trimmed the wick to nothing and pinched it out, and took the boy by the shoulder, not hard, to wake a son, and told him to go to his pallet, the block would keep. He went mumbling that he had the stack nearly right. I picked the gouge up off the floor and felt the edge with my thumb in the dark, Hermes's method for reading a blade you cannot see, and it was keen, he kept it keen, the boy who would cut the blocks when Eros and I were under the ground. I set it on the bench where his hand would find it in the morning, and left, and did not look at the block, because I could not have read it close enough to spoil my own pleasure in not needing to.
