# Chapter 24: High Ground

There is a sound a yard makes when it is working and nobody is fighting about anything. That summer I learned how rare it was and how little I had ever stopped to hear it.

Evening. The pressmen had gone, Eros last, ink to the elbow, a sheet of the Minerva-prize tales held away from his tunic like a man carrying a baby he isn't sure of. The river was going from pewter to nothing. Somewhere down the wharf a man was loading grain by torchlight and cursing the man above him in the easy way of people who do it every night. In the shed the water clock ticked its lie, off by a quarter of an hour and gaining, and Tyche had already corrected it by the sun and would correct it again at dawn, and the correcting had become something she did with one hand while reading something held in the other.

I stood by the well with a cup of my own filtered water, which still tasted of charcoal and of being right, and I counted.

The things. Eleven men and women on the wages, not counting the wharf-women who stitched piecework and the carters. A press that still ate a sheet in four and still threw off more printed paper in a week than the whole city of Rome had owned in writing the year I fell into it. A forge across town that great men sent money to because of a bronze tube I had made burst on purpose in front of them. A filter in two of the public fountains, where it mattered. A spyglass that did not work, sold to people who did not know it did not work. A contest that put a baker's dream of a self-grinding mill into the hands of children who now drew, in the dust of the Subura, carts with no mules and ships with no oars. And underneath all of it, unfinished, a pile of copper discs and zinc strips and shallow vinegar bowls that I had been fiddling with for a week and about which I had not yet told Heras, because I wanted to be sure before I showed him something new and half-wrong.

I had counted myself once, on a wax tablet in a cell, for whoever found my body, a list of what I knew with half of it wrong. This was the other list. Twelve years long, and I stood in the dark over my own well and could not make it feel like mine.

From up here you could see a long way.

---

I had walked through the markets that morning, which I did sometimes to be among the city without being asked anything by it.

You came up from the wharf through the cattle market and the new figures were everywhere, spreading into Rome's low commercial life the way water finds the low places. A fish-seller had them chalked on a slate, the morning's count and the price, in the quick hooked hand the slate-writers had developed, which I would not have recognized as my own digits if I hadn't known to look. A boy ran them off on his fingers for a woman buying eels and got it right faster than the woman's own slave with his counters, and the slave gave the boy a look I knew from the inside. Nobody had taught that boy from a book. The marks had simply gotten into the city by being easier to carry than the thing they replaced.

In a colonnade off the cattle market a man was selling small painted clay figures off a board, the kind they sell for the festivals, gods and gladiators and lucky phalluses, and among them, between a Hercules and a little Venus, were two of mine. A balloon, fat and gray-painted, with a stick man hung under it in a basket the size of a thumbnail. And a device with a long body and many little wheels under it, painted with a smoking stack at the front, that I had never built and never would and that the potter had copied off a printed prize-tale because it sold. A horseless cart. A train, in everything but the rails, which the potter had left off because rails would have looked like nothing to a buyer and a smoking stack looked like power.

I stood and looked at it for too long. The man selling them took me for a buyer and started his patter, told me the smoking one was a Thulean engine, a wonder from the far north where the cold makes men clever, that it would one day drag the grain of all Egypt up the Tiber with no men and no beasts, that his sister's boy had seen the drawings. He had a whole story. It was a good story, and it had drifted a long way from mine, picked up a sister's boy and the grain of Egypt and a smoking stack that meant it had been near some fire I'd set, the cannon or the contest or the general rumor of the man who flew, and come out the far end as a two-as toy a man could buy his child.

I bought the train. I gave him more than he asked and told him to keep the patter, it was a good patter, and he thought I was a soft fool and I let him.

I have it still. It sits where I can see it. A clay engine that pulls nothing, made by a man who copied a dream printed by my press from a tale entered in my contest by a boy who will never know my name, sold to me as a marvel from a country that does not exist by a man who will go to his grave certain that somewhere in the cold north it is true.

---

The contest reading that year was held, as always, in Macer's house, under Macer's name, the Macer Prize, paid out of my coin and crowned with my judgment, with Macer at the head of the room taking the credit for a thing he privately thought was a waste of a perfectly good dinner. I had stopped minding. Letting Macer own it was the price of it being allowed to exist. Letting the gods own the water filter was the price of the filter. Letting Heras call the boiling "Greek wisdom" was the price of the boiling. I had a lot of children, and not one of them had my name on it, and most days I told myself that was the design.

A girl read that year. Eleven, twelve, freeborn, the daughter of a wool-factor who had brought her along because the wife was sick and the man didn't know what else to do with her, and she'd heard them reading and she'd written something on the back of one of her father's tally-sheets, in the new figures where she'd run out of words, and somebody had put it in the basket as a joke.

It was about a ladder.

A ladder of knowing. She'd written that a man could not jump to the moon but he could build a step, and on the step his son could build a step, and she had drawn it, a stair going up into a sky full of the little painted stars children draw, and a stick figure on the bottom step holding up a torch for the ones above him who weren't born yet. It was a child's drawing. The perspective was wrong. The stick man's torch was bigger than his head.

I had to go and stand by the wine for a while.

Crispus was there, of course, oiled and elegant and citing somebody, and he made a remark about the spectacle of a barbarian teaching Roman children to dream of staircases to heaven, the impiety of it, the bad taste, and the room laughed, and I parried it with a joke against myself that let everyone feel generous. I won the room. I always won the room now, because Caesar had named me and no man of letters wants to be the one who turns out to have been mocking the emperor's wonder.

But I had drawn that ladder myself, in the dark, in a script no one in that room could read, years before that girl was old enough to hold a stylus. I had drawn it as the whole point of everything. And a child I would never meet had reached up out of her own head and grabbed the exact design of it and drawn the stick man holding the torch for the ones not born yet, and she did not know, she could not know, that the man clapping for her by the wine was the one who'd lit the torch and that he was standing in a room full of people and there was no one in it, not one, he could turn to and say: she got it. She got the whole thing. Look.

I gave her the prize. A child's tale beat a baker's machine and a senator's son's war-engine, and I made the case for it cleanly and Macer grumbled that there was no profit in stairs and paid out the hundred denarii into the wool-factor's astonished hand. The girl regarded me with the flat incuriosity of a child who has received exactly what she expected. I told her the torch was too big. She told me it had to be, so the ones above could see it.

Then she went to find her father.

---

I keep the phone.

By now even I have to reach for what it is. A flat piece of black glass and dark metal, the size of my palm, heavier than it looks, with a single eye of glass at the back and rounded corners worn smooth where my thumb used to ride. Romans rub a worn coin or a god's bronze foot, a rubbing they do without thinking, and I have done it to the phone for twelve years without meaning to and the metal there is brighter than the rest.

It does nothing. It is whole. There is not a scratch on the glass that matters. It is simply asleep and there is no waking it, no fire I can build, no wheel I can turn, no clever Roman trick and no clever Thulean one either, because what it needs is a current I cannot make and could not explain and have given up grieving for the way you give up grieving for a country.

That night, after the well and the counting, I took it out.

I do that. Not often. There is danger in it; a man who held that up in a market would be a witch by sundown and a dead witch by the next festival, and I keep it wrapped in oiled wool in a box under a board only Tyche knows about, next to a wax tablet that says Marcus Ulpius Danihel and made me a citizen, two relics of two countries that cannot write to each other.

I held it and I rubbed the bright place with my thumb and I did what I do, which is I tried to remember a song.

There was a song. There were a thousand songs; the glass was full of them once, I had carried a whole shouting world of music in my pocket and never once thought it was a wonder. And now I cannot keep one. I can keep the arrangement of one, the rise and fall of it, the feeling in my chest where the chorus went, and the words come up in pieces and the pieces are wrong, I know they are wrong, I am misremembering them as I misremembered the clock and the third powder and the boiling-point of water on a mountain, and there is no one, there is no glass, there is nothing in this entire world that can write the true words back to me. The song is in a place that cannot send to here. Same as the current. Same as my mother's voice, which I cannot any longer be sure I am remembering and not inventing.

I hummed a few bars of the wrong song over a dead piece of glass in the dark of my own rich house with my freeborn wife asleep down the hall - Marcia, six months along now, which was still a fact I would forget for an hour and then remember again like a door I kept walking into - and eleven people on my wages and the emperor's favor on my name, and I have never in my life been so completely alone, and I am not going to write any more about it than that, because the rule of this whole place is that you do not get to say the loud true thing out loud, you make a toy and a filter and a contest and let the gods take the credit, and the loud true thing stays in a box under a board.

I put the phone back. I put the citizenship tablet on top of it. Then I took the tablet off again, because it felt wrong, the new country lying on top of the old one like dirt over a grave, and I put the phone on top instead, and that felt worse, so I left them side by side, and that was the only honest way to keep them, two flat things that knew nothing of each other, in the dark, in a box, in Rome.

---

A few days later there was a commission waiting in the workshop from a farrier in the Capua district, brought by a carter who'd heard the workshop paid for quality targets in iron.

I almost missed it in the pile.

The farrier's name was Statius. He hadn't come himself - he'd sent his nephew with a roll of wax tablets and a covering letter in the rough hand of a man who writes sometimes rather than often. The letter said he'd heard the workshop paid for tables of iron consistency. The tablets held a list: iron color against readiness for working. Not my colors - not the ones I'd been building toward with the pile and the galvanometer chain, nothing formal. His grandfather had started the table and his father had kept it and Statius had added twenty years of his own horseshoes to it. Fifty years of heat readings. Cherry, bright cherry, orange, lemon-yellow, white. Against each color: what the iron does under the hammer, whether it will crack at the fold, how long it stays workable before it cools past use. He'd calibrated it against thousands of actual horseshoes - calibrated it against consequence, meaning when he got it wrong a horse went lame on a mountain road and a rider broke his neck.

It was cruder than my own data. It covered a narrower range. It was also more useful than my own data, because mine had been built in controlled experiments - the same batch, the same bellows, the same conditions across tests. His had been built in every condition a working forge produces: wet days, dry days, different ores, different coal, a bellows on its last legs versus one that was new. His table had already been wrong in every way it could be wrong, and it had been corrected against consequence. Mine had been wrong in a clean workshop with nobody's neck on the line.

I paid the prize - a year's skilled wages into the nephew's startled hands - and I copied everything. Then I went and got Heras, who had come by that afternoon on no particular errand, which was itself the errand.

He read the tablets carefully, without expression, drinking the wine I'd poured him without looking at the cup.

"Your farrier has done a century of work," he said eventually. "More carefully than your workshop has managed in five years."

"Forty years, technically. With his grandfather."

He set the tablets down and looked at me over the rim of the cup. "You're telling me a man who shoes horses has better temperature data than you do."

"For the practical range, yes."

"And you're surprised."

I said I was. A little.

"Why?" He was watching me the way he watched patients who were sure they knew what was wrong with them. "The man has been solving the same problem every day for forty years. The problem was: how hot is the iron, and will it hold the nail, and will the horse be lame next week. You have been solving the problem three days a month in a clean shed. Who do you expect to know more?"

I didn't have an answer that wouldn't embarrass me further.

Heras set the cup down. "The city is full of Statiuses," he said. "Every trade. Every generation. Men who have been solving the same problem under real conditions for so long that their knowledge has a different quality than yours. You've built a mechanism for paying them to show it to you." He picked the wine back up. "That's the useful part. The access."

He did not say anything about what the mechanism was called or where I'd gotten it from. Neither did I. I added the tablets to the encyclopedia that night, with a note about source and method, in the hooked-mark notation I used for things I was certain of.

Statius would not know that entry was there. He would go to his grave thinking he'd sold a table to a rich man's workshop for a year's wages. I thought about that and decided it was the best version of the transaction, and went to bed.

---

Heras came back to the yard a few days after the farrier's tablets, at dusk, with a flask of something Greek and resinous.

He was an old man now and let himself be one, which had taken him a while. He sat on the bench by the dead forge-corner, and we drank and watched Pamphilus's wife hang washing in the last light and said nothing for a good while, which between Heras and me was most of the conversation.

"The little wool-factor's girl," he said eventually. "With the stairs."

"You heard about that."

"Everyone heard about it. Crispus has been dining out on the impiety of it for a week. He has a phrase now. 'The barbarian's heaven by ladder.'" Heras tasted his wine. "It's not bad, as phrases go. He'll wear it out."

"He always does."

"Mm." He looked at the river. "You gave a child a hundred denarii for a drawing of a staircase. Macer is telling people it proves you've gone soft."

"Macer thinks crop rotation is a soft idea."

Heras smiled the faint way he did and turned the flask in his hands. He had the cleanest hands I had ever seen, still, age-spotted now, and they never quite stopped being a doctor's. "He asked me what you're for."

"For."

"What you are for." He did not look at me when it mattered; he looked at the washing. "He's owned you, he's sold you, he's dined off you for twelve years, and he sat in his own house watching you crown a child's drawing of a staircase to heaven and he leaned over to me and asked, in earnest, with money on his mind: what is the man for. What is the thing under all the things." He drank. "I told him I didn't know. Which is the truest thing I've said all year."

I should have made a joke. I had one. I felt it come up and I felt it be wrong and I let it go past.

"You've made the figures," Heras said, mild. "I'll grant you the figures. The boiling, the water, the washing of wounds - I've stood over the people it's saving, I'll grant you that and I'll thank you for it as long as I draw breath. Those are gifts. A man could give those and the gods would smile and Crispus could choke."

He set the flask down.

"And then there's the rest of it." He held up a finger, then a second, then a third, counting. "The tube that bursts. The white salt that took two years and Geta's hand. The drawings of carts that carry the grain of Egypt with no men, which means no men working and no men eating - have you thought that through? Or the ships with no oars, which a child draws now because you taught a city to draw them."

He stopped. I waited.

"I'm a man who has watched a great many people die, Daniel, and I have learned to be suspicious of any gift addressed to people who can't yet say no to it."

The river finished going dark while he talked. I let it.

"You think it's pride," I said.

"I think," Heras said carefully, "that I cannot tell the difference, from where I sit, between a man doing a duty no one asked him to do and a man who has decided he knows better than the whole world that made him." He picked the flask back up. "I have known you twelve years and I cannot tell the difference. I don't think you can either. And I think that's the most interesting thing about you and the most frightening, and I am too old to be frightened of much."

He let that land.

"Macer wants to know what you're for so he can price it. I want to know because I'm fond of you and I'd like to be sure, before I die, that I helped a man and not a fire."

I could have told him. Sitting there in the dark with the one man alive who came closest - I could have said: I'm from a place where the staircase is built, all of it, up past the moon, and I saw it, and I fell off it into your stubble field, and every gift and every tube and every loose number is me trying to lay one more step under your people's feet because I cannot get home and the only thing worse than being stuck at the bottom of the staircase is being the only one who knows the staircase is there. I could have said it. He would not have believed the from-a-place part. He would have believed the rest.

"I'm not for anything," I said. "I'm a guy who got lucky with a map and never stopped running."

Heras regarded me for a long moment with those clean old eyes that had punctured my germs and my Thule and my every comfortable lie for twelve years, and he did not puncture this one, which was how I knew he'd heard the size of it.

"All right," he said, and drank. "A guy who got lucky."

He left when the wine was gone. He patted the bench twice when he stood, the wood not me, told me to drink less of his country's wine and more of my own boiled water, and went up the wharf into the torchlight, an old man walking carefully on stones.

Before bed, I went back to the workbench. I had the Volta pile set out there - the copper discs, the zinc strips, the shallow bowls of vinegar I'd been assembling since the farrier's tablets arrived. I hadn't showed Heras yet. I wanted one more test.

I touched the two ends of the wire to my tongue. The small sharp bite of it, a live thing with no body, a current crossing twelve hundred years of empty air to find me in my own workshop at the edge of the Tiber - I had thought I would get used to it. I had not.

I wrote in the cipher that night: *first measurable current in the pile. held for thirty breaths before the vinegar weakened. compass needle deflects forty degrees at one palm-length from the wire. Heras has not seen this yet. tomorrow.*

Then I added the Statius tablets, and went to bed.

---

I stayed out late a few nights after that, until Pamphilus's wife had banked the kitchen fire and the only light was the watchman's brazier at the gate and the city's dirty glow over the rooftops, which was Rome at night: a million small fires pretending to be sleeping.

I had it. I could see it from up here. A man who came into this world with a dead phone and a head full of half-remembered drawings now had a wife, a child coming, a household, freed people who chose to stay, money he couldn't feel, the emperor's name laid over his own, and a city that drew his dreams in the dust without knowing they were dreams or whose. I could see the press running tomorrow and the figures spreading and the books I was building in secret, the ladder, rung by rung, in a script only one freedwoman could read. All of it laid out below me like the lamps of the wharf.

High ground has a military logic to it.

I knew enough soldiers to know what it was.
