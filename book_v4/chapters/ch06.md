# Chapter 6: Cheap, Mine, Undeniable

I sat with it for three days before I touched a single thing, because the idea was the only part I owned outright and I didn't want to spend it cheap.

That sounds wise. It wasn't. I sat on it for three days because I was a slave in everything but the word, and a slave can't just walk out and buy cloth. Everything I would need belonged to somebody. The fire belonged to the household. The cloth belonged to the household. The rope, the oil, the time, the hands, all of it priced and watched and not mine, and I lay up in the loft turning the bag over in my head and running into the same wall every time: I had nothing to build it out of and no money to get the nothing.

So the first work wasn't cloth. It was reasoning my way to why this and not something else, because I'd already burned a year on things that didn't take.

I'd given them the numbers, the best thing I had, a whole machine for thinking handed over for free, and what had it bought me. Heras showed them to a few men who kept accounts and they squinted and said the figures were clever and went on cutting their tallies into wood the way their fathers had. You couldn't make a man see the zero if he didn't want to. He'd just decide it was foreign and go home. Same with the little ones. I'd had the truth in my hands and Heras had taken it apart in an afternoon because I couldn't show it to him, and a truth you can't show is worth exactly what a lie is worth, which here was your neck. Everything I'd tried, a man could wave away. The numbers were a trick. The creatures were witch-talk. The map was a curiosity in a drawer.

A thing that flew, you couldn't argue with. That was the whole of it. A bag would go up into the open air over the town or it wouldn't, and if it did, there was no haruspex alive who could stand under it pointing up and tell the crowd it hadn't. You can call a man a liar about a drawing. You cannot call him a liar about the sky.

And it was cheap. That mattered more than the rest of it put together. I could not buy a workshop. I could not buy a single craftsman's day. But hot air was free, fire I could beg, and cloth, even scrap, even rag, I could maybe scrounge a piece at a time without anyone deciding I was worth stopping. I could build it slow, with my own hands, out of what fell off other people's work, and nobody had to give me permission for a beggar collecting rags. Cheap, mine, and impossible to argue with. I'd said the three words to myself in the dark the night I thought of it and they were still the only three that held up in daylight.

What I did not have was the faintest idea how to actually do it.

---

I started with the lightest material I could find, except the lightest material in Rome was already someone's garment. There's no paper here, which I keep forgetting even now. There's papyrus, and papyrus is money, sheets of it, scraped and pressed and sold, and the household had exactly none lying around for a foreigner to set on fire. What they had was an old man's worth of accounts on wax and a few brittle scroll-ends too stained to keep, and I wasn't getting near those.

So the first model was nothing. Was rag. I begged Stichus for it.

The boy hauled water and ran errands and was ten years old and treated me, on the good days, like a half-broken dog he'd been told to feed. I'd spent the winter making him laugh, which is a thing you can do across a language if you're willing to be the joke, and I cashed some of that in. I wanted the scraps off the cloth the women cut, the trimmings, the bits too small to mend with, the ravelings that swept up off the floor. He thought I'd lost my mind, which was the going opinion, and he brought me a fistful of linen offcuts anyway because I'd promised him I'd show him a thing the gods do, and at ten a thing the gods do beats almost any reason not to.

Linen is wrong for this. The first time I found it out was small and almost gentle. I cut and I had nothing to cut with but a borrowed knife that I had to give back, and I tried to sew a little bag out of four scraps of linen the size of my hand, and it came out a sad puckered article the size of a wineskin, and I held it over the household fire by the neck and felt the warm push up into it and the whole sorry thing just sat in my hand and got hot. It didn't even try. Linen's heavy. The cloth weighs more than the warm air inside a thing that small can ever lift, and I knew that, I think, in some back part of my head, I'd just hoped the knowing was wrong.

Stichus laughed at me. Fair.

But it had pushed. That was the fact of it. Held over the fire it had filled, gone taut, wanted to go somewhere and only failed because it was too small and too heavy, and I lay awake that night oddly steadied, because the failing had told me the problem. Bigger. Lighter. The bag had to be huge and the skin of it had to weigh almost nothing, and those two wants pulled against each other, and somewhere in the pull was the whole puzzle I'd spend the next years losing to.

---

I needed something lighter than linen and there isn't anything, not here, not that I could get. No silk a beggar can touch, no oiled paper, no nothing. So I did the only clever thing I managed that whole first month, and I'm still a little proud of it, which tells you how thin the month was for pride.

I made it out of the thinnest stuff in the building, which was the worn-through cloth nobody would miss, the parts of old tunics so threadbare you could see the fire through them. Stichus and I picked them apart for a week. I built the second model the size of a feed sack, light panels of see-through cloth, and I shaped it like the campfire smoke wanted to go, narrow at the bottom where the heat came in and belling out above. No equations. I had the vague idea that a shape like a teardrop standing on its small end was right because that was the shape smoke made when it rose calm, and that was the entire engineering, a memory of smoke.

I needed sustained heat under it and an open mouth to let the heat in, and that was the part that nearly ended the project on a charge of arson. You can't lift a hot-air bag with a brazier you carry over. The air cools the second it's in the bag and the thing wants to come down as fast as it went up, so you have to keep feeding warm into the mouth while it's tethered low, and the only way I had to feed warm was open flame, and open flame under a bag of see-through rag is precisely as stupid as it sounds.

The first time the thin model went up it was the best moment I'd had in the world in over a year.

I did it in the little yard behind the workshop, early, before the household was properly awake, with Stichus holding a string tied to the bag's mouth and me feeding a small fire under it through a cone of green wood I'd rigged to funnel the heat up. It took forever. I burned my knuckles twice getting the fire to behave, and the bag just hung there filling, going round, going taut, and then the string came up tight in the kid's fist and the whole light see-through thing stood up off the ground and pulled, lifted, hung in the morning air at the end of the line with the fire-shimmer rising into it, a made thing, my made thing, floating.

Stichus screamed. Good scream, the gods-do-a-thing scream. I started laughing like an idiot.

It flew for as long as I fed it and not one breath longer. The second the warm stopped coming the thing sagged and sank and settled back onto the dirt like it had never had a thought of leaving. The going up is free. The staying up is the whole fight. And the fight was the heat, the endless feeding of heat, the fire you have to keep alive under a thing made of cloth, and there is no version of that which is safe, and the model had just shown me both halves of it at once, the lift and the leak, married together, the warm pouring in the bottom and pouring straight out through every gap in the thin cloth as fast as I could make it.

Heras came around the corner of the workshop while it was still up. He'd been sent for the household, some morning complaint, and he stood there in the gate with his satchel and watched the rag bag hang at the end of the string over a small dangerous fire in a yard that wasn't mine, and I watched his face do the thing his face did, which was nothing, deliberately nothing, the held-flat nothing of a man not letting you see he's interested.

He said it was very pretty. He said it with the same careful voice you'd use to tell a child his mud horse was very lifelike. And then he asked me, in the dry register, whether I planned to burn down the household I was a guest in, and I looked at the green-wood cone scorched black on its inner face and the cloth gone brown at the bag's mouth where the heat licked it, and I let the fire die, because he had a point and the point was on fire.

---

The household had a point too, and they made it less kindly. You cannot run an open flame in a city of wooden lofts without people noticing, and Rome's towns burn, everyone's terrified of fire, and rightly, the whole place goes up like kindling on a dry month. The woman who ran the kitchen, who I'd never gotten a name out of and who ran more of the place than the master did, came out the second morning when she smelled the smoke wrong and saw the scorch and the rag and the foreigner crouched over a fire too close to the wall. She didn't need Latin. I lost the yard. I lost the morning fire. I was told, in words and a thrown wooden spoon, that if I wanted to play with flames I could do it well away from anything that mattered, which meant the open lot past the midden where they slaughtered, and it meant I'd lost the easy fire and now had to beg coals and carry them.

It cost me, all of it, in the small steady way that everything cost me there. I'd burned through a month of Stichus's goodwill and a fistful of rag and the kitchen woman's tolerance, and what I had to show was a feed-sack of worn-out cloth that flew for as long as I fed a fire I wasn't allowed to feed anymore. I had a blister gone to a blood-blister across two knuckles where I'd grabbed the hot cone. Small wound. I cleaned it with boiled water gone cool, washed it, watched it like a hawk for the red line, because I'd seen what a cut did here and I wasn't going to die of a balloon before the balloon could do anything.

But the thin model proved the lift, and now I had to make it big, and big meant linen.

---

There's no getting around the linen. Worn-out rag flies because it weighs nothing, and it weighs nothing because it's half holes, and half holes leaks the heat out as fast as you put it in, so a rag balloon can only ever be a toy on a string. A real one, a big one, one that might someday carry more than its own weight, has to hold the heat, and holding the heat means a tight weave, and a tight weave means linen, real linen, the good cloth, which weighs like sin and leaks at every seam and burns.

And linen, the good cloth, I could not just have. This was the wall again, the one the whole project ran into, the one a free man with a coin never even sees. I needed an arm's length of decent linen and the household was not going to hand a length of decent cloth to the foreigner to set on fire. So I bargained, and I bargained from a position of owning nothing, which is the worst seat at any table.

I went to Heras. He was the only man who'd ever paid full price for my mind and the only one with a denarius to his name who'd hear me out, and I made the pitch over a sick man's leg one afternoon, in pieces, in my bad Latin, with my hands. I told him I needed a length of close-woven linen and grease to seal it and rope and a brazier I could leave standing, and I told him why, the whole why, the flying, the man-cannot-call-the-sky-a-lie of it, and I watched him decide I was a fool and decide, separately, that he wanted to see it anyway.

He didn't fund me out of belief. He bought me a single length of linen and a pot of mutton grease out of curiosity, and because, I think, he'd watched the rag bag hang in the air over that yard and some part of the man who'd spent forty-five years explaining the world wanted, against his judgment, to see whether the Thulean could make fire fly. He gave me the cloth and he gave me a lecture with it. Linen burns, he said. Grease burns worse. You will weigh it down sealing it and then you will set the seal alight and then you will have a heavy bag that is also on fire, and I want, he said, to be standing well back when you do.

He was right about all of it, which I'd demonstrate thoroughly, over the next hour, in the slaughter-lot past the midden.

---

I sealed the linen with mutton grease rubbed in warm, because I half-remembered that you have to close the weave or the heat pours through, and grease was the only sealant I had. The cloth went from cloth to a heavy, stinking, greasy hide that the heat couldn't pour through. It solved the leak and made everything else worse. The sealed panels weighed nearly double. The bag I sewed out of them, big as a man could reach around, sat on the dirt of the lot like a dead animal, and when I got the brazier going under it through the green-wood cone and fed it steady and fed it and fed it, it filled, slowly, sullenly, and it did not lift. It got hot. It strained. The whole greasy mass swelled and trembled and wanted to and could not, because I'd added so much weight in the sealing that the warm air inside, even good and hot, could not carry the skin that held it.

So I made the fire bigger. Of course I made the fire bigger. That's what you do, you're cold so you build the fire up, you want lift so you make more heat, and a man with no equations and a thing that almost flew is going to feed the fire until the thing flies or stops him. The flame came up off the brazier higher than I meant, into the cone, up into the mouth of the bag, and the greased linen at the lip caught.

It didn't explode. I'd half feared a fireball and got something quieter and worse. The grease at the mouth lit with a low blue-edged crawl and the flame walked up the inside of the bag along the seam, a finger of fire climbing a wall, and the linen browned and split where it burned and the heat I'd worked an hour to trap came howling out the new hole and the whole thing collapsed onto the brazier. That was the end of an afternoon and a length of Heras's linen and a pot of grease and most of my faith in myself, all of it gone up in a stink of burning fat over a slaughter-lot, with me beating at it with a board and breathing smoke that smelled like the worst day at a kitchen.

I put it out. I burned my forearm doing it, a real burn this time, a line of shiny red up the inside of my arm where I'd reached past the flame, and it blistered and it hurt for a week. I cleaned it the way I'd taught Heras to clean things, boiled water gone cool, watched it and watched it, because I'd seen what a wound did here and I wasn't going to lose an arm. I sat in the dirt next to the smoking ruin and I was eighteen and a long way from anywhere and I'd just set fire to something I'd begged for, and I wanted, badly, to be done.

Heras had, as promised, been standing well back. He came and looked at the wreck with the expression of a man whose prediction has been confirmed in every particular and who is too decent to enjoy it more than a little. He looked at my arm and made me show it to him and grunted at the cleaning, which from Heras is praise. And he said, in the dry voice, that the air had carried the fire very well.

Which it had. The fire had flown beautifully. That was the joke and the wound of it at once.

---

I understood, sitting in that lot, the size of what I'd walked into. The cloth had to be light or it wouldn't lift, and it had to be sealed and heavy or it wouldn't hold the heat, and those two could not both be true at my scale or with my materials and I did not know the trick that made them both true, if there was one. The seams leaked. The seal burned. The heat had to be sustained and the only heat I had was open flame, and open flame under cloth is a fire waiting for a reason. Bigger only made all of it worse, more cloth, more weight, more seam, more flame. A toy on a string I'd already managed. The real balloon was three engineering problems stacked on top of each other, and I had no money, no workshop, no cloth, no metal, no hands but a ten-year-old's and a doctor's bemused curiosity, and I was not free to go get any of it.

I needed money. That was the bottom of it. I needed a free man's ability to walk into a market and buy good cloth by the bolt and a craftsman's day by the dozen, and the awful circular joke of it was that the balloon was supposed to be the obstacle that earned me the money, and the balloon needed money to build, and I sat in the slaughter-lot with my arm on fire and saw the loop close around me clean as a noose.

I'd had it up. The little one, the rag one, it had flown, and the failing of the big one didn't unsay that. Hot air rises, I'd watched it carry a made thing off the ground at the end of a string, and the rest, the cloth, the seal, the heat, the money, the hands, all of it was an obstacle with edges, and an obstacle with edges is a wall you can chip at, year on year, the way you chip at a language until one morning you can hold a conversation. I'd done that once already. I'd walked into this place with no words and clawed my way to bad Latin a humiliation at a time, and the balloon was going to be the same, a long grind of small failures, scorched cloth and begged coals and a doctor lending me a length of linen because he wanted to see the trick.

---

I built one more before the spring was out, because I couldn't not. Smaller than the burned one and bigger than the toy, a modest thing of linen I'd lightened by giving up half the seal, hoping I could split the difference between leaking and burning and find one breath of cloth that did neither. I made it in the lot in the long evening light with Stichus holding the line and Heras leaning on the midden wall pretending he had somewhere better to be. I fed the brazier careful this time, low, no higher than the cone, coaxing the warm in steady.

It filled. It went round and stood up and the linen drew taut and held, and the warm went in and stayed in long enough, this once, this one time, and the line came up tight in the boy's small fist and the bag lifted, the whole heavy honest linen weight of it, up off the trodden dirt of the slaughter-lot and into the gold evening air, a hand's height, then a forearm, then higher than the boy's head, hanging there on the warm with the fire-shimmer pouring up into it and my heart going like a fist on a door.

It held the air for the length of maybe ten breaths. I counted them. Six, seven, eight, the linen straining at the seam I'd sewn by lamplight with a borrowed needle, nine, and on the tenth the seam let go, a long soft tearing I heard before I understood it, the warm air punching out through the opening seam in a gust I could feel on my face, and the bag folded along the rip and came down, not fast, not a crash, just a tired settling, sinking back through the gold light it had climbed, down past the boy's wide face and onto the dirt, where it lay breathing out the last of its heat into the evening, torn open along one seam, still warm, still smelling of mutton fat and smoke, done.

I stood over it in the slaughter-lot with my burned arm and my fistful of string and I watched the warm air leave it through the tear, and I knelt down and put my hand against the cloth to feel the heat go.
