# Chapter 52: A Generation Later

The gears came in a cedar box with a sliding lid, and the lid stuck, and Procula stood over it in the cold of the press-room with her thumbnail under the lip and worked it loose a little at each end until the wood gave and the smell came up out of it. Old grease gone to wax. Iron that had been wept on by the river air for longer than she had been alive and had taken a brown skin she could rub away with her spit and a rag to find the gray underneath, still good, still cut clean. She lifted out the straw, gone the color of a wasp's nest, and laid the gears on the bench in the order they came, which was no order, a jumble a dead man's hands had made and forty years had pickled.

She was twenty, that spring, or near it. Her mother had been bought into the storeroom as a girl and freed in the great widow's will and had married a freedman of the press. Procula had been born free into the smell of lamp-black and had never spent a day out of earshot of the screw coming down. She kept the day-book for the casting end of the fund now, as her mother had kept the storeroom book and the woman before her mother. A line of women, each teaching two others to read the marks, then teaching them the other thing: the homeland script that ran the wrong way along the page and made a sound in your head like no Latin ever made. She had been six when she learned the marks, eleven when old Vibia set the cipher in front of her and watched her find the first word, and made her swear the oath every keeper swore — to set it down whole, and to teach not less than two.

So she could read what was scratched on the box. Most could not. The lid had a line cut into the underside in the foreign letters, faint, a careful hand gone shaky at the end of it, and she had read it the first day the box was given over to the casting shed and had read it a hundred times since, standing where she stood now with the gears spread out cold in front of her.

BEGIN WHERE I FAILED.

---

The fund paid her father's cousin Saturius to fail. That was the joke the wharf made of it, said as a sneer, and the deed that bound them all meant it as the highest praise. A man came under the fund on a living wage and a hand or two and coal and metal for a term of years. What they kept of him for the judging was the record of his trying, every attempt set in the book with the date and how it broke, so the next man need not break it the same way twice. Saturius had spent eleven years on the self-feeding pot, the engine of fire and water the founder's own contest had set the children dreaming of before any of them were born. He had got it to spin a little wheel and scald his arm and never once to pull a cart, and he would die at it, cheerful, because the steel that could hold the fire's full strength was not there yet and would not be in his lifetime, and a man could lay a rung he would never stand on. Felix had said the same before him. The book had Felix's hand near the front, in the marks and the cipher both, the failures of a man dead these thirty years. Saturius's followed, then Procula's own.

She had asked for the clock.

---

Two years of trying, and most of it was wrong. The founder had left the clock honest and incomplete and marked where it lied. She read his pages on it the winter the box came down. He wrote that a hanging weight will fall and turn a wheel and that this is easy, and that the trouble is all in the not-falling, in the catching, in some little part that must let the wheel go a step and then hold it and then let it go a step, even and even and even, no faster when the weight is full than when it is nearly down. He drew teeth that caught on a swinging bar and a swinging bar that caught on teeth, and at the foot of nearly every drawing he had put the hooked stroke, the mark the keepers all knew, the founder is guessing here, do not trust this, climb past it. On one page he had written under the drawing, in the failing hand: it wants to be even and it will not be even, the falling is greedy, find the thing that makes it patient. And then the hooked stroke after, twice, because he did not trust that he had even asked the question rightly.

She built his drawings first. All of them. That was the rung he had laid and she meant to stand on every plank of it before she cut her own. She had Saturius's apprentice cut the teeth and filed the swinging bar herself and hung it on a pivot of hardened pin in two cups of bronze, and set the little weight to fall, and the wheel ran away under the bar exactly as the founder had said it would, greedy, the full drop spending itself in a breath, the bar knocked aside, the teeth a blur, everything stopped against the frame with a buzz and a smell of hot metal. With the bar heavier the wheel ran away slower and still ran away. With it lighter the wheel would not turn the bar at all and sat dead. She wrote each one in the book with the date and how it failed, because that was the oath under the other oath, and some nights she read the founder's hooked strokes and her own beside them and could not have said whose were whose.

---

What she understood, she understood with a piece of cloth in her hand.

She had been turning the swinging bar over. It caught the wheel and was kicked off it and swung free and came back and caught again, and the catching was the whole problem, where the greed got in, the wheel shoving the bar away too hard so the next catch came too soon. She sat with it a long time. The press was going on the far side of the wall, the long even shudder of the screw, fifty to a page, a press that did not hurry no matter how much paper was stacked to feed it, and she had heard it all her life and did not hear it now. She was thinking about a swing. A child's swing, the kind they hung in the colonnade for the little ones, a board on two ropes, and how a child on it goes out and comes back in the same time whether you push her hard or soft, the far swings only wider, never quicker, the rope deciding the time.

The rope decided the time. The length of the rope. Push harder and the arc grows but the return is the same. The length of the rope sets the period. The period belongs to the rope.

She put the bar down and picked it up and felt how it wanted to swing of itself, slow, in its own good time, if only the wheel would stop bullying it. She had the founder's drawings backwards, or he had drawn them backwards, and it did not matter which, only that the wheel must not drive the swing. The swing must rule the wheel. The wheel must come asking leave to move and be let go a step only when the bar in its own slow swing came round to open the gate, and take from the wheel only the least push, enough to keep its swinging up against the drag of the air and no more, the weight's full strength held back behind a gate that opened a crack at a time on the swing's own count.

She had no word for it. There was no word for it, in Latin or in the cipher, because the arrangement had never stood up in the world for anyone to name. She made the pivot truer. She made the bar longer and hung two small weights on it she could slide along its length, in to swing quicker and out to swing slow, and cut the gate-teeth fresh and shallow so the wheel could push the bar only the littlest amount and could not shove past it. She filed and hung and let the weight fall and watched the wheel buzz away to nothing, and slid the bobs, and filed the teeth shallower, and hung it again, eleven times, twelve, the lamp guttering, her hands black to the wrist and one knuckle bleeding where the file had jumped.

On the thirteenth hanging the bar swung out and the wheel let down one tooth and the bar swung back and the wheel held, and the bar swung out and the wheel let down one tooth, and held, and the sound it made was one she would have to teach the wharf a word for. A small dry knock, and a wait, and a small dry knock, the same knock and the same wait, the weight falling all the while behind the gate and being made to fall patiently, a step and a wait and a step, even and even and even while she stood and did not breathe and counted it against the press through the wall, which went down and up four times to the bar's seven, and stayed at four to seven.

She let it run. She did not touch it. She watched the weight go down the full length of its drop, slow, slow, the wheel turning under the patient bar, and when the weight came to the floor and the knocking stopped she found she had been standing so long her feet had gone, and that her face was wet, and that she had not made a sound the whole time for fear of frightening it.

---

A clock wants more than the patient step. It wants the step counted and shown, a wheel of hours, a face, a hand, a way to wind the weight back up without everything running away while you wind, a year of fitting after the first knock. The hard rung was the step, and the step was climbed.

She fitted it through the spring. She geared a slow wheel off the fast one to drag a single pointer round a wooden dial marked in the divisions of the day, the day split in two and the halves in twelves as the sundials split them, uneven by the season as Roman hours had always been uneven, because she had no other hours to give it and the city kept none. She set one weight to last a half a day on its drop and a second to take over while the first was wound, and stood everything in a cedar case taller than herself, against the press-room wall, leveled with wedges and a bowl of water until the bar swung true.

That same spring, the plague had been four years gone from Rome. It had been hard. She had lost her father's cousin Saturius to it, and the old pressman, and the woman who had run the east storeroom for thirty years. Two-thirds of the press-room's crew had sickened, and half of those who sickened had survived, which was more than the generation before had seen in other outbreaks, the old women said, more than the city had a right to expect. The physicians still followed the procedures that had been written down in the books before any of them were born. When the city recovered this time it recovered faster, the streets filling back up within two seasons rather than five, the markets loud again, the children running again. She had not known Daniel. She had only grown up in the smell of lamp-black and the sound of the press, and had not died of the plague, and was now standing in the press-room in the good spring light, fitting a pendulum clock.

And then she did what the deed did not require and no one asked of her. She took down the fund's book, the one with Felix's hand near the front and the founder's pages on the clock copied into it in the cipher, and turned to the place where he had written BEGIN WHERE I FAILED and under it the hooked stroke twice, and on the next leaf, which he had left white with only a note that the page was for the one who came after, she set down in the homeland script, small, as old Vibia had taught her, what the swinging bar had taught her with the cloth in her hand. That the wheel must not drive the swing. That the swing must rule the wheel and take only the least push to keep itself up. How to slide the bobs, in to quicken and out to slow. Under it her own hand, and the date, and that the step held against the press at four to seven and did not drift across a full day's drop. She did not put the hooked stroke after any of it. She read it three times to be sure it was true before she let it stand without the mark, because the mark was a debt to the ones not born.

---

They held the contest on the Quinquatrus, as it had been held every spring since before the founder died, in Minerva's name, the priests paid out of the old endowment to bless what they had once been ready to burn. Procula went up to hear the year's tales read out, the boy with the chariot that ran on a tamed bolt of the sky's own lightning, the girl with the boat that needed no rowers and no wind, the same dreams the printed sheets had been spreading into the children's games for three generations now, a long way ahead of any metal. She had brought no tale. She had brought the case on a cart, two men sweating under it, set it at the edge of the festival ground and let it run, and the wharf stood around it as the Subura crowd had once stood around a bag of greased cloth that rose and burned.

It knocked. A step and a wait and a step, dry and even, the pointer crawling down its first division of the morning so slow you could not catch it moving and could not, after a while, deny that it had moved.

An old man pushed forward and stood at the case with his arms crossed. "What's it for," he said.

She told him it told the hours.

"The sundial tells the hours," he said. "Costs nothing. Doesn't knock."

"The sundial can't tell them in the dark," she said. "Or under cloud. Or at the bottom of the sea."

He laughed at the sea and went away.

A boy named Gaius stood at it the whole afternoon with his lips parted. When the first weight ran low and she reached in to shift the drive to the second, he pressed close and said, "Can I watch how?"

She showed him the drive-pin and how to ease the second weight onto the wheel without letting the bar lose its swing. She showed him the bobs on the bar and let him slide the nearer one out a notch and count the new beat against his own pulse, slower by three in ten, and then slide it back until the press through the wall came round to four again. He did it twice to be sure he understood. That was the oath, not less than two: he would know what he had learned when he could do it again without her hands at his.

She did not know the man who had laid the bottom of it. She knew the name they all knew, the Thulean, the founder, the man who made fire fly and came down out of the ice with the marks and the press and the books, a name on a deed and a hand in a book and a line cut shaky under a cedar lid. She had read his honest failures and stood on them and climbed the one rung he had marked the most carefully because he could not climb it himself, and she had never once thought to wonder what country a man comes from who has a word in his script for a kind of frozen rain that falls on no street in Rome. The founder was from the cold north. The cold north had a word for it. There was no further to wonder, and the truth of him went on lying quiet under the press-room stone and under the dirt of a grave she had never seen.

---

That night she could not sleep for the knocking, which she could still hear though the case stood three walls off, or thought she could, the sound her body had learned. She got up in the dark and went to it and stood with her hand flat on the warm cedar, listening to the patient step tell the dark its even count, and thought, without words, about the next rung.

For the clock drifted, only slow, slower than any water-clock the founder ever geared, and slow was not still. It gained on the cold mornings and lost in the wet, its pulse running with the weather, and a clock that gained and lost was no use to the purpose the founder had written about in the cipher among his failures: the great hulls going west on the blank water, the wanting to know how far west you had gone by the hour the sun stood high over you set against the hour it stood high over the home you left behind. There were ships that had made that crossing now, the first hulls returned before she was born, and others since, and the captains dead-reckoned their westing by compass and count and came back close enough and sometimes close enough was enough. But a man could hold his longitude truly only with a clock that did not gain or lose for a month together while the deck heaved and the salt got in. There was a prize for it still, the founder's prize, never won, the coin of it grown fat with the waiting. He had marked the page everywhere with the hooked stroke and written that he did not know how, only that whoever learned it would learn it from a rung to write down small and true about a thing that swung even in a moving ship, which he had never seen and could not draw, only name and leave white.

She took her hand off the warm wood. The step went on without her, even and even, a rung made solid that a man had only been able to point at in the dark. Somewhere above it, in the white part of the page, the next one waited to be cut. She would not reach it, most likely. The swinging thing that does not care that the world heaves, and the spring steel that must hold it — these belonged to the one after her, or the one after that. But she had two girls to teach now, and a solution to write down small and true under the founder's, for whichever of them was quick enough to stand on it and reach the one above.

She went back through the dark to her bed by the feel of the wall, and behind her in its cedar case the patient step kept the count of the climbing night, and far over her head, where the dial did not reach and her hands could not yet go, the next rung waited, white, and the one above that.
