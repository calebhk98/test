# Chapter 37: Apollodorus Falls

The temple came to the wharf before the news did, in the mouth of a man delivering rope. He was a carter I had used for years, a freedman with a head for stone because he hauled it, and he set down his tally and stayed to talk because the city was talking and a man likes to be the one who tells it. Caesar had drawn a temple, he said. The biggest in Rome, two goddesses back to back under one roof, Venus and the City herself, sitting, each in her own half. Drawn it with his own hand, they said, no architect's between. The carter thought it grand. I thought, while I checked his count, that Hadrian had been at that very drawing the afternoon I first sat in the light he chose for me, the standing men around the cork model, the reed going at the margin. He had been telling me about a roof while he meant me to admire it. He had built it now, or begun to, and the city was admiring. I marked the rope received and said it sounded grand, and meant nothing by it, and the carter went off pleased to have been believed.

---

What reached me a few weeks on was not grand. It came through Heras, who was past sixty-eight now and slower on the stairs and still the man Rome's Greeks told things to, because a physician is in every house and forgets nothing. He came to the yard out of his way, which he did not do for small matters, and we sat in the tally-room where the water-clock dripped its unreliable hours, and he turned a cup of my watered wine in his hands a long time before he used it.

"Your friend the bridge-builder," he said, "has been telling the city about the emperor's temple." He gave me the sideways look he used when he was watching to see if I already knew.

"What has he said."

"That the gods are sitting too low. That if Venus and Roma ever wished to rise from their thrones and walk out of their own temple, they would crack their heads on the ceiling, because Caesar set the floor of the building above the natural rise of the ground and gave the seated figures no height to stand into." Heras drank, finally. "He said it as a man names a fault in a wall. To engineers, at a yard. But he said it, and a thing said to engineers does not stay at the yard, and Caesar is the architect."

I sat with the clock dripping between us. I knew the observation at once for what it was, true and unanswerable and exactly the kind of true thing he had built sixty years on saying out loud, the seated goddesses too big for the box their maker had drawn, no recourse in stone, the fault plain to anyone who would put a man's height against a span. It was the gourds again, said better, said about a finished thing the whole city could walk into and see. "When," I said.

"A while ago. It is only now that the man who carried it to me thought it worth carrying." Heras set the cup down. "I do not bring it to make you do anything. There is nothing to do. I bring it because you will hear it from a worse mouth and I would rather you heard it from mine."

"There may be something to do."

"There is not," Heras said, with the flat finality he had used on me about miasma thirty years before, "and you know there is not, and you are going to go and try anyway, because you cannot help it. I have watched you do it with water and with wounds. Go, then. But go knowing he will not take it from you, and come back, because I am too old to teach another barbarian his manners."

---

I went the next morning, on foot, with no escort and no tablet, dressed plainly, which was itself a calculation, because a freedman in good wool calling on a fallen master draws an eye and I wanted no eye on me at his door. He was not yet fallen. That was what I had failed to account for, walking there. He was at a yard near the river where they were squaring blocks for some other work of the reign, and he had a place in it still, a stool under an awning and men bringing him their plumb-lines to settle disputes, white-headed, the bad knuckles worse, the same weather-left-out frame holding the same certainty. He was past seventy and looked like a man they would have to take apart to move. When he saw me he did not get up.

"The flying man," he said. "Come to watch real work. Sit, you make the boy nervous standing."

I sat on an upturned half-block where the dust was. We talked stone a while, because there was no reaching him any other way, and because I wanted it. He told me a wall I would not have known to look at had a flaw in its footing that would show in twenty years and that no one would thank him for naming, because the man who pays for a wall wants to be told it will stand, not when it will not. He asked, as he always asked, when I had last failed at something with my own hands, and I told him I had promised an emperor a clock for the open sea and could not build one for a dry room, and he made the short hard sound that was his laugh. A boy from the yard came with a plumb-line that had tangled its string; Apollodorus took it without looking up from me, worked the knot free with the bad hand alone in perhaps four breaths, and sent the boy back. Then he set the tool across his knee. "The sea will teach you the depth of your well faster than you can lower the bucket," he said.

Then I said it, because there was no clean way in and the dust was getting in my throat.

"They are saying in the city what you said about the temple. The goddesses cracking their heads."

He was not ashamed. I had hoped, walking there, for some flicker of a man who knew he had gone too far, and there was none. There was a small flat pleasure, the pleasure of a man whose true statement has traveled.

"Because it is true," he said. "He set them too big for the room. A child with a stick and a wall could show you." He paused here to watch a column of stone pass on a sled, tracking it as he had always tracked moving weight, the appraisal so practiced it seemed involuntary. "You drop a height of stone where a goddess is to stand and you measure the standing, you do not draw the goddess to fill the pretty oval and then build the roof down on her. He drew the picture first and asked the stone to agree. The stone will not agree. The stone never agrees. That is the only honest fact in the trade." He looked at me sidelong. "You came to tell me that."

"I came to tell you to stop saying it."

The awning-shadow moved on his face. He understood me; he had always understood me faster than I wished.

"Ah," he said. "The barbarian has come to teach me how to live."

"I have come to tell you who the man is." I kept my voice down, which with him was a confession in itself, because he despised a lowered voice. "The man you told to go and paint his gourds, at the Forum, the year they dedicated it. The bearded Spaniard with the roll under his arm. You remember telling him." "I remember. I would say it again. He drew melons and called them domes." "He is the emperor."

I had thought the word would do something. I had carried it across the city imagining the moment it landed, the certainty draining from his face. It did not drain. He looked at me with the patient attention of a master who has watched a slow apprentice arrive, breathless, at something already settled an hour ago.

"I know what he is," Apollodorus said. "I knew what he was when he had the roll under his arm. A man who designs is a man who designs, and his rank is the least true fact about him. The melons are still melons. He is emperor of melons now, that is all." He spread the bad hand on his knee, the thickened knuckles, the old break that had set wrong because he had gone on working through it. "You think I do not know I said it loud."

"Then you know what you are handing him."

"I said it loud on purpose." He watched the bob of his plumb-line sway and steady. "At the Forum, so the men around him would hear an old man tell the truth and remember that it could still be told. That is worth more than the temple." He lifted his eyes. "And you. You bend. I have watched you do it for twenty years. You have a true statement and you fold it small enough to fit through a great man's door and you call that surviving." The bob hung between us. "It is surviving. You will survive me, and you will survive him, and you will fold your true things smaller and smaller until there is nothing left to fold, and you will die a rich man no one ever heard say a single thing straight."

"You are for staying alive," I said. "He keeps a ledger. He kept the small mark thirty years and now he can settle it whenever he likes, and you are handing him the morning."

"A man right for sixty years does not start lying to power at seventy because the power has learned his face. I would have to become you to take your advice. I would rather take my chances with the stone."

There was nothing under that I could get a finger into. I had come to spit, gently, into a well, and found the well had a man already standing at the bottom of it looking up, perfectly at peace, with no intention of climbing out. We talked a little more, stone again, because we were two men of one trade and there was nowhere else to put what sat between us, and when I rose to go he gave me the plumb-line. "Take it," he said. "You build things that float. You will never once in your life have a use for a tool that finds the truth straight down. Keep it to remember which of us was the better engineer." The hard sound again. "Go fold something."

I took it.

---

I tried the other road too. I had a province half-broken in the new figures by then, the second one, and the work put me in rooms near men who were near Hadrian, fiscus freedmen and a procurator's secretary and, once, a man who carried drawings up to the Palatine and brought corrections down. This last was a Greek named Theophanes, a careful man who valued my marks because they made his accounts of stone and labor uncheatable, and who owed me nothing and risked nothing, which is the only kind of door a man like me is given. I went at it sidelong, dull, a comment dropped between two real matters so it would not stand up and be seen. We were over a tally of cartage for the temple footings, and I asked, as a man asks who is only making conversation while the ink dries, whether the master of the old bridges was consulted on the great work, since no one living knew foundations as he did, and it would be a shame to lose the use of such a man over a quarrel about a roof.

Theophanes did not look up from his figures.

"There is no quarrel," he said. "The emperor designs. The work proceeds. Old men say things at yards." He made a mark, and then, because he was not unkind and had understood me exactly, he added, "I would not carry that sentence up the hill, friend. Neither the part about the bridges nor the part about the roof. A man who reminds Caesar that another man knows foundations better than he does has done the other man no favor, and himself less. The emperor does not need to be told whom he is angry at. He keeps that himself."

He turned to the next column. I sat with the cartage in front of me, the rows of figures, the weight of stone going up the hill toward two goddesses who would never stand. The column of sums was in the new marks, the full column, with no old Roman reckoning set alongside it the way I had seen at every fiscal tally two years before. Theophanes had not even laid out the parallel. There was a time when that would have been notable; now it was only how the numbers went, and neither of us remarked on it.

That was all of it. That was my word to Hadrian for Apollodorus, said to a clerk three rooms from him, hedged so far down it was barely a word at all, and turned aside before it could become one, by a man doing me the kindness of pretending I had not said it. Pushing further would have put my own name in a sentence beside Apollodorus's, in front of a man who carried sentences up the hill, in the season when the one thing keeping me alive was being too dull to carry. I weighed the master of the bridges against my own thin walls, in the half of a breath it takes to weigh such a thing, and I let the sentence die in Theophanes's careful Greek, and I went back to the cartage, and left the yard when the count was finished and the ink was dry.

---

He was exiled first. That was how it reached me, in pieces, over a season, the shifting weight of a sealed crate coming apart on a long haul. Banished from the city, his works left to other hands, the bridges and the Forum and the column going on without him as though they had built themselves, because a completed structure forgets the man who held the plumb-line. Then, a while after, the other word, carried in the same flat manner, a man dead in exile, an old man, the cause not named because the cause is never named, only that he had said something he should not have, about the temple, about a roof, and had not stopped saying it when the city grew cold around him. The men who carried the news put it as: a man who would not learn his place.

I do not know the morning it was decided. I never saw the room. I have built nothing in my life that I trust more than I would have trusted his account of why a wall stands, and I do not have his account of his own falling, because there is no relief that carves the man the chisel was told to leave out, and the man who could have told me how it truly went was the man it went to.

---

Marcia knew the day the second word came, before I said it. She read a short load through wood. "He would not bend," she said. It was not a question. She had heard me come back from the yard months before with the plumb-line in my hand and had asked nothing, and she asked nothing now, only stated the figure at the bottom of the column.

"He would not bend."

"And you bent." Also not a question. Marcia did not soften things; she found their level and set them down. "You went and you said the careful word to the careful man and it died where you wanted it safe to die, and you came home, and we still have the wharf." She made a mark in her tally, the day's loads, the bales and the barrels. "I am not reproaching you. I would have told you to do exactly that, and I am glad you did it without my telling you, because it means I do not have to be the one in this house who counts what a friend is worth against the walls." She set the stylus down. "Eat. You have not eaten since the carter."

I ate. The clock dripped its bad hours. Lucanus, who was ten now and knew the new figures faster than the clerks and did not know yet what kind of world he had been born a citizen of, came in and asked who had died, because the house had the smell of it, and Marcia told him a builder, an old one, the man who built the bridges in the war his father served, and Lucanus asked whether the bridges were still standing. I told him yes. He went quiet a moment. "Then he did all right," he said, and went to find his bread. Marcia glanced at me once and then went back to the tally.

---

That winter the survey went on. I had its reports through the fiscus, the African coast measured a headland further south than the grain fleets ran, the figures coming back in the new marks to the single scale, a yard breaking ground on the western shore where the pilots would learn hulls that could not yet be built for a sea no one would let them cross. The plan I had pointed an emperor at was crawling forward exactly as I had built it to crawl, slow and bounded and gated on a clock I could not make, and I could not take any pleasure in it, because the man who would have understood precisely what it cost to aim it and stand back was the man I had let the careful sentence die rather than be killed for.

That same winter Vibenius sent a letter, the first in years. He was old enough now to know a debt when he saw one, and old enough to settle it, and what he wanted in return for the word he had spoken on Minerva's day was a small thing: a clerk's post for his steward's son at one of my yards, work the boy could learn by doing, a proper wage. I gave it, with a note that the steward's son would be taught the new figures if he wanted them. I had expected the calling of the debt to cost more. It cost only that.

One night I sat in the press-room and took the plumb-line out and hung it from a nail in the roof-beam, away from the door, where no one would ask what it was doing there. It found its level and held it. I looked at it a while. Then I went and found Tyche where she was banking the lamps and asked her to write, in the cipher, at the bottom of the page where I had been setting down the dead ends of the clock for whoever came after me, what Apollodorus had told me about drawing the picture first and asking the stone to agree. She asked whose words they were. I told her a better engineer's. She wrote them in the marks that only the two of us could read, and blew the lamp, and the plumb-line hung in the dark.
