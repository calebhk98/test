# Chapter 20: Tyche

In the winter before I went to Macer's, a freedman in imperial livery arrived at my door before dawn. I walked up to the Palatine in the gray with the river mist on my boots and lamp-smoke still in my hair from the night's work.

Trajan was in his study, a room that smelled of wax and horse-leather and whatever he'd eaten for breakfast, which was apparently eggs. He stood at a table with a map of the eastern provinces laid flat and two officers sitting against the wall. He waved them out when I came in.

He asked me two questions without preamble, no warmup, the way he always worked. "Can it carry fire?"

I had half a dozen careful things I could have said. I said the one that was true. "Yes. A wicker basket with a clay fire-pot and dry straw. The risk is the envelope - the linen grease burns. But a balloon can carry burning material low over a target before the pilot loses the bag. You'd lose the envelope and probably the man, but you'd deliver the fire."

He looked at the map. He didn't write anything down. One of his habits: he carried things in his head and put them down later, or didn't. "Can an enemy shoot it down?"

"From the ground, at operating altitude, with bows or slings? Hard. The target is slow and high and the wind moves it unpredictably. A skilled archer might puncture the envelope but it wouldn't fall fast - it'd drift down." I stopped, because the answer had another half. "But if an enemy had balloons of their own and sent one up to our height, it's vulnerable. A man at the same altitude has a clear shot at close range. The envelope has no armor. One burning arrow and it comes down in fire."

He looked at me then - the look he gave things that were use-shaped rather than pretty. "So the counter is to acquire the same capability." I told him that was the counter.

He walked around the table slowly and stopped at the northern edge. "I'm assigning a permanent unit. Trained operators. The Praetorian camp - they have the ground and the discipline, and I want it under close watch while it's young. Full establishment. Doctrine written. Officers who understand it, not soldiers who happened to be near a flying bag once." He looked up. "I'll need you to come in and work with whoever commands it, early on." I said I understood.

"Good." He turned back to the map. "I told you once: the water is worth more than the fire. I meant it. But the fire is what it is, and I'd rather it be mine than theirs."

I walked back down in the gray morning and thought about the man at altitude with the burning arrow. I had told him the truth on both questions. He'd heard both answers and decided to proceed anyway, which was his right and probably the correct military reasoning, and knowing it did not make me feel better for knowing it. The balloon had been my answer to a different question. It was now part of the answer to this one, and I could not trace where the line between those two things was, or whether there was a line at all. There wasn't much point in standing in the street thinking about it. I went back to the yard.

***

The trouble with buying a person you care about is that you have to do it in front of a man who knows you can't walk away.

I went to Macer's in the spring after the contest, on a clear cold morning with the river smell coming up from the Tiber. I made the mistake of dressing well. I had a toga now, a citizen's toga I still couldn't drape without Pamphilus pinning the back fold, which I'd worn maybe six times and put on because I thought it would make me look like a man Macer had to treat as an equal. He took one look at me in his atrium, the leather smell thick around us and a clerk waiting at the side with a tablet, and laughed from his chest, his shoulders shaking.

"You came to buy a girl in your wedding sheet," he said. "Sit down, Thulean, before you trip on it." I sat down and tried to look like I'd planned to.

I had rehearsed this. I'd told myself I would be light about it - the freedman buying back household furniture, of no more weight than the cloth tally she kept. I'd told myself not to let him see it mattered. I had been telling myself that for two years, since I'd said *Pamphilus first* and watched her take it without a flicker, and I had failed to think hard enough about what it meant that I'd needed to keep telling myself.

"The girl Tyche," I said. "She keeps my tally. I'd like to buy her, the same way I bought Pamphilus. Name a price."

"Pamphilus," Macer said, "is a porter with a strong back and a collar I'd riveted on him because he'd run twice. I sold you a man worth four thousand because his legs were good and his head was empty and I was tired of feeding something that wanted to be elsewhere. You paid over the odds because you wanted him free that week and I knew it. Four thousand for a porter. I told my wife and she didn't believe me." He let that sit. "Tyche keeps your tally."

So he knew. I had spent five years hiding it, hiding her, telling Onesimus she was slow and letting her do the donkey-work of the cloth count because she was reliable and dull, keeping her sums off any tablet that left the yard, teaching her in the back of the tally-room with the door shut and a cover story ready - because of the warning he'd given me the day he handed me the workshop, that he would sell the cleverer slave to a factor if I made his hands worth more than he could keep. I had built her whole invisibility on that one sentence, and I sat there in my toga and understood that he had seen through all of it, possibly from the start, and had been letting me think I was clever.

"How long have you known," I said, and he shrugged the big shoulders. "You hid a woman who does in her head what my counting-house does with an abacus and a freedman and an afternoon. You hid her by telling Onesimus she was slow. Onesimus has been keeping books since before you were born on whatever rock you crawled off, and he came to me three years ago and said your slow girl had caught two errors of his that the abacus missed and that you'd told her to stop." Macer's mouth did something dry. "I let you keep the secret because it was working for me. A clever beast that nobody knows is clever stays cheap and stays put. I've owned a hundred slaves, Thulean. You think I can't smell the one that's worth money."

"Then name it." He named it: "Forty thousand," flat, like a measurement.

I had brought, in a strongbox on a cart with two of Macer's old yard men I'd hired as guards because I trusted them more than strangers, eight thousand sesterces. Forty thousand was most of everything I had. It was the contest fund and the press and the next two years of the yard. A senator's furniture. A small farm. A frontier officer's pay for more years than Celer had served.

"She's a tally-keeper," I said, and heard my own voice climb. "She's a woman of twenty-four who can do sums. Forty thousand buys a Greek tutor and a cook and a litter team and you know it."

"It buys what a man will pay." He was calm, unhurried. "A Greek who can read Homer to your sons is worth a Greek's price, there are shiploads of Greeks. A woman who can audit a grain factor's books faster than the factor and won't run because she's got nowhere better, sold to the right house - that woman is worth what you cannot afford to find out. I've had two offers I didn't go looking for. I told you once: a clever beast doesn't stay secret in a city this size. People come into your yard. People watch a woman reckon."

"You said you'd sell her to a factor." "I said I would if you made her worth selling. You did. I didn't." He leaned back. "I held her for you, you ungrateful barbarian, against two men who'd have given me what I'm asking. I'd rather sell her to you and keep you sweet than sell her to someone else and lose the man who flies. Forty thousand. I'm not gouging you. I'm telling you what you let her become."

There it was. He'd made me look at the number she was, and the number was high because of everything I'd taught her. He wasn't cheating me - the price was true - and I sat in my wedding sheet and did sums in my head about what I could sell.

I wanted to break his face with the gold ring still on it. I wanted to tell him exactly what I thought of a world where a person was a line in a counting-house, and I had the words, I'd had the words for years, the whole speech, and I knew with total clarity that the speech would cost me the deal and her the freedom and would change precisely nothing about the law. So I sat and calculated.

"Thirty," I said, because you don't take the first number, even when you've already lost.

"Thirty-eight," Macer said, "and you bring your water-barrels and your foreign filter into my country house at Tibur, free, and you teach my steward's boys your shopkeeper's numbers, so that when you're dead or grand and gone I'm not the last man in Rome doing accounts the slow way. Thirty-eight and a string. I'd rather have the string than the two thousand."

"Done," I said, before I could think about it, because I'd have given him fifty.

He had the clerk write it: a *bill of sale*, one slave, female, called Tyche, of his household, sold to Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified, for thirty-eight thousand sesterces. The clerk's stylus went through the wax and the figure was written in Roman letters because Macer would not have his own sale recorded in my marks.

He saw my face when the clerk turned the tablet for my mark. "Don't," he said, almost kindly. "You're going to free her the moment you're out my door. You'll feel clean about it, and that's your business. But you bought her, boy. Whatever you do after. Sign your grand name."

I signed my grand name.

***

He couldn't let me leave without the joke. He walked me to the atrium door himself, his hand heavy on my shoulder over the toga, and at the threshold where his porter could hear and the morning's hangers-on could hear he said, loud and good-humored, "Thirty-eight thousand for a woman of twenty-four. Marcus Ulpius Danihel, the emperor's own freedman, who doesn't keep a woman, who's never been seen to keep a woman, and pays a small farm for one with short hair and a quick tongue." He patted my shoulder. "I'd have sold her cheaper if you'd told me what you wanted her for. Buy her a comb."

The hangers-on laughed. The porter kept his face still.

I had known it was coming. I'd known since I started planning this that there is exactly one story Rome tells about a man who buys a young woman and keeps her in his house, and that the story does not change because the man is a freedman with a yard and a strange reputation. It does not change because the truth is that I thought of her as the closest thing I had to a sister in a thousand miles of strangers. Rome did not have a slot for *the closest thing I had to a sister*. It had a very well-worn slot for the other assumption. I'd thought I'd weathered this in advance. I hadn't. Standing in the door with men laughing about what they assumed I'd been doing to a woman I'd watched work for six years, I felt the particular sick heat of being talked about inaccurately in a way I couldn't correct without sounding like exactly the kind of man who'd protest too much.

"For her sums," I said, which was true and got another laugh, because of course it did.

Macer grinned, not unkind, just a man who found the world funny. "Tell yourself stories, Thulean," he said. "Just don't put her name on a contract next to yours where a magistrate can see it, or the story tells itself."

That stopped me on the step, and I think he meant it to. Because under the dirty joke there was a real warning, the kind Macer gave for free when he could afford to: the gossip wasn't a danger to me. I was a man, and the worst Rome would think of me was the ordinary assumption it made of every man with money and a household. The danger was hers. A freedwoman living unmarried in the house of the man who'd bought and freed her, doing his books, going where he sent her, was a woman whose reputation was something other people got to decide. The deciding had already started, in Macer's doorway, with men I'd never see again. I had been about to make her free, and I had not once, in two years of planning the silver, thought hard about what I was making her free *into*.

***

I freed her in the yard that afternoon, badly.

I'd meant to do it properly - the full ceremony, *manumissio* before a magistrate, the rod laid on the shoulder, the turning, the whole legal machinery that makes a freedwoman a citizen of limited kind. I'd looked into it. I had standing now to do it right, and I wanted it right for her, the real kind, the kind with the name and the cap and the law behind it. But the magistrate's court took days to get into and I had a tablet in my hand that said I owned her, and I could not stand to own her for days. So I did the informal thing first, in the tally-room, in front of Pamphilus and Heras, who I'd asked to come. I said the words: that she was free, that I freed her, *inter amicos*, among friends, witnesses present. I told her we'd do the court part as soon as I could get the date, for her citizenship, for the proper kind.

I had imagined this moment for two years. In the version in my head she wept, or she didn't weep, or she said something I could carry around afterward, and in all the versions I had pictured her face turning toward me in a particular way. I am ashamed of how much I'd wanted that.

She stood by her table with the day's tally still open on it and listened to the whole ceremony with her arms folded, and when I finished she said, "How much."

"What?"

"How much did he take you for. I keep your books. I'll find it in a month when I balance the year. You can tell me now or I can find it and know you didn't tell me."

"Thirty-eight thousand," I said. "And the filter at Tibur, and teaching the steward's boys the figures."

Something moved in her face and was gone, fast. She looked at the open tally. "Thirty-eight. More than nine of Pamphilus." She was running the arithmetic in her head, I could see her doing it, the calculation of herself, and I understood that I had told her the one number I should have lied about, and that lying would have been worse. "You priced low on the porter and they laughed at you. You priced high on me and they laughed at you. Any number and they laugh. Wrong way around, either one."

"Tyche."

"I'm not thanking you," she said.

It landed exactly where she'd aimed it. I'd told myself I didn't want her gratitude, and that had been a comfortable lie, because some part of me had bought it in advance with the silver, had been waiting for it. She'd seen that clearly, and she'd refused to pay. "I'm not asking you to," I said, which was the lie I deserved.

"You hid me." She wasn't loud. Heras had gone very still by the door, and Pamphilus was looking at the floor. "Five years. You taught me the figures and then you told the old man I was slow and you told *me* to stop catching his mistakes so I'd stay cheap and stay in the yard. You did it to keep me from being sold to a factor. I know. I'm not stupid. I heard the threat the same day you did - I was carrying the bread. I know you were saving me." She put her hand flat on the tally, on her own neat columns. "But what it was, every day, was you deciding how much of me Rome got to see. A woman who can do this" - the hand pressed flat - "made herself small for five years so that the man who'd been good to her could keep being able to be good to her. You don't know what that is. You decided I'd be slow. For my own good. And now you've decided I'm free. For my own good. And the comb."

Her chin went up. "I heard about the comb. The wharf heard about the comb before I did."

The Tibur joke had beaten the cart back to the yard. Of course it had.

"That's the part I got wrong," I said. Pamphilus shifted his weight by the door and said, low, "Truth." One word, aimed at the floor. Heras hadn't moved. "Macer's right that it's a danger. But he's wrong about where it lands. It's not on me. It's on you. A woman in my house, freed by me, unwed, keeping my books - they've already started telling the story. I should have thought about that two years ago and I didn't. I'm sorry."

She looked at me for a long moment, working out where the error was, the way she looked at a column that didn't sum.

"In a city that already has a story about me," she said, "with your bought name on me, and no man and no family, the silver was the simple part. There's still everything after it."

Heras spoke from the door, dry, in Greek, which she half-followed now and pretended not to. "You solved a problem and bought yourself a harder one." He paused, something like a resigned uptick at the corner of his mouth. "It's what you always do."

***

I let her set the terms. Because she was right that they were hers to set.

She would stay at the yard, she said, because she had nowhere better and the work was real and she liked the work, which was as close to a kindness as she'd give me that day. She would be paid a real freedwoman's wage, on a real reckoning, no peculium-fiction - money she could keep and bank and that I could not take back. Her name would go on nothing of mine: no contract, no shared lease, nothing a magistrate or a wagging tongue could read as the other story. She would have her own room with a door that barred from the inside, in the front building with Pamphilus and his wife lodged between her room and mine, so the geography of the yard itself argued for her. And the court date, the proper *manumissio*, the citizenship - I would get it within the month or she would know I'd lied about that too.

"And one more," she said, and almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile of the afternoon and it was at my expense. "Don't say anything to me like a man in a story. You have a yard and a borrowed name and more enemies than you count. Teach me the marks you won't teach anyone. The real ones. The ones in the little book you keep against the dead glass, at night when you think we're asleep. The marks that aren't Latin and aren't Greek."

I had thought no one had seen me writing English. I sat there in the quiet tally-room and understood that she had been watching me from the beginning, taking the measure of the thing I kept back, and had now said the price of her staying plainly enough that no one could pretend they'd misunderstood.

***

I'd kept the script back from everyone for eleven years. Heras had glimpsed a line of it once and called it a homeland script and I'd let him, because that was the cover I'd built for it, that the people of Thule wrote this way in their cold north. It was useful precisely because no one in the world could read it but me, which made it the only truly private possession I had, the only place I could put down the things I couldn't say to anyone.

The encyclopedia was going to be in it. Everything I carried that mattered - the ladder I wanted to leave so that someone someday could climb past the steel wall and the flux and the rest. All of it had to go down in a script that the priests couldn't read for blasphemy and the rivals couldn't steal, in a hand that was mine and dead and unfalsifiable, the script of a country no one could check. And it could not stay only mine, because I was one bad cut from dead and then it would all die behind my teeth, which had become the biggest fear I had.

I got the little book out and the dead phone fell from the cloth onto the table, the black glass, and she looked at it once, hard, taking the measure of it, and then let it go without a question.

I made a line of letters: *A B C D.* I said them aloud. I made her name in it - T-Y-C-H-E - and turned the book so she could see. I watched her face do what I'd seen it do over the figures five years before: the stillness that meant the machinery inside her had started and would not stop.

"It goes left to right," I said. "Like Latin. There are only these, twenty-six marks, the same twenty-six, and they make every word there is. You don't learn a new mark for a new word - you spell the sound. The figures were the sounds of numbers. These are the sounds of everything else."

She put her finger on her own name. "*Tyche.*"

"That's the *y* sound. We argue about that one even at home." I stopped. I never said *at home.* I had spent eleven years not saying *at home.*

"At home," she said, and filed it, and didn't ask.

She copied the four letters under mine, slowly, getting the *D* backward, which everyone does, and then again, right, and then her name. Then - unprompted, having reasoned that the sound at the end of her own name probably existed and probably had a mark - she made a fifth letter, *E*, and held it up.

"Is there one like this. I think there's one like this."

There was. I told her there was. I was handing her the only country I had left, written in marks no one else could read, and in the first lesson she had already started reaching past where I'd shown her toward the marks I hadn't yet taught.

Outside, Pamphilus was getting the cranes quiet for the evening, and Heras had gone, and the lamp needed trimming. Tyche bent over the little book in the bad light and made the letter *E* again, larger, until she had it the way she wanted it.
