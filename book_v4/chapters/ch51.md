# Chapter 51: A Hand Not His

*From Daniel's private notes, in the homeland script, undated - the last pages of the third copy, in his hand:*

*Tyche found me this morning going through the physician's reports from the western voyage - the mortality figures, the quarantine protocols Zoticus carried out to the ships. She asked what I was reading. I told her. She waited.*

*I said: the protocols we wrote for the outbound crews - the burial rules, the water discipline, the camp-separation procedure - I designed them to keep the sailors from killing everyone they met on the other shore. That was the use I had in mind. But Zoticus has been running the same protocols in the eastern army camps for eleven years. And the physicians he trained have passed them to physicians they trained. If the right plague comes out of the east - and it will, I have always known it will - those camps may bury fewer. Not because of anything I will live to see. Because I was trying to do something else entirely and this came along with it.*

*She was quiet for a moment. Then she asked whether I had filed the latest voyage report in the second copy or the third.*

*I had not. I filed it.*

GAIUS NORBANUS RUFUS TO HIS FRIEND SEXTUS CAECILIUS PRISCUS, GREETING.

You ask me, with the studied carelessness of a man who has already decided his own opinion, what I made of the printed library in the portico off the Argiletum, and whether the Thulean was the prodigy the stall-keepers swear he was or merely the most successful charlatan of our fathers' age. I have let your letter sit a market-day on my table while I turned the question, and I find I can give you both answers and neither, which is the only honest report a man can make of a thing everyone already knows.

I went, as you bade me. The portico is the one past the booksellers, where the smell of fish from the lane below contends with the smell of the lamp-black they use on the sheets, and loses. What struck me first, before I had read a word, was the noise of the place. A library, properly understood, is the conversation of the great dead, conducted in the silence they have earned. This was a market in disguise: apprentices with ink to the elbow, a fuller's boy, a girl no older than my sister's youngest sounding out the lines with her finger and her lips, a Syrian who could barely manage his Latin aloud reading the same sheet four men had read before him because it was chained there for any hand. The books do not wear out at the rate the readers do. That is the marvel.

You will say I am a snob, and you will be right. But hear me before you laugh. I took down the famous treatise, the one they say the man left after him, rendered into our tongue as far as the lines and the angles and no further, with a note at the foot in plain Latin that the rest exists and may be sought by those fit to climb to it. A pretty conceit. I read the page on the figures. And Priscus, I tell you, the cursed marks worked. I set my own abacus against them, here in my own house, and the marks were faster, and a clerk three streets off could check my sum without trusting my honesty. They are everywhere now, cut in the milestone at the second mile; my own steward keeps the wine-tally in them and thinks I do not know.

So much for the legend's better half. Now the man.

What everyone knows of him you know already, so I will set it down only to sort the true from the improved. He came, they say, out of Thule, that island Pytheas swore he sailed to and Strabo, with more sense, called a fable. The far north, past the last of the tin, where the sea grows thick like a lung and the sun forgets to set and then forgets to rise, and the men live on the ice and read the stars because they have nothing else to look at half the year. From that nursery of cold he came down to us with the marks and the press and the rest of it. The tale adds that his countrymen sent ships across the western sea, which is no longer a tale, Priscus, as you know well as I. The western crossing is old news, surveyed and charted now in the new figures, the ships going out from the Gaulish yards on a schedule twice in a generation since my grandfather's time. Whether the Thulean's countrymen were ahead of our pilots or only ahead of our records is a different question, and a man of honest mind keeps the two questions in separate columns.

He made fire fly. That much is past dispute, for there are old men who saw it, or whose fathers swore they saw it, which among us is the same evidence and better. A bag of greased cloth filled with the breath of a fire, that rose over the Subura roofs and came down burning, and the crowd cried witchcraft and then cried wonder. The army took it up the river to scout the Dacians, and there I have heard it both ways, that it was a great eye that won the war and that it was a fairground trick that killed a soldier and was put away. Apollodorus the bridge-builder called it a clever toy that does one thing, they say, and Apollodorus was a sour man who died badly for the sin of speaking his mind to one who outranked him in everything save mind. I am inclined to trust his judgment on technical matters regardless.

And the frauds, Priscus, do not let them tell you there were none. He sold the rich a glass that was to show the small invisible animals he claimed swarm in foul water and crawl into a man to eat him from within. A senator's wife I will not name paid a year's perfume-money for one and saw through it only a smeared rainbow and her own fog. He could no more show them than the augurs can show us the will of the gods, and he charged more for the failure. Yet the man also taught the legions to boil their drinking water and to dig their privies downhill of the well, and the flux that used to empty a camp faster than the enemy now empties it slower, and physicians who never heard his name do the practice he taught and credit Asclepius, who is welcome to it. I have it from the physicians my own father consulted that the camps which kept the boiling and the quarantine burial of the dead through the recent sickness — this eastern plague that came on the armies in Verus's late campaigns, which we are not yet through — buried fewer than the camps that kept the old way. A man may be a mountebank with one hand and a benefactor with the other.

Here is the part that will interest you, who keep a tame Stoic to scold you. There was a great quarrel in our fathers' day over the impiety of the enterprise. The grammarian Crispus, whom your father knew, made the charge and made it well, and the phrase he coined has outlived them both: the barbarian's heaven by ladder. For the Thulean's whole project, his marks and his books and his contest of mechanical fancies, was a staircase the foreigner proposed to build up past the gods, rung upon rung, each man standing on the last, climbing out of the place the gods set us. Crispus called it the oldest impiety in a new dress, Prometheus with a printing-frame, and the phrase has the ring of truth a phrase keeps long after the man who said it is proved a fool or a knave. The colleges took it up; there was a reckoning of some sort, a heifer to Minerva, the whole enterprise dedicated into her keeping so that the gods got their cut, which is how we settle all our impieties. The contest is still held on her festival. The priests are paid. Heaven, it seems, was bought rather than climbed, which is the Roman ending to every story.

Now I will tell you the thing I went there half-meaning to tell you, and it is a small thing, and it has stayed with me past its size. I fell to talking with the freedman who keeps the place, a careful fellow who had it from his master who had it nearer the source. He told me how they buried the Thulean. With him in the ground, against all sense, they put an amulet of his homeland: a tablet of black glass, polished smooth, the size of a man's palm, that took no inscription and served no use the freedman could name. A savage's charm, he called it, the kind of dark mirror these northern peoples carry to frighten off the dead, and the man would not be parted from it living and was not parted from it dead. They put it beside him with some bronze plate of his citizenship, the two together, and shovelled the both of them under. I had a laugh of it. Think, Priscus: a man who gives Rome the figures and the press and cannot let go a lump of barbarian glass at the end, must take his idol down into the dark with him like a Pharaoh. They are children under it all, these clever foreigners.

The freedman told me one other thing, and I give it to you as he gave it, without weight, for it is the sort of saying these establishments grow like mould on a wall, to flatter their founder. They asked the man once, near the end, where he had learned all he knew, and he would never say more than the cold north and the long road no one comes back along. But a woman keeps the books there still, very old, a freedwoman who was his clerk fifty years, and the freedman has it from her that the man told her, or told someone, that the knowing was never his own, that every rung of his ladder was set down before him for any hand to find, and that he had only carried the fire back. A pious enough sentiment, and convenient, since it turns the impiety into an offering and saves the heifer. I do not believe he believed it. But the woman believes he said it, and she is teaching it to a slave-girl she has bought, who is teaching it to two more, and the marks spread, and the sheets spread, and the milestone is cut.

So there is your report. Was he a prodigy or a fraud? He was a foreigner, Priscus, the dead keeping their accounts in a column we cannot audit, and we will never know in what proportion. I bought, on my way out, a child's toy from the stall under the portico: a little clay cart with a smoking stack and four painted wheels, a Thulean engine, the model his contest set the city's brats dreaming of, a cart that runs on its own fire and was never built. My grandson rolls it across the floor and roars to be the fire. I watch him go.

Enough. Tertia sends her duty to your wife; the Falernian you praised was corked, send no more of it; and if you are in the city for the games, my table is yours.

Farewell. Written at Rome, on the day before the Ides of June, in the consulship of Clarus and Cethegus.
