# Chapter 44: The World Bends

The new figures came to the Aurelian milestone the spring I turned six-and-fifty, and I learned it from a carter who could not read them.

He had stopped his mule at the wharf gate to ask Pamphilus the way to a warehouse, and while they argued about which lane it was he jerked his thumb back up the road and said the magistrates had cut a new stone at the second mile, and a man could tell his distance off it now in shopkeeper's marks, which he supposed was progress if you were a shopkeeper. He said it as a man states the weather: a thing observed, not cared about. Pamphilus sent him down the right lane and came in and told me, because Pamphilus knew, alone of nearly everyone, where the shopkeeper's marks had come from.

I walked out to see it. I would not have done it at forty, walked an hour up a hard road to look at a rock, but my eyes were going and my legs were still good and I had reached the age where I would rather use the part of me that worked.

The stone was new-faced, the chisel-marks still pale, an aedile's name carved across the top in proper Roman letters, the whole apparatus of who had paid for the road's repair and under which consuls. And below it, where the older milestones gave the distance to the city in numerals the size of a man's hand, IIII and the rest, this one gave it in mine. A two and a circle. The circle was the trouble. Zero is the mark no Roman wants, the mark for the nothing that is somehow a something, the one I had set down on a wax tablet thirty-nine years before in a cell to a Greek physician who thought I was a lunatic, and here it was cut a thumb deep in stone on the public road, official, paid for, a magistrate's reckoning of how far a man stood from Rome.

I put my hand on it. The cut was sharp under my fingers, the grit of fresh stone, and I stood there long enough that a woman driving a goat went around me wide, in the way you go around a man talking to a wall.

I had won that fight cheap and lost it complete. Cheap, because the figures cost me nothing to give and spread themselves on speed alone, fish-sellers' slates and grain-factors' day-books, because a man cheating you with Roman numerals can hide a mistake in a column and a man cheating you in mine cannot. Complete, because somewhere in the years between the slate and the stone they had stopped being the Thulean's marks and become simply the marks, the standard form, and no one cutting that milestone had thought of me any more than the carter had. I stood there with my hand still flat on the grit and my palm going numb from the cold of the new stone, and I started the walk home.

---

Theophanes took me to the portico the next month, which he did from kindness and a Greek's pride in a collection, not knowing he was showing me my own reflection in a bad mirror.

It stood off the Argiletum behind the booksellers, a colonnade a rich freedman of the imperial house had endowed with his name on the architrave, two long rooms of shelving open to any man who could read and behave. I had heard of it. I had not gone, because I had a horror of going, the dread of a man who builds a child and is afraid to look at what it became. Theophanes wanted my eye on a question of cataloguing in the figures, and I went for that, and forgot it the moment I came through the door.

The smell hit first: ink, the flat metallic ink we mixed with lampblack and gum at the press, the smell of the wet sheet coming off the block, and under it the rawer smell of new-cut pine boards and the size in fresh-bound covers. A library smells of dust and cedar oil and old papyrus going to powder. This room smelled of something being made faster than it could age.

The shelves on the left were the old library, scrolls in their pigeonholes, a few hundred of them, the accumulated copying of generations of slaves' wrists, each one different, each one a man's eyes and a man's errors. The shelves on the right were mine.

I do not mean they were mine. I mean they had come off the press, and there were more of them than there were scrolls on the other wall, and they were the same. That was what the eye could not stop on. A scroll is a single creature; you cannot mistake one for its neighbor. These were ranks of the identical, the times-tables Eros had cut thirty years ago, the rules of the new figures, the geometry primers, cheap Homer and cheaper Aesop, the printed prize-tales from the contest sold for an as and bound up by some industrious soul into a fat ugly codex of pure invention, sixty copies of a thing where there had once been one. Bound flat, in the codex form, because you cannot shelve a stack of printed sheets like a scroll and the form had had to change to hold the flood. The columns of them, the same spine and the same spine and the same spine, made my eyes do a thing I did not like, a kind of vertigo, the multiplication of a single object until it stops being an object.

And there were people reading them. A wool-dyer's apprentice, by the stains to the wrist, mouthing the geometry primer with his finger under the line. A girl, which stopped me cold, a slave by her dress, free to be in that room because a printed sheet costs almost nothing and a scriptorium does not let a slave girl handle the master's Cicero. The cost had come down until reading was an occupation a poor man could spend an idle hour on.

Then Theophanes showed me what had brought him here. This year's judges' prize had gone to a woman, a linen-bleacher's widow from the Transtiberim, for a design of a self-cooling malting vat that no one had tried yet, and her winning tale was among the new acquisitions, two dozen copies identical. Theophanes had been in the room when the panel voted and wanted to tell me there was less argument than he'd expected. I had expected less argument than there was. We were both wrong in the easy direction. I'd imagined the city would fight it for a year; instead the man who objected loudest did so once, at moderate volume, and was quietly reminded that Minerva herself had set the rules for this contest, and that Minerva was known to favor industry over its sex. He objected again more quietly. Then he stopped. "You do not look pleased," Theophanes said.

"I am pleased," I said, which was true. I was also watching the wool-dyer's boy and his finger under the line and calculating whether the linen-bleacher's widow's plans would reach him, and whether a man who bought such a vat would pay the woman who designed it or have his smith build one and call it his own. I told Theophanes to shelve the printed works on the figures and the scrolls on the Greek alphabet and the two would have to learn to live on the same shelf, and he laughed and said that was very like me. I let him think it was a joke about cataloguing.

On the way out I stopped at the doorman's bench, where the room's tally-board hung on its peg. He was logging the day's visitors in the new figures, which made the book auditable, as I had wanted - but the column beside the visitor count was a different matter. A tax agent's mark, a subagent of the fiscal census, had been recording which districts each visitor came from and for how long. The doorman was proud of his tally; it kept the room orderly and let the endower's steward track patronage. That was all it was. It was also a clean accounting of who read what, and where they lived, and how often they came. I thought about what that column would be worth to a man who wanted to know which neighborhoods were literate and which were not, and who frequented which texts. The figures were my marks and I had not built that use into them. I had not built any use into them. The marks did not care who held the book.

There had also been a question, on the anonymous track, that had kept the panel talking past dusk. A submission with no name, written in a hand no one on the panel recognized, describing a method of calculating compound interest through a kind of shorthand the writer had built from first principles. The panel had voted to award it, then a member recognized the working as derivative of a table Daniel's own press had been printing for seven years, and the vote to reveal the author ran three against two. We kept the anonymity. The author was found afterward to be a silversmith's boy of sixteen. He was from Lugdunum. There was controversy, which I expected; there was argument about whether it would have won if they had known, which I could not answer, and there was a loud petition from a Gallic equestrian who had submitted something longer and more elegant and wanted the committee to reconsider. The committee declined. I filed the complaint in the same pile where I filed the complaints about the linen-bleacher's widow, and went home.

---

I would not have gone to the western yard at all if Hadrian had not sent for the survey in person, and the survey lived at the yard, and so I went down to the coast in the late summer in a wagon that hurt my back, an old man fetched to read his own map to younger men who could read it better.

It was south of the Garonne mouth, a working yard the program had bought into, sheds and slipways and a stink of pitch and oak-sap and the green reek of new-sawn timber, and pilots' talk going in three languages over the noise of the mauls. The survey itself was nothing to look at, leaves of the African coast in Ulpia's clean copying and the men's rougher field hands, leagues and soundings to the one scale in the figures, a coast crawling south a headland a season. I read what I was wanted to read, and answered what I could, and felt the old fraud's sweat that I knew less than the men I was instructing.

Then the yard-master, a leather-faced Gaul named Bituitus who treated me as a clerk's curiosity and the program as a slow miracle, took me out to the long slip to see what they were building, because he was proud of it and wanted a Roman to be proud with him. "The third one swims," he said, in his coast-Latin. "I feel it in her. The first two were learners. This one knows what she's for."

She was up on the ways, half-planked, ribs to the sky. She was wrong. Wrong is what I mean. She was nothing the Romans built. The grain ships that crossed to Alexandria are broad shallow baskets that hug a coast and run before a known wind; everyone at that yard knew it, had buried men who learned it. This hull was deep and narrow and high in the bow, double-planked, the timbers heavier than reason, built to be hit by water from any direction and shrug and stay shut, built by men who had lost two hulls already to the open gray and were building the third to lose better and go further. They had a name for the seam-method that I could not follow in their speech. They had drowned a man getting here. The hull on the ways was an honest answer to a question Rome had never thought to ask before I asked it for them in a senator's house with a lying map. I stood at her keel and looked up through her ribs at the sky and could not speak for a while, and Bituitus took it for the right kind of silence and let me have it.

She could cross. I knew that, standing there. Two ships had proved it already; the first crossing was twenty years behind us, the trade winds were mapped, the current structure was understood well enough to navigate by. What she could not do was hold a line of longitude. The hard half, the westing, wanted a clock that would keep true time through a month of pitching and salt and cold, and the prize for that clock had stood unwon since the year I named it and would go on standing, because I had failed to build a clock that kept true time across a still tally-room, and I knew no man living who could. She could reach the western coast. She could not tell you with precision where on it she stood when she arrived, or find the same landing twice in successive seasons. The hull was real. The grid to navigate it precisely was a generation of clockwork past me.

I had told Hadrian the west was uninhabited, and I had said it knowing it was probably a lie, and these men were spending their lives and now and then a life itself learning to sail that lie out past the point where a man could turn back. Bituitus put his hand on a rib and said he believed the third one would swim where the first two had not. I said I believed him, and meant it, and we walked back to the sheds.

---

The boy I met on the road back, near the second mile, by the milestone with the circle cut in it.

He was perhaps nine, a carter's get by the look of him, left to mind a parked wagon and a dozing mule while his father did business off the road, and he was doing what a boy does with an empty hour, which is making a world. He had a stick. He was drawing in the dust of the road's edge, and talking, half to the mule and half to no one, and I stopped my wagon because my driver wanted to water the beasts at the trough by the stone and because I had heard the boy say a word I knew.

He was building a ship in the dust. A ship with no oars and no sail, he was explaining to the mule, which did not care, a ship that ate fire in its belly and breathed smoke out a stack and walked on the water faster than the wind whether the wind blew or not, and it would go past the edge of the sea to the country no one had found yet, and he, the boy, would be the captain, and he would be rich, and he had seen it in a tale his uncle had bought printed for two coins and read to him until he had it by heart.

He had Felix's steam-ship. The contest-tale of a self-fueling pot that drives a hull, the tale that won the year a counting-house slave stood up and named the metal that does not yet exist, printed cheap and sold for an as and bound up by some industrious soul and read aloud by a carter's brother to a boy of nine who now believed completely, with all nine years of himself behind it, that such a ship existed and that he might one day stand on its deck.

The pot does not exist. There is no engine in the world that drives a ship; the bridge from the fire to the turning had been in my head once at seventeen and had worn out of it years ago with no sign of return, and the steel to hold the fire if I could remember the bridge is the steel Hermes has failed at for thirty-three years with no door in it, and Felix learning at his elbow no better luck. The boy in the dust had the dream a clean generation ahead of any possibility of the engine. He had it whole, with the confidence of a child, and he drew its stack in the dust and made the smoke-noise with his mouth and steered it past the trough where my mules were drinking, out over the edge of the only world he knew, toward a coast that I had put on a map.

I gave him a coin, which frightened him, an old stranger leaning down off a wagon. His father came around the wagon-tail at the wrong moment and started apologizing for the boy bothering a gentleman, and I said the boy had not bothered me, and we went on. Behind us I heard the steam-noise start up again the moment the wagon was a safe distance off.

---

What I carried back up the road had four edges and would not fold flat.

Felix was a quarter-mile from the cattle market that month, on my foundation's coin, failing at the pot the boy had just sailed in the dust. I had paid for both ends of it, the failure and the dream, the man who could not build it and the printed tale that taught a nation of children to want it. Hermes was failing at the steel that the pot needs and the hull needs and the cannon needed, and that knowledge was loose now in the world, sixty copies the same, faithful to whoever held the page. Zoticus had two former soldiers on his patient rolls who had come through the eastern camps using the boiling-water discipline, and they would go home and tell their households, and their households would tell their neighbors, and in a generation some clerk would attribute it to Asclepius and call it always-known, and by then it would be always-known. The physician-line did not need my name; it needed only the practice to keep working, and the practice kept working. Forty physicians on my coin now, with a printed manual for the camp surgeons, and three times that many working the same methods on their own because the methods were observed to reduce deaths in their wards.

I sat in the wagon with the road going under me and tried to tally the four signs into a ledger that balanced. The milestone, the library, the hull, the boy with his stick. They would not sum to a figure I could use. The doorman's tally-board hung in my thinking and would not leave: someone had already built a census around a reading room, and the marks did the counting as cleanly as they counted anything else, and I had not designed that use, and had not been asked, and the marks did not care. I tried the count again and it did not balance again, and at some point between the second mile and the city gate I put the figures down.

That is what I have to say about it. I put the figures down. I had been doing sums about this enterprise since I was a frightened seventeen-year-old in a ditch, and the sums had always been something to hold onto, and I sat in that wagon with my hands empty and the road going under and I could not pick the numbers back up. They sat in me without diminishing until the wagon came through the gate and the wharf-stink hit me and somewhere in my chest something that had been very tight went slack from simply running out of room to keep it clenched.

Marcia had tomatoes on the table from the cook's garden behind the warehouse. The western plants, flat and red, they put them now in sauces over pasta and sell the dish at three Subura thermopolia with different names; we had grown them for more than fifteen years and I still found them startling in a Roman kitchen. She did not ask how the trip was. She saw whatever was in my face and she set a bowl in front of me and sat down and we ate without talking, and the ordinary taste of it was the first entirely concrete thing I had encountered since the milestone.

After supper I went into the press-room, where the day-shift was breaking up and Vitalis was setting the bench to rights, and stood among the racked blocks and the smell of the ink that was now the smell of a public portico off the Argiletum, and I watched the boy rack the day's cut blocks and damp the inking-pads and bank the stove, the ordinary closing of an ordinary day's work, the machine I had built to outlive me being put to bed by a hand that was not mine and would do it the same tomorrow whether I lived or not. Vitalis asked if I needed light to see by, since my eyes, and I said no, I knew the room, and he wished me good night and went, and left me in it.
