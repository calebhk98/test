# Chapter 47: Old

There is a step on the wharf, the third up from the water, where the stone has worn to a dish from two hundred years of men's heels, and that is the step that told me.

I had gone down to the boats for a tally I could have sent Chloe for, a count of the morning's hides off a barge out of Aquileia, and I went down myself because I had been sitting since the lamp-trim and a man my age stiffens at a bench like a cart left out in the rain. Going down was nothing. The water was bright and the wharf stank the good familiar stink of pitch and fish and wet rope, and I took the count off the bargeman in the new figures and corrected him twice and felt about as clever as I ever feel anymore, like a man at a party remembering a joke from a country no one in the room has heard of. Then I turned to come up, and at the dished step my chest closed like a fist.

The wind went out of me and would not come back when I called for it. I stood on the worn stone with one hand on the mooring-post and breathed at the air and the air would not fill me, and there was a roaring high up behind my ears, and the bright water went gray at its edges as a lamp goes when the oil is low. I have seen men die of the chest. I have stood in army camps and watched older legionaries fold quietly at the latrine and never get up, and I had filed it, the way I file everything, under *the heart, or the great vessel off the heart, nothing to be done.* I stood on the step and did to myself what I had done to a thousand frightened soldiers. I told myself the one thing I could not prove, that it would pass. And it passed. The fist opened. The air came. I went up the rest of the steps one at a time with both hands going post to post like a child on a ladder, and at the top I leaned on the gate of my own yard and waited for my legs to belong to me, and a carter I have known thirty years asked was I well and I said the heat, and it was not hot.

I knew exactly what it was and exactly that I could not touch it. That is the part I had never managed to picture, in all the years of picturing my death. I had given this city water you could drink without it killing your children. I had stood over Celer with clean cloth and boiled water and washed hands and watched the foulness in him win anyway, and learned the floor of what I had, learned there was a wall I could build against the little ones coming in and no door in it for what was already inside. The heart is inside. The years are inside. There is no boiling them out. The man who frightened a whole priesthood claiming to know the invisible could stand on a worn step with his own death rehearsing itself in his chest and do nothing but wait to see whether it was the performance or the real night.

---

The body had been going for years, of course, in the slow way, before the step made it sudden.

The eyes first, long back, so long I have stopped grieving them and only work around them, the work held out at arm's length until my arm tired and then read to me by Ulpia in the flat voice she uses for figures. A clean loss, a thing taken all at once a decade gone, and a man can make peace with a clean loss. The slow losses are the ones that wear you, because they ask you to surrender the same ground every morning.

The hands. My hands are cold now in a way that has nothing to do with the weather, cold from the inside, the cold of a stone that the sun cannot reach the middle of, and they ache in the joints in the wet, the knuckles thickened so the old gold ring Marcia gave me will not come past the swell of them and I have stopped trying to take it off. In the morning the fingers will not open straight. I work them against each other under the blanket before I rise, opening and closing, until they will hold a stylus, and even then they hold it like a man holding a child's hand, afraid of crushing and afraid of dropping. The letters I make have grown until ten of mine fill a line that once held forty. I cannot write small. That is the plain fact of it, and it is the fact that changed how the books got finished.

Marcia has the cook do me a barley pap in the mornings, sets it down by my hand and takes hers, and she is sixty-eight and her own back has gone crooked from forty years over a tally-board, so we are a fine matched pair of ruins at our own table.

---

So I dictated. There was no help for it.

It went badly at first and then it went well, and the going-well of it was the strangest grace of that whole long last labor. I would sit at the bench with my eyes shut, which I found I could do now without shame, an old man with his eyes shut talking into a quiet room, and Tyche or Ulpia would sit across with the homeland script and a clean leaf, and I would speak the rungs in the homeland tongue, slowly, and they would write them small and true where my hand no longer could.

Tyche is fifty-seven and her hand is faster than mine ever was and steady as a level, and she takes my words and sets them down and stops me. She stops me more than ever. *You said this surer than you wrote it last winter,* she says, and reads me back the old page where I had marked the same thing with the hooked stroke, the little crook that means *the Thulean is guessing here,* and asks which of the two of me she should believe, the one who is tired tonight and wants to sound certain, or the one who marked his own doubt three years ago with a sharp eye. I tell her the younger one. We keep the crook. She has read my secret language for thirty-odd years and I think she does not entirely forgive me for the years she read it as a slave who could be sold for knowing it, and that unforgiveness is part of why she is the best keeper a thing could have. She owes the books no flattery. She will not let me lie to the unborn to spare my old man's pride.

One evening that spring, after Ulpia had gone home, Tyche and I sat with the copy-manifest spread between us - the working copy she would keep, the collegium chest under three-keyholder rule, the lead-cased buried copy, the partial Latin at the Argiletum. She was noting which sections each location held, checking for gaps, and she asked me without looking up whether I had thought about what the keepers should be told if both she and Ulpia were gone before the books were opened. I said I had not. She pressed the pen against the wax and waited. So I told her: any keeper who can prove they have copied the full ladder to two new hands before they die can open the lead case. She wrote it at the bottom of the manifest. She read it back. I told her to put the same note in the collegium copy, and we left it there.

And then, because the night was quiet, I asked her about the copies we had not yet thought about - whether the lead case would hold if the river flooded badly, whether a second buried copy made sense. She was silent a moment, the pen going still in her hand. Then she said the lead-cased copy was where she'd put the Dacian gold and it had held thirty years of river. I said that was enough. We rolled up the manifest. She set the lamp lower and tied the roll.

Ulpia takes the work differently from Tyche. She is four-and-twenty and she does not stop me, she questions, after, when the leaf is done, pressing her weight against a beam to test it. *You said the push equals the weight times the change in the moving. You said it with a crook. Is the crook for the rule or for your having it?* And I have to tell her the truth, which is that I am sure such a rule exists and unsure I have its true face, that I have the two ends of the rope and a man wiser than I am must find the middle, and she writes that down too, my uncertainty about my own uncertainty, because a true account of what I know includes how I know it. She is better at this than I am. She was raised by Marcia and Tyche to trust no hand, even her own, even her father's, and the law that will not let her keep a counting-house under her own name has made her ferocious about the one thing it cannot bar her from, which is being right.

We put down the lights in the sky as far as I could swear to them and the dark past that marked dark. The little rooms in the blood, written as a story and called a story. The little ones in the water and the wound. The slow changing of creatures over ages that men in my country killed each other over and that I believe and cannot prove. The crops the western ships brought back, and what the land does with them - the corn that grows taller than a man, the root that keeps through winter, the red fruit that Marcia has growing now in a clay pot on the kitchen sill, which her cook calls the Garonne apple and uses with everything. All of it down now, small and true in a hand that was not mine, the bottom rungs oak and the top rungs honestly chewed.

But braided into the relief of it, the same size: I had spent that same winter reading the expedition physician's final reports from the western voyages. The mortality numbers at the coastal points of contact - thirty, forty, fifty percent of the native settlements, in some places higher. I sat with those figures for a long time. I am the man who taught Rome to count them. And I had seen, or thought I had seen, the same curve once before - the camp fever, the men coming in from a direction a disease had never traveled, their bodies with no speech for what hit them, falling before anyone had found words. I thought about the eastern army, when it comes back. The eastern provinces, crammed with people who have never met whatever pathogen it brings home. I wrote about it. I wrote it in English, in the plain teaching voice, naming the thing by its approximate date and its entry point as well as I could guess, and I told whoever read it that the rule was the same as the rule at the western shores: the sickness that enters a new population through a single point of contact kills in a pattern, and the pattern is manageable if you hold the point. Zoticus has the quarantine doctrine in writing now. I gave it to him two months after the chest episode, on a morning when my hands were bad and he was cleaning a back tooth I should probably lose. He read it standing at my bench, his cloth still in his hand. He said he would need to talk with the camp prefects about the eastern frontier camps. I told him to start with the ones on the Syrian road.

The relief of those pages being down was a real weight off some mornings, a slackening, like setting down a load I had carried so long I had forgotten my arms were holding it. And also: the knowledge I have learned not to reach for a name for. That the load was knowledge of a country two thousand years from this room, set into hands that would never know what country it was, who took my dictation and wrote it down because I asked them to.

---

There was one rung I would not get, and that spring I stopped pretending I would.

The clock. The true clock, the one that ticks the same in summer and winter, the one with the little catch that lets the wheel go one tooth at a time and no faster, that I have wanted since I was nine-and-twenty and had Hermes cut me a set of gears and watched the regulator run down or run wild and never once keep an honest hour. I had carried it forty years. I had told Apollodorus to his face I could not build it, and told Hadrian I could not build it and watched the whole ocean program break against the not-building of it, because a ship cannot find its westing without a clock that keeps true at sea, and a man cannot reach a country he drew from memory without finding his westing. The clock was the lock on the door at the bottom of everything I had wanted for myself, and I have known for years it would not open in my life, and knowing a thing and putting it down are two different acts.

I put it down on a clear cold morning when my hands would barely hold the stylus. I had Ulpia bring me the cipher-book where my failures live, the clock dead-ends among them, the gears that ran wild, Apollodorus's plumb-line maxim that I had her copy in there the year he died, *you draw the picture first and ask the stone to agree.* I had her read me back what I had written about the escapement over the years, which was a great deal of wanting and no answer, page after page of a man circling what he could feel the logic of and could not seize, as your tongue circles the name it has lost. And I told her to write, under the last of it, in the plain teaching voice, not the wanting voice: *I never found this. I believe a small catch can be made that lets a wheel pass one tooth at a measured beat and holds it true against a falling weight or a coiled spring, and that the man who makes it will be able to keep time on a still bench and then, if he is very good, on a moving deck, and that the moving deck unlocks the sea and the sea unlocks the whole western half of the world. I could not make the catch. I have drawn here every wrong shape I tried so you need not waste your years trying them. Begin where I failed. Do not begin where I began.*

She read it back. She asked, because she has Marcia's habit of asking the cost of a thing before she'll sign it, whether I wanted it left in the books at all, since it was a confession of failure in a work I meant to teach by. I told her to leave it. A ladder that hides where its maker fell teaches a man to fall there again. We ruled it off.

Then I told her I was setting a prize - not leaving the matter as a closed account, but a standing prize, publicly announced, for the craftsman who solves the escapement and can demonstrate it at sea. The prize would run from the workshop fund. The same model as the Macer Prize, but specific: one problem, one solution, one winner. She wrote it down. I said: the drawings in the cipher-book to go with the box of failed parts, to Felix's workshop, to the craft-failure fund, with a note that here was a rung I had not been able to cut and a man should be paid to fail at it for ten years and then another man after him.

She carried the box out, and I sat at the bench with my eyes shut and listened to her go. Setting down the water-cup after Celer stopped needing it - that is the size of it. The matter was over and my hands were empty and the emptiness, after the first strangeness of it, was only quiet.

---

Macer is ninety-four and not for this world much longer, and they carried him to my table at the turn of spring because Marcia thought I should see him while there was a him to see.

He is a husk now, the big shoulders gone to bone under the skin, the broken-veined face fallen in around the jaw that was once like a beam, and he sat where they set him and turned the heavy gold ring on his finger with his thumb the way he has turned it for half a century, except now the ring turns loose on the wasted finger and he turns it anyway, the gesture outliving the hand it was made for. His mind comes and goes. For a while he thought I was a steward and asked me sharply about a grain contract on the Rhine that I think was settled before I was born into this world, and I answered him in figures and he grew calm. Then he came back to himself all at once, the light coming back when the cloud goes off the sun, and looked at me down the table and knew me, knew everything, the cell and the balloon and the map and the boy he had bought for less than a season's mules, and he said, *You still here, Thulean.* As a question and not a question. And I said I was. And he said, with the old mean dry pleasure that is the last thing in him to go, *Outlived the lot of them, you and me. The clever ones go first.* And turned his loose ring, and went away again behind his eyes, and they carried him home before the meal was over, and Marcia and I sat at the wreck of the table and did not say what we both knew about how few more turns of spring were left in him.

---

I will tell you what I do now in the last light of the day.

When the writing is done and the keepers have gone home with their leaves and the press below has finished biting its sheets without me, I climb to the flat roof of Caepio's warehouse, which is Marcia's, where she dries her pepper and her rope. I climb it slowly, resting on the worn step. I do not go up for the city's sake, though Rome from there at dusk is considerable, the smoke of a hundred thousand suppers and the river going copper and the long shadow of the column I bled for laid across the Forum. I go up because the warehouse roof holds the heat of the day after the wharf has gone cold, and an old man's hands take a long time to warm. Up there the brick under my palms is still warm from a sun that has already set, and I stand with my two cold hands flat on the warm brick and let the day's heat come up into me, a borrowed warmth, going out of the brick into the dark whether I take it or not. I take what I can hold before it is gone. I stay up there until the brick is as cold as I am, and then I go down the dark stair by feel, and lie down beside Marcia, and wait to see what the night will be.
