# Chapter 14: What It Costs

The war did not end the way I had pictured wars ending. I had a teenager's idea of it, gates thrown open, a king kneeling, a single afternoon when the thing was over and you could feel it stop. What happened instead was that the camp got quieter by degrees, fever-breaking quiet, in sweat and small mercies, so that you only knew it afterward.

It came down through the ranks as rumor first, which is how everything came down. Decebalus had sent men to talk. Then it was that Decebalus had sent men to talk and Trajan had let them stand there long enough to learn the difference between a king and a man asking for terms. Then it was a name for it: the king would keep his head and most of his hills and give back the engineers and the standards and the deserters and tear down the forts and call himself a friend of Rome, which everyone in the camp understood to mean a man being let live on a leash until it suited Rome to take the leash off. Nobody cheered. A century down the line from us had been told three times over four days that they were going home and then not going home, and the third time a man threw his cup at the tent pole and got flogged for it, so by the time it was real the men received it with the suspicion of men who had stopped trusting the news.

I remember the morning I knew it for certain because Saturninus came to where I was packing the bag down into its crate and stood there picking at the splinter in his thumb he'd had for a week and said, "They're saying we winter south of the river." He did not say we won. He had buried too many to the flux to think in those terms. He said it flat, with a sideways suspicion that it might start again the moment he said so out loud.

So that was the war. The great machine I had ridden north inside of, all the carts and grain and marching forts thrown up new every night, slowed and turned and began, hugely, to drain back south. I had thought I would feel something at the end of it. What I felt was tired in a way that sleep did not touch, and a low steady relief that I was ashamed of, because relief meant I was getting to go home and Sabinus was getting carried home in a different kind of box.

---

The bag went into its crate and I nailed the lid down myself. A clear morning, white sky, my hands steady. That night, the night Sabinus died, I had burned a corner of cloth at the river and that was grief of one kind, the kind that wants to destroy a thing with its hands. This was after. This was the other kind.

The tethered bag I crated carefully. Celer's mark was on a tablet somewhere that said the army had use of it still. The tethered bag could stand. It saw the far bank in still air and killed nobody doing it, and a thing that does one small honest job and kills nobody is a thing I could nail a lid over and let live.

The other one I did not crate because there was no other one, by then, only the makings of one: the basket willow and the long planks and the patterns I had cut for a wider envelope, all the careful improvements I had been turning over for the next attempt before there was no next attempt. I gathered the patterns, the cut paper shapes of a bag built to carry a man higher and safer than the last bag had carried a man, and I did not burn them. Burning would have been a feeling. I folded them flat and put them at the bottom of the crate under the tethered bag, where they would not be found and would not be used, and I nailed the lid down over both, the thing that worked and the thing that had wanted to, and that was the closest I came to a ceremony.

Apollodorus would have called it sentiment. Maybe it was.

What I had decided was simpler and harder than grief. I had put a man into a design I had not finished testing, in weather I had already called wrong, and he had died. The rule I made for myself on that trampled ground was an engineer's rule, not a prayer: I won't rush untrained people into an untested design. Every version of manned fire-flight I had run up to that point fell under that sentence. Sabinus had not had fifty hours of tethered practice. The basket had not been stress-tested at altitude in crosswind. He went up because the army needed him to go up, and I had let that override the engineering, and there was a man in the dirt because of it. That was the full accounting. The rule was against rushing a design that holds a human life. Until I knew a design was sound and the person going up in it knew what they were doing, nobody went up.

The tethered bag was still tethered, still tested, still flown with rope and sandbag. The unmanned kite work I had been running before the campaign, the small low glider frames with no fire and no basket and nothing alive aboard, those were different, those were allowed, those I would pick back up when I was back in Rome with a yard to run them in. The rule covered manned fire-flight. It did not cover everything.

Naso watched me nail down the lid. He had drifted over from the mules, slow, the ankle never having set quite true, and he stood with his ruined hand against his chest and his good one hooked in his belt and watched me drive the last peg. When I straightened up he looked at the crate and then at me and there was a question in it that I answered before he asked.

"That one's done," I said. "The one that goes up with a man in it. That's done, until I know it's right to do it again."

He worked his jaw. Naso had been a talker, before, by the small accounts I'd heard, a recruit who had put his hand up for the high job because the high job got noticed. The fire had taken some of that out of him along with the use of the hand. He thought about it a while.

"Good," he said. Then, because he was eighteen and could not quite leave a thing alone any more than I could: "You'll build it again." He said it without heat, flat, the certainty of a man who had seen enough to expect things.

"When it's ready," I said. "Not before."

He looked at the hand against his chest, the fingers curled in on themselves where they'd healed wrong, and something in his face that wasn't unkind and wasn't soft either settled into place. The look of a man who has paid for his own opinion and feels entitled to it. "Everybody says no," he said. He went back to the mules.

He came south with the baggage. I made sure of it, which was the largest thing I had the power to do: a word to Saturninus and a word that Saturninus carried to someone with a tablet, that the maimed one was useful with the mules and should be kept on the strength and not turned loose at the river to beg his way home on a ruined ankle. A man with one hand can still mind mules. The work does not stop for anybody, and as long as there was work he had a place, and a place was the only thing I could buy him.

---

Celer found me the day before the column I traveled with broke camp.

He came on me at the horse-lines at a grey hour, in a plain tunic with no marks of rank on it, which I had learned to read as a man going off duty into himself for an hour. He had a wineskin and he did not offer it, which was Celer, and he stood next to me at the horse-lines looking at the horses, both of us looking at the horses, because that was easier.

"South tomorrow," he said.

"South tomorrow."

"You'll be glad of the city." He said city with a frontier man's delivery: half sneer, half hunger for it.

"I'll be glad of a roof that doesn't get struck every night," I said, "and bread that isn't more grit than bread, and a day where nobody asks me to put a man over a hill."

He let that sit. A horse down the line stamped and blew. Celer had rope-burn scars across both palms now, healed pale, from the day he'd hauled on the lines that didn't save anyone, and he stood there with those hands flat on the rail and he did not look at me and he said, in the flat ledger voice, "I wrote the letter. The legate has it. Can't be done from that side." A pause. "I left your name out of the part that mattered and put it in the part that didn't. You scouted, you signaled, the bag served. The rest of it's a soldier's death and soldiers die." He turned his head a fraction. "You understand me."

I understood him. He was telling me he had taken it onto his own back where the army could see, the whole weight of it, so that the thing would not be hung on a Thulean slave who could be sold or whipped or worse for an officer's mistake. He was telling me I was protected, and that the telling cost him. He was not going to say any of that. He was a man who would rather be flogged than caught caring out loud.

"Thank you," I said, and the words came out smaller than what they were for, which was always going to be the case with those two words.

"Don't thank me," he said. "I let it go up." He drank from the skin then, finally, one long pull, and handed it to me, and I drank, and it was bad wine, and we stood at the horse-lines drinking bad wine in the grey, two men who had stood at the edge of a trampled patch of ground together, and we did not say his name, Sabinus, we did not say it, and the not-saying of it was the realest thing that passed between us. After a while Celer pushed off the rail.

"Build me something that doesn't need a man hung off it," he said, which from him was nearly tender. "Or build me nothing. Either one." He went off toward the command tents and the war that was ending around us, and I did not see him again before the column moved.

---

The road south was the road north run backward and it was nothing like the road north. North I had ridden inside the machine half-drunk on the scale of it, the carts and the grain and the marching forts thrown up new every night, a teenager's awe at how cleverly Rome could put twenty thousand men anywhere it wanted them. South I rode in the same machine and saw what the cleverness was for. We passed the markers of the road we had built going up. We passed graves. The flux had not signed any treaty with anybody and it came south with us in the men's guts, and at one camp a man two fires over from mine was loud in the night and quiet by morning, and they wrapped him and we moved on, and I boiled my water and Pamphilus boiled his and I gave a cup of the boiled stuff to a muleteer who would not take it twice because it tasted of nothing and a man wants his water to taste of the river. One cup. I gave it once and he drank it once and that was all my power there, against the flux that was riding south with us.

I thought about the flux a great deal on that road. I had filed it away going north, a problem I was powerless against, but somewhere between the Loaf and the Alps the filing-away curdled into something with a harder edge. I had spent two years and a man's life teaching fire to lift a basket, and the basket killed, and the cup of boiled water that bored a muleteer would have saved him and a hundred like him if I could only make a hundred like him drink it. I had been building the wrong thing. Wrong because of what it was for. The balloon had only ever had one honest customer and his name was war, and war had taken my best work and put a man in it and burned him, and I had let it, because letting it was the price of being worth keeping.

I did not have a word for what I decided on that road. I was a slave. I would build what I was told to build or I would be a slave who built nothing, which is a slave with a shorter life. But inside that, in the inch of choosing a man owns even in a cage, I could choose which of the things they wanted from me I gave eagerly and which I gave slow, and which I pretended I could not do at all. I could put my hands toward the things that kept men out of the dirt and drag my feet toward the things that put them in it.

I started making that list. Press, numerals spreading, the filter I had sketched and never built. Rag paper, because papyrus costs too much and the press can't run without what it prints on. And at the bottom of the other column, the things I would bury: the powder. The white salt and the charcoal and the third thing I had written ??? for in a cell two years ago and had never let myself chase, because I had seen now what fire did to a man and I was not going to be the one who taught fire to do it on purpose. Not the balloon with a man in it. Not with the powder. Not yet. Maybe not ever, not while I had a say in the order I built things in, and I meant to fight for that say.

It was a small freedom and it was the only one I had and I took it.

---

Rome came up out of the haze on a hot afternoon and I cried, which surprised me, a few hard ugly tears I scrubbed off before Pamphilus could see, because the city was loud and filthy and reeked of garum and dung and wet wool and it was the first thing in two years that smelled like being alive instead of dying, and because Sabinus would never smell it, and the two things came up my throat at once again and I let them, walking, with my eyes streaming and my face turned away, in the safest privacy a slave gets, which is the privacy of being beneath notice in a crowd.

Heras was at the wharf yard when the steward's man brought me to it. I do not know how he knew the day. He was a freedman with no business at a timber wharf and he was there anyway, sitting on an upturned crate in the shade of the shed with a scroll he was not reading, and when I came through the gate he looked up and took me in for a long moment, the whole length of me, the same reading he'd given me the first day when he was deciding whether I was mad. He had heard. Of course he had heard. A man had died in my machine on the Danube and that kind of news travels faster than the man who survives it.

He did not ask what happened. That was Heras. A lesser friend would have asked, would have wanted the story, would have offered the thing people offer, that it was not my fault, that I had warned them, that the army overruled me, all of it true and all of it useless. Heras knew it was useless. He was a physician. He had stood over more dead men than I would ever count and had long ago run out of things to say that helped.

"You've gotten thin," he said. "And you stink. The frontier agrees with no one." He stood, his knees doing the thing every man's knees over forty did, and he picked up his scroll. "There is a bath two streets over that I have been told does not have a corpse in the cistern this month. We are going to it. You are going to be quiet and I am going to talk about Epicurus, who has the great advantage of being dead and therefore unable to put men in baskets, and you are going to listen or pretend to, and tomorrow we will look at the water."

"The water," I said.

"You wrote me about the water." He had stopped at the gate. "From the road north. A bad letter, half of it about a bridge. But there was a thing in it about the flux and the boiling and how the army drinks from wherever it stops and dies of it. I kept that part." Those dry clever eyes had punctured every grand thing I had ever brought him and had, every time, quietly kept the part that worked. "You came back wanting to build a different thing than you left building. I can hear it in you like a cough. Don't tell me what it is yet. Tell me at the bath, after you don't stink." He went out into the lane and waited for me to follow, an old Greek who had decided to be my friend on no evidence and had never once let me make a speech, and I followed him, and we did not say Sabinus either, Heras and I, not that day. He had set the man down between us by naming the water, and we both knew it, and we left him there, present and unspoken, and went to wash the frontier off me.

---

The yard was Tyche's now, belonging to her in the clean sense of a place run by whoever didn't leave.

I saw it the next morning, properly, in the early light before the wharf got loud. The well, the shed, the tally-room, all of it standing, all of it run. There were cloth bolts stacked where I had left chaos. There was a tally on the wall in the new figures, my figures, in a small fierce hand that was hers, columns of receipts and outlays that balanced, that she had kept balanced for a year and more with nobody to check her and Onesimus the old accountant surely sneering at every numeral. She had not let it slide. She had not let them take it from her. A girl of sixteen with a slave's collar of caution still around everything she did had held my whole small enterprise together by sheer stubborn competence, and the wall of figures was her saying so, in the only language that was safe for her to say it in, which was the language of work done right.

She came out of the tally-room when she heard me and stopped in the doorway. She had grown. Two years. She had the look on her she'd had the first day, the watching look, the slave's quick read of a man's mood before she committed to a single word, and she read mine, and whatever she read made her not say the thing people say.

"You ate on the road," she said instead. Not a question. An accusation, almost, that I had managed to feed myself without her arranging it. The first thing she had ever said to me had been about bread.

"Badly," I said. "There was grit in everything. I think I ate a denarius' worth of the actual road."

The corner of her mouth moved, which from Tyche was a great deal. Then her eyes went past me to the gate, counting the same count she always ran, who had come, who had not.

"Pamphilus," I said, before she could ask. "He came south. He's well. As well as he is." I did not say the other names because there were no other names, because the man who had not come back had never been mine to bring, and she knew that too, and she let it go by, and went back to the tally as if the conversation were over, which with her it was.

I stood in the yard a while after she'd gone in. A workbench under a lean-to, a well, a shed, a wall I was kept behind, the finest cage I had. The river smelled like a river. Somewhere out past the wall the wharf was waking up, men shouting timber tallies, a cart, a dog. On the bench were the tools I had left, a knife, a needle, the awl, all of them just as good for other work, all of them waiting to be told what they were for now.

I picked up a scrap of wax tablet and the bronze stylus and I sat down on the upturned crate Heras had warmed and I started, not with the balloon, not with anything that flew, not with the powder I knew the army would want from me the moment somebody clever asked the right question, the white salt and the charcoal and the third thing I had written ??? for in a cell two years ago and had never let myself chase, because I had seen now what fire did to a man and I was not going to be the one who taught fire to do it on purpose, not yet, maybe not ever, not while I had a say in the order I built things in and I meant to fight for that say. I started with the water.

I drew the well. Then I drew, badly, from a half-memory of a thing I'd seen in a textbook diagram once and could not fully reconstruct, the cut-open guts of a slow filter, the layers, sand over charcoal over gravel. I got the layers in the wrong order and knew it and turned the stylus and scraped them flat and tried again, and got it wrong a second way, and that was honest, that was the right kind of failure, the kind you can grind your way out of with a man who won't be impressed and a girl who won't let the figures slide and enough mornings. I would get it wrong a hundred more times. Sand does not sterilize. I knew that much, I'd told Heras that much. You still had to boil. But you could make the water clear enough that a man would drink it, clear enough that it didn't taste of the river he was afraid to leave behind, and a man who would drink it was a man you could keep out of the dirt, and there were a great many men.

Tyche came back out with bread and set it on the bench beside the tablet without a word, because I had said I'd eaten badly and that was the same to her as not at all. I scraped the filter flat and started it a third time. The light came up over the wall. The stylus dragged in the wax, sand over charcoal over gravel, and I leaned into the work, and behind me the wharf got loud, and the day started.
