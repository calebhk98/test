# Chapter 22: Marcia

Getting what you wanted turned out to arrive as paperwork.

By the autumn after the cannon I had more money than I knew how to hold and more obligations than I could carry in my head, and the two were strangling each other. There was the contest, on my coin through Macer's name. The press, Eros the pressman I now paid by the month. The yard, the wharf rent, Pamphilus and his wife and the new hand, Geta on his lifetime wages in his room off the well, the niter beds I had to pretend belonged to a man in volcano country, the sulfur that came up the river under another name, Hermes's forge swallowing money on steel that worked one batch in four. Army drafts to honor and salon invitations I couldn't refuse without insulting somebody who could end me, and a patron, Macer, whose dawn door I still stood at like a good client, and a higher patron, the emperor's house, whose man from the cannon demo kept turning up to look at things and say little.

And there were the contracts. That was the part killing me. A freedman doing business in Rome does it inside a thicket of law and custom grown over centuries to favor the people who grew it, and I was a foreigner with a bought name who could not always tell when he was being robbed. I had been cheated on cloth. I had been cheated on cartage. I had signed a lease for the second shed that, when Heras read it back to me slowly, turned out to let the owner take the shed and everything in it if I was late on a payment by a single day, and I had not been able to see it because the Latin of contracts is its own language, dense and barbed, and I read at the speed of a clever child.

Tyche kept the books beautifully. She could tell me to the as where the money was and where it had gone. What she could not do, what neither of us could do, was the other half, the part that wasn't arithmetic. She was a freedwoman of twenty-four, her word weighed at a freedwoman's weight, and she could no more walk into a shipping factor's counting-house and make him respect a clause than I could. We were stuck, the two of us, at the edge of the same trap: see it clearly, still not get out. So I went looking for a man to run the legal and commercial side, and I found a woman instead.

***

Heras sent me to her. He'd been treating her household for years, a daughter's fevers and an old slave's bad chest, and he said it the way he said things he'd already decided about, dry, over the top of a cup.

"There's a widow on the Aventine side keeps her dead husband's wharf business better than he did," he said. "Caepio's. You'll know the warehouses, the ones with the good roof, the only roof on that stretch that doesn't leak. She's a freedwoman. She buys and lets storage, lends short against cargo, factors for men who can't be bothered to factor for themselves, and she has not been cheated in eleven years that anyone can name. If you want someone who can read a Roman contract and tell you where the knife is, that's her. She'll cost you. She doesn't need you." He set the cup down. "Her name is Marcia. Don't be charming at her. She's had charming men in her warehouse her whole life and she counts the spoons after."

I asked why she would take it on, and he said, "People who don't need you and take you on always have reasons. Those are the ones worth having."

***

The warehouse smelled of pepper and damp rope and the particular sourness of stored hides, and it was the driest, best-kept building I'd been in on that wharf - the floor swept, the cargo tallied on slates hung from nails by lot, the slaves moving like men who knew idleness would be noticed. She had a room off the loading floor with a high window, a table, a strongbox, and a stool for her and no stool for anyone else, which I understood the moment I was standing in front of the table with nowhere to put myself.

Marcia was a head shorter than me and built solid, a woman of about thirty-five in a good plain dark stola with her hair up and pinned hard - no curls, no paint, a ring of keys at her waist and ink on the side of her right hand. Her face was square and unhurried and her eyes ran down me once, took the measure, moved on to whether the measure was worth her morning.

"You're the Thulean," she said. "The one who flies."

"I don't fly anymore."

"I know. You stopped after the soldier." She said it flatly, a fact she'd filed, and I felt the floor tilt a little, because that was not the public story. The public story was that the balloon was the emperor's eye and observed from a tether. The soldier who died had been written down as a volunteer and the manner of it kept off the rolls. She watched me find out that she knew and she let me find out, and I understood I was dealing with someone who collected the true version of things and kept it in a strongbox with the rest of her assets.

"Heras tells me you're drowning," she said. "He used a kinder word. He's a kind man. You've outgrown your own counting. You sign things you can't read and you pay men who laugh about it on the way home. You want someone to hold the business end so you can go on making your toys."

"They're not toys."

"Everything is a toy until it makes money," Marcia said. "Then it's a business, and a business has a hundred ways to die that have nothing to do with whether the thing works. You know the thing works. You don't know the hundred ways. That's why you're in my warehouse instead of me in your yard." She picked up a slate, glanced at it, set it down. "What do you make in a year, clear, after the silver goes out the door for cloth and coal and wages and the rent and the press and the prize you pretend belongs to Titus Flavius Macer?"

I opened my mouth to give her a number and realized I didn't have one. I had Tyche's number, which was real, but it was the gross, the river of coin in and out, and the question she'd asked - what's left, what's actually yours when the year is done - I could not answer, and she watched me not be able to answer it.

"You don't know," she said. "Of course you don't. You know what came in. A child knows what came in. Sit down."

There was no stool. I stood.

"That was a test," she said. "You failed it. Now I know what I'd be dealing with."

***

I walked out of that first meeting stung, went back to the yard, and told Tyche about the woman who'd asked me a question I couldn't answer. Tyche put down her stylus and said, "She's right, though," and then we spent the rest of the afternoon working out the answer to the question, what was actually mine when the year was done, and it took us both until the lamp. The number, when we had it, was smaller than I'd have guessed, and the smallness of it was the most useful reckoning anyone had done for me in months.

I went back with the number written down, put the tablet on her table, and said, "There. That's what's left. Now tell me what you'd do that I'm not doing."

Marcia read it slow, going back twice. Then she told me, without softening any of it, exactly where I was bleeding. The lease that could take the shed. The cloth contract where I bought by the bolt and paid by weight and the bolts had been getting heavier with damp on delivery and I'd never thought to check. The cartage I paid by the trip when the volume said I should pay a man by the month and own the carts. The prize money that went out as a clean gift and could have gone out as a lever of obligation. She had the whole accounting of my enterprise mapped in the time it took me to drink a cup of her watered wine, which was good wine, better than I served.

"You're frightened of the law," she said, "because you don't have it a citizen has it from the cradle. So you sign to make the fright stop. Don't. The law is a tool. It's a worse tool for a freedman than for a senator, but it's still a tool, and a freedwoman's learned to use a worse tool than yours and eat well." She tapped the tablet. "I'll take it on. The books, the contracts, the suppliers, the side of it you handle badly because you handle people who can hurt you badly. I'll be a fool for any man with a stripe on his tunic so they go on underestimating me, and I'll take them apart at the warehouse where there's no one to perform for. You'll go on making your steel and your powder and whatever else, and you'll show me everything, because I won't manage a business whose insides I'm not allowed to see." She stood, moved to the doorway, glanced down at the loading floor a moment, then turned back. "I'll take it on. But I want a share."

"A share," she said. "A wage means you can stop me - tell me I'm done and hand me my coin and watch me walk out the gate with everything I know in my head and nothing of yours in my hands. A share means we sink together, which means I fight for the ship. The difference is who I work for. On a wage I work for you. On a share I work for the enterprise, which is mine too, and I have reasons of my own to keep it alive."

I had come looking to hire a clerk. I stood in her dry warehouse and understood I was being offered something I hadn't known to want, and that the offering was on her terms, which she'd worked out before I came in the door, because Heras had told her about me days ago and she'd had time to decide what I was worth to her.

"Why," I said. "Heras said you'd have reasons. You don't need me. Caepio's runs. You eat well. Why take on a foreigner with a strange reputation and more enemies than friends."

She was quiet a moment, and it was the first time her face did anything that wasn't deliberate.

"Because Caepio's is mine until I die and then it's a freedwoman's estate with no man to it and the law and a dozen cousins of my dead husband come for it like dogs," she said. "Because I'm thirty-five and I've spent eleven years making something that the moment I close my eyes goes to people who never swept that floor. Because a widow's standing is a held breath." She looked at me directly. "And because you are building something nobody has the shape of yet - the numbers and the printed sheets and the prize and whatever the powder turns into - and a deep early stake in something like that, before the men with stripes notice it's worth fighting over, is a different proposition from any ordinary investment." She picked up her slate again. "I'm not doing you a kindness. I want in while in is cheap. Now we understand each other."

We did. She was right about everything she could see, and didn't know the one fact that wasn't her business, and I found, standing in the pepper smell, that I trusted that combination.

***

Tyche and Marcia were always going to be a problem, and they were, for about a month, and then they weren't, and I had almost nothing to do with the resolving of it, which is the only reason it worked. Marcia came to the yard to see the books, the real ones, and Tyche put them in front of her with the look of a craftsman handing work to a rival craftsman, daring her to find the flaw. Marcia read them. She read for a long time, the new figures and all, which she didn't know and didn't pretend to, asking short questions, and Tyche answered short, and the two of them circled the table in their separate silences while I tried to be useful and was told twice - once by each of them - to leave it.

What broke it was an error. Marcia found a figure in a cartage account that didn't sit, a sum the carter had been shaving for a year, small enough that it had slid under even Tyche's eye because it was hidden in a tariff Tyche had taken on trust, a wharf custom she'd had no reason to question. Marcia laid her finger on it the same flat way she'd laid it on her own mistakes - just the finger, no comment - and Tyche went still, and I braced for the worst, and Tyche said, "Show me how you saw that."

So Marcia showed her. The arithmetic Tyche had cold. What Marcia had that Tyche didn't - eleven years of knowing which customs were robbery dressed as custom, which a person only learns by being robbed and surviving it - she laid out plainly, and Tyche absorbed it and reached for the next column before Marcia had finished, and by the end of the afternoon they had stopped circling and were both bent over the cartage accounts hunting for the rest of it. They found four more. I made myself scarce and let them.

They were never going to be friends in the soft sense. They were two women who'd each made themselves out of nothing in a city that owned women in different ways - Tyche out of a slave's hidden cleverness, Marcia out of a widow's held breath - and each of them recognized in the other what survival had cost and respected it and did not need it cooed over. What they built was an alliance, the kind worth ten friendships, and the terms of it they set between themselves and never told me, which was correct, because it was theirs. I learned later that Marcia paid Tyche, out of her own share, a fee for the figures Tyche taught her, because Marcia did not take instruction for free any more than Tyche took freedom with thanks, and the two of them had worked out a coin price across that table without my knowing, and when I found out I had the sense to say nothing.

Tyche stayed family, apprentice, the keeper of the cipher. Marcia was the partner, the one who held the gates. The women drew the line between those two things themselves, cleaner than I could have, and kept to their own sides of it. Within a season the contracts were clean, the cartage thief was gone, and the shed lease had been renegotiated.

***

Around this time Hermes and I got the first small pump to run. It was a bad pump. A waist-high cylinder of bored iron, a piston that leaked steam at three joints, a wooden valve I'd carved six times before it seated even approximately right. Efficiency, if I'd known how to measure it, was probably under two in a hundred. But when we fed fire under the boiler and I pulled the cord to open the valve, the piston moved. Water ran up the pipe and out through the spout into the bucket. Hermes watched it, said nothing for a while, then said, "It keeps stopping."

"It'll stop less," I said. "Keep it running for an hour and see what it does."

He kept it running for three hours. It stopped, he bled the joint, started it again. He was not impressed. But a flooded mine gallery that a hundred men couldn't keep dry - this pump, even this pump, could work on that problem all day without sleep. I wrote it down in the cipher that night, the dimensions and the valve cut and the part I still couldn't solve, which was making it stop doing the piston-jam on the fourth stroke. I wrote: this is the Newcomen insight - the steam pushes; the pressure does not have to hold between strokes.

I also wrote down, in a second column, a different kind of account. I had been eating Roman food for ten years and I was tired, privately, of the gap between what I knew existed and what was on my plate. A red fruit that grew on vines in the western land. A starchy tuber that could feed an army. A brown seed pod, bitter, that you ground and mixed with honey and drank hot. I drew rough pictures from memory and under each one wrote what sailors should bring back: seeds, cuttings, specimens packed in sand, everything plant-like that grew in quantity and looked like food. I added one line in Latin, so anyone could read it: any man crossing the ocean must carry citrus, lemons or oranges or whatever grows with acid in it, because a sailor who cannot hold a rope cannot work a sail. I'd seen enough of Magellan in the eighth grade to know what scurvy looked like at the end. Then I went back to the cipher for the parts I didn't want anyone to read.

***

We married a little over a year after the day I failed her test, in the spring, by which point we had been running the business together long enough that the marriage was the smaller change of the two. It was Marcia who said the word first, of course it was, because she said the hard words first about everything.

"We should marry," she said. We were at her table, the strongbox open between us, doing the year's reckoning. "Hear me before you make a face. You're a citizen. You can marry. I'm a freedwoman and so it's a marriage the law allows, freedman to freedwoman, no stain either of us can give the other we don't already carry. We've been partners a year." She paused and squared the tablet she'd been working on. "Married, your enterprise and my warehouse are one estate, and the law that comes for a widow's goods can't come the same way for a wife's, and if there are children they're freeborn, both of us free, and freeborn is a different word in this city than what either of us started as. Their word weighs full. They can hold office, theirs can, if they're clever and we're careful. I've watched you go strange in the face every time someone calls you the emperor's man and I know you're alone in a way I can't put my finger on. I'm not going to put my finger on it. That's yours. But I'd rather be alone next to you than alone in this warehouse waiting for my husband's cousins." A beat, the only concession to sentiment she permitted herself: "I like you, which is more than most people who marry can say. Well?"

I had been asked to do a great many things in Rome by a great many people who had power over me. This was the first time in years someone offered me a choice and let it be mine to take or not, laid out plain, no leash on it. I sat in her warehouse with the pepper smell and the open strongbox and a woman who'd told me she liked me as the fifth reason on a list of better ones, and I understood that the better ones were why I could believe the last one.

"Yes," I said.

"Don't," she said, "look at me like that. We're doing the year's reckoning. We'll be sentimental on our own time, if we turn out to have any in us, which I doubt and don't mind." She put her hand flat on the table near mine - near, not on - the ink-stained right hand, and left it there while she found the next slate.

***

We married without much ceremony, which suited us both. There is a form for it, the words, the joining of right hands, a few witnesses, and we did the form because the form is what makes the law hold. Heras stood as witness and looked insufferably satisfied with himself the entire time. Pamphilus and his wife came. Pamphilus, when it was done, looked at me and said, "You done good," which was a complete sentence for him, and he meant it, and I did not laugh. Tyche stood at the back with her arms folded and afterward said to Marcia, not to me, "He's careless with money but he's not a liar, watch the first and trust the second," which Marcia told me later was the most useful wedding gift she'd ever been given.

Marcia kept Caepio's. She did not fold it into me and vanish into a wife. She kept her own warehouse, her own keys, her own slaves whom she worked hard and freed promptly and who stayed on wages after. She ran her end and I ran mine and we ran the joined middle together, and she went on being a fool for any man with a stripe and counting the spoons after. She had her own designs on what we were building, designs that ran out past me, and she let me see the edges of them. She wanted the printed sheets sold by the thousand. She wanted the prize to grow teeth, to bind the men who won it, to become something the enterprise owned rather than gave away. She wanted the enterprise built to outlive me, she said so, doing sums, in the flat voice she used for everything that mattered - not a strongbox locked to a single name, but a structure with a shape and people inside it who couldn't afford to let it fall. She finished her column and went on to the next.

***

The night after the wedding she came to the yard, because the yard was where the year's work was and she wanted to see the powder figures before she'd let her name be joined to a man who'd lost one hand off a workman already. I showed her the corned powder and the cannon drawings and Hermes's failed steel, the brittle batch beside the good one, indistinguishable to the eye, the whole stubborn wall of it. She asked the cost of each thing and the chance it would ever pay and whether the imperial house could take it from us if it worked, and I answered all of it, even the parts that frightened me, because she'd married into the frightening parts and had the right.

At the end she picked up the little book, the one in the cipher, with the dead glass folded in the cloth, and she looked at the black glass once, hard, the same measuring look she'd given me in the warehouse, and at the marks that were not Latin or Greek or the figures, and she did not ask. She set it back down exactly where it had been, squared to the edge of the table.

"Tyche reads that," she said.

"Tyche reads that."

"Good," Marcia said. "Then if you die with your head full there's one who can read it down and one who can sell it, and between us the work lives." She blew out the second lamp, the one we didn't need, because she did not waste oil any more than she wasted words or trust, and in the dark of the tally-room with the river going by outside and the cranes quiet she put her ink-stained hand over mine on the closed book, flat, the whole hand this time, and left it there.
