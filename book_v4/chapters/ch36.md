# Chapter 36: The Ocean Gambit

I broke his province in eight months, and the breaking of it bought me the door I had spent a winter pretending not to want.

Lugdunensis, in Gaul: a fat tidy province with a fat tidy lie running under its grain figures. I ran the accounts both ways, the Roman wall and the new stair side by side, and where they disagreed I marked it and went no further. Three places. Two were a clerk skimming cartage. The third was older and ran up into a name I did not write down, and I left a clean gap in the column where a stroke had gone missing across nine years, and let the gap do the accusing.

I did not see Hadrian for the better part of a year. He was teaching me something about patience. I let him. The dull foreigner in the back room who had ruined a great deal of wax, that was the part I had asked to play, and it had been honest when I asked it and a strategy inside the month. The door was open now. What I wanted was past it. I had carried it twenty-three years.

---

There was a woman from the western shore living in my compound. She had come back with the Gaulish captain Bituitus's second probe, the one I had quietly funded three years before the audience with Hadrian and told no one about, four ships out of the Tagus mouth following the trade-wind line I had written in the cipher and given to the pilot under my seal. She was somewhere past thirty, and she sat in the tally-room most mornings watching Tyche work the figures and had learned to count in the new marks faster than any Roman clerk I had ever tested. Her name was something I could not hold in my Latin mouth and she had accepted, without enthusiasm, that we called her Illa. She had two words for the shore she came from: the first meant something like "the people's land" and the second was the name of her own kin-group, which I did not repeat because it was theirs.

She knew there were ships. She knew there were more people like the Gauls than she had seen in her whole life. She did not know what any of it meant and neither did I, not for her, and we did not speak of it directly because we had not yet settled between us whether we were friends or something else. I have set it down here plainly, in the script she cannot read, so that it is on the record even if it is on no other record. When I walked into Hadrian's house to tell him the western shore was uninhabited, I was walking from a compound where a woman from that shore sat counting my figures. I knew it. I made a choice.

---

The summons, when it came, came badly dressed. High summer, the city stewing, a clerk arrived at the wharf in the worst hour of the afternoon with an escort of two and the manner of a man told he is carrying something but not what. The tablet wanted the accounts of a second province in the marks, Belgica this time, at the house I knew. More wax and more clerks and the same back room, said the tablet, and I knew it was none of those things, because a summons that is only more of the same does not come with an escort.

Marcia read the tablet twice and set it down flat. "He has decided what you are for. Or he is about to, and wants to look at you while he does it. You will give him Belgica." I had Celer's strap on the bench and had been working the buckle without knowing. "He had a year to be bored by how well you keep your head down. A man like that gets suspicious of a quiet man for the same reason he gets suspicious of a loud one. He thinks the quiet is for something." She slid the strap away from me. "Is it for something."

I did not answer her, which was an answer, and she let it be one. That night I went down to the press-room where Tyche kept the second key, and we took the box out from where I am not allowed to know it lives now, and I unrolled the map for the first time in years.

It had been recopied twice, the linen gone soft. The Mediterranean was honest. Africa was a guess that ran too narrow to a point I had no right to. India was small and wrong. And across the western edge, past a blank I had drawn wide on purpose, the two continents sat where a seventeen-year-old had put them from the memory of a screen, badly, unmistakably, a long broken shore curving away into ice and into nothing, a strait at the waist I could not have placed within five hundred miles and had drawn anyway because I knew it was there.

Tyche held the lamp. She looked at the western shore for a long time, and then she set the lamp down on the bench. "You are going to point him at your house," she said, in the cipher, so the words could not be in the room in Latin.

"I'm going to point him at the ocean."

"Your house is on the other side of the ocean." She rolled it for me, careful of the soft linen, and put it in my hand. "Twenty-three years making sure no one could follow you home. Now you are going to draw the road."

She picked the lamp back up and went ahead to unlock the gate. I stood in the dark a moment with the map in my hands, then followed.

---

The house on the slope was the same and the man in it had aged barely and all at once, gray come into the beard at the jaw, the restlessness banked down into something heavier. He was past forty-five now and I past forty, and we had both been emperor and barbarian for a year and knew the parts. He let me get the bow done before he spoke.

"Belgica," he said. "Sit where the light is. You found me a thief in Gaul."

"I found Caesar a gap in a column. Caesar found the thief."

"You left the gap exactly the width of a thief." He almost smiled. "You did not write the name. You will tell me you did not know it."

"I knew the name, Caesar. I am a freedman who counts. The naming of great men is for great men."

He waved Belgica's tablets to a corner where they sat untouched the whole afternoon. "Then a matter crossed my table I cannot forget," he said, coming around the table, the forge-heat of his attention full on. "A man copying your Gaul accounts said the foreigner once drew a map of the whole world on a wax tablet, before he could speak a word of Latin, to save his neck. Was it true."

So the door had opened itself. I felt the whole winter of work tip and start to slide, and made myself slow.

"It was true, Caesar. A frightened boy with a stylus, drawing badly, to seem worth keeping alive. The magistrate copied it onto a leaf, and I have regretted some of what I drew ever since."

"Regretted." The word caught him. He collected the things other men regretted, because a regret is a door left ajar. "Show me the man's map. You have had twenty-three years to get it less wrong. Show me the world."

---

A whole winter of work had assumed I would pry his hand open, and instead he had put the map in my hand and asked, and the danger in being asked is that you answer fast. I drew it slowly, the Mediterranean first, because everything else had to ride on the part he could check.

He checked it. He put his own knowing against my coast, Spain where he was born, the gulf of Gaul, the boot, Greece, the long African shore his grain fleets ran, and found it righter than the maps in his own library, and his breath changed. He straightened. Then Egypt, the Red Sea, Arabia, on east, and here I stayed honest, told him India was a guess and likely wrong, that I did not know where the eastern lands stopped and would not pretend.

Silence. His eyes on the eastern edge.

"And the war the dead emperor spent himself on lay there." He set two fingers on the rivers, on the cities he had given back. "You served it. You buried men there for a province I struck off the rolls in a morning." A trap and an honesty at once.

"I did, Caesar." Celer's road was under those two fingers. I did not let it into my face. "I buried a friend there, holding a road to a city you were right to let go. I have thought about which way the error ran. It did not run in the letting go."

He took his fingers off the map. Something in him settled, because I had handed him, without being asked, the admission he most wanted and could not be seen to ask for. I had not planned to spend Celer there. I spent him.

"Go on," he said, lower. "You stopped at the edge. There is more of the leaf."

"There is the ocean, Caesar."

"Homer's Ocean. The river that runs round the world," he said, a scholar's word.

"A width, Caesar. Water with a far side." And I drew it, the wide blank I had always drawn, and then across it, slow, the long broken shore, north into ice, south into ice, the strait at the waist, the two great unnamed lands where no Roman map had ever put a mark but the word *Oceanus* and a drawing of waves.

The room went quiet. He bent over the western shore, and when he straightened his face had hunger in it.

---

"Land," he said.

"Land. A man's height of guess in every line of it, Caesar. There is a far shore. I do not know its true distance, nor its true shape, nor whether the strait is one strait or fifty. A boy drew the bones of it and a man has not improved them much." I gave him the doubt straight. "What I know is that it is there." I paused. "Uninhabited, as far as I have been able to learn." The word went into the room. I did not look at the door. "Rome has reached east until the east threw her off, south until the sand stopped her, north until the cold and the trees stopped her, and the one direction she has never put a keel into is west, into the open sea, where the world's true size is written and no one has read it."

"Because no one comes back," he said. "Sailors who go west of the Pillars come back along the coast or do not come back. You cannot crawl along a shore that is not there."

"No, Caesar. You cannot." The turn, the one Marcia and I had built and rebuilt, the one that had to be his idea inside the hour. "Which is why no one will ever go for plunder. There is nothing out there a man would cross a year of open water to steal that he could not steal closer. A general would be a fool to want it. But you have just sold three provinces. You are the emperor who counts. The one prize out there worth a freedman's afternoon is the true shape of the world, measured, mapped, in Rome's hand, the first and last time it will ever be done, because there is only one first time, and the man whose reign does it owns it forever, the way Alexander owns the East in men's mouths though the East threw his heirs off inside a lifetime."

I had said it too fast at the end, and I shut my mouth and let the room hold it. Then he did the thing I had watched him do before, taking a thought that had arrived from outside and walking into it until it was his. I let him do it. Watched myself let him.

"You are describing," he said slowly, as if the thought rose in him for the first time, "a project no general could be trusted with and no merchant would pay for, that returns nothing but knowledge. An undertaking only a state can do, and only a certain kind of master of a state would think worth doing." He looked up. "Alexander conquered what he could reach and wept that there was no more. A greater man would have measured the part he could not reach, and not wept." The two fingers scooped the air over the western blank, the gourd-drawing gesture, possession made with the hands. "I would be the man who knew the shape of the world."

"Caesar would be that man," I said. Let him be the one who knows.

"Do not agree so quickly. I have told you." But he was already gone into it, pulled under by his own wanting, and I had lost him to the idea. Then he came back, because his hunger had teeth on both ends.

"Thule," he said. I had known it would come and still felt it under the skin.

"You drew the north too." He put his finger above Britain, on the blank where a physician had once said a flat two-syllable word to a boy who could not speak Latin. "There is your country, that writes in marks, that no one has reached and you alone have left. And here, west." The finger swung down to the great shore. "You will tell me Thule is out there, and that this is how you know there is a far side, because you crossed it once and forgot the way. Scouts, the report says. Thulean scouts who know the western water. Tell me about your scouts."

The whole lie stood up in the room and asked to be defended. The way to die in front of this man was to defend something he had already half-seen through. So I gave him a third of it, and held the rest, and watched something settle in him.

"Caesar, I will tell you what I tell no one, since you have read most of it." I let the wax leaf lie between us. "I do not know if any man living has crossed that water. I have said scouts because a frightened man says scouts, the way a frightened man draws a map. What I had, coming here with nothing, was the knowledge that the far shore is there. How I came by it is the one thing I have never made a Roman believe, the same as Thule. Write me down a liar about the road and you will not be wrong. The shore is still there. The marks still cannot be cooked. A man can be a liar about where he is from and a true map still be a true map. Strabo called Pytheas a liar, and Pytheas had still seen the north."

He held my eyes a long time. I had handed him permission to disbelieve the lie, in order to keep the truth, and he was clever enough to feel the trade and take it knowing it was a trade.

"I think you are a liar," he said, without heat, almost with pleasure. "I think there are no scouts and no Thule, and you came from somewhere you will carry to your grave, and I find I do not care, because the map is the map and a man's hidden country is his own affair until it is mine." He tapped the western shore. "But I will keep the matter of where you are from." He meant the ledger, and let me see he meant it. "Now. Tell me why no state has done it, since the why is always the true price."

---

This was the part I could not lie my way past, and did not try, because the wall was real and I had broken myself on it. "Two reasons, Caesar, and the second has beaten me." I drew a hull on the wax, crude. "First, ships. Yours crawl from headland to headland and run for harbor when the sky turns. The open ocean will break them. You would need hulls and rigging Rome does not build, fit to take a far sea for weeks out of sight of land and claw back up into a wind. That is years and money, and it is the easy reason." I set the reed down. "The hard reason is this. A ship out of sight of land does not know where it is. How far north or south, any pilot finds by the sun and the stars; that is old. How far east or west he has come, no man can find. There is no star for it. A fleet could sail a year toward your shore and not know on any day whether the shore was a week ahead or a month, or whether a storm in the night had set them a hundred miles off the line."

He had gone still, bent slightly over the crude hull. Listening in the way of a man learning the price of a thing he has already decided to buy.

"And the solution."

"Is a clock, Caesar." Here I had to be most careful, because the survival law and the truth pointed the same way. "Carry on the ship a perfect clock set to the hour of the home port, and read the true hour of the sun where the ship is. The gap between the two is your westing, every day, how far around the world you have come." I let it sit. "And I must tell Caesar, because his own men will tell him inside a season, that I have tried for years to build a clock that keeps true time standing still in a warm room, and failed. I have a water-clock in my tally-room that a girl corrects against the sun twice a day, because it cannot be trusted past a morning. The instrument the voyage needs is one I cannot make. I know no living man who can."

The room was silent. A hard problem offered honestly, at the size of a reign: a man like this could not leave it alone, would make it his own, and would not blame me when the wall held, because I had warned him.

He stood a long while over the crude hull and the word *clock* scratched in the wax. "So you bring me," he said at last, "a far shore you cannot reach, in ships I do not have, steered by an instrument no man can build." Something in the beard was almost a grin. "It is the most honest thing anyone has tried to sell me in a year."

"It is the truest thing I have, Caesar."

---

He did not launch a fleet. I had not let myself believe he would, and he did not. What he sanctioned, over that summer and into the next year as the orders worked down through the fiscus, was bounded and exact, and I learned it in pieces, from clerks.

A survey first. The world Rome already held was to be mapped properly, the true coasts taken and set down in the new figures to a single scale, beginning with the seas he already sailed, the African shore pushed south of where the grain fleets went, a season at a time, no man sent past where he could come home from. A school of pilots and shipwrights at a yard on the western coast, the location unsettled, to build and try hulls for the outer sea, slowly, at a cost he named and would not pass. And the clock made a standing problem of the reign, with a purse behind it, offered to any craftsman who could keep true time through a month at sea, and I was to write down everything I knew of why it was hard and where I had failed, so the men who came after would not waste their years on my dead ends.

I wrote citrus into the provision list for any ship sailing the outer sea and described the symptoms to the quartermaster without naming the cause: teeth loosening, bleeding gums, a man too weak to hold a line. He wrote it down without comment.

It was a tenth of the weight in my chest, the cautious near edge of it, and it was real. Imperial wax on it. Rome had turned her face toward the water she had always run from.

When he asked whether the pilots should plan for establishing settlements on the far shore, something went through me that I did not name in the room. The unnamed people on that shore, and the ships that would carry whatever was alive in Roman lungs, and the distance between what I knew about that crossing and what I had told him. I thought of Illa counting figures in my tally-room. I said nothing. "A survey first, Caesar," I said. "Let the pilots learn the sea before asking the sea to carry a city."

He accepted it and moved on.

That night, alone at the bench, I wrote a cipher entry. I set out what I had chosen and what the choosing would do. The record, plainly: a state program aimed at a shore that was not empty, run by men who would not know what they carried in their blood or their lungs, sanctioned by an emperor I had convinced by design. I chose this. The cipher said so. I left it there and did not go back to change it.

---

"You will write the why of the clock," Hadrian said, gathering the leaves, mine among them now, the western shore folded into his keeping. "And you will tell no one the part about your scouts, since you have admitted there are none, and I prefer my survey to rest on a true map and not a barbarian's tale. The far shore is mine to find. Thule is yours to keep lying about. We will each have what we want." He paused at the door of his own attention, half gone from me. "You brought it to me and not to a general, which tells me you were afraid of what a general would do with it, which tells me you are afraid of the right things. Go and do Belgica. And send up the clock."

---

They walked me down the slope at dusk, the smell of the city coming up to meet me, fish and lamp-soot and the day's heat off the stones. The lie was loose now, going up the rolls and out to the pilots and into the reign, the uninhabited shore, the year's reachable sail, the country a frightened boy had drawn to stay alive. It might find a hull that could carry it and men willing to sail past the edge of what they knew. Or the yard would rot and the pilots find better wages, and the whole effort come to a line in some clerk's accounts, and the map go into a chest where no one opened it. I had aimed it anyway, at a shore I could not name and a sea I could not cross.

At the wharf the lamp was lit. Illa sat in the tally-room doorway with a wax leaf in her lap, working a column of figures. She did not look up. I did not stop. I went past her and up to where Marcia sat at the table with the Belgica tablets already squared, and she saw what I was carrying before I set it down.

"He took it," she said.

"A tenth of it. A survey. A yard. He would not feed the ocean." I sat. "He kept the map."

"He kept the map." She did not make it a reproach. She made a mark and slid the Belgica accounts across.

I took them up. The first column was cartage, the second was grain, the third was nothing yet, and I set my reed to it and began.
