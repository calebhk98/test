# Chapter 12: To the Danube

They gave me three days to pack a workshop into a wagon, and I spent the first one arguing with Tyche about whether she was coming.

She was not coming. That had been decided over my head, by men who put marks on tablets and never saw the people the marks moved. Pamphilus was coming because Pamphilus was strong and could cut cloth and did not run, which were the three qualities the army wanted in a man and the only three it asked about. Tyche was staying to keep the yard and the cloth-tally. I told her this was because the work in Rome mattered. She looked at me with the expression she used for a sum that didn't balance, and went back to counting bolts. She was sixteen. A war camp was no place for her, and I could not protect her there, and I could barely protect myself.

So it was Pamphilus and me and four bolts of the close-woven linen, two spare mouth-hoops of seasoned ash, a chest of needles and grease and waxed thread loaded onto a mule cart in the gray before dawn, Celer's aide ticking each item off a list, and the dead phone in a wrap of rag inside my tunic where it had ridden against my ribs for three years.

I had never been north of Rome. I had barely been out of the yard. You think you know what a road is. I had spent my whole life on roads, on the interstate with my mother's hands at ten and two, on the cracked asphalt between the hardware store and home. The Via Flaminia changed my mind about that inside the first morning. It ran north out of the city straight as a drawn line, paved in fitted stone worn smooth and slick down the middle where ten generations of wheels had polished it, crowned so the rain ran off, with stone gutters at the edges, and it did not bend for hills. That was what got into me first, the arrogance of it. A modern road bends. It respects the land, takes the easy way because the easy way is cheaper. This road went over. It cut the hill or bridged the valley or ran a causeway across the marsh on piers, and it did this for the better part of a hundred and fifty miles before we even reached the mountains, and then it kept doing it through the mountains.

Every mile there was a stone, waist-high, telling you the miles to Rome and the emperor who'd had that stretch repaired. I read them out of boredom and then couldn't stop, because they were a clock, counting down the distance from the only place in this world I half understood toward a river I had only ever seen as a blue line on a map I'd drawn myself, badly, from memory.

The road was also full. That is the part I can't make anybody who hasn't seen it believe. I had pictured an army as a crowd of men with spears, a mass of helmets. What came up and down the Via Flaminia was a machine, and I was a part going into it, and it ran day and night. Grain carts, mostly. More than I could count, ox-drawn, axle-deep and slow, hauling wheat north to feed men I hadn't met yet. Mule trains of leather and iron and tent-hide. A drove of remounts, hundreds of horses, the smell coming downwind a quarter mile ahead of them. A wagon of nothing but nails. A wagon, I swear to God, of nothing but the lead pipe and bronze fittings to plumb a bath house, going to war. Pay chests under guard. Messengers on the imperial post who did not slow for anyone. And soldiers, marching in their centuries with the easy rolling pace of men who would do twenty miles and then build a town.

I'd spent three years telling myself a quiet story where I was the clever one, the man from the future with the secret edge. And then I rode north for two weeks inside the supply train of Trajan's army on its way to take a kingdom, and the story got very small. These people could not make a lightbulb. They thought sickness was bad air. They had no zero until I gave them one and they mostly still didn't want it. And they had organized the feeding and moving and arming of tens of thousands of men across the spine of a continent with a precision I could not have begun to design with a pencil and a year to do it. It ran on tallies and stages and depots and men who knew their jobs and a road that did not bend for hills. It ran on the assumption, baked into every stone, that Rome would still be here in two hundred years to use the road, and Rome was right about that, and I knew it, and it made the back of my neck cold.

I had been thinking of myself as the future arriving. Riding that road, I started to understand I was a tool being carried to a job, and the hand that held me had been holding tools like me for a long time.

---

The first night out of the depot at the river crossing, I learned the latrine fact, and the latrine fact was where the machine stopped being abstract and started being terrible. I'd assumed an army on the march slept where it stopped. Threw down a blanket, posted a watch, slept. That is what tired people do. This army dug a town. Every single night.

They came off the road in the late afternoon while there was still light, and a man rode ahead and paced out the ground, and then the whole column turned into surveyors and ditch-diggers. They squared off a rectangle the size of a small city, dug a ditch around the whole of it, threw the spoil up into a rampart, and topped it with the sharpened stakes that every legionary carried two of, lashed to his pack, all day, for exactly this. Inside, the streets ran in the same grid every night, the same gate names, the commander's tent in the same place, so that a man could be dropped blind into any marching camp from Britain to Syria and find the latrine in the dark. And in the morning they filled the ditch back in, pulled the stakes, and marched, and did it all again at the next stop. Twenty miles and a fort, twenty miles and a fort, the length of the world.

I stood at the edge of the ditch the first evening, useless, holding a mule's lead, watching eight thousand men build a fortified town in about three hours as a routine chore they were too bored by to talk much during, and I thought about my four months of work to make a bag of hot air stay up for two hundred breaths.

But underneath that, the latrines. They dug them in the same place in the grid every night, downstream, downhill, a long trench with a rail. Roman soldiers, by and large, are cleaner than the people back in the city. They wash. They bathe when they can. The camp had rules about where you went and where you didn't, and the rules were enforced.

And the camp was sick anyway.

I noticed it gradually, a sound that had been going a while before I heard it. The flux. The bloody flux, they called it, or just the camp-sickness, the thing that emptied a man out from the inside until he was a husk and then, often enough, until he was a body. I started counting, because counting is what I do when I'm scared, and the count was bad. We hadn't seen a Dacian yet. Men were dying every few days, carried out in the morning of nothing but their own guts turning to water, and the soldiers spoke of it as weather, an annoyance, a tax, the cost of being on campaign.

An optio I shared a fire with one night, a leathery man named Saturninus who had been on three campaigns, told me without my asking - when I asked why the man in the next tent had been carried out - that the flux took more of them than the enemy ever did. Said it as flat as if telling me the river was high. Every campaign. You expected to lose a man to a sword if you were unlucky and ten to your own bowels if you were not.

I sat there with my posca going sour in a wooden cup and reached for what I knew and came up holding most of nothing. It was the water. I was nearly sure it was the water, below all those latrines and all those animals and all those men, drawn because it was there and it was wet. I knew, cold and certain and useless, that if you boiled every cup that went into every man in that camp you would empty those morning carts. And here was an army of tens of thousands drinking from a fouled stream because no one had ever made them stop, and I had no standing to make anyone do anything, because I was a slave on loan, a foreign witch-cloth curiosity who made the fire fly. The day I stood up in a war camp and told centurions their men were dying because tiny invisible creatures were swimming in the water and climbing inside them, I would be either laughed at or flogged, probably both.

So I did the modern thing, the only thing I had. I filed it. Later. When you matter more. When someone has to listen. You will come back to the water. I boiled my own water and Pamphilus's, and we did not get sick, and I told myself that proved nothing, because I'd taught myself that it proved nothing, and I drank my clean water next to men drinking the bad, and I went on north.

The camp surgeon came by my fire that same week. He was cleaning an arm wound on a soldier - pouring wine directly onto the open flesh, one slow trickle from a clay cup. I watched him do it and didn't say anything for a moment. Then I asked him how strong the wine was. He looked at me. "Strong," he said. "That is the point. The raw wound wants the sharpest wine."

I thought about the stills I hadn't built yet, the concentrated distillate I knew could be made from wine if you boiled it through a closed vessel and caught what came out the other end. That would be sharper. Much sharper. Sharp enough, I was fairly sure, to do something real to a wound before the fever took hold. I had nothing to offer the surgeon that was better than what he held, not yet, and he would not have believed me anyway, and the soldier was beginning to make a sound that ended the conversation. I filed that one too.

---

We came down to the Danube in the third week, and the first thing I saw was the bridge, the beginning of it, and the man building it.

The Danube there was wide and gray-brown and fast, a real river, the kind that has drowned armies, the kind the Romans had crossed before only on boats lashed into a bobbing road that the spring floods could tear apart. And someone had decided to put a permanent crossing across it, stone piers standing up out of moving water, and over them a roadway, and over that an army and its grain carts and its bath-house pipes, and the someone was standing on a raft of timber in the middle of the current with his sleeves pushed up, shouting at a crew working something I recognized and had never seen.

A cofferdam. I didn't have the word for it then in any language, but I knew what it was the second I saw it, because I'd watched a video once of how the Romans built bridges in rivers, and here was the actual thing, men driving a ring of timber piles into the riverbed with a pile driver, a big iron-shod weight hauled up on ropes through a frame and dropped, hauled up and dropped, a slow brutal hammering, to make a wall, to pump the river out of the inside of the wall, to lay stone on the dry ground at the bottom of a river.

I stood on the bank and forgot, for a while, that I had a job. Pamphilus said my name twice before I moved.

---

His name was Apollodorus, and I'd heard it on the march without it meaning anything. Apollodorus of Damascus. The emperor's engineer.

I knew there was a man in the river building the impossible with his hands and a thousand other hands, and I felt, before any reasoning confirmed it, that he was the first person I had met in three years who lived where I lived. Who saw a problem and saw the answer and then ground his life up making it real against the resistance of the actual world. I had been so lonely in that specific way that I hadn't had a word for the loneliness, and then I saw him drop a pile into a river and I almost wanted to cry, which would have been humiliating, so I didn't.

Celer took me to him the next day, because Celer was practical and the engineer outranked everyone in the matter of the river and a tribune does not waste a tool's first meeting on ceremony.

Apollodorus was older than I'd thought from the bank, fifties maybe, gone gray and burned dark, with the hands of a man who still picked things up himself, scarred and thick across the knuckles. He had a Greek's quickness in the eyes and a builder's stillness in the body, and when Celer brought me up he did not stop what he was doing, which was reading off a set of figures from a tablet to a clerk, until he'd finished the column. Then he set it down.

"This is the one with the fire bag," he said. His Latin was better than mine, fluent, Greek-accented, fast. "The army's eye." "Yes." He studied me for a while, the way a man reads a load-bearing wall, taking it apart, and I made myself stand still and be taken apart. "Hot air," he said. "You heat the air inside a bag, it weighs less than the air outside, it rises. Like a cup pushed mouth-down into water wants to rise."

"Yes." I was almost dizzy with it. I had explained the principle to a hundred people who heard the words and understood the spectacle and missed the argument entirely, and he had it whole and right in two sentences and had clearly had it before I opened my mouth.

"And it falls when the air cools, which is always, because you cannot carry enough fire." He went to the next tablet. "So it is tethered. A rope and a high point. You look down on the enemy from your high point and count his men and his rafts." He said it flatly, describing a known machine. Then: "It is a clever toy that does one thing, and the one thing is worth doing, which is more than most clever toys."

I didn't know whether I'd been praised or filed under toy. I think he meant both, exactly and without contradiction.

"Yours doesn't move," I said, before I could stop myself, nodding at the river. "Mine goes where the wind goes. That's the whole problem with it."

The corner of his mouth shifted. The ghost of where a smile would be on a man who didn't spend them.

"Mine moves," he said. "Constantly. The river takes a foot of my work every day I don't beat it back. You think a thing in the ground is still." He turned and looked at his cofferdam, at the river shouldering against the timber. "Nothing is still. The water wants in through every gap in the wall faster than the men can bail it, and the wall wants to spread, and the piles want to walk in the mud. I am holding back the whole weight of a river with sticks and the time it takes to lay a stone, every hour, and the day I stop holding, the river has it back." He turned to me again. "You and your wind. At least your enemy is honest. The wind doesn't pretend to be your friend the morning before it takes everything."

He was not going to make it easy. I knew that before I'd finished hearing it.

---

We did not become friends. I told myself a hopeful story on the bank and the man corrected it within a week. He was proud the way a wall is plumb, all the way down, no give in it. He knew exactly how much he was worth and was not interested in being modest about it for the comfort of a tribune's pet foreigner. When I tried, the third day, to suggest something about his pile driver, a counterweight notion I half-remembered, he heard me out with a patience that was worse than rudeness and then explained, precisely, why what I'd described would not work in moving water with the timber and rope he actually had, and he was right, and I had been guessing, and he let me see that he knew. I went away with my face hot.

But the next morning he came and found me at the yard where we were setting up the balloons, which he had no reason to do, and stood watching Pamphilus stitch a seam, then asked me, abruptly, with no warm-up, what the bag did in wind. He wanted the number, not the courtesy. I told him the truth, which was that I didn't have a good number, that in a real wind the tethered bag leaned and pulled and the rope sang, and that what looked clean over a slaughter-lot in Rome on a still day was a meaner animal here on the river, where the wind came down the water in shoves and the cold thinned my fire's bite and the bag came down faster and fought me the whole time. He listened to all of it. Then he said the truest thing anyone had said to me about my own work.

"A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day," he said. "The job is the thing you can do on a bad one." And he went back to his river.

I wrote it down that night, in the books, in English, where no one could read it but me. A Greek bridge-builder gave it to me on a cold bank of the Danube while men died of bad water two hundred yards upstream, and he never knew he'd given me anything, and I never told him.

There was one afternoon, in the middle of that week, when I got a look at the load calculations he kept on a set of tablets - the weight distribution across the pier caps, the spacing. He left them on a crate while he went to argue with someone about timber quality. I'm not proud of this. I looked at the figures and saw where, if you ran the load through triangulated timber frames between the piers instead of simple horizontal beams, you'd pick up a third again in span before flex became a problem. I made a sketch in the margin of my own notes, in English, with numbers. Then I waited a day and mentioned it sideways, through Celer, framed as a question about an old bridge I'd seen that used angled bracing. Apollodorus sent back three words via the same aide: he knew about triangulated trusses. He had made his decision for other reasons. End of discussion.

He was right that I didn't know his reasons. He may have been right about the decision. The balloons, on the bad day, were everything he'd said and worse.

Celer wanted observation, and observation was the safe use, the use that didn't put a Naso in a basket over a fire, and even the safe use was a fight. We picked a rise back from the river where the army's outriders had pushed the Dacian scouts off, a bald cold hill with a view north into the broken green country where Decebalus's people were, somewhere, watching us watch them. The first morning the wind was wrong and we got nothing up at all. The bag filled half-heartedly in the cold, leaned, and lay down on the grass like a sick animal, and the soldiers detailed to help me looked at the witch-cloth and at each other, and I heard the word for fraud and pretended I hadn't. The second morning was still, and it worked, and that was almost worse, because it taught the army to expect it.

We got the big observation bag up on a single heavy tether to maybe a hundred and twenty feet, no basket, no man, just the bag and a long line and, lashed below the mouth on a light frame, a set of bright-dyed cloth strips I could haul on a second line to signal down, since no one could shout that high. From up there on a clear cold morning a man with good eyes could see over the first ridge into the dead ground the Dacians used, count fires, see a column moving on a track we couldn't see from the hill. Celer stood at the base of the tether with his head back and his recruit reading the strips and a clerk taking it down, and for an hour the bag did the one thing it could do, and Celer got his count, and a patrol went out that afternoon to a place it would not otherwise have known to go.

It worked, on a still morning, on a good day, exactly as Apollodorus had warned me, and the days here were not still, and the men I'd taught to haul the line were not Pamphilus, and the cold ate my fire, and the wind when it came took the bag sideways so hard one afternoon that the tether stake pulled and we lost forty feet of altitude in a breath and very nearly lost the bag entirely over the lines of the camp, where a flying fire-bag coming down on the tents would have been the end of me, contract or no contract.

Pamphilus and three swearing soldiers got it down. One of them grabbed the tether line with both fists and planted his heels and wouldn't let go even when the rope burned his palms, and when they finally had the bag staked triple he sat down in the mud and said something short and filthy and specific about the bag's ancestry that made the other two laugh. We staked it triple after that. I stood in the cold mud with my hands shaking and understood that I had built a tool that worked on Tuesdays, and that an army does not get to choose to fight only on Tuesdays.

There was something else that week. One of the men in the tent cluster next to mine had been off duty for three days with loose teeth and bleeding at the gums, his skin going a bad color around the jaw. I stood at the entrance to his tent and the word "scurvy" came out of my mouth before I'd thought it, quietly enough that only Pamphilus heard. Then I stopped. I'd always associated it with sailors - long sea voyages, no fresh food. But we were on land. Then again, we'd been on land for weeks eating camp rations, mostly grain and salt meat, no fruit, no green anything. I stood there second-guessing myself until I decided that if I was wrong the cost was some citrus rind and the man would be no worse off than he was already. I told the cook the soldier needed the sour rinds from the lemons the officers kept for the posca, told him it was a physician's treatment from the east and to give the man as many as he'd eat for five days. The cook gave me the look everyone gives me before they decide to do it anyway, because I'm strange and probably harmless and occasionally right. The soldier's gums stopped bleeding by the fourth day. I was more surprised than the cook.

And underneath all of it, every hour, in Celer's eyes and the aide's tablet and the last letter from Macer that had been read to me, was the other pressure. They had their eye in the sky now, tethered and safe. And it was not enough. The whole machine of the army had decided that a man who could see down from a hundred and twenty feet was worth a fortune, and a man who could be carried up and over and look down into the king's own hills was worth a kingdom. Naso's ruined hand was three weeks behind me on the road and already, I could feel it, being counted as a price and not a verdict.

---

On the last clear evening before the weather turned, I went up to the bald hill alone while there was still light, and stood at the staked-down tether, and looked north at the country we were going to take.

It was beautiful, which I hadn't expected. Forested ridges going blue in the distance, woodsmoke from villages of people who did not yet know how thoroughly the machine on this side of the river was going to arrive in their valleys. Somewhere out there a king named Decebalus was deciding how many of his people to spend, and on this side a road crawled north a fort at a time, and in the river to my left a Greek held back the whole weight of the water with sticks and time, and I stood between them in borrowed cloth, a slave on a string, with the dead glass of my old life against my ribs and a bag of hot air leaning in the wind above me.

Down at the camp they were carrying out the evening's dead, two of them, both from the flux, neither from a sword.

I held the rope.
