# Chapter 46: Hadrian Dies

The news came to me at my own bench, in my own city, on a day I had spent on a dispute about the price of pearwood.

A carter put his head in at the wharf gate in the middle of the afternoon, a man I bought river-mud bricks from twice a year, and said the emperor was dead at last, flat and neighborly, a bridge down on the Tibur road, a trouble that would make my morning longer and was no concern of his beyond that. *At last.* The two words carried the weight of a long year's waiting. Everyone had known for twelve months that the man was dying. The only news was that the dying had finished.

I thanked him and went back to the pearwood. The first hour held. I went on with the pearwood because a block-cutter named Vitalis had ruined a face of Greek setting it wet against the grain, and the society that owned the press would dock the cost of the board against the quarter's rents whether an emperor lived or died, and the figure did not change. So I stood in the press-room with the boy and we worked out what the board had cost and what his next month would cost him for the waste of it, and the marks I drew were the same marks I would have drawn yesterday, and the press behind us went on biting its slow sheets because I had built it years ago to not need me. On the day it mattered, it did not need me.

---

The dying had been a long public business, the kind the city follows with the attention it gives a chariot team, knowing the horses by name and laying money on the outcome. The man had swollen with the water-sickness, the dropsy, the body filling with the fluid it cannot pass, the legs going and then the belly and then, they said, the lungs, so that toward the end he could not lie flat for drowning in himself by inches in his own bed. He had gone down to Baiae for the warm shore and the soft air and the baths the rich go to die at, and he had not got better, because there is no getting better from that, and a man as clever as he was had known there was no getting better, and the part the city told and retold with the relish it keeps for the great brought low was that he had wanted to die and been kept from it.

He had asked for the knife and been refused. He had asked a physician for a poison and the physician had killed himself rather than hand it to Caesar, which the porticoes thought either the noblest or the stupidest thing a Greek ever did, depending on the portico. He had bared his own breast to a hunting-servant who knew the killing spot, a Goth or a Jazyge, some northern man kept for the boar, and the man had bolted. A philosopher would have told you a god does not get to choose his hour. The man himself, I think, would have told you that an emperor who could order the death of any man in the world had discovered the one death he could not order was his own, and would have written an epigram on it, because he wrote epigrams on everything, and one of his, four small lines to his own soul, his little wandering pleasant soul going off now into the cold and the dark, was already going round the city in two translations before the body was cold. Little soul, little wanderer, the guest and companion of the body. I heard it from a wine-seller at the head of the wharf lane.

It reached me clean and slow and entire, in other words. A death long expected, in a known place, of a known sickness, told by men with nothing at stake in the telling, arriving at the end of a known road. There was nothing in it for me to be afraid of, and I went looking, pressing at the bruise of it, for the fear, and it was not there.

---

I had spent two decades being careful of that man.

I had built my whole conduct around a single read of him: that he kept a ledger, that he forgave nothing he wrote in it, and that what he wrote down hardest was any morning a man had made him feel small. I governed every audience by it. Show him enough to be worth keeping and never enough to be out-known. Let him take your map and call it his thought. Praise the half-egg dome you privately think will crack and pay to watch it not. I had watched what he did to a better man than me, an old engineer with a plumb-line and no give in him, who would not learn to bend and was exiled for it and died out of Rome of nothing anyone would name.

I walked home along the wharf in the dusk with the stiffness in my hands that had come on me in the last year, the joints slow to open in the cold air, so that I had to plan my grip on the wharf-rail where before I had not planned it. I gripped the rail at the bad footing by the crane-post and stood there composing the careful answer I would give if I were called to the Palatine, the small useful truth and the smaller deference, the chewing motion that goes on after the food is gone. There was no Palatine to be called to. There was a body at Baiae and a will I had not read and a city holding its breath, and I stood at my own gate composing answers to a question no living man was any longer going to ask me, and the grip I had kept on the rail for twenty years went on gripping nothing.

Because under the relief, and it was relief, there was something I had not budgeted for and did not like to look at. He was the only other restless mind I had met in this world. The only one who lived as I lived inside my own head, never landing, always reaching past the matter in front of him for the one behind it, building and counting and unsatisfied. We had circled each other for years as two predators circle, each knowing the other could kill him and each finding the other too interesting to do it yet, and there is a kind of company in being hunted by a man who understands you that you do not get from being loved by men who do not. He had taken my map and made it his and I had let him because letting him was how I lived, and somewhere in the letting there had been something close to the pleasure of being read correctly, the only correct reading in two decades, even though the reader meant me no good. Heras had kept me honest and was dead. Apollodorus could have met me as a peer and was dead, and partly of this man. And now this man was dead, and the count of people in this world who could see me whole, friend or enemy, had gone from one to none, and I stood at the gate where relief and grief were filling the same space and could find no seam between them.

---

The new wind I read in a week, off the men whose living depends on guessing it before it blows. He had named the heir already, months back, in the spring, a thing the city had chewed over and largely approved. A grave, decent senator of middle years, unhurried, rich, well-liked, of no faction anyone feared, a man who had managed a province without scandal and buried two children without theatre and was known chiefly for having never put a foot wrong, which in Rome is a career. Antoninus, they were calling him. The porticoes liked him for the reason they had been uneasy about the last one: he was not interesting. He would not go anywhere. He had no wall he burned to build in the cold north, no Greek shrine he ached to roof, no shore he meant to stand on with the wind off the unknown. He would stay in Rome and in his villas a day's ride from Rome and govern the small house perfectly kept that the last man had always said he wanted and never once stopped traveling long enough to keep. A man who would die, you felt already, in his own bed, of age, having gone nowhere and lost nothing and refused every adventure that came up the road to him.

I did the cold arithmetic Marcia would have done if I had not, and most of it came out warm. My institutions did not care who wore the purple. The press was a printing-branch of a burial-club and ran on rents I had tied to it with a deed no emperor would bother to untie. The contest was Minerva's now, endowed in the goddess's own name, its old enemies converted into its paid keepers, and a stay-at-home pious emperor was if anything a friendlier sky for a prize dedicated to a goddess than the restless cosmopolite had been. The figures were cut in the public stone and could no more be called back than a river. The patronage fund paid Hermes's heirs and paid Felix to fail at the pot for another term of years off settled capital that asked nothing of any living man's favor. I had spent the back half of my life building precisely the institution an emperor's death cannot reach in and take, and on the test day it did not reach in.

All of it came out warm but the one matter, and the one matter was the one that had ever needed a living emperor's whim, and now there was a different living emperor with a different whim, and his whim went nowhere near the sea.

---

The ocean was his. The dead man's. It had been his prestige from the first hour I had threaded the map into his hunger for being the one who knew the shape of the world, and it had lived these seventeen years on exactly that, on a restless emperor's wish to have done what no Caesar had done, the African coast pushed a headland further south each season because he liked to be told it had been, a yard on the Garonne mouth building deep hulls because he liked to think of them, a standing prize for a sea-clock no one had won because the unwon prize itself was a kind of glory, a question Rome had posed that the world had not yet answered. It had never been a war or a colony or a road, a project that paid. It had been one man's long reach past the edge, funded because the man wanted to be the kind of man who reached.

The new man did not want to be that kind of man. He wanted to be the man who balanced the books and mended the roads and did not gamble. I knew, standing at my gate, that within a year or two some procurator going through the dead reign's commitments with a sharp pen and a mandate to stop the bleeding would come to a line that read: a yard in Gaul, building ships that do not return, against a prize no man has claimed, toward a coast no living person has seen, on the word of a barbarian about a map. And the pen would pause, and the procurator would ask whose project it had been, and the answer would be a dead emperor's, which is the answer that ends projects. It would not be killed in a rage. It would lapse. The next season's grant would be smaller and the season after that there would be none, and the hulls would rot on the ways for want of pitch, and Bituitus the yard-master would go back to building grain-ships for men who wanted grain-ships, and the survey would stop at the headland it had reached, and the prize would be quietly let drop, and no one would ever again, in my lifetime, be paid by the empire to ask how far it was to the place I had drawn on a wax tablet to a magistrate forty years ago and regretted drawing the moment the clerk copied it loose.

The road west. The quiet hope I had never quite said out loud even to Marcia, that under the prestige and the survey and the careful talk of knowing the shape of the world, I had wanted there to be a yard, and hulls, and a prize for a clock, because someday a hull might cross, and a hull that could cross to a country I had drawn from memory was the only road I had ever been able to imagine, however far-fetched, to the only country that was mine. I had never believed in it the way you believe in a thing you will do. I had believed in it like a man who holds a key to a house he can no longer find. And the man who had paid for the key without knowing what it unlocked was dead, and the man taking his place would melt the key down for the bronze in it, and I found, standing there, that I did not even have the standing any more to argue for it, because to argue for it I would have to be the dead emperor's marvel again, the Trajan-creature, the Hadrian-creature, and I had spent the last twenty years stripping that name off myself so the regimes could not use it against me, and a stripped name cannot ask a new emperor to keep an old emperor's ships.

So I let it go. Not all at once and not cleanly, but I let it go, and went home to a smaller thing.

What I did not let go of, because I had been careful not to, was the program itself. The survey was his. The prestige-hulls were his. The prize for a sea-clock had lived on his wish to be the kind of man who asked the question, and that wish was buried at Baiae. But the crossings were not his. I had not let them be. The collegium at the Garonne yard was endowed off the fund's own capital, bound to nothing that died with a man — its charter named the ocean, not the emperor, as the reason for it, and its income came off the rents of three paper-mills that had been generating since I first set the press on its block. The merchant investors I had brought in over the last decade had seen the prospectus, and the prospectus was not prestige: it was the tobacco and the rubber and the strange resins from the western landings, things whose value had been proven in the counting-house and not in a poem about Caesar. The rubber had already changed what the steam pumps could do. A man with a sound business interest in what was growing on those western shores would not stop funding hulls because an emperor died. The third hull was finished; a fourth was contracted. I had built it so the throne's withdrawal would hurt and not kill, and on the day the throne withdrew, it hurt and did not kill.

None of that was comfort, exactly. The road west had been my private faith as much as it had been his prestige, and a program that runs on other men's economics is a different faith from the one I had held. But it ran. That was what mattered. It ran without him, as I had meant it to, as I had spent seventeen years ensuring it would, and the third hull would cross whether any emperor knew its name or not.

---

Ulpia was at the storeroom table when I came in, with the lamp drawn close, reading the African survey to herself in the low voice she used for figures, the headlands and the soundings I could no longer take by lamplight. She was twenty that summer, grave the way her cognomen promised, doing a man's reckoning under a borrowed name because the law would let her do it no other way, and she would go on doing it after I could not, and she would teach it to Chloe, who was learning the cipher fast, and Chloe would teach it to someone I would never meet. The script that held the bottom rungs of the ladder lived in four young heads now and a copy cased in lead, and would outlive the next emperor and the one after that and the yard on the Garonne and me. That had been the wager. That was the wager paying.

She heard me and stopped, and asked, not whether I was well, because she does not ask a question she has the answer to. She had got that from her mother. She asked whether the western survey went on now, the African coast, whether she should keep the soundings current or close the column. A practical girl. She had understood, faster than I had wanted her to, that an emperor's death is a thing that closes columns.

I told her to keep it current a while yet. Keep it as it stood, in good marks, true to the last headland, so that whoever opened it after both of us, in whatever year, under whatever sky, would find the coast where we had left it and not have to start the chain again from the river-mouth. I could not pay for one more league of it to be sailed. I could pay for the league already sailed to be kept honest and waiting, against a traveler.

She wrote down what I told her, in my own marks, and ruled it off, and asked me to check the rule, because her mother had taught her never to trust a tired hand, even her own. I told her the figure stood. Then she carried the leaf with the open columns to the end of the table and set it down face-up, square to the edge the way Marcia squared a tally, and weighted the curling corner with a stone loom-weight out of her mother's basket so the draft off the wharf could not take it in the night, and left it lying there in the dark, true to the last headland, a thing not closed, only laid down.
