# Chapter 46: The Day the Grip Went

The news arrived flat, without ceremony, the way most things arrive when they have had long enough to age on the road.

A carter I bought river-mud bricks from twice a year put his head in at the wharf gate on a Tuesday afternoon in July and told me the emperor was dead at Baiae, neighborly and offhand, a bridge blocked on the Tibur road that would make my morning longer and was otherwise no concern of his. I thanked him. He went. I stood in the press-room doorway a moment, and then went back to the pearwood.

Vitalis had set a face of Greek text wet against the grain, the letters swelling and splitting before the impression dried, and the society that ran the press would dock the wasted board from his quarter's rents regardless of who lived or died in the south. We worked out what the board had cost and what his share of the next month would carry, and I drew the marks the same way I would have drawn them the day before, and the press behind us went on biting its slow sheets. I had built it years ago to keep running on other hands. On that afternoon it proved it could.

---

The dying had been a long public business. The city had followed it the way it follows a chariot team through a close season, knowing the horses by name and laying money on the finish. The man had gone down with the water-sickness, the body filling where it could not drain, the legs heavy and then the belly and then, they said, the lungs, so that toward the end he could not lie flat without drowning in himself by inches in his own bed. He had gone to Baiae for the warm shore and the soft baths and had not improved, because there is no improving from that, and he had known it, and the part the city told and retold with the particular relish it keeps for the great brought low was that he had tried to die ahead of schedule and been prevented.

He had asked for the knife and been refused. He had asked a physician for a specific poison and the physician had killed himself rather than hand it to Caesar, which the porticoes called either the noblest or the most pointless thing a Greek had done in recent memory, depending on the portico. He had shown his own bare breast to a northern hunting-servant, a Goth or a Jazyge kept for the boar, who knew the killing spot as any such man does, and the man had bolted from the room. A philosopher would say a god cannot choose his hour. He himself, I think, would have written an epigram on it, and he had already written one, four small lines to his own soul, to his little wandering pleasant soul going off now into the cold and the dark. It was already going round the city in two translations before the body was cold. I heard it from a wine-seller at the head of the wharf lane.

---

I had spent two decades being careful of that man.

My whole conduct around him had rested on a single reading: he kept a ledger, forgot nothing he wrote in it, and what he wrote hardest was any morning a man had made him feel outpaced. I had governed every audience by this. Show enough to be worth keeping and never enough to be out-known. Let him take your map and call it his own thought. Praise the dome you privately believe will crack. I had watched what he did to a better man than me, a Damascene engineer with a plumb-line and no give in him at all, who would not learn to bend and was exiled for it and died out of the city of nothing anyone would name aloud.

The walk home along the wharf in the dusk had a stiffness to it I had noticed for the first time the previous winter. My hands were slow to open in the cold morning air now, the joints planning themselves before I gripped anything, so that I had to think through the action of closing my fingers on a rail where before the hand had simply closed. I gripped the bad footing near the crane-post and stood composing the careful answer I would give if I were called to the Palatine tomorrow, the small useful truth and the still-smaller deference, the chewing motion a man keeps on after the food is gone. There was no Palatine to be called to. There was a body at Baiae, and a will already executed, and a city holding its breath, and I stood at my own gate composing answers to a man no longer living to ask them, and the grip I had kept on the rail for twenty years gripped nothing.

Because under the relief of it, and it was relief, there was a second thing I had not planned for and did not like the look of. He was the only other restless mind I had encountered in this world. The only one who lived as I lived, inside his own head, never landing, always reaching past the matter in front of him for the one further back, building and counting and perpetually unsatisfied. We had circled each other for years, each knowing the other could ruin him, each finding the other too interesting to do it yet, and there is a company in that kind of danger that you do not get from the men who simply want you well. He had taken my map and made it his and I had let him, because letting him was how I survived, and somewhere in the letting there had been something close to the pleasure of being read correctly. The only correct reading in two decades from a man who meant me no particular good. Heras had kept me honest and was dead. Apollodorus might have met me as an equal and was dead, and partly at this man's hand. And now this man was dead, and the count of people in this world who could see me whole, friend or enemy alike, had dropped to none.

I stood at the gate for a while and then went in.

---

The next wind I could read in under a week, off the men whose living depends on guessing it before it blows. He had named his heir months back, in spring, and the city had chewed it over and largely approved. A grave, decent senator of middle years, unhurried, well-liked, of no faction anyone feared. He had managed a province without scandal, buried two children without theatre, and was known chiefly for having never put a foot wrong, which in Rome amounts to a career. Antoninus, they were calling him. The porticoes liked him for the same reason they had been uneasy about the last one: he was not interesting. He would not go anywhere. He had no cold northern wall burning in his mind, no Greek shrine he ached to roof, no shore he meant to stand on with the unknown wind off it. He would stay in Rome and in his villas a day's ride from Rome, and govern the small house perfectly kept that the dead man had always spoken of and never once stopped moving long enough to build.

I did the cold arithmetic Marcia would have done first if I had not. Most of it came out warm. My institutions had been built not to care who wore the purple. The press was a printing-branch of an old burial-collegium now, running on rents I had tied to it with a deed no emperor would bother to undo. The contest was Minerva's, endowed in the goddess's name, its old enemies converted into its paid keepers, and a stay-at-home pious emperor was if anything a friendlier sky for a prize in a goddess's honor than a restless cosmopolite had been. The decimal figures were cut into public stone now and could no more be called back than a river. The patronage fund paid Hermes's heirs and paid Felix to chase the problem for another term of years, off settled capital that needed nothing from any living man's goodwill.

The semaphore line Rome-to-Rhine was a harder read. Hadrian had funded the relay stations directly, and the new man's procurators were already going through the dead reign's commitments with sharp pens and mandates to stop the bleeding. I expected the relay expansion to slow. The line already built would likely hold - the military value had proved itself in three seasons of operation and the army did not give up working tools lightly. But new stations, the extension south, the Adriatic branch: those would wait.

All of the arithmetic came out warm but the one thing, and that one thing was the program that had ever needed a living emperor's appetite for the unknown, and the man taking his place had no such appetite.

---

The ocean had been his. The dead man's. It had lived these seventeen years on exactly that - on a restless emperor's wish to have done what no Caesar had done. The African coast pushed another headland south each season because he liked to be told it had been. A yard at the Garonne mouth building deep hulls because he liked to think of them. A standing prize for a sea-clock no craftsman had won yet, because an unwon prize is a kind of glory too, a question Rome had posed that the world had not answered. It had never been a road or a colony or a proven trade route. It had been one man's long reach past the edge of the map, sustained because the man wanted to be the kind of man who reaches.

The new man had no intention of being that kind of man. Within a year or two I could predict a procurator's pen arriving at a line in the dead reign's accounts that read: a yard in Gaul building ships that do not return, toward a shore no living person has seen, against a prize no man has claimed. And the pen would pause, and someone would ask whose project this was, and the answer would be a dead emperor's, which is the answer that ends projects. It would not be killed in anger. It would lapse. The seasonal grant would shrink the first year and vanish the second, and the survey hulls would go without pitch, and Bituitus the yard-master would go back to building grain-ships for men who wanted grain-ships, and the imperial survey would stop at the last headland it had reached.

That was the imperial program. I had known since Hadrian's first gray year that it would go this way, which was why I had not allowed the whole thing to rest on it.

The crossings were different. Those had not been his, because I had spent seventeen years making sure they could not be taken from me by an emperor's death. The collegium at the Garonne mouth was endowed off the fund's own rents, its charter naming the ocean as its purpose and not any emperor's name. The merchant investors I had brought in over the past decade had seen the prospectus, and the prospectus was not prestige: it was the rubber and the tobacco and the strange resins from the western landings, things whose value had been proven in the counting-house and not in a poem about Caesar. The rubber alone had changed what the steam pumps could do - better gaskets, tighter cylinders, longer intervals between failures. A man with a sound business interest in what grew on those western shores would not stop funding hulls because an emperor's tastes ran to mending roads. The third hull was done. A fourth was contracted. I had built the program to survive the throne's withdrawal: it would hurt without imperial prestige behind it, and the hurt was real, but it would not stop.

And there was the private road west, the one I had carried separately from the program and never quite said aloud even to Marcia. Under the survey and the hull-building and the careful framing around what a cost-counting emperor would value - under all of that had been a quieter thing, the belief I had held lightly for thirty years that if a hull could cross, and cross reliably, and find the coast I had drawn from memory on a wax tablet to a magistrate who could not understand what he was looking at - that somehow, somewhere in that direction, there might be a way back to a country and a time that were mine. A key to a house I could no longer find. I had never held it as a plan. I had held it the way a man holds a stone from a riverbed he will never see again, for the feel of it in the hand, not because he expects to return it.

The man who had paid for the program without knowing what he was really paying for was dead. The man taking his place would fund nothing of the kind. And I found, standing at my gate in the early dark, that the stone had gotten lighter than I remembered, and lighter again, and by the time I went inside it was nearly nothing, and I set it down somewhere in the crossing of the threshold and did not go back for it.

The program ran. That was what mattered. It would run without him, and without my private hoping, as I had spent seventeen years building it to run, and the third hull would cross whether any emperor knew its name or not.

---

Ulpia was at the storeroom table when I came in, the lamp pulled close, reading the western survey aloud to herself in the low voice she used for columns of figures - the headlands and the soundings, the coastal profiles I could no longer read by lamplight without holding the sheet at arm's length. She was twenty that summer, level as her cognomen promised, doing the reckoning that would have required a man's name in front of it if the law had been asked, and she was not asking the law. She would go on doing it after I could no longer, and she would pass what she knew to Chloe, who was taking to the cipher faster than any of them, and Chloe would carry it to someone neither of us would meet. The bottom rungs of the ladder lived in four young heads and a copy cased in lead, and would outlive the next emperor and the yard at the Garonne mouth and me. That had been the wager from the beginning. On that evening it was paying.

She heard me cross the threshold and looked up.

She asked about the western survey - she had got the habit from her mother, of asking only what she did not already know. Whether the survey went on, she said. The African coast, the soundings columns. Whether to keep the headlands current or close the record.

I told her to keep it current. Keep it exactly as it stood, in good marks, true to the last headland, so that whoever opened it after both of us, in whatever year, under whatever reign, would find the coast where we had left it and not have to begin the chain from the river-mouth again. I could not pay for another league of it to be sailed. I could pay for the league already sailed to be held honest and waiting.

She asked whether I wanted Lucanus to take a copy of the survey, in case. I said yes - let him have the current draft, with a note about the longitude gap and the sea-clock prize still open. He would know what to do with a document that needed safe hands and a bit of law around it.

She wrote down what I said, in my marks, and ruled it off, and held the leaf toward me to check the ruling. I told her the figure stood. Then she carried the leaf to the far end of the table and set it down face-up, squaring the edge with the flat of her palm the way Marcia had always squared a tally before she called it done, and she set a stone loom-weight from her mother's basket on the curling corner so the draft off the wharf could not take it in the night.

It lay there in the lamplight, open to the last headland we had reached, nothing closed, only set down.
