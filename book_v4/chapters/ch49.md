# Chapter 49: Securing the Ladder

A man who has spent his life finding the gap a clever cousin could get a hand into does not stop because his eyes have. He only does it slower, and aloud, and with other people's hands.

I had thought, those first dull weeks after Marcia, that the securing was done. The deed was sealed. The press ran. The contest had Minerva's priests for paid watchmen and the patronage fund had its board of three and the books were written nearly to the top of the ladder. I was a passenger. But a passenger can still count, and one night with the old column running behind my eyes I counted the books, where the copies were and were not, and sat up and could not lie back down, because I had built something to outlast me and left its only readers in one city, in heads that would also stop.

So I sent for Ulpia in the morning and told her we were not finished. She had her mother's way of hearing a thing she thought she had already settled, the slight stillness, the held breath. She was two-and-thirty by then and held the books and the deed and the survey and a household forming around her, and she said, in the flat voice that was Marcia's voice come down a generation, "We have three copies and the Latin. You wrote it yourself into the plan."

"I wrote a plan," I said. "A plan is a thing on a leaf. I want to put my hands on each copy before my hands are no use for anything but lying flat on a page."

---

We did it across that winter, the four of us who could read the cipher and I who could no longer see it, in the slack hour after the press shut and before the household ate, in the back of the storeroom where Marcia had finished her deed.

There were the three full copies and the partial Latin, and I made each of them be brought and read to me, a passage at random, so I would know with the only sense I had left that the marks on the leaf were the right marks and not a fair clean copy of a wrong one. Tyche read me a page of the second working, the long division with the remainder drawn as an *r*, and I stopped her on a carry she had set a column wide, and she looked, and it was hers that was right and my ear that was old, and she was quiet on the point in a way that had been consistent for the last decade.

Tyche's copy was the working one, the mother of the rest, and it did not stay in one place. It moved as it had always moved, kept now under the floor of one room and now another, known to her and to Chloe and to no clerk. It was the living text. When a child of the keepers found a clearer Latin word for a rung, or when one of them caught a mistake of mine, the correction went first into Tyche's copy and then out to the others at the year's turn, the measure of the market checked against the temple measure.

The society's copy was sealed. It went into the collegium's chest under the same seal as the deed, lodged in law, a thing the burial-club's own rules forbade them to open except in the presence of three of the board, so that no single keeper's death and no single keeper's treason could lose it or sell it. One of the three must always be a keeper of the cipher; one must be a master of the press. Marcia had taught me that arrangement. I had only to apply it to the deed that mattered most.

The third copy was the one for the dark.

I had Vitalis case it. He was the right hand for it, a press-man with a wood-carver's father in him, and he made the thing the way a man makes what he intends to lose on purpose. The leaves were waxed against the wet and folded into oiled hide and that into a vessel of lead, the seams run with pitch and the whole sealed and weighted, so a man digging a foundation a hundred years on, or three hundred, would strike it and pry it and find inside a fat case of strange marks he could not read and would either burn for a witch's leavings or carry, if the gods were kind, to someone who would sit with it. We argued where to put it. We put it under the cold floor of the press-room itself, beneath the stone the great screw is bedded in, because the press is the one part of all of it I could not see going away. A man may forget a tomb. He does not forget the machine that feeds him, and where a press stands men come back to lay another, and where they dig, a digger who finds something strange will pick it up.

And the partial Latin, up through the geometry, that one I did not hide. That one I let breathe. It went into the printed library off the Argiletum among the codices, a strange treatise by the Thulean, mathematics and the four workings and the figures and the lines and angles, all of it true and all of it tellable, with the cipher's deeper rungs left out of it on purpose. A man who climbed the Latin to the top of the geometry and wanted the next rung would find at the head of the next blank page a note, in plain Latin, that the rest was written and kept and could be sought. The Latin was the open door; its only task was to make a hungry man ask.

---

The honest marks I made them keep in every one, and this was the rule I would not bend on, and the only rule in the whole securing that Tyche enforced harder than I did. A rung I was sure of got no mark. A rung I had built but doubted got the hooked stroke, the little dragging tail off the last letter that meant *the Thulean is guessing here, prove it before you stand on it.* And the rungs I did not have at all - the joining of heat to motion that I had owned once and lost, the far rooms of the planets, the binding of one small piece of a thing to another to make a new thing - those stayed white. Blank, with a note across the white in a hand large enough that even I could nearly see it, that the rung was missing, that I had not faked it, that the climber must build it himself and not look for it here and fall.

Chloe read me one of those blank-page notes, the chemistry one, the year we sealed the copies. She read it fast and clean and without weighing it, and then she stopped, and she said, "You could fill it. Some of it. You could guess and we would never know it was a guess."

"You would," I said. "Tyche taught you the mark. The mark is so you would."

"A future man would not."

"A future man is the whole point," I said. "A faked rung does not break in the room where it is faked. It breaks under the weight of the one who trusted it, a hundred years up, where no one I will ever meet can catch him." I had her read the note again, and the hooked stroke before it, and I made her promise me the thing I had already made Tyche promise and Ulpia promise and would make Vitalis promise, that the blanks stayed blank and the hooks stayed hooked through every copying, world without end, that no kind hand smoothed my doubt into a lie to make the book look finished.

---

Before the copies were sealed I did the one thing I had written into my own securing plan and kept finding reasons to defer, and Zoticus came to the yard on a morning in late winter, carrying the physic chest. He was a careful man, Heras's student and student's student combined in one quiet person, and he had been running the physician-line for eight years already without me in it. I gave him the pages, the full set of the quarantine protocols: how a city should be sealed in sections, how a camp should be watered and gated against the sick, how a physician managing a house epidemic should leave himself an escape from it, the theory of the little ones in plain Latin as far as I had been able to put it. He had most of it already, absorbed from Heras and from the work. But he had not had it as a document, and a document can be copied and sent where a man cannot walk.

"When does it come," he said, and I told him: fifteen years, twenty, from the east, and it would be worse than anything in the living memory, and this was the difference between bad and catastrophic, if men kept doing it even when the theory seemed mad.

He looked at the pages for a long time without answering. Then: "And you will be dead."

"I will be dead." I tapped the papers again. "So will Zoticus, before it passes. Write it into the student-notes. Teach it before they need it. That's the thing."

He folded the papers and tucked them inside the chest lid, picked up the chest, and left. I listened to his sandals on the yard stones until the sound reached the gate and stopped.

I had also written the endowment-note into the deed that week, the one for the Prize for Demonstrated Truth, and I sat with the sealed copy in my hands a long while before I set it down. The prize fund was there, capitalized, its instructions plain: hold it in trust for no fewer than eighty years, or until there are enough claimants to argue the question seriously, whichever comes later. Lucanus had read that clause twice before he wrote it clean, and said only, "That is a longer horizon than most men plan for." I told him I was planning for people not yet born. He had taken that in the same way he took most things from me, a brief pause, a decision not to press, and then the pen moving again.

---

The keepers I confirmed the way you check a rope you mean to hang a life on, hand over hand, end to end.

Tyche was five-and-sixty, and old, and the oldest rung of the chain herself, and the danger I had sat up over was that the chain still bent back to her in too many places. She had told me this herself years ago, *you put its only key in one aging head, mine,* and we had answered it once with the children, Ulpia and Vitalis and Chloe, four young hands for one old one. But four hands in one city in one generation are still a network a fire or a plague or a bad emperor can destroy in a season, and I wanted the chain to run past all of us, down into hands neither of us would live to shake.

So we wrote the chain into the deed. The collegium's bond required that the cipher be taught, always, by a keeper to no fewer than two, that no keeper should be the last to know it, that a keeper who held it and taught it none was in breach. Vitalis was bound already to teach two block-cutters their craft; we bound him to teach two the script besides. Chloe, free now, took a girl of the storeroom and a boy of the press under her, as Heras had once said of her, *keep her in figures,* and she kept them. The chain would not narrow to a point again. It would fan, downward, into the workshop and the press and the trade, into hands that came up through the craft and not through my blood, into a Felix not yet born who would one day read a strange treatise in a portico, want the next rung, and go asking.

Lucanus would not be a keeper. I had long stopped grieving it. He had the law and the basilica and his unbearded citizen face, and he wanted nothing of the workshop or the script, and his line, if he had one, would climb the cleaner ladder that I could never climb, the one whose lowest rung was being born free. I did not begrudge him the door I had been shut out of. The ladder I had built was for whoever was hungry.

---

I sent for Lucanus on the matter of the law, because it was the one part of the securing that wanted his trade and not mine.

He came in his advocate's clothes from the Basilica Julia, eight-and-thirty and stiff with me in the particular way a son is stiff who has reckoned the exact weight of the chain his father's name puts on him. We did not warm to each other in an hour. But he sat at the storeroom table where his mother had drawn her deed, and read the deed of trust through with the eye of a man trained to find the gap, and found two.

He found that the board of three could, by the deed's own wording, be filled by men a future procurator might lean on, and we tightened it to require always one keeper of the cipher and one master of the press among the three. And he found that the contest's endowment to Minerva, that maneuver of Marcia's that turned the priests into the contest's paid guards, could be undone by a priest who stopped believing the goddess was owed, and we could not tighten that one. Lucanus wrote the gap into the margin of the new wording in his hand, a noted defect that could not be drafted around, and set the pen down.

"The faith clause," he said. "You can bind the land and bind the income, but you can't bind a priest to believe."

"No," I said. "You can't draft faith."

He drew the new wording himself, in the fine Latin he had from his years at the bar, and his hand on the leaf was a freeborn citizen's hand setting in law a thing his freedman father had built, and neither of us said anything about that either. Before he rolled it he asked me where the share of the ring was to go, the gold Macer left me, the worth of it. I told him to put the worth of the ring into the patronage fund, into Felix's failing pot, so that a dead contractor's gold would pay a free smith to fail at steel for ten more years. Macer would have called me a sentimental fool, and he would have been right.

The ring itself was not in the deed. The ring was a private matter, and I had not yet settled it, and there was a reckoning I had to make with the ring and the glass beside it that I would make with only one other soul in the world.

I will set down one more thing from that afternoon. Before Lucanus left he stopped at the door and looked back at the table, at the deed, at the storeroom as a whole, and he said, in the easy voice of a man making an observation rather than a judgment, "You built a road that runs all the way through."

I did not say anything, and he went on.

"All tracks lead to Rome," he said. "That's what they tell you at the bar. Everything built in the empire finds its way to Rome eventually, all the roads, all the coin, all the people." He picked up the rolled deed. "You ran it backward. Everything built here finds its way out."

He left. I sat with that a while, in the room where Marcia had drafted the original deed in a hand I could still see in memory, clear as the day.

---

There was one entry in the encyclopedia I could not make complete. I sat with it the night before I sealed the last copies, and the ship I had sent west in 149 was either home or it was not. I had arranged for a messenger to come directly to the yard the day the hull cleared the Tagus. No messenger had come. The program would continue - the fourth hull was under construction at the Garonne mouth, the collegium deed funded it, Bituitus's men knew the route - and someone would eventually know whether the second organized crossing returned, and what it brought back, and what it cost the people on the other shore. I would not be that someone. I wrote it into the last page of the ocean section in the only honest way I could: *The ship departed in the forty-ninth year of Antoninus's reign. I do not know if it returned. Correct this account when you can. The route is proven. The land is inhabited. The rest is someone else's to learn.* I put the hooked stroke before the whole entry and moved on.

---

I sent the rest of the household home early the night I settled the box, and kept Tyche.

We sat in the room over the floorboard, the two of us, an old man and a woman not much younger, the only two living souls who had ever known the box was there or what the box held. She had kept it through the terror when she would not tell even me where it was. She had laid Macer's ring into it the spring of the will, beside the dead glass, and nailed the board down over the both of them. There was no one else I could do this with. There was no one else who would not need it explained.

She pried the board up without my asking and lifted the box out and set it on the table between us and worked the oiled wool back, and there it was. The dead glass. Black and smooth and cold, the thing I had carried in my hand the morning I woke in a stubble field with a city two thousand years too young at the bottom of the valley, the thing that had held my mother's number it could no longer call and a picture of the Colosseum new, and that had gone dark in a loft on the fourth night and never woken, and that I had kept, wrapped in wool, for fifty years, the one object in this world that was proof, if proof could be read off it, of where I was truly from. The ring lay against it, gold against dead glass, two things from two men who never met. And under them the citizenship tablet, *Marcus Ulpius Danihel,* the name a dead emperor's law had made me, the proof of the country I had been forced to become.

Tyche did not touch the glass. She never had. She looked at it on the table and said, "You have decided."

"I have decided," I said, and she crossed her arms on the table and waited.

"You will tell me the deciding," she said, "or you will not, but you will not pretend to me there was no deciding. I have known you fifty years. You do not handle this in the open with anyone."

So I told her what I had decided about the glass and the tablet. She had built her own true picture of the country I was from - from fifty years of slips and half-sentences, a *back home,* an *at home,* a smooth word for a kind of weather Rome did not have - and she was too good a person to make me confirm it. What she had not heard was the choice I had made about the books and the glass. The books go on, I said. The books are the gift. Every rung of them is a thing a man can be told and use, the figures and the workings and the lines and the laws and the honest blanks where I ran out, and they will stand in three copies and a lead case and an open door off the Argiletum, true and tellable forever. And this, I said, and I put one stiff finger on the cold glass and did not pick it up, this cannot be told. There is no rung under it. A man who is shown it cannot climb to it. It is the one thing I carry that is only a danger to whoever holds it, a witch's relic, a thing that would get you burned or get you taken apart by men who wanted to know how it was made and could not be told because the making of it is a thousand years of rungs not yet built. The figures I can leave to the world. This I can only leave to the ground.

"Bury it with me," I said. "In the wrappings. No one opens the box after. The ring goes back to its worth in the fund, as I told Lucanus, and the gold itself can melt into a smith's hearth where it does some good. The tablet goes with the glass, both of them, into the wrappings, into the ground. The two papers of where I am from, the false one Caesar made and the true one I cannot prove, they go down together where they cannot do any harm and cannot say anything to anyone, and the part of me that can speak stays up here in a book a child can read."

Tyche was quiet a long time. The lamp wanted oil and I did not call for it.

"You could give it to me," she said at last. "I have kept it fifty years. I could keep it fifty more, if the gods were absurd about it, and then it would be someone's to keep after."

"And what would you tell them it was?" She did not answer that, because there was no answer, and we both knew there was no answer, and the not-answering was the closest we ever came in fifty years to saying the whole thing plain. She had carried the weight of the secret almost as long as I had, the only one who had, and she was telling me she would carry it still, to spare me the ground, and I was telling her no, that the carrying ended with the carrier, that I would not hand her or any child after her a thing that could only ever be a danger and never once be a rung.

"Then it goes down," she said, "and the work stays up." She folded the wool back over the glass and the ring and the tablet, her hands sure where mine had grown uncertain, and she did not nail the board this time. She set the box back into the floor and laid the board loose over it, because the next time it came up would be the last time, and there would be other hands than ours for that, and we both knew whose, and we did not say it.

---

We sat after, with the lamp guttering, and did not talk of it again. She told me instead what the press had cleared that quarter, in the new figures, and I made her say it twice, as Macer once did with me, and did the old arithmetic in my head against the wages and the cloth, the head still doing that one thing clean when the eyes and the hands had quit, and I told her she was paying her pressmen too much, which was true, and which I had taught the whole house to do, and she laughed the dry working laugh of someone who has heard a man's only joke for fifty years and finds it funny anyway because it is his.

When she left I sat on alone in the dark over the loose board. There was nothing left to secure. The rope was checked end to end. My mind had let go of the glass under the floor already and gone a quarter-mile off to the lead case under the cold press-room stone, the strange marks folded in their oiled hide and their pitch and their lead, lying there patient as a seed under the one machine men always come back to, waiting for a foundation-digger not yet born to strike it with his spade and stop, and pry, and carry it up into the light to someone who would sit with it and begin to climb.
