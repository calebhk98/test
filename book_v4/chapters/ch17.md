# Chapter 17: Citizenship

A wax tablet came to the yard with a seal I'd never seen, and a man who handed it to me and not to Pamphilus, which was the first wrong note. Strangers don't hand tablets to slaves when there's a freeborn man three feet away. I broke the cord and read it slow, the case-endings swimming and then settling, and the gist of it was that I was to present myself in eight days at a house on the Palatine, with my apparatus, and that the request was made in a name I had to read three times before I believed it.

Tyche read it over my shoulder, which she was not supposed to do in front of strangers and did anyway, and her breath came out in a small sound. The man with the satchel watched us both and said nothing about the slave girl reading Latin. He had bigger items on his agenda than that.

"Whose house," I said, because I wanted him to say it.

"You read," he said. "Eight days. Bring the flying thing and the water and the new figures. Bring the man who keeps your accounts." He looked at Tyche when he said the last, which meant someone had already told him a great deal about my yard, more than I'd told anyone, and that was the second wrong note and the worse one. Someone had been counting my barrels for me. He left, and I stood in the cold holding the tablet, and Tyche stood next to me holding the lamp though it was day, and after a while she said it. "Macer didn't send this." I said no. She said Macer didn't even know about this. I said no, he didn't, but he was going to. About to.

***

Macer knew within the hour, because of course he had a man on the wharf, and he came down himself before the noon meal with his cloak thrown on crooked and the gold ring going on his thumb like a man working a problem. He read the tablet twice, holding it to the light, and when he was done his broken-veined face had not moved at all, which with Macer meant it had moved a great deal. He set it down on the workbench, flat, with one finger.

"You wrote to somebody," he said.

"I didn't write to anybody. Who would I write to? I can barely write."

"Somebody wrote about you, then." He turned the tablet over as if the back would tell him who. "Celer," he said, almost to himself. "Celer's gone back up to the army, the second war's coming, they're stockpiling already, leather and grain, I've got the contracts. Celer talks. The flux talks louder." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I had not seen on it before. Fear of the emperor, maybe, but also of what I'd quietly become while he was counting me in mules. "You understand what this is," he said, and I answered it straight.

"I understand he wants to see the balloon."

"He does not want to see the balloon." He tapped the tablet once. "Men want to see the balloon. He's a soldier. Soldiers don't pay for marvels, they pay for things that come home alive. He wants the water. He's taking an army back into those hills in the spring, and he buried more men in the last war to the bloody flux than to the Dacians. I sold the leather for the tents they died in, and somebody up there has told him there's a Thulean by the timber wharf who drinks the worst water in Rome out of a barrel and doesn't die. He wants the water and the figures for the men who count the grain, and he wants to look at the man and decide if he's a fraud or a tool. And you're going up there, boy, to be a tool, because the other thing he does with frauds I am not going to describe in my own yard." He stopped to pick up the stylus on the bench and turn it over in his fingers. "Bring me into none of it," he said, sharp. "You're mine, a thing I bought, that's the truth on any tablet, and if he wants to be told so I'll tell him on my knees and glad to. But you go up there and talk about water. Not Thule. Not the gods. Not the figures past what a quartermaster needs. Water, and the flying eye, and how many men it brings home. That's all you are for eight days. Can you be that small."

"I've had practice," I said, "being small," and he almost smiled, the mean half of a smile, and went away up the wharf with his cloak still crooked, and did not tell me good luck.

***

I did not sleep those eight days. I packed the show bag, the good one, the pale gray greased linen that went up clean, and checked every seam twice by lamp and made Tyche check them after me. A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day - Apollodorus had given me that and I'd paid for it in a man's life, and I was not going to have the lie come apart in the air the way it came apart over the Loaf. Two barrels of foul wharf water, two of the filter, a clean cup. A sheaf of the figures, a sum laid out the long Roman way beside the same sum in the new marks, the one a wall to climb and the other a stair. The abacus too, because the room is never convinced by the thing alone, it wants to see the thing beat what they already have.

And then, on the third day, when Tyche was out and the yard was quiet, I stood at the bench and looked at what I was bringing and added two sticks.

I'd been thinking about Eratosthenes since the cell, since the first time I'd tried to explain to Heras what I knew about the world and realized the problem was not my Latin, the problem was that I had nothing in my hands. Numbers he could dismiss. A map he could dismiss as imagination. But a stick in the sun, and a shadow measured, and the angle named - that was a thing he could see and touch and check. I'd carried the demonstration in my head for five years and never staged it properly, never had a room where it would matter. I thought this might be that room.

The morning of, Tyche came with me, because the tablet had asked for the man who kept my accounts and the man who kept my accounts was a slave girl of nineteen. I'd thought a long time about substituting Onesimus and decided against it, because if I was to be weighed I'd be weighed honest, and the honest truth of my yard was that the cleverest set of hands in it was hers. She wore her good tunic, the undyed one without the patch. She did not look afraid. She had got very good at not looking afraid, and I knew what it cost because I was wearing the same face.

The Palatine is up. That sounds stupid written down and it's the truest thing I can say about that walk. You climb out of the noise of the city, the carts and the garum and the cookshops, and the higher you go the quieter it gets and the bigger the stone gets, and the men at the doors stop wearing tunics and start wearing the look. By the time we'd been passed from one set of them to the next four times and stood in a colonnade with a floor so polished it gave back the winter sky, I'd stopped being able to feel my hands. There was no incense. I'd expected marble and incense and gold, and instead it smelled of cold stone and lamp-oil and, faintly, of leather and horse, a soldier's smell, and that told me about the man before I'd seen him.

They took the bag and the barrels from us and set them up in a smaller room than I'd pictured - a working room, a long table scarred from use, a brazier going, tablets stacked at one end. A clerk was there, an old freedman with ink to the second knuckle, two officers in the dust-colored tunics, and a thin sharp man in his thirties standing apart who watched everything and said nothing. Then a soldier came in from a side door wiping his hands on a cloth as if he'd come from some other work, broad, gray at the temples, with a square plain face that had been out in weather for thirty years, and everyone came to attention in a small way, a settling, and I understood that this unremarkable thick-necked man drying his hands was the most powerful human being on the surface of the earth.

He looked like a centurion who'd done well, like a man you'd ask to look at your cart axle. There was a scar on the back of one hand, old, white. He weighed me with his eyes.

"You're the Thulean," he said. His Latin was plain and quick, the army's, no salon in it. "They tell me you fly."

"I make a thing fly," I said. My voice came out steadier than I'd any right to. "I don't go up in it. Caesar." I'd practiced the word a hundred times and it still felt like a stone in my mouth.

"Wise," he said, "if the stories are true," and that was the war already in the room, the dead at the Loaf, somebody had told him everything, and I felt the cold go down my back. He said it dry, not cruel, as Celer said things, and I understood these were men of the same trade and the trade was killing and they spoke of it plain, at a soldier's volume. "Show me the flying eye first," he said. "Then the water. I care about the water." He sat on the edge of the table and folded his arms and waited to be either impressed or wasted.

I did not fill the bag. There was no room and I wasn't risking fire under that roof, so I laid the packed bag out on the table, the full eleven feet of it, and unrolled it and showed him the seams and the mouth-hoop and talked, fast and plain, about what it did and only what it did. Tethered, in still air, up to a hundred and twenty feet. A man with good eyes in a basket - and here was the part I had to hold to - or no man, and a glass if the glass were any good, which it wasn't, I told him the glass was a blurry toy. The far bank of a river. The smoke of a camp behind a hill. Signals down a line in colored cloth, a code, a thing seen from height that the men on the ground cannot see.

And then I heard myself say the rest, the part that mattered.

"I built two men under it against my own judgment. One of them burned and survived. One of them died. I won't rush untrained men into an untested design. Give me a year before the spring with your best volunteers and your most careful sergeant - men who'll go up fifty times tethered before they go up free, men who know every seam and every knot from their own hands - and I'll give you an eye that doesn't die on its first flight. Those are the terms. Give me less than that and I'm wasting your time. Either choice, you can have a tethered eye in the campaign, no man under it. Useful on still days, useless in wind, better than nothing. But with the year, you'll have something that matters."

The room was quiet. I heard the brazier. The thin man by the wall had stopped moving.

"The one who died," the emperor said. "Whose man."

"A volunteer. Marcus Aurelius Sabinus." I had not said the name out loud since the Danube. It came out wrong in that polished room, too small. "He had a boy who minds goats."

"You remember his name," he said, flat, a statement. He looked at the officer nearest him, some small soldier's calculus passing between them I couldn't read, and looked back at me. He sat with it for a moment. Then: "A man who'll give me the truth about what his own thing can't do is rarer than a man who flies." He dropped it on the table, watching what I'd do with it. "Crispus says you're a fraud."

"Crispus is right about almost everything," I said. "I am foreign. My figures are foreign. My country can't send you a letter. He's stacked it all true. He's only wrong about whether the work works, and I can settle that with a cup of water instead of an argument. I'd rather. He beats me at arguments."

That pulled a single breath out of the thin man by the wall. The emperor's face didn't change.

"Before the water," he said, "what else."

I'd intended this from the moment I packed the bag. I picked up the two sticks from the satchel and set them on the table. He looked at them. The officer nearest him looked at them. I set one stick vertical on the floor, in the winter light that came through the high window at its angle, and measured the shadow with my hand-spans and told him the number, seven. Then I told him that in Alexandria, on the longest day of summer, the stick cast almost no shadow. He knew Alexandria. He'd campaigned in the east. I told him that the distance between Rome and Alexandria was known - road-distance to the port, sea-days, reckoned - and that if you knew the distance and the shadow-difference, you could calculate the size of the sphere that would produce that geometry. I wrote the number on a tablet: two hundred and fifty thousand stadia, near enough. The circumference of the earth. Visible, touchable. Two sticks and an angle.

His eyes had changed. He hadn't moved, hadn't leaned forward, but the quality of his attention had shifted, the settling of a man who stops doubting whether you know something and starts asking what else.

"Your engineers use this," he said.

"No one uses it. I think the man who found it was shelved because it made the maps inconvenient." The thin man by the wall almost smiled. "But it works. And so does the water."

So I did the thing I'd done in the salon, except it mattered more and my hands knew it. I drew a cup of the foul water and held it to the light, brown, a thread of something turning in it, set it down, then poured the rest of that barrel slow through the filter into a clean jar, the sand taking the worst of it, and it came out near clear. I held that up too, and told him the truth Heras made me always tell: that clear is not safe, that you must still boil it, that the sand cleans what the eye sees and the fire kills what the eye misses. Any quartermaster taught it in a morning. Then I poured a fresh cup off the day-old boiled-and-filtered barrel I'd carried up cold, and drank it down in front of him, the worst water in Rome, and set the cup on his scarred table.

"How many men," he said. "To the flux. On the Danube. The last war." He was leaning forward off the edge of the table, and the affable weather was gone and underneath it was the cold doing-of-sums about the lives of men. "I lost more to the latrines than the line. You're telling me a barrel of sand and a fire saves them."

"Some of them. Not all. I've got no way to count the dead I'd have saved against the dead I didn't. But fewer. Camps sited above the fouling. Water boiled by century, not by whim. Latrines downstream and limed. The men who keep clean and drink clean don't go down with it as the rest do." I'd run out of careful and kept going. "You want to take Dacia in the spring. You'll take it. But you'll lose a legion's worth of men squatting in their own filth in those valleys before you ever see a Dacian, and I can give you back a piece of that legion for the price of fuel and digging. The most boring thing I make and the only thing I make that's killed nobody."

He was quiet a long time. The brazier hissed. Somewhere down the hill the city went on at itself, faint, under the stone.

"The figures," he said at last. "The ones my grain-counters fight you over."

I'd meant to show them myself. Then I looked at Tyche standing there in her good undyed tunic with her face very still, and I thought: if I'm to be weighed honest. "She's faster than I am," I said. The room looked at the slave girl. She went to the table and ran a column of provisioning sums in the new figures faster than the old freedman could do half of it on the abacus, and when she finished she stepped back to exactly where she'd stood and folded her hands and looked at nothing, which was the most frightened thing I'd ever seen her do.

The emperor looked at her a moment, then at me, something almost like amusement in it, and almost like the other thing, the cold sum.

"You teach slaves to keep an empire's accounts," he said.

"I teach whoever learns," I said. "She learned in a week. I didn't make her clever. I just gave her the marks." It was the closest I came, in that room, to the sentence I was not allowed to say, and I think he heard it under the words, because his plain face went still for a beat I couldn't read, and then he stood up off the table and the audience was over, no warning, as a soldier ends a thing when he's decided it.

"Wait outside," he said.

***

They put us in the cold colonnade with the polished floor and we waited a long time, long enough that the winter light moved on the stone, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the walls shouldn't hear. I counted the columns. I counted them four times and got a different number twice, which would have made me laugh on any other day.

When they brought us back in the emperor wasn't there. The thin sharp man was, with the old inky freedman, a stack of tablets gone over, and a document on real papyrus that the freedman was waiting to read. Macer was there too. They'd sent for him while I waited, fetched him up the hill, and he stood by the door in his good wool with no stripe and his face like a wall, summoned to the Palatine to be told about his own property by men who didn't ask. He did not look at me. He watched the freedman with the papyrus, steady, as a man waits by a table for a bill to be read.

The freedman read it. It was in the careful dead Latin of law and I caught maybe two words in five and got the rest from the faces, and the two words were enough. The first was a price, a fair sum, more than mules and less than a marvel, what Caesar's household had agreed to pay Titus Flavius Macer for the foreign craftsman in his keeping. The second came at the end, and I'd been waiting a long time to hear it, and when it came I didn't feel what I'd thought I'd feel.

Bought. Then freed. I stood there understanding it one phrase behind, that the imperial household had purchased me from Macer and then manumitted me, that I was, as of the reading of this papyrus on a winter morning, a freedman, *libertus*, of the household of the emperor, an imperial freedman. Not a citizen by birth. More protected than many senators. A man whose patron was the state itself.

And then the freedman read the name.

A freed man takes his patron's name. The praenomen and nomen of the man who frees you; your own name kept on at the end as the cognomen, the little that's left of who you were. My patron was Marcus Ulpius Traianus. So the freedman read it out slow and clear: Marcus, the emperor's first name, given to me. Ulpius, the emperor's family name, the gens, mine now, mine and every other freed slave of his house, a name that told anyone in the empire whose man I was. And then, last, smallest, the tail, my own name, the one my mother gave me, which the freedman could not pronounce and rendered into the dead law-Latin as Danihel, two careful syllables, a stranger's word.

Marcus Ulpius Danihel.

I'd dreamed of being free. I'd lain in the loft and on the road and in the cold yard and ached to be free, to make a will, to own myself, to free Tyche, to free Pamphilus, to stop being a thing on a tablet. And I was, all at once, with a stroke of a freedman's reed. A collar had come off and a finer one had gone on warm, a chain with the emperor on the end of it, his name on me, the orders for the spring already bearing my name. His man. Bound to go back into the valleys where Sabinus had died, with my boring water and my flying eye, in the service of the war I'd dragged my feet from for two years.

I stood on that polished floor and tried to feel like a free man and felt instead like a tool that had been picked up and found worth keeping. Underneath the relief of what I'd wanted for so long there was a weight I hadn't expected, and it was this: the balloon would go back to war. Better this time. Trained operators, men who'd go up fifty times tethered before they went up free, safety protocols written down, voluntary consent at every step - the vow I'd made after Sabinus covered untrained men in untested designs, and this was neither. It was a calculation and it was the right calculation and I'd made it myself and it still sat in my chest like a stone. Whatever the eye spotted from a hundred and twenty feet would be used to kill people who were, from where I stood, abstractions, Dacians in a foreign country, men with families, sons who mind goats. Sabinus had a family. The men whose camps I'd place above the fouling to save them from the flux - they'd live to fight. I was holding both ends of the same rope and I knew it.

I had no answer for it. I had only the same arithmetic I'd been running since the Danube: fewer Romans dead to the flux because of my water, fewer Romans dead on the first flight because of my protocols, and whatever the eye enables, balanced against what ignoring it enables. The balance always came down the same. That did not make it feel like much.

Tyche, beside me, had gone white, because she'd heard the part I'd missed in the law-Latin, the freedman still reading, a list of what a freedman of the emperor's house could lawfully do that a slave and a peregrinus could not. He could hold property in his own name. He could go to law. He could make a will, and his will would stand, and his writings be his own to leave. He could marry, lawfully, as a man with a name.

I heard the will part and something that had been clenched in my chest two and a half years let go. The first clear thought I had as a free man was: now I can write it down and leave it where it won't die with me. Now there can be the books. Now if a cart takes me on the wharf tomorrow the ladder doesn't go in the ground with the body. I'd been writing notes for whoever found my corpse since the second night in the cell, and now I could write them into something the law would let me leave behind.

Macer crossed the room. He came and stood in front of me, and for a moment I thought he might do something I had no name for, and then he put out his hand, the one with the gold ring, and gripped my forearm hard, businesslike, his thumb over the scar.

"Marcus Ulpius," he said, and I heard him taste it, the emperor's name on the thing he'd bought for less than a season's mules. Under his voice ran arithmetic, the swift remaking of a sum. He'd lost the asset, the property, the thing on the tablet that he owned, and gained in its place a client in the emperor's own household, a freedman of the Ulpii who'd stand at his door at the salutatio, a connection an army-contractor stockpiling leather for a Dacian spring could spend for years, and he was already spending it. "Caesar paid me fair," he said, which was Macer for it cost me, and "you'll come to my door of a morning like a proper client and we'll see what the war's worth to us both," which was Macer for I'm glad, you bastard, in the only tongue he owned. He let go my arm. "Get a toga," he said, looking me up and down. "You can wear the thing now, God help us. You'll look like a barbarian in a bedsheet, but it's the law." And then, at the door, half turned: "Thulean."

"Macer," I said.

"The crow won't touch you now," he said, low, meaning Vibenius, meaning Crispus and his graver word the law knew, meaning the priest weighing me in the shadow at the salon. "No haruspex reads a bad sign over a man Caesar's just named for his own house. Crispus can call you a fraud till his teeth fall out. He bought you for the water. Give him the water, and don't go up the hill of yourself in the spring." And he went out, his boot-heels loud on the polished stone, and was gone.

***

They gave me a tablet to carry down, a thing that said who I was now, in case the men on the doors didn't believe the barbarian in work boots was an Ulpius. Tyche carried the empty cup. We went down off the Palatine into the noise, the carts and the garum coming back up to meet us, the city not knowing or caring that a thing had been bought and freed and renamed in the quiet stone above it. Somewhere on the steps Tyche stopped and asked the only question that mattered to her, low, in the quick clean Latin she'd never once slowed for me.

"Can you free us now," she said. Us. Not her. She'd thought of Pamphilus first, the collar she'd watched me fail to take off, and only then, maybe, herself.

A freedman of the emperor's house could hold property in his own name. He could free his own slaves. I had no slaves yet, owned no one, the two at the yard were Macer's still and I'd have to buy them, and I had a peculium that was mine now in law and not in sufferance, and a patron who was the state, and a war in the spring with my name on the orders.

"Yes," I said. "When I can buy them. Pamphilus first." I didn't say her name and she didn't ask me to. We went down the hill, the cup empty in her hands, into the city that owned the name they'd put on me.

At the bottom of the steps I looked back up at the stone, all of it, the colonnades and the door-men and the cold smell of leather and horse. Then I tucked the tablet that said Marcus Ulpius Danihel inside my barbarian tunic, against the dead glass of the phone I still carried - two relics of two countries that couldn't write to each other - and went to find a place that would sell a freedman a toga he'd look like a fool in.
