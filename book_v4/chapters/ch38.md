# Chapter 38: Consolidation

The accounts of Aquitania came back to me in the spring with a column I could not read, and for three days I blamed the press.

It was a fair thing to blame. We had pulled the sheets in a hurry, the ink had been too thin, and the small figures at the foot of a tally, where I packed the carry-overs tight to save a line, had come up faint and broken. I held the page at the window and got the lamplight from the side, as you read a worn stamp on a coin, and the numbers swam and resolved and swam again, sixes that might be eights, a four that wanted to be a nine, the whole bottom course of the column gone to a gray smear my eye would not settle. I sent the sheet back to Eros to re-pull. He re-pulled it. It came back the same.

I sent it back again, and Eros, who had set type and cut blocks for me for twenty years and did not frighten, came himself this time and stood in the door of the tally-room with the sheet held out at a hand's distance and said, carefully, like a man who has decided someone is being a fool about something, that the figures were clean. He laid it on the bench. He put his thumb under the carry-over line. The figures were clean.

I bent close, and close, and at the distance where I had read figures my whole life the page was fog. At the length of my own arm, where Eros stood reading upside down without trouble, they came clear.

I had been holding the work farther off for a year. I had told myself the light was bad, the ink was thin, the clerks wrote small. I had moved the lamp, trimmed the wick, had Eros ink heavier, and never once said the plain thing, because the plain thing was that the trouble was not on the page.

"It's the light," I said to Eros, and took the sheet, and held it out at arm's length, and read it to him to prove the light, and he agreed it was the light, and went back to his press, and was kind enough not to mention that he had read it at a hand's distance while I read it at a cubit.

---

I will tell you what a man of my country would have done, because it is the joke of the thing, and I am the only one who will ever get it.

He would have walked into any shop on any street and bought a pair of glass disks in a frame and hooked them over his ears and gone back to reading. A few coins. A thing so common that children wore them and old men lost them down the backs of couches and no one thought it a marvel. I had ground lenses. I had spent four years and a fortune of Demetrios's patience grinding them, dished and bubbled and striped, that made the moon a smear and a senator's wife see colored fog, and not one of them, held at any distance from my own failing eye, did the one small honest work a lens is good for, which is to take a near thing that has gone soft and make its edges hard again.

I knew the geometry it wanted. A reading lens is the easy lens, the fat one, the one a beginner grinds. I knew the principle in the same bone-deep way I knew the rack of them at the hardware counter, the little turning stand, the row of magnitudes numbered for anyone to pick up. And I sat in my tally-room holding Aquitania at arm's length and could not make the glass do it, because the glass we could pour in Rome was full of seeds and waves and would not take a true curve from Demetrios's iron dish. The trouble that defeated me was the same trouble that had defeated the spyglass, only now it was defeating me where I lived, at the table where the best of my work got done.

When I finally said it to Heras, he did not find it as funny as I did. He held my face in two fingers under a window, turned it to the light, looked into the eye and not at it.

"The eye hardens," he said. "The commonest thing in a man past forty. The lens behind there grows stiff and will not pull itself round to a near thing anymore. I have it. Every man I have ever physicked who lived to read by lamplight has it." He let go my face. "There is nothing for it. You hold the work farther off until your arm is too short, and then you have a boy read it to you, and then you go on by what you remember and what they tell you. That is the road. I am further down it than you."

"There's something my people had for it," I said.

"Of course there was," Heras said, without heat, because he had stopped years ago being surprised that my people had a remedy for everything and that I could make none of it. He had decided long ago that this was either the truest part of my strange story or the cruelest part of my lie, and had made his peace with not knowing which. "And you cannot make it."

"The glass won't take the curve."

"Then it is a remedy your people had and you do not," he said. "Which is most of what your people had, as far as I have ever been able to tell."

He went back to his stool and picked up the Hippocrates scroll and held it out straight, arm extended, and read.

---

What I had, in those years, was a settled place, and it was smaller than I had once meant to stand in, and I had learned to stand in it.

I rendered provinces. That was the work the emperor kept me for, and there is a clean pleasure in taking a province's accounts both ways, the Roman letters and the new figures, and finding the gap where a procurator's freedman had been eating, and laying it out so plain that the man could be lifted out of his post without a word spoken about why, the column itself the only accuser, naming the year and the sum. Hadrian liked that. He was a man who wanted the doors of his house counted and the count to be true. In return he gave me the use of my own press and my own clerks and my own wax, and no coin, and his memory, which he had told me twice he would not lose, and which I treated as a thing kept on a high shelf that could come down on me in any season.

He was away most of those years. A traveling emperor, gone north to the wall they were building across Britain to keep the painted men out, gone to the Rhine, gone to walk the frontiers he had decided to hold instead of widen, building everywhere he went, a basilica here and a bath there and his own great round temple rising in Rome with its eye open to the sky. I felt him by the way the men nearer the top of the hill carried themselves, tightening or easing as word came down of where he was and what mood the road had left him in. The terror was behind us. The Four were nine years dead and the city had stopped flinching at a knock, and a man could walk to the baths again without rehearsing what he would say to a stranger who asked him the wrong question.

I was useful, and leashed, and let alone, and I had a wharf that made money and a press that made more, and a wife who ran the books better than I ever had, and I was, if you had asked the carters and the wharf-women, a fortunate man.

There were things I had not expected. Crispus stopped mattering, for instance. A geographer in Alexandria published a corrected survey that put Thule exactly where I had always said it was, and Crispus had staked the fraud charge on the geography, and the geography died under him quietly and without drama. I heard about it from Eros, who had it from the booksellers in the Argiletum, and that was how a ten-year grievance ended: a secondhand note from a man who cared more about the print run. Crispus was still alive, still dining out, still someone you didn't want in a room that was deciding something. He had stopped coming for me. I felt something smaller and more embarrassing than relief.

Vibenius had died two years before, of old age, in his own house, with his accounts square and his debt to me unpaid and un-called. He had held the weight over the priestly colleges for two decades, leaning neither into me nor away, and now the balance shifted to men I knew less. I did not grieve him. I did not celebrate. I noted it in the English cipher and moved on, which was probably what he would have wanted.

The ocean program crawled. I had its reports through the fiscus, dry as bone. The African coast was mapped a few headlands further south than the season before, the survey resting on the map and never on the scouts, as the emperor had ordered. The yard on the western shore had built two hulls and lost one in a gale within sight of its own slip and was building a third. The prize for a clock that would keep true time a month at sea stood unwon, and Daniel in the ground would not change that, because I had hung the whole gift on a wall I had told the emperor plainly I could not climb. The pendulum clock on the wall of my tally-room kept its beat, true to the minute by Tyche's noon-shadow measure, and it had no more idea how to be carried on water than I had. The survey was almost nothing, very slowly, in the right direction, for a long time, and that was all I could say for it.

One other thing I had not expected: the contest had produced a man I needed to be ashamed of.

His name was Marcellus, a freedman's son from the Transtiberim, one of the contest winners from three years back, a clever piece on water-lifting screws adapted for mine shafts. I had given him the prize and written to two mine-operators about his idea. He had taken the introductions, gone to both operators, and presented himself as my agent with authority to negotiate on my behalf, which he was not and had never been, and extracted, between them, something close to eight thousand sesterces in advance payments for improvements he was not capable of making and did not make. When the operators wrote to me, I paid them back from my own purse, with interest, and wrote to Marcellus to stay away from the contest and out of my name. He wrote back a letter that was more aggrieved than apologetic, which told me everything about how he had convinced himself none of it was wrong.

I told no one but Tyche. She noted it in the cipher without comment, which was worse than commentary.

I was six-and-forty. Macer was past seventy-five and would not die. Heras was past seventy and aging as an old wall ages, the footing still sound and the face going. I was holding my own ledger at arm's length, and would soon need a boy.

---

I came to it in Marcia's storeroom, of all places, the driest room on the wharf, the one that smelled of pepper and rope, because that was where she did the part of the books she would not do where the clerks could see, and where I went when I wanted to talk to her rather than to the partner who ran my accounts.

She had Aquitania in front of her, my Aquitania, the sheet I could not read, and she was reading it with no trouble at all, her own near sight not yet failing, and she had found something in it I had missed: a freight charge entered twice in two quarters under two carters' names that were the same man.

"You missed this," she said. "A year ago you would not have missed this."

"A year ago I could see it."

She put the stylus down. Marcia did not waste a beat pretending I had not said what I said. She had heard the whole story from Heras, who told her things he did not tell me he told her, and she had watched me hold the work off, and she had been waiting, I think, for me to bring it into a room myself rather than carry it around the yard like a stone in my shoe.

"So," she said. "You will go blind to small figures by fifty, near enough, like Heras. And you cannot fix it. And the work you are kept for is small figures." She said it flat, finding the level. "We should talk about what you are building, because you have been building as though you had thirty years and good eyes, and you have neither."

"I have the press. I have the contest. I have the survey crawling at the coast."

Marcia set down the stylus and moved the Aquitania sheet aside and pulled a second sheet toward her, and her pen started moving while she talked, as though the argument and the accounting required equal attention. She had been waiting for this conversation. The evidence was in the pen.

"The contest is yours. You fund it, Macer takes the credit, you judge it, and the day you die it is a story Macer's heirs will not pay for. The press is yours, and Eros's hands are old, and the second pressman is a borrowed man, and there is no boy learning to cut blocks because you have not made it anyone's living to teach him." She did not look up from what she was writing. "The survey is the emperor's, and emperors change their minds about the sea between one bad mood and the next."

I waited. She was not done.

"You have spent twenty years making yourself the one man who knows how to do each of these things. That was clever when you needed to be too valuable to kill. It is stupid now, because you are going to die with all of it still living only in your head and your hands, and your head is starting to go, and your hands will follow."

A sum dropped out of a column I had been refusing to total. I had known it since the rail carts at the quarry, that I would not win in my lifetime. I had said it to Heras and to myself a dozen times - the steel will not come, the clock will not come, the ocean will not be crossed by men I taught - and I had said it as a man says a thing he does not yet believe in his body. Standing in Marcia's storeroom, holding a sheet I could not read, listening to a woman whose eyes still held, I believed it in my body.

"So the work changes," I said slowly. "It stops being the steel. It becomes whoever carries it after me."

"Now you have it," Marcia said, and went back to her column.

---

I went to Heras with it, because Marcia had given me the figure and I wanted to hear the argument, and Heras was the only man left who would argue with me without being afraid of where the argument went.

He was in his own room off the colonnade, with his feet up because the stairs had cost him, and he had a lamp pulled close and was reading a Hippocrates he had read four hundred times and could not have needed the words of, and he had it at arm's length, the scroll held out as I held Aquitania. Neither of us said anything about that.

"Marcia says my head's going," I told him, "and that I've built everything so it only works while I'm alive to work it. She's right. I've been thinking about it wrong for twenty years. I kept trying to do the thing. Get the steel. Get the clock. Cross the sea. Win it before I die." I sat on the stool by his feet. "I'm not going to win it before I die."

"No," Heras agreed comfortably, as a man agrees that it is raining.

"So the work changes. It stops being the steel. It becomes the men who'll get the steel after me." I was finding it as I said it, finding the arrangement of the problem out loud. "Keeping men at a thing for a hundred years is a body's work - a body with a name and money of its own and rules and a door, that goes on hiring the next craftsman whether I'm in the ground or not. A body that pays a man to chase steel for ten years and fail and chase it again, because no one man can afford to fail at steel for ten years, and that's exactly why no one has the steel. The press has to be a body. The contest has to be a body, with its own coin, not mine."

I stopped. Heras waited. He had always been good at waiting.

"And the books most of all. The ladder. I've been writing it like a private correspondence, a few pages when Tyche has time. It's the only one of these that doesn't need money or an emperor or a body to outlive me. It only needs to get written and hidden well enough to be found. And it's the one I keep putting last, because the figures pay and the books don't, and now my eyes are going and I've spent the good years of them on a province's cartage."

Heras let the Hippocrates roll shut.

"You have just described," he said, "the work of the rest of your life, in a tone of voice a man uses to describe a funeral. Why?"

"Because it's all for after I'm dead. Every part of it. I won't see any of it work. I'll spend the last twenty years I've got building a staircase and never climb a step of it."

He was quiet a moment. Then he did the thing he did rarely, which was to drop the irony entirely, and it always landed harder than the irony.

"You are six-and-forty," he said. "I am past seventy. I have spent my whole life keeping men alive who would die anyway, a year sooner or a year later, and burying most of them in the end, and I have never once in fifty years cured a complaint that did not come back." He tapped the scroll against his knee. "You think this is a sorrow I have not sat with."

He picked up the scroll again and held it out at arm's length, looking at the words as though he were checking whether they were still there.

"The man who wrote this died six hundred years ago," he said. "I read his words tonight by a lamp, with my arm out straight because my eyes are gone, and he is in the room. That is the only kind of living-after there is. The rest is statues, and statues fall down on schedule, as your bridge-builder liked to say." He set the scroll on the table beside his knee. "Write the books. Build your bodies. Stop mourning at your own work like a fool. You are being the man another man reads by a lamp in six hundred years with his arm held out straight."

He waved a hand toward the door. "Now go home. It is late, and I am too old to comfort a barbarian past the second cup."

---

It was late when I came back across the yard, and there was a lamp still in the tally-room, and it was Ulpia at the bench.

My daughter. She was nine that year and should have been asleep, with a printed sheet of the times-tables in front of her and a stub of charcoal, copying figures.

She was the second child, born the year I came back broken from the East, freeborn where I was made and Lucanus was made - a small fierce square-faced girl with Marcia's flat way of looking at you as if pricing you. She had her own name, which had cost an argument, because the law gives a freedman's daughter his nomen and a feminine of nothing else, so she was Ulpia and only Ulpia on the rolls. I had wanted a cognomen for her as Lucanus had one, and Marcia had said a girl needs no cognomen, no one will ever write her on a contract, and I had said this one will, and we had named her Ulpia Severa, the grave one, the serious one, because she had come out of Marcia unsmiling and stayed that way. The name had turned out to fit her the way a cast fits what it was poured on.

"You should be asleep," I said.

"Lucanus said girls can't do the tables past the seven," she said, not looking up. "I'm doing the nine."

I looked over her shoulder, by reflex, to check her work, and the figures were a soft gray fog. I stepped back the length of my arm, and then back again, and from there, at the wrong end of my own reach, in the bad lamplight, I could just make out that she had it right: the whole nine course down the page, the carry where the carry went, in a child's hand that pressed too hard.

"You have it right," I said.

"I know," said Ulpia Severa. She did the next line and did not ask why her father had backed halfway across the room to read a thing a span from her hand.

I stood there at the far edge of the lamp's reach and watched her fill the page from a distance that had nothing to do with the dark. I thought of the book that was a few pages long in a script no Roman could read, that started with these same nine courses and meant to climb from them to the arc of the moon's road and the engines I would never build. It would have to be written soon, while there was still an eye to write it and a hand to copy it and a child at a lamp who could be taught the cipher.

She pressed too hard with the charcoal. The figures were large and dark and exactly right. I put out the tally-room lamp, and she picked hers up and went to bed, and I stood in the dark for a while with the press ticking as it cooled and the wharf outside doing what the wharf always did at that hour.
