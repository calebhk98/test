# Chapter 23: The Things That Don't Work

The men stop talking. The fire ticks. Somebody scratches under his tunic. And the device sits there on the bench being exactly as dead as a brick, while everyone waits for me to explain why I lied to them.

I got to know that silence well the winter Marcia took over my books, which was the winter I tried to build a mechanical clock. The tally-room already had that quiet. The wharf men had stopped bringing me problems by mid-afternoon, and the only sound was Tyche's stylus on wax and whatever Hermes was hammering at the back of the shed, and outside the river doing its old indifferent thing.

It started, like most of my worst ideas, with me being smug. I had money now, real money, the kind that buys bronze by the ingot and a man's whole month of skill without flinching. I had Hermes, who could cut a gear so clean you could shave with the teeth. I had a memory full of clocks. Every house I had ever lived in had clocks in it, on walls, on stoves, on the little square of glass I still carried dead against my chest. I had watched a documentary once about a man in a leather apron building a tall clock for a museum, and I remembered the brass guts of it swinging away behind a glass door, patient as breathing.

So I told Hermes I was going to build a machine that told the hours by itself, with no water, no sun, no slave to watch it. A thing of wheels. He looked at me with the look of a man pricing your funeral.

The wheels were easy. That was the trap. I drew a train of them, big driving wheel down to small, and I knew without thinking too hard that a falling weight on a cord would turn the big one, and the train would step the motion down, fast wheels off slow. Hermes cut them over three weeks. They were beautiful. I hung the weight, a lead cylinder Pamphilus cast in sand, and let it go.

The whole train spun up like a dropped top and the weight slammed the floor in about the time it takes to say a prayer. That was correct. That was the problem doing exactly what falling weights do.

A clock is a machine that stops itself, over and over, a precise number of times, by something that lets the wheels forward one little bite at a time and no more. The moving was the easy part, the part any falling weight would do for free. I knew that. I knew it the way a man knows a word he can't spell. There was a piece, a small ugly piece, that caught the wheel and released it and caught it again, and it ticked, and the tick was the whole machine. Everything else was just gears. The tick was the clock.

I could not remember the piece.

What that was like: I sat on a stool in front of a pile of perfect bronze and I closed my eyes and I went looking in my own head for something I had seen a hundred times, and it was not there. I could see the documentary. I could see the man's hands, the apron, the brass swinging, a sort of anchor-shaped bit rocking on a pin, two little points dipping into a toothed wheel, click, click, click. I could see it and I could not build it, because seeing a thing swing is not knowing how its faces are angled, how deep the points bite, how the rocking gets fed by the wheel it's holding back. The angles were the secret and the angles had never gone into my head. I had watched it pleasantly, learning nothing.

I tried anyway. God, I tried. I bent a strip of bronze into the anchor I could see and pinned it over the toothed wheel and rocked it with my finger. It would catch, sometimes, one tooth, and stick. Or it would slip the whole wheel at once and the weight would go to the floor. I filed the points. I changed the angle. I made Hermes cut three different toothed wheels, coarse and fine. I rigged a crossbar to swing the anchor with a weighted arm, thinking the swing itself would set the rhythm, and the swing just died, every time, like a child on a swing nobody pushes. There was a piece that fed the swing and I did not have it. The profile that took the kick from the wheel and gave it back to the swing was the profile I couldn't remember, and round it went, the dog chasing the tail it had already lost.

Hermes watched me ruin a week of his work and finally said, between hammer-blows on something useful, "It wants to go or it wants to stop. You are asking it to do both."

"Yes," I said. "That's exactly what I'm asking it to do."

"Then you are asking for a quarrel, not a machine." He set the hammer down. "What does it look like, in your country, the part that quarrels?"

And there it was. I opened my mouth to tell him and I had nothing. Nothing. I, the man from the land of clocks, the wizard who drew the whole world and made fire fly, could not describe to an honest smith the one small bronze argument at the center of every mechanical clock a clockmaker would ever have to build. It existed. Somewhere a man not yet born would file it in his sleep and never think twice about it. It was maybe a hand's-breadth of metal and it was as far beyond my reach as the moon, and I had told these people I came from a place where such things hung on every wall.

A specific flavor of shame, knowing a true thing and being unable to use it. When you don't know, you can go find out. There was no finding out here. The book was my own head and the page was torn out before I arrived.

So I built the water clock I could build and called it the clock I'd promised.

The Romans already had water clocks. *Clepsydra*, Heras called it, the water-thief, and they used them to time speeches in court so a windbag couldn't talk all day. A pot, a hole, water dripping out, marks on the inside to read the falling level. Crude, and everyone knew it was crude, and the problem with it was simple and ancient: a full pot pushes the water out fast and an empty pot dribbles, so your hours at the start of the pot are not the same length as your hours at the bottom. The water lies as it leaves.

I knew the fix for that, or half of it. A second vessel, kept always brim-full and overflowing, feeding the first through a fixed jet, so the pressure never changed. And here my gears were not wasted, because I floated a cork in the catch-vessel, ran a cord from the cork over a little wheel as the water rose, and made the wheel turn a drum, and on the drum a painted pointer that crept around a dial of the hours like the face of a real clock. A toothed rack, a pinion, a slow proud finger going round.

It was gorgeous. We mounted it on the wall of the tally-room in a frame of oiled cedar, the catch-vessel glinting, the float-cord whispering over its wheel, the pointer ticking up the painted hours in a hand Tyche lettered. Men came off the wharf to look at it. Macer came, and stood in front of it with his thumb working his ring, and said it was the prettiest piece of foolery on the river and asked what it was for, and I said it told the hours, and he said so did the sun, for free, and walked out. He came back twice more to look at it, which from Macer is a hymn.

It did not keep time.

It kept time loosely across an afternoon, wandering a fistful of marks off the sun, and the next day it would wander the other way. The overflow vessel helped, but the jet silted, and the water was thicker on a cold morning than a warm noon and crept slower, and the float stuck a hair on its cord and then jumped, so the pointer hitched. Heras, who is the only man I let measure me honestly, took to setting it against a sundial at noon and writing down its lie, and the lies were never the same lie twice. A clock that is wrong the same amount every day is a clock you can correct. A clock that is wrong a different amount every day is a wet decoration.

"It is very beautiful," Heras said, which is what he says about my failures, the same words he used for the first balloon that wouldn't lift. "I would not plan a funeral by it."

I kept it on the wall. It shows everyone who walks in that the Thulean, the man from the country of wheels, can make water crawl around a circle and cannot make the wheels argue properly with a weight. Tyche reads the sun and corrects the pointer twice a day with her thumb. I watch her do it sometimes.

The glass was a longer disappointment, and a more expensive one, and the only one I brought entirely on myself with my own big mouth.

It was the contest's fault, partly. Three years of the prize and the stories had gotten ambitious in a way I had not planned and could not stop. The first year it was eagles pulling chariots. By the third year half the entries were about glasses. Glasses to read a letter on a far hill. A glass to see the moon as a country, mountains and seas and the little cities of moon-people. And one, the one that haunted me, a glass so strong it showed the little invisible ones I had told everyone climb inside you when you sicken, the tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond-water, finally caught and counted. Somebody had listened to me talk about the invisible world and reasoned, correctly, that if it was there, a strong enough glass would show it. The boy who wrote it asked, in the last line, when I would build the glass that proved my own story.

I had been telling that lie for ten years. The invisible ones. I believed in them. I had no way on God's earth to show them to anyone, and now a child had written a tale where I did, and the tale was being printed by Eros and sold for an as in the Subura, and people were waiting.

I had made a single lens before, years back, a fat little disc of glass that made small writing big if you held it just right, a thing for an old man's failing eyes. That worked. A magnifier is easy. You polish a lump, you get a bigger blurry thing. What the stories wanted was a tube with a glass at each end, the far one to gather and the near one to spread, and the two together to drag a far thing close. I knew the principle. I had held one at a baseball game once, somebody's father's binoculars, the green field jumping suddenly huge and crisp into my eye. I knew it could be done. The Romans had glass. Beautiful glass, glass cups you could read a coin through, glass beads, glass for windows in the rich houses.

I bought the best glass on the river and it betrayed me at every step.

Roman glass is clear, and Roman glass is full of ghosts. Hold a good blown cup to the light and you see them, threads in the body of it, faint snakes of denser and thinner glass twisting through where it cooled unevenly, *striae* the Greek glass-cutter called them, and little round bubbles caught like held breath, some a pinprick, some big as a lentil. For a cup it doesn't matter. For a window it doesn't matter. For a lens it is the difference between an eye and a wound, because every bubble bends the light its own wrong way and every thread smears it, and a lens is nothing but a thing for bending light all one right way at once.

I hired the Greek, Demetrios, a glass-cutter with hands like a spider and no patience for me. I had him grind discs against a dished iron block with wet sand, working the curve I drew, and the work was murder. Grinding a sphere by eye, with no way to test the curve but to stop and look through it and guess, is a skill that takes a man a lifetime to learn and I was asking him to learn it in a season for a barbarian's whim. He'd grind for a day and we'd hold the disc up and the curve was lumpy, too deep on one edge, and there was no fixing it, you ground it down and started the shape again and lost the day. Out of a dozen blanks we got two that were near enough to round to bother mounting, and both of them had ghosts in them I couldn't grind out because the ghosts went all the way through.

We made the tube. Leather over a wooden form, a glass at each end, the far one barely curved, the near one a deep little dish, the spacing slid back and forth until a far thing swam into a kind of focus.

I looked through it at the far bank of the river, at a man unloading a boat. The man was closer. Two times closer, maybe a little more, near enough that I could have told you he wore a brown tunic if I hadn't already known. He was also dim, a world seen through a dirty pane. He was soft at the edges, never quite sharp no matter how I slid the tube. And he wore a rainbow. A thin skin of color clung to every edge of him, blue down one side and a sick yellow-red down the other, so the man and the boat and the far roofs all looked painted by someone whose hand shook and whose colors ran. The ghosts in the glass laid a faint smear over all of it, like looking through a window someone had breathed on.

A slightly closer, dimmer, blurrier, rainbow-edged view of a thing I could already see fine with my own eye.

I stood there on the wharf holding it and the whole bright tower of what I'd promised leaned and didn't quite fall. The war-glass that lets a general count an enemy three valleys off. The moon as a country. The drop of pond-water teeming with the proof of every cure I'd ever pushed. All of it, the whole future, supposed to start in this leather tube, and what came out the other end was a fuzzy man in a brown tunic wearing a rainbow he didn't ask for.

Marcia tried it. She slid the tube until she found the boat, lowered it, and asked me what it cost to make.

I told her, glass and Demetrios and the iron block and the weeks. Her face did its adding-up, quick and private. "And a child can see the far bank by walking to it," she said, "for nothing." But she didn't make me throw it out. She had me make three more, ground a little better each time, and she sold them, God help me, to rich men who wanted the newest toy and didn't care that the toy was bad. A senator's wife paid more for a blurry rainbow tube than Demetrios earned in a season. "A bad toy that no one else has is still the only one," Marcia said, wrapping it in wool. "Make me ten."

So there are perhaps a dozen of my spyglasses in Rome now, in the houses of the very rich, and every one of them shows a dim two-times rainbow, and the men who own them tell each other it is the wonder of the age, and not one of them has seen a single invisible creature or one mountain on the moon. The boy who wrote the story is still waiting. He will wait until he dies. I will not build the glass that shows the little ones. There is no such glass on this side of a hundred patient lifetimes of clearer glass than Rome can pour, and I told a true thing I cannot prove and dressed it up as a marvel I cannot make, and the dressing-up is the part I'll answer for.

I kept the first tube. It and the water clock sit near each other in the tally-room, the two prettiest things I have made that don't do what I said they would.

The rails I want to tell last, because the rails worked, and that was the worst of the three by a long way.

This one I did not get from a documentary. I got it from being a kid who liked trains, and from the simplest physics there is, the kind even I can't garble. A wheel rolling on a smooth hard road takes a fraction of the muscle that dragging a sledge does, because rolling beats sliding, and if you keep the wheel from wandering off the smooth part by giving it a lip that rides against a rail, you can run a heavy load down a fixed line with almost nothing pushing it. No new metal worth speaking of. No chokepoint. Wood and a little iron for the wheel-rims and the flanges. The most certain improvement I had tried to build since the numerals.

There was a stone-cutter's yard up past the brickfields that hauled cut blocks down a long slope to a barge landing, and the hauling was done by men. Slaves, chained at the ankle in a coffle when they weren't under the load, dragging the blocks on greased rollers and timber sleds down a churned mud track, twenty and thirty of them to a big block, in the heat, all day. I had walked past it a hundred times on the way to the brick I needed and made myself look somewhere else, because looking and being unable to stop it is a thing you can only keep up for so long before it eats through you.

I went to the owner, a hard dry equestrian named Maximus, with Marcia, because by now I knew better than to talk money without her in the room. I told him I could move his stone with a quarter of the men. I laid out wooden rails, squared oak baulks pinned to sleepers, set the gauge to his cart, built four cars with flanged wheels Hermes turned, and ran a line down one edge of his slope at my own cost, to show him, because Apollodorus once told me a demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day and I wanted this to be a true thing on a true day.

It was. We loaded a block that had taken twenty-six men to drag, a real monster, onto a car on the rails, and four men walked it down the slope at a walk, leaning back on ropes to hold it from running, and at the bottom two men pushed the empty car back up. Four men and a slope did the work of twenty-six men and a whip. Maximus stood at the top and watched the block go down smooth and quiet where it used to scream on the rollers, and I watched his face, and I let myself have one clean breath of the feeling I almost never get here, which is a true machine doing a true job in front of a man who can use it.

Then we sat down to the arithmetic. Marcia did it, because she does it better than I do and because I think she already knew where it went.

The rails cost so much in oak and iron and the cutting and laying, she said, naming the figure. The wheels and the cars, this much. To rail his whole yard and the long haul to the landing, this much, a sum that made Maximus's mouth go flat. Against that, she said, set what he'd save. And then she said the thing I had been not-thinking for a month, in the flat voice she uses for the hard word.

"You own the men, Maximus."

He did. That was the whole sum, right there, and the rest was just writing it down. He owned the men already. He'd bought them years ago, the price long since paid and forgotten, and a slave who hauls stone costs him a bowl of grain and a wall to chain him behind and nothing else, and if one dies under a block there are always more off the boats from Dacia cheaper this year than last, because we had just dragged a whole nation south in chains and I had counted some of that haul myself in the new figures. The men were a cost he had already paid and would pay again in bodies, not coin. The rails were a new cost, in money he had now and iron he could sell, to replace a labor he already owned and didn't have to buy twice.

"So," Marcia said, and tapped the tablet where the two columns sat, "you would spend good iron and good silver to stop using men you have already bought and cannot sell for much. The rail saves labor. You have labor to burn, Maximus, bought and fed and going nowhere. What you are short of is iron and silver." She let it sit. "It is a fine machine. It is a machine for a man who has to pay his haulers. You do not have to pay your haulers."

Maximus looked at the rails, and at the block sitting at the bottom of the slope where four men had set it down easy. He was a practical man; he saw it work. He never once said it didn't work. He said it was clever. He said the Thulean was always clever. And he said he would think on it, which is what a man says when he has already thought, and he sent us home, and he kept his coffle and his rollers and his mud track and his twenty-six men, because his twenty-six men were free to him now in the only sense his ledger understood, and my rails were not.

I tried the capability argument afterward, the one I should have led with. There are places where rails make sense even with slaves to spare: a long steep mine shaft where three hundred stone want moving at once, an incline where the load itself would kill a coffle on wet ground. I took the figures to two army engineers who were boring drainage tunnels on the Caelian, thinking they might see what Maximus couldn't. One was politely bored. The other said he'd look at it when he finished his current contract, which would be in three years, and he seemed to believe this. The rails are a tool for a man who is short of muscle and long on iron and time enough to think it through. Rome is short of none of those things and long on none of those things and I ran out of arguments before Rome ran out of backs.

I tore out the demonstration line a month later when Maximus wouldn't buy the oak it sat on. The wheels are in the shed. Hermes turned them so true.

I have built things that hurt people. I put a man up in a balloon and watched him burn out of the sky. I made a powder that took Geta's hand and his eye in a shed I built. Those are heavy and I carry them and I have written them down where I write the heavy things. The rails were a different weight, and lighter, and somehow they have worn a deeper groove. Nobody died. Nothing burned. I built a clean, true, simple machine that took the killing labor off men's backs, and it lost, fair and square, in the open court of arithmetic, to the plain fact that a man already owned the backs and a back costs less than a wheel.

---

That spring a block of tenements in the Subura caught and burned to the footings in half a night. I was two streets away when the glow came up and I could feel the heat on my face standing in a crowd of people with no buckets. Fourteen dead. The buildings were all wood-frame above the first floor, all packed together, and the wind was from the north, which was lucky, or it would have been a hundred.

I went to the aedile's office three days later and laid out what I knew about fire codes: mandatory spacing, tile roofs or none, water storage, a watch with tools not just eyes. The man who heard me out was called Gavinius and he had the particular face of a man who has been told things he cannot pay for. He asked who owned the buildings on the Subura block. I named two senators and an equestrian. He nodded slowly and rolled the tablet back toward me. "I would be very interested," he said, "if any of those men were interested." His voice had the particular flatness of a man who gives the bad news often enough that it no longer costs him anything.

I wrote the fire prevention notes up anyway and gave them to Tyche to copy into the files.

Also in those weeks I ran an experiment Heras had been asking about, with a stack of copper discs and zinc discs and little squares of cloth soaked in brine, alternating, all pressed together in a tower. He'd heard about the Baghdad jar - the clay pot with the iron rod and the copper tube I'd shown him years back, the one that gives a small tingle when your tongue bridges the gap. This was bigger. Twenty pairs of discs. I touched the ends with wire and the wire grew warm. I touched two long wires to my own tongue and got a jolt that put stars in my eyes and made Heras, who was watching from the doorway, come two steps closer. He asked how I knew it was current and not spirit. I said I didn't, except it followed the metal wherever I put the metal and stopped when I broke the path. He stood over the stack for a long while, poking at it with a stylus, not touching. Then he said, dry as sawdust, "It has very little obvious use." Which from Heras means he will be thinking about it for a month.

The next day I wound a long stretch of wire around an iron nail and touched the wire's ends to the disc-tower and the nail lifted iron filings off the bench. Heras was there again. "It has become a lodestone," he said. "Yes," I said, "and it stops when I lift the wire." He looked at that for a while. I didn't explain what I was going to do with it, because I didn't know yet.

I set down in the cipher, that night, what I knew about a needle that floats on water and points always north, a trick of iron and magnetism I had been working at in the back corner of the tally-room, getting the alignment right, losing it, getting it back. I wrote it for whoever came after, who would need to know which way was north when there was no shore to navigate by.

---

That evening I went into the tally-room where the water clock was lying about the hour and the dead spyglass sat beside it, and Tyche was at the table with the wax and the English letters, working down the cipher I'd given her, building it letter by letter as a clock should be built. I sat down across from her and pulled a fresh tablet over and started to write out, in the small flat hand I save for the books, everything I knew about a wheel that rolls on a rail and why four men can do the work of twenty-six. I got to the flange and stopped and drew it twice before I got the lip on the right side. Tyche watched me get it wrong and then right, and she didn't say anything, and she went back to her letters, and outside on the wharf somebody was still hauling something heavy the old way, the rope creaking, the men counting the pull in a language I dream in now, and I wrote it down.
