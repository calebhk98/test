# AGENT RULES — Read this before writing or reviewing a single word

This file is law. Every writer and every reviewer reads it first. If a draft
breaks these rules, it gets rejected and rewritten. No exceptions.

## The one rule underneath everything

**Render experience, do not describe it from outside.** The moment the prose
steps back to label, annotate, or explain what a scene is *doing*, it has left
the story and become commentary on the story. Put the reader inside the moment
and trust them to feel it.

## Assume an intelligent reader

The reader is smart. They do not need things spoon-fed. Do not explain a joke,
a motive, or an implication that the scene already carries. Leave room for them
to do the work. If a chapter can be fully predicted from its first page, the
chapter has failed.

## Banned moves (these are AI tells — instant rejection)

1. **Outline notes as prose.** Never describe the shape/function/purpose of an
   event instead of rendering it. If a sentence says what a scene "requires,"
   "forces," or "sets up," delete it and write the scene.
2. **Negative narration.** Do not narrate what *isn't* happening to imply
   meaning. "He didn't mention it. Nobody reacted. She said nothing." Silence on
   the page is already silent. Render what IS there.
3. **Telegraphing twists.** Never call a thing "unexpected," "surprising," or
   "what no one anticipated" before/while it happens. Earn the surprise
   structurally; let the reader feel it.
4. **Narrator stepping outside time.** No "This was the last time they spoke,"
   "He didn't know how important this would be," "before everything changed."
   The narrator does not get to arrive from the future to assign weight.
5. **Announcing emotional weight.** No hollow punchy declarations of depth
   ("The party moved on. He didn't."). The punch only lands if the reader
   already carries what the character carries. Earn it or cut it.
6. **Dramatic irony by declaration.** Don't state the gap between what a
   character knows and what the reader knows. Build the architecture so the gap
   simply exists.
7. **Exposing subtext.** Never write the thing that should stay under the
   surface. Show the behavior of someone hiding grief; do not say grief is
   hidden. Name it and the reader has nothing left to discover.
8. **Dialogue that delivers information.** In charged scenes people deflect,
   lie, change the subject, talk around the point. Reveal character through what
   is avoided as much as what is said. Avoid "as you know" exposition dumps.
9. **Beat-tag clichés.** No nodding, sighing, chuckling, brow-furrowing,
   shrugging, head-shaking as punctuation between lines. "He chuckled" is a
   near-universal AI tell. If a beat is needed, make it specific to THIS
   character in THIS moment.
10. **Stacked incoherent imagery.** Don't pile metaphors that don't build one
    controlling image. Imagery is cumulative; every detail should deepen the
    same impression.
11. **Scene closings that restate the takeaway.** Don't end a scene by
    summarizing its meaning ("she knew nothing would ever be the same"). End on
    a concrete image, a line, a physical detail the reader can sit with.
12. **Fake details.** No category labels standing in for specifics. "Elegant,"
    "sophisticated," "refined" tell a category, not a place. "The carpet
    absorbed every sound" is a detail. Be concrete and physical.
13. **Repetition across scenes.** Do not re-explain established dynamics, states,
    or plot context. If the reader already has it, don't restate it. (Writers:
    you are given a canon log precisely so you don't re-explain.)
14. **Uniform hedging.** Don't apply the same caution to every moment.
    "Perhaps," "it seemed," "in some ways" at a constant rate flattens
    conviction. Vary certainty. Dramatic beats should be certain.

## Punctuation and phrasing rules

- **No em dashes (—).** Use a normal dash (-), or commas, or a full stop.
- **No epanorthosis / correctio.** Banned constructions: "It's not X, it's Y,"
  "not just X but also Y," "less X than Y" as a rhetorical pivot. State the
  precise point directly and back it with a concrete detail or reason. Do not
  define a thing by what it isn't.
- **Use conjunctions naturally.** "So," "and," "but," "yet" at the start of a
  sentence or joining clauses is fine and human. Don't strip them out for a
  clipped, list-like rhythm.
- Vary sentence length deliberately. AI default is uniform medium-length
  sentences; break that pattern with intent.

## Positive craft targets

- **Specificity over evocation.** A real detail beats an evocative-sounding one.
- **Voice.** Daniel is a 2020s American teenager. His narration carries that:
  dry, self-deprecating, occasionally crude, prone to undercutting his own big
  moments with a joke. He is NOT a Victorian narrator. (See style guide.)
- **Mistakes are real.** Daniel misremembers, guesses wrong, gets humiliated.
  Do not let him be smoothly competent. Wrong-but-plausible is the target for a
  high-schooler's recall.
- **Earn the tech.** Every invention costs sweat, money, failed prototypes, and
  social maneuvering. No montage where a thing simply works.
- **Subtext on slavery and literacy.** The book's argument against slavery is
  made through events and inventions, almost never through Daniel lecturing.
  Keep it under the surface.

## When reviewing

Quote the offending line, name the rule it breaks, and give a concrete fix.
Reject for: canon violations, predictability, any banned move above, voice
drift, or "the agent cheated" (summarized a scene instead of writing it, skipped
hard material, or padded with filler). Be specific. A vague review is useless.
