# Chapter 8: Fire That Flies

The bag was the size of a man hung head-down.

I want that to be the first true thing, because everything else that day got bigger in the telling and I have spent years now hearing the bag described as wide as a house. It was not. Folded, it was an armful. Open and full, it stood maybe eleven feet from the mouth to the round of the top, a fat teardrop on its small end, pale gray linen gone darker where the mutton grease had soaked the seams, and it smelled like the inside of a cold lamp. I had stitched it in nine days. The seams were folded twice and run twice and greased over the run, the way I had finally taught my own clumsy fingers to do them, and the mouth was a hoop of green ash-wood I had bent and lashed so the cloth would gape open and hold its shape over the heat instead of sucking shut. There was no string. That was the whole point and the whole terror. There would be no string.

They had given me the corner of a square for it. Not a good square. Heras told me on the walk in that the good squares had been spoken for since the emperor came, fire-eaters and a man with a trained bear and a Syrian who could throw eleven knives, and that ours was a hard packed open lot off the lower end of the Subura where two lanes ran together, a place that filled with whoever the better squares spilled. There was a butcher's block at one edge with the flies on it and a fountain at the other, roaring, the same bottomless fountain roar I had heard at the gate a few weeks before, and overhead the insulae leaned in until the sky was the usual crooked bright river between them. I stood in our corner at the wrong hour of the morning with my heart going like a fist on a door and thought, in plain English, in my own head where no one could hear it, this is the dumbest place in the world to light a fire.

I had two helpers and neither was mine. Stichus, the water-boy, ten years old and gray with fear, holding the coal-pot against his chest like it might run. And a big slow household slave the steward had sent, a man named Davus, to do the lifting and, I understood without being told, to make sure the household's barbarian did not bolt into the crowd. Heras stood off to one side in his good cloak with his arms folded, being a Greek physician of Pergamon, a man whose word a buyer might trust. And somewhere behind me, in the press, was the man.

I had still never seen him. I want that on the record too. A year I had burned his cloth and slept under his roof and never once laid eyes on the equestrian who owned the air I breathed, and I did not lay eyes on him that morning either, not knowing it. The steward had come at dawn, a dry narrow man with a tablet, looked at the bag the way you look at a dog you have been told bites, and said the master would watch from a place where he could be seen watching, and that I was to make it go up and not make a fool of the household, in that order. Then he went and stood at the edge with a knot of other men in good tunics, and one of them was the man, and I did not know which, and I had a fire to build.

---

You cannot fill a balloon quietly.

I had learned that over the midden, where there was no one to perform for. On the ground the thing is dead cloth and you are a man crouched over a fire feeding a sack, and it takes time, real time, more time than a crowd will give you before it decides you are a fraud and goes to see the bear. I had counted it out at home. From cold bag to lift was something past two hundred breaths if the fire was right, and the fire is never right at first.

So I did the cheap trick first, the one I had worked out lying awake. I had Davus and Stichus hold the mouth-hoop open between them, low, and I built the fire under it small and fast, dry split kindling I had begged and hoarded for a week, and the moment the bag had a belly of warm air in it and started to want to stand on its own, before it could lift, I had them walk it up. Just up. Two men standing, lifting the hoop to head height with the half-warm bag swaying above it like a drunk getting to his feet, so the crowd could see the shape of the thing come up out of nothing, see it want the sky before it had the sky. It is a magician's move. It buys you the two hundred breaths.

It worked better than it had any right to. The bag came up gray and shivering and huge over our heads, eleven feet of it nodding in the air off the fountain, and the corner of the square that had been ignoring the foreign idiot with the kindling went quiet in a patch and then less quiet, and people stopped. I heard them stop. There is a sound a crowd makes when it decides to look at you and it is the worst sound in the world if you are not ready and the best if you are, and I was somewhere in between, sweating through the borrowed tunic they had finally given me so I would not stand there in my witch-clothes.

Then the long part. The fire under the open mouth, fed and fed, the heat going up into the bag, Stichus white and rigid with the coal-pot, Davus holding the hoop and turning his face from the heat, and me crouched in the smoke with my burned hand, the old shiny line up my right forearm and the newer one across the back of the same hand, both of them screaming in the warmth, feeding the fire and watching the cloth and counting. Sixty breaths. The bag fattened. A hundred. It pulled up against Davus's hands and he had to lean down to hold the hoop on the ground. A man in the crowd shouted something I did not catch and others laughed and the laugh had an edge that could go either way. A hundred and forty. The grease-smell of the seams. I had a sick clear thought, the one I always had now over an open fire with greased linen above it, the lip, watch the lip, the lip is what kills you, and I made Davus tip the hoop a half-inch off true so no part of the cloth sat in the actual flame.

A hundred and eighty. The bag stood straight up off the hoop, straining, the whole pale body of it lit warm from inside by the fire under its mouth, and the crowd noise changed. It went up the scale. Two men do not hold a thing like that down for long once it truly wants up, and I felt the moment coming the way you feel a sneeze, and I shouted the one Latin word I had drilled into Davus and Stichus until they hated me.

Now.

They let go.

---

I have tried for years to say the next part right and I always fail, so here is the failure.

It went up. It went up the way nothing in their world went up, no smoke, no bird, no thrown thing that has to come down, it went up and kept going, a man-sized gray shape climbing straight off the fire into the river of sky between the insulae with no hand on it and no string, and it turned slowly as it rose the way they all do, showing its fat side and then its small end, and the whole square made one sound.

I cannot write the sound. It was not a cheer. A cheer has words in it somewhere. This had no words. It came up out of a few hundred people at once and it was the sound you would make if the dead sat up, a kind of open vowel of the body, and under it, fast, before the gasp was even finished, it began to come apart into the other things. Delight, yes. A woman near the fountain was laughing and pointing and shaking the man next to her. Children screaming the good scream. But other voices already coming up through it, harder, and I knew enough Latin by then to catch the shapes of them as they went past my ears like thrown stones.

What god. Whose is it. What man does this. And the one Heras had warned me about, the one that can get a foreigner torn apart in a Subura lot before the household guards can reach him, the word that turns a marvel into a working, a witch's working, said sharp and afraid, and then said again, picked up.

The bag rose maybe sixty, seventy feet, clear of the rooflines, out into the open bright. And then it did what it always did, what I had not been able to fix in a year and was not going to fix that morning, because the truth under all the wonder was that I had no way to keep the heat in it once it left the fire. It stopped climbing. It hung. The warm air in it was already cooling and the cloth was already losing the fight, and the thing that had gone up like a soul going up began, slowly, in front of all of them, to come back.

It did not come back the way it went up. Going up it had been straight and certain. Coming down it was a drunk again, sagging on one side, tipping its mouth, and the little morning wind that runs down those lanes off the river took it and pushed it sideways, downhill, toward the leaning fronts of the insulae, and the gasp turned in an instant into a different noise, a scatter and shriek, because a thing that flies is a wonder and a thing that flies at your face is a fire coming at your house.

It clipped a rooftop. The small end of it dragged across the clay tiles of a five-story front, caught for half a breath on a line of strung washing, tore a hanging tunic loose and dropped it, and the bag, half collapsed now, slumped over the roof edge and down the front of the building in slow folds. There was still fire-warmed air and grease in it. A woman two floors up was screaming and hauling in her washing, and a man on the street below was beating at a tumble of cloth that had come down half-alight, a corner of my seam smoldering where it had finally, after a year of trying to kill me, touched something it could burn. People scattered from under it. Someone went down and was hauled up. The smoldering corner caught a fold of the dropped washing and there was real flame for a moment, low, on the cobbles, and a dozen men stamping at it and shouting, and the bag, my best bag, nine days of folded seams, lay over the street in a gray heap with smoke coming off one edge of it.

No one had died. I knew that even then, watching, with my heart somewhere up in the place the bag had been. No one had died, a tunic was burned, a woman was screaming about her roof, a man had a scorch on his arm from the washing. By the standard of the thing it was nothing. By the standard of the square, where a few hundred people had just watched fire fly off a foreigner's hands and come down on a house, it was the worst possible nothing, because now the question hanging over the lot was not is it a wonder but what do we do with the man who made it, and that question, in a Roman crowd that has just been frightened, has an old and ugly answer.

I felt Davus's hand close on my arm. Not to comfort me. To hold me where I was, so I could not run, because the steward had told him to, and because running is the one thing that turns what god into kill it.

---

Then the crowd parted from the temple side, the way a crowd parts for someone it is afraid of in the other direction, and the old man came through it.

I knew him before I could have known him. Gray, still, important, walking slow while younger men cleared the way ahead, and the same flat reading look he had put on me across two hundred people at the gate weeks before. Aulus Vibenius. He had been here the whole time, I understood, standing at some good vantage with his college around him, watching the household's curiosity the way he had watched the entrails on his board, and now he came out into the open lot toward the heap of smoking cloth and toward me, and the noise of the square fell off around him until I could hear the fountain again and a baby crying somewhere and the man still stamping out the last of the washing.

He stopped at the fallen bag. He did not look at me yet. He crouched, an old man's careful crouch, and he put two fingers on a fold of the gray linen the way Heras put two fingers on a pulse, and he rubbed the grease of the seam between them, and he smelled it. He looked up the front of the building where it had dragged across the roof. He looked at the bit of sky it had come down out of. He took, I swear, more time than the crowd had given me to fill the thing, and the crowd gave him all of it and more, because he was the one man in the lot whose verdict could not be argued with, and every frightened person in that square was waiting to be told which thing they had just seen.

When he stood and turned to me he spoke loud enough to carry, in formal Latin, slow, so that I caught most of it and the crowd caught all of it, and I have turned it over so many nights that I no longer fully trust my own memory of the words. But the shape of it was this.

He said the sky had been shown a thing today. He said the gods send signs in many ways, in the liver and in the flight of birds, and that a fire that rose of itself and turned in the air and came down upon the city was a sign, and that he had read it. He let that sit. He looked at me, the burned hands, the wrong clothes under the borrowed tunic, the foreign face, the whole of what I was, and he said the foreigner from the cold edge of the world had sent fire up to the gods and the gods had let it rise, which no god does for a thing the gods hate. And then, in the same breath, in the same grave even voice, with no change in his face at all, he said that the gods had also turned the fire back down upon the rooftops of Rome, and that a wise city would consider, carefully, whether a gift had been shown to it or a warning.

That was all. He did not say bless it. He did not say burn it. He gave the crowd both doors and stood in the middle of them, an old man who had spent fifty years learning exactly how much to say and not one word past it, and he let four hundred frightened people decide for themselves which one they had walked through. And the genius of it, which I only understood later and hated, was that whichever way it went now, he had read it true. If they made me a wonder, he had blessed it first. If they tore me apart, he had warned them. The haruspex is never wrong. That is the whole craft.

The crowd took the gift door. Just. I felt it tip the way I had felt the bag tip, slow and then all at once, the laughing woman by the fountain louder now, men calling out, someone near me reaching to touch the hem of the borrowed tunic as though that did anything, the word that had been witch a minute ago turning over in mouths into something nearer to marvel, the gods let it rise, the gods let it rise, you cannot argue with the sky and you cannot argue with Aulus Vibenius. But he had left the other door open and I had felt the draft from it, and I stood there with Davus's hand on my arm and the smoke still going up off my best bag and understood, in a way I had not understood the night before sewing it, that going up had only made me visible. A man can be visible and dead in the same hour. I had spent a year wanting to be worth something and I had got my wish in a Subura lot with a priest reading my fire like a liver, and worth something, here, meant a thing other men decide what to do with.

Vibenius looked at me one more time before he went. The same flat three-count he had given me at the gate. Then a younger priest was at his elbow and he turned and the crowd opened for him and closed behind him and he was gone.

---

I did not get to gather up my bag.

I had crouched down over it, the smoke gone to nothing now, to fold the wet smoldered corner away from the good cloth, because nine days, because I would need the green hoop again whatever happened, because crouching over the work was the only thing in the lot that still made sense to my hands. Stichus was crying without sound, the coal-pot still clutched to him. Heras had come up beside me and was saying something low and dry, satisfied and wary at once, the same two things he was always both of. I do not remember what. I remember the grease and ash on my fingers and the way the crowd noise was sliding back toward festival, on to the next square, the bear, the knives, a thing seen and survived and now a thing to tell over wine.

Then there were sandals in front of me, planted, two pair, good leather, and I looked up the length of two clean tunics to two men I did not know, household men, broad, with the steward's dry narrow face appearing behind them. The steward did not look at the bag. He looked at me the way you look at a sum that has just come out larger than you wrote it.

He said the master would see me. He said it the way you would say a wall had decided to move. Not the steward, not Heras, not a messenger with a tablet at the workshop, none of the ways the household had spoken to me for a year through every mouth but the one. The master. The man whose cloth, the man whose roof, the man I had never seen and could not have picked out of the knot of good tunics at the edge of the lot. He would see me. Now. Today.

The two broad men did not touch me. They did not have to. They stood and waited while I closed my fist around the green hoop and the folded gray cloth and got up off my knees in the ash, with Heras already at my shoulder saying he would come, he would come as far as the door, and the fountain roaring, and the last of the smoke of my best bag thin and blue going up between the leaning houses into the same bright crooked river of sky I had just thrown it through, where it had hung for a moment with no string and no hand on it, in front of all of Rome, before it came down.
