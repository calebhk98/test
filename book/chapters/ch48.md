# Chapter 48: The Subtraction

The world I had built around myself began to come apart from the edges in, the way the cheap wool tunics did, the hem first, a thread at a time, so that you go on calling the thing a tunic long after it has stopped being one.

Pamphilus went that spring, the spring of my three-and-sixtieth year. He had been the first man I ever bought free, with four thousand of Macer's sesterces and Hermes's cold chisel against the rivet of his collar, and he had answered my question about where he would go with another question, *go where,* and stayed at the wharf forty years on real wages and outlived a wife and a child and most of his teeth, and he died in his bed in the room between the storerooms one night without troubling anyone. He was past seventy. There was nothing to say it was but the years. I paid for the burial out of my own purse and not the house's, and could not have told you why it mattered the coin came from me and not Marcia's tally, except that I had bought him once with another man's money and wanted, the second time, to pay with my own.

Eros went the next winter, the letterer, the man who had cut the first crooked Greek Homer into pearwood under my eye and then spent thirty years pulling sheets off the press until his right shoulder hung lower than his left. He had taught Vitalis the cutting before he died, which was the only thing I had asked of him at the end and the only thing he had cared about either, that the boy's lines were clean. So the press did not so much as cough when he stopped. It went on biting its sheets. I stood in the press-room the morning after his pyre and watched the new pressman, whose name I am ashamed to say I had to be told twice, lay a sheet and pull and lay another, and the thing I felt was a cold thing, the machine carrying on across the gap where a man had stood as if the gap were a thing it had been designed to close.

There were others, that stretch of years, the small subtractions, an old carter, the woman who had sewn the first show-bag's seams a lifetime ago and whom I had thought already dead, a clerk of Onesimus's line. You reach an age where the names you have known the longest start going out, one and then a dark stretch and then another, and the room you are standing in gets larger and colder by exactly the light each one took with it, and you stop being surprised, which is its own kind of grief and a meaner one than surprise.

---

Macer did not go out like a lamp. Macer went out like a beam coming down, a thing you have leaned a roof against for so long you have forgotten it was ever a separate object, and then it is on the floor and the roof is on the floor and you are standing in the open with the sky where the dark used to be.

He was ninety-six. Men spoke of it as a freakish thing, an obscenity almost, the way they speak of a flood out of season; the priests of his guild had a word for a man that old that was not quite a compliment. He had outlived three emperors and the use of his own legs and the whole generation of contractors he had cheated and been cheated by, and he had gone on turning the loose gold ring on his wasted finger in a chair by a brazier in his son's house, the son a soft heavy man of sixty who feared him still, and I went to see him in the last of it because Marcia said go and because a man does not refuse Marcia twice in the same season.

The room smelled of leather even then. I do not know how. He had not sold a hide in fifteen years and the house was his son's and reeked of the son's perfume, and under it, faint, banked, the leather, as if the man had soaked in it so long the smell was leaving him slowly. He knew me at once that day, which was not always so anymore. He looked at me down his ruined nose with the broken veins gone gray in the fallen skin and said, *They told me you were sick in the chest.*

I said I had been, in the spring, and it had passed.

*It does not pass,* he said. *It waits.* He turned the ring. *I have had it waiting on me thirty years. You learn to keep it standing in the corner like a creditor you are richer than.* And he laughed, or did the thing that was left of laughing, a dry working of the chest with no sound to it, pleased with himself, pleased to be the one telling the Thulean how a thing worked for once instead of the other way.

We did not say tender things. We had never once in forty-five years said a tender thing and we did not start with him dying, which would have shamed us both. He asked me what the press cleared in a year now and I told him, roughly, in the new figures, and he made me say it twice and did the arithmetic against the cost of the cloth and the wages in his head, the head still doing that when the legs and the bladder and the rest of him had quit, and he said I was paying my pressmen too much, which was true, and that I always had, *and look,* he said, *look where it got you, a warehouse and a wife and your figures on a milestone and not one denarius more than a tight-fisted man would have, you sentimental fool.* He was the man who had owned me. He had bought me for less than a season's mules off a physician's tip, when I could not speak the language well enough to argue the price, and he had penned me in a guarded yard and let me use a peculium that was *not yours, I let you use it as your own,* and he had stood at the wall on the Caelian the day they tried me for my life and spent nothing to save me, and told me later to make myself small and that he would not burn for me. And he had never, in all the years he was afraid, given me up. He could have. There were seasons it would have cost him nothing and bought him a great deal. He let me stand in his atrium and told me to my face he would betray me slow, at an old man's memory, if they pressed him, and then they pressed him, twice that I knew of and likely more I never heard, and the memory stayed slow.

I have tried, since, to make a single clean shape of what I owed him and what he owed me, and I cannot, and I have stopped. He was the cage. He was also the only wall between me and worse cages for the years I needed a wall and could not build my own. A man does not weep for the bars of his cell, and I did not weep, and a man does not entirely fail to feel the cell go either, because the cell is where he learned the language and made the thing and met the wife and lived, in the end, the only life he was going to get. The grief, if it was grief, had no clean edge to take hold of.

He died about a month after, in the chair, in the afternoon, the ring still on him. His son sent word by a slave and I went and stood the correct distance at the pyre, a client at a patron's burning, which is what I legally was to the end, his man, freed past him by an emperor but his man in the rolls and at the rite. There were not many of us. The men who would have stood close were all ash themselves.

The will was the most Macer thing he ever did. He left me the heavy gold ring. Nothing else, no coin, no share, no word of affection for a clerk to read out, just the ring, worth a fair sum in gold, the one he had turned on his thumb when he was thinking for as long as I had known him, which I had assumed without once thinking about it would go to the soft frightened son. The clause, his clerk told me, drafted in Macer's own brutal commercial Latin, ran that the ring should go *to the Thulean, who will know what it cost.* That was all. No name. He had known me forty-five years and put me in his will as the Thulean, the curiosity he had bought cheap and that had made him rich, and that had never once stopped being, to Macer, a barbarian from a place that probably did not exist. And the ring would not go past the swell of my own ruined knuckles either. I could not wear it any more than he could at the end. I had Tyche put it in the box under the floorboard where the dead glass of my first country lies wrapped in oiled wool, two things from two men who never met, one of whom owned me, mine now and no use to anyone, *who will know what it cost.*

---

After Macer the house was running well past me and I knew it.

I would come to the bench in the slack hour with some scheme half-made in my head, a better way to gum the binding so the codices did not split at the spine, and Vitalis, who was thirty now and ran the press as if it were a limb of his own body, would hear me out with the patient face a grown son gives an old father, and then tell me, kindly, that they had tried it two years back when I was abed with the winter cough, and it cracked worse, and the better way was already in use on every sheet in the room. Chloe kept the storeroom day-book in a hand so clean it shamed the clerks of my prime, free now, manumitted out of Marcia's own will against the day, and she read the English cipher better than I could write it. Ulpia had the survey, the African coast laid to the last headland and held there, true and unworked, against a future hand. And Tyche held the whole of it, the cipher and the books and the figures and the thread of the enterprise gathered in her sixty years like reins in a driver's fist, and she did not need me to tell her how to drive.

I had become a passenger in the cart I built. There is a particular humiliation in it that no one warns you of, that the success of a thing you make to outlast you is the exact measure of how little it needs you while you are still alive to watch. I had wanted, since I was a frightened boy writing notes for whoever found his body, to leave a ladder that would stand without me. It was standing. People were climbing it. And I stood at the bottom of it now with my hands too stiff to grip the rung and watched them go up, and was glad, and was the loneliest I had been since the cell, and neither of those cancelled the other.

---

Marcia I had thought would bury me.

She was younger by thirteen years and built like a thing meant to last, square and unhurried, and she had run her own warehouse and then ours through three reigns and a terror and never once been ill that I saw past a day in bed with a flux she beat by force of contempt. She had said it herself, years back in the storeroom, that a thing that is yours dies when you do, and she had meant me, plainly, that I was the perishable one, my head going and my hands following, and she had built the whole succession of the enterprise on the assumption that she would be there to hand it on after me. We both built on it. It was the floor we stood on.

It came on as an ache in the gut that summer, the summer I was sixty-six and she was three-and-seventy, a deep ache that woke her in the night, and then she could not keep her food, and then she went yellow at the eyes and under the nails the way a thing goes that has something gone wrong deep in it where no hand can reach, the kind of slow rot inside that Heras would have named in a breath and shrugged at, because there was no door into it, the same wall I had stood against over Celer, no medicine in this whole world to make. Zoticus came and was careful and good and did the thing Heras taught him, which was to tell it plainly. He told us both, in the storeroom, that it was in the liver, that it would not turn, that it would take some months and they would not be easy, and there were poppy things he could give for the worst of the pain and nothing for the rest.

Marcia heard him out the way she heard a man quote a price she had already decided was too high. Then she asked him how many months, because she had a thing to finish, and he said he could not say, before the cold, likely, and she said that would do, and sent him for the poppy, and went back to the tally-board.

She finished it. It was the deed of trust that moved the warehouse and her own estate clear of Caepio's cousins forever and into the society and the children, the legal wall around the work, drawn so tight no relative and no procurator and no emperor's reaching freedman could pry it loose, and she sat over it with Ulpia reading her the small clauses she could no longer see for the pain and the poppy, and made Ulpia read each twice, and corrected the lawyer's man once on a wording that would have left a finger's-width gap a clever cousin could get a hand into, and closed it. She closed the gap she had spent thirty-five years teaching me to find. Then she let them put her to bed, which she had refused to do until the deed was sealed, and she did not get up again to any purpose.

I sat with her. The one thing I could still do with my ruined hands was sit by a bed and put a cup to a mouth, which I had learned on a road in the east a long way from her, and I did it. She did not want talk. She had never wanted talk. We had been married thirty-five years and most of what passed between us had passed in the medium of work, a tally slid across a table, a contract she would not let me sign, a figure I could not read that she read me in the flat assessing voice that took the world as it was and gave it back at its true weight with nothing added and nothing forgiven. In the bad hours, when the poppy was thinning and before the next dose, she talked a little, and it was never of love, which would have sounded like a man pricing a thing he was afraid of losing. She talked of the house. She told me which of the cousins would try, and how, and which clause would stop them, and that I was not to soften toward the Ennii in the matter of Ulpia, who could marry where she liked or not at all now the deed was hers. She told me Tyche was to be obeyed in everything to do with the money and I was to be humored and ignored, in those words, *humored and ignored,* with the ghost of the old dryness in it, because she knew exactly what I had become in my own house and loved me without one grain of the illusion that I still ran it.

She died before the cold, as Zoticus had said, on a gray morning with the river fog at the window. The breathing went long and shallow toward the end the way Zoticus had told me Heras's had, the spaces between getting wider, and I sat counting them the way you cannot help counting, the arithmetic that is the thing my hands and my head do when the rest of me cannot be trusted, and one space ran out and did not fill, and I went on counting it for a while into the quiet before I understood the count had no more numbers in it.

---

There is a side of the bed.

For thirty-five years there had been a weight on the other side of the bed at night, a warmth, a particular shifting and settling that I had stopped hearing the way you stop hearing the river, a breathing that was the floor under my own bad sleep. The bad sleep was the same after. The black hours and the rats in the warehouse and the river and the bad accounting were all the same. What was gone was the breathing. I would surface in the dark certain it was dawn, the way I always had, and lie there with the old column of the dead running its figures behind my eyes, Sabinus and Geta's hand and Celer and the price of captives, and I would reach, with the worn animal habit of forty years, for the sound of her breathing to set the floor back under it, and there was the river, and there was no floor.

And there were the tallies. She kept the day's loads on a board she would not let the clerks touch, and balanced it last thing and left it by her hand for the morning, and I had a habit, the years my eyes were going and after they were gone, of finding the board she had dropped asleep over and setting it where her hand would fall on it when she woke, because I could not read the figures but I knew the hand, knew the shape of her sevens that did not look like ones, and I would square the board under the dropped stylus by feel and leave it. There was no board now, no hand to drop it and none to find it. I went on, the first nights, half-reaching in the dark for a board that was not there, my fingers patting the empty table by the bed for a thing my body had been doing for years and did not know yet how to stop, and I let them, because telling them to stop felt like a second burial, and they stopped on their own after a while, and that was worse again.

---

I am alone in the house now in the way a man is alone in a house full of other people. Tyche comes most days and runs what I no longer run. Ulpia has the books and the deed and a household of her own forming around her, free of the Ennii. Chloe keeps the day-book. Vitalis runs the press. Zoticus looks in. The children of the children make noise on the storeroom floor. The work goes on without me and the keepers climb the ladder, and I sit at the edge of it, an old foreigner with two relics under a floorboard, the dead glass of a country no one will ever believe in and a dead man's ring that will not fit my hand.

I had thought, when I was young and frightened in this place, that the hardest thing here would be to be the only one who knew. I had not reckoned on this. To be bought and freed and married and surrounded for forty years, and then to come out the far side of it with the work standing and the people gone, and the room around me as large and as cold as the field I woke in.

There is a thing I do now in the slack hour. I have Chloe set the storeroom day-book open on the table before me, and I put my two stiff hands flat on the leaf, on the figures I cannot read, the marks I taught this house and this city and a milestone on the Aurelian road, and I leave them there a while on the cool page. The page gives nothing back. It is only the work, going on, in a hand that is not Marcia's and not mine. After a time I take my hands off it and tell Chloe she may close it, and she closes it, and squares it on the table, and sets the stylus across it for the morning the way someone in this house has always done, and goes home to her own evening, and I sit in the chair until the light is gone and there is nothing left to look at that I could see anyway.
