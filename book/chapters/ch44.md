# Chapter 44: The World Bends

The new figures came to the Aurelian milestone the spring I turned four-and-fifty, and I learned it from a carter who could not read them.

He had stopped his mule at the wharf gate to ask Pamphilus the way to a warehouse, and while they argued about which lane it was he jerked his thumb back up the road and said the magistrates had cut a new stone at the second mile, and a man could tell his distance off it now in shopkeeper's marks, which he supposed was progress if you were a shopkeeper. He said it the way you say a thing that has nothing to do with you. Pamphilus sent him down the right lane and came in and told me, because Pamphilus knew, alone of nearly everyone, where the shopkeeper's marks had come from.

I walked out to see it. It is a thing I would not have done at forty, walked an hour up a hard road to look at a rock, but my eyes were going and my legs were still good and I had reached the age where I would rather use the part of me that worked.

The stone was new-faced, the chisel-marks still pale, an aedile's name carved across the top in proper Roman letters, the whole apparatus of who had paid for the road's repair and under which consuls. And below it, where the older milestones gave the distance to the city in numerals the size of a man's hand, IIII and the rest, this one gave it in mine. A two and a circle. The circle was the trouble. Zero is the mark no Roman wants, the mark for the nothing that is somehow a something, the one I had set down on a wax tablet thirty-seven years before in a cell to a Greek physician who thought I was a lunatic, and here it was cut a thumb deep in stone on the public road, official, paid for, a magistrate's reckoning of how far a man stood from Rome.

I put my hand on it. The cut was sharp under my fingers, the grit of fresh stone, and I stood there long enough that a woman driving a goat went around me wide, the way you go around a man talking to a wall.

I had won that fight cheap and lost it complete. Cheap, because the figures cost me nothing to give and spread themselves on speed alone, fish-sellers' slates and grain-factors' day-books, because a man cheating you with Roman numerals can hide a mistake in a column and a man cheating you in mine cannot. Complete, because somewhere in the years between the slate and the stone they had stopped being the Thulean's marks and become simply the marks, the way you write a number, and no one cutting that milestone had thought of me any more than the carter had, and that was the whole of what I had wanted and it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone of its own. A man wants to be the one who taught the world to count and also wants the world to count without thinking of him, and he cannot have both, and I had the second, which is the one that lasts.

I walked home. The figure stayed in my hand the way the cold of a coin stays.

---

Theophanes took me to the portico the next month, which he did from kindness and a Greek's pride in a collection, not knowing he was showing me my own reflection in a bad mirror.

It stood off the Argiletum behind the booksellers, a colonnade a rich freedman of the imperial house had endowed with his name on the architrave, two long rooms of shelving open to any man who could read and behave. I had heard of it. I had not gone, because I had a horror of going, the way you do not visit a grown child you are afraid you have ruined. Theophanes wanted my eye on a question of cataloguing in the figures, and I went for that, and forgot it the moment I came through the door.

The smell hit first. Not the smell of a library, which is dust and cedar oil and old papyrus going to powder. This was ink, the flat metallic ink we mixed with lampblack and gum at the press, the smell of the wet sheet coming off the block, and under it the rawer smell of new-cut pine boards and the size in fresh-bound covers, the smell of a thing being made faster than it can age.

The shelves on the left were the old library, scrolls in their pigeonholes, a few hundred of them, the accumulated copying of generations of slaves' wrists, each one different, each one a man's eyes and a man's errors. The shelves on the right were mine.

I do not mean they were mine. I mean they had come off the press, and there were more of them than there were scrolls on the other wall, and they were the same. That was the thing the eye could not stop on. A scroll is a single creature; you cannot mistake one for its neighbor. These were ranks of the identical, the times-tables Eros had cut twenty years ago, the rules of the new figures, the geometry primers, cheap Homer and cheaper Aesop, the printed prize-tales from the contest sold for an as and bound up by some industrious soul into a fat ugly codex of pure invention, sixty copies of a thing where there had once been one. Bound flat, in the codex form, because you cannot shelve a stack of printed sheets like a scroll and the form had had to change to hold the flood. The columns of them, the same spine and the same spine and the same spine, made my eyes do a thing I did not like, a kind of vertigo, the multiplication of a single object until it stops being an object.

And there were boys reading them. Not the sons of senators with a Greek tutor at the elbow. A wool-dyer's apprentice, by the stains to the wrist, mouthing the geometry primer with his finger under the line. A girl, which stopped me cold, a slave by her dress and free to be there reading because no one minded a girl reading a printed sheet the way they would have minded her in a scriptorium handling the master's Cicero. The cost had come down until literacy was a thing a poor man could spend an idle hour on, and the printed sheet, being worth almost nothing, had escaped the lock and key that guards the things worth something.

Theophanes was talking about the catalogue. I told him to set the printed works on the figures and the scrolls on the Greek alphabet and the two would have to learn to live on the same shelf, and he laughed and said that was very like me, and I let him think it was a joke about cataloguing.

I had wanted books cheap so that knowledge would stop being a thing you had to be rich to climb toward. I got it. The wool-dyer's boy had it under his finger. I stood in the new room with the new smell and could not make the gladness sit still, because every one of those sixty identical spines was a thing that did exactly what it was set to do, said exactly the same words to every hand that opened it, and a press does not know whether the words are a times-table or a libel, and the same flood that put geometry in a poor boy's idle hour would put whatever a powerful man paid to have set, sixty times, the same, into sixty hands at once, and no scribe's tired wrist to slow it or get a word wrong and break the spell. I had handed Rome a thing that says one thing to everyone, faithfully, and would never once ask what the thing was.

---

I would not have gone to the western yard at all if Hadrian had not sent for the survey in person, and the survey lived at the yard, and so I went down to the coast in the late summer in a wagon that hurt my back, an old man fetched to read his own map to younger men who could read it better.

It was south of the Garonne mouth, a working yard the program had bought into, sheds and slipways and a stink of pitch and oak-sap and the green reek of new-sawn timber, and pilots' talk going in three languages over the noise of the mauls. The survey itself was nothing to look at, leaves of the African coast in Ulpia's clean copying and the men's rougher field hands, leagues and soundings to the one scale in the figures, a coast crawling south a headland a season the way a snail crosses a road. I read what I was wanted to read, and answered what I could, and felt the old fraud's sweat that I knew less than the men I was instructing.

Then the yard-master, a leather-faced Gaul named Bituitus who treated me as a clerk's curiosity and the program as a slow miracle, took me out to the long slip to see what they were building, because he was proud of it and wanted a Roman to be proud with him.

She was up on the ways, half-planked, ribs to the sky like the inside of a whale, and she was wrong. Wrong is what I mean. She was nothing the Romans built. The grain ships that crossed to Alexandria are broad shallow baskets that hug a coast and run before a known wind and would open like a flower in a real sea; everyone at that yard knew it, had buried men who learned it. This thing was deep and narrow and high in the bow, double-planked, the timbers heavier than reason, built to be hit by water from any direction and shrug and stay shut, built by men who had lost two hulls already to the open gray and were building the third to lose better. They had a name for the seam-method that I will not pretend I followed. They had drowned a man getting here. The thing on the ways was an honest answer to a question Rome had never thought to ask before I asked it for them in a senator's house with a lying map.

I stood at her keel and looked up through her ribs at the sky and could not speak for a while, and Bituitus took it for the right kind of silence and let me have it.

She could not cross. I knew that, standing there. She might float in a sea that would sink the grain fleet, she might claw off a lee shore that would have ended a Roman captain, and she could not point her bow at the western blank on my map and arrive, because no man aboard her could tell how far west he had come. Latitude they had, off the sun and the pole, the easy half. The hard half, the westing, wanted a clock that would keep true time through a month of pitching and salt and cold, and the prize for that clock had stood unwon since the year I named it and would go on standing, because I had failed to build a clock that kept true time across a still tally-room, and I knew no man living who could. The hull was real. The dream the hull was built toward was a coast I had drawn from a dying phone's memory and a course no instrument on earth could yet hold.

I had told Hadrian the west was uninhabited and a year's sail off, and neither of those was a thing I knew, and one of them was very likely a lie, and these men were spending their lives and now and then a life learning to build the ship that would carry that lie out past the point where a man could turn back. Bituitus put his hand on a rib the way I had put mine on the milestone, and said, in his coast-Latin, that the third one would swim, he could feel it in her, and I said I believed him, and meant it, and that was the worse half of the day.

---

The boy I met on the road back, near the second mile, by chance, by the milestone with the circle cut in it.

He was perhaps nine, a carter's get by the look of him, left to mind a parked wagon and a dozing mule while his father did business off the road, and he was doing what a boy does with an empty hour, which is making a world. He had a stick. He was drawing in the dust of the road's edge, and talking, half to the mule and half to no one, and I stopped my wagon because my driver wanted to water the beasts at the trough by the stone and because I had heard the boy say a word I knew.

He was building a ship in the dust. A ship with no oars and no sail, he was explaining to the mule, which did not care, a ship that ate fire in its belly and breathed smoke out a stack and walked on the water faster than the wind whether the wind blew or not, and it would go past the edge of the sea to the country no one had found yet, and he, the boy, would be the captain, and he would be rich, and he had seen it in a tale his uncle had bought printed for two coins and read to him until he had it by heart.

He had the steam-ship. He had Felix's steam-ship, near enough, the contest-tale of a self-fueling pot that drives a hull, the one that won the year a counting-house slave stood up and named the metal that does not yet exist, printed cheap and sold for an as and read aloud by a carter's brother to a boy of nine who now believed, with the whole of his nine-year-old belief, that such a ship was a thing the world contained and that he might one day stand on its deck.

It does not exist. I want to be exact about that, the way I was exact with the milestone and the hull. There is no pot in the world that drives a ship; the trick of it, the bridge from the fire to the turning, had been in my head once at seventeen and had worn out of it years ago and not come back, and the steel to hold the fire if I could remember the trick is the steel Hermes has failed at for thirty years and will die failing at, and Felix after him. The boy in the dust had the dream a clean generation ahead of any hope of the thing. He had it whole, with the confidence of a child, the way I once had a country whole, and he drew its stack in the dust and made the smoke-noise with his mouth and steered it past the trough where my mules were drinking, out over the edge of the only world he knew, toward a coast that I had put on a map.

I gave him a coin, which frightened him, an old stranger leaning down off a wagon, and his father came around the wagon-tail at the wrong moment and started apologizing for the boy bothering a gentleman, and I said the boy had not bothered me, and we went on, and behind us I heard the steam-noise start up again the moment the wagon was a safe distance off.

---

The thing I carried back up the road was not one thing and would not resolve into one.

Felix was a quarter-mile from the cattle market that month, on my foundation's coin, failing at the pot the boy had just sailed in the dust, and I had paid for both ends of it, the failure and the dream, the man who could not build the thing and the printed tale that taught a nation of children to want it. Hermes was failing at the steel that the pot needs and the hull needs and the cannon needed and I had not built and never would. The cannon I had built, the one that burst on purpose to argue for the steel, had taught Rome a thing too, taught it that a tube of metal and the right gray powder will throw a stone across a river and that the powder is dung and ash and the yellow rock from the volcano country, and that knowledge was loose now, off in the world doing whatever loose knowledge does, sixty copies the same, faithful to whoever held the page.

I had set out to teach a slave-holding empire to count, and read, and dream, and build ships that go where its masters had never let it imagine going, and I had done some fraction of all of it, and the milestone had my figures and the portico had my books and the western slip had my hull and the boy in the dust had my lie, and not one of those things knew my name or cared what I had wanted it for. They served. That was the property I had not thought hard enough about when I started giving things away in a cell to stay alive. A figure serves the man who reckons with it, an honest grain-factor or a tax-farmer skinning a province. A book serves the page it carries, true or false. A faster ship goes wherever its owner points the bow. A powder throws the stone the gunner aims. I had spent thirty-seven years making Rome stronger at the things Rome chose to do, and I had never once been asked, and would never be asked, what Rome chose to do with strength.

The wagon brought me back through the gate at dusk, into the wharf-stink and the noise of my own house, and I did not go up. I went into the press-room, where the day-shift was breaking up and Vitalis was setting the bench to rights, and stood among the racked blocks and the smell of the ink that was now the smell of a public portico off the Argiletum, and I watched the boy rack the day's cut blocks and damp the inking-pads and bank the stove, the ordinary closing of an ordinary day's work, the machine I had built to outlive me being put to bed by a hand that was not mine and would do it the same tomorrow whether I lived or not. Vitalis asked if I needed light to see by, since my eyes, and I said no, I knew the room, and he wished me good night in the easy way of a young man to an old one, and went, and left me in it.
