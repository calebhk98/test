# Chapter 36: The Ocean Gambit

I broke his province in eight months, and the breaking of it bought me the door I had spent a winter pretending not to want.

It was Lugdunensis, in Gaul, a fat tidy province with a fat tidy lie running under its grain figures. I ran the accounts both ways, the Roman wall and the new stair side by side, and where they disagreed I marked it and went no further. Three places. Two were a clerk skimming cartage. The third was older and ran up into a name I did not write down, and I left a clean gap in the column where a stroke had gone missing across nine years, and let the gap do the accusing.

I did not see Hadrian for the better part of a year, which was its own message, and I read it the way Marcia read a slow-paying client, that he can pay and is teaching you that you cannot make him. So I was the dull foreigner in the back room who had ruined a great deal of wax, exactly as I had asked to be, and the asking had been true when I made it and a lie within the month. The door was open now, and a man does not stand at an open door forever telling himself he prefers the wall. What I wanted was past it. I had carried it twenty-three years.

---

The summons, when it came, came badly dressed. High summer, the city stewing, the river dropped to its stones, and a clerk arrived at the wharf in the worst hour of the afternoon with an escort of two and the manner of a man told he is carrying something but not what. The tablet wanted the accounts of a second province in the marks, Belgica this time, at a house I knew. More wax and more clerks and the same back room, said the tablet, and I knew it was not, because a thing that is only more of the same does not come with an escort of two.

Marcia knew it too. She read the tablet twice and set it down flat.

"He has decided what you are for. Or he is about to, and wants to look at you while he does it. You will give him Belgica." I had Celer's strap on the bench and had been working the buckle without knowing. "He had a year to be bored by how well you keep your head down. A man like that gets suspicious of a quiet man for the same reason he gets suspicious of a loud one. He thinks the quiet is for something." She slid the strap away from me, the way you take a thing from a child without making it a contest. "Is it for something."

I did not answer her, which was an answer, and she let it be one. That night I went down to the press-room where Tyche kept the second key, and we took the box out from where I am not allowed to know it lives now, and I unrolled the map for the first time in years.

It had been recopied twice, the linen gone soft. The Mediterranean was honest. Africa was a guess that ran too narrow to a point I had no right to. India was small and wrong. And across the western edge, past a blank I had drawn wide on purpose, the two continents sat where a seventeen-year-old had put them from the memory of a screen, badly, unmistakably, a long broken shore curving away into ice and into nothing, a strait at the waist I could not have placed within five hundred miles and had drawn anyway because I knew it was there.

Tyche held the lamp. She had read the framing back to me in the cipher a hundred times and said none of it now. She looked at the western shore the way you look at a thing a man should not have, and said, in the cipher, so the words could not be in the room in Latin, "You are going to point him at your house."

"I'm going to point him at the ocean."

"Your house is on the other side of the ocean." She rolled it for me, careful of the soft linen, and put it in my hand. "You spent twenty-three years making sure no one could follow you home. Now you are going to draw the road."

There was nothing to say to it that would not have been the loud thing, and we did not do that to each other.

---

The house on the slope was the same and the man in it had aged the way a hard man ages, barely and all at once, gray come into the beard at the jaw, the restlessness banked down into something heavier. He was past forty-five now and I past forty, and we had both been emperor and barbarian for a year and knew the parts. He let me get the bow done before he spoke.

"Belgica," he said. "Sit where the light is. You found me a thief in Gaul."

"I found Caesar a gap in a column. Caesar found the thief."

"You left the gap exactly the width of a thief." He almost smiled. "You did not write the name. You will tell me you did not know it."

"I knew the name, Caesar. I am a freedman who counts. The naming of great men is for great men."

He waved Belgica's tablets to a corner where they sat untouched the whole afternoon. "Then a thing crossed my table I cannot forget," he said, coming around the table, the forge-heat of his attention full on, "and it has your smell on it though your name is nowhere near it. A man copying your Gaul accounts said the foreigner once drew a map of the whole world on a wax tablet, before he could speak a word of Latin, to save his neck. Was it true."

So the door had opened itself. I felt the whole winter of work tip and start to slide, and made myself slow.

"It was true, Caesar. A frightened boy with a stylus, drawing badly, to seem worth keeping alive. The magistrate copied it onto a leaf, and I have regretted some of what I drew ever since."

"Regretted." The word caught him the way I had known a word would. He collected the things other men regretted, because a regret is a door left ajar. "Show me. Not the boy's. The man's. You have had twenty-three years to get it less wrong. Show me the world."

---

A whole winter of work had assumed I would pry his hand open, and instead he had put the thing in my hand and asked, and the danger in being asked is that you answer fast. I drew it slowly, the Mediterranean first, because the whole of it had to ride on the part he could check.

He checked it. He put his own knowing against my coast, Spain where he was born, the gulf of Gaul, the boot, Greece, the long African shore his grain fleets ran, and found it righter than the maps in his own library, and his breath changed. Then Egypt, the Red Sea, Arabia, on east, and here I stayed honest, told him India was a guess and likely wrong, that I did not know where the eastern lands stopped and would not pretend. The honesty about the east bought the rest. He liked a man who said *I do not know* about the right thing.

"And the war the dead emperor spent himself on," he said, "lay there." He set two fingers on the rivers, on the cities he had given back. "You served it. You buried men there for a province I struck off the rolls in a morning." This was a trap and an honesty at once, the way his best ones were.

"I did, Caesar." Celer's road was under those two fingers. I did not let it into my face. "I buried a friend there, holding a road to a city you were right to let go. I have thought about which way the error ran. It did not run in the letting go."

He took his fingers off the map. Something in him settled, because I had handed him without being asked the thing he most wanted told and could not be seen to ask for, that abandoning the east had been wisdom and not weakness, said by a man who had paid for it. I had not planned to spend Celer there. I spent him.

"Go on," he said, lower. "You stopped at the edge. There is more of the leaf."

"There is the ocean, Caesar."

"There is the Ocean. The river that runs round the world. Homer's Ocean," he said, fond, a scholar's word.

"No, Caesar." It was the one place I would correct him, chosen in advance, because the gambit needed him to learn one true thing from me and feel its size. "A width. Water with a far side." And I drew it, the wide blank I had always drawn, and then across it, slow, the long broken shore, north into ice, south into ice, the strait at the waist, the two great unnamed lands where no Roman map had ever put a thing but the word *Oceanus* and a drawing of waves.

The room went quiet the way it had when he caught the stolen stroke. He bent over the western shore the way he had bent over his dome, and when he straightened his face had hunger in it, which I had wanted and been afraid of.

---

"Land," he said.

"Land. A man's height of guess in every line of it, Caesar. There is a far shore. I do not know its true distance, nor its true shape, nor whether the strait is one strait or fifty. A boy drew the bones of it and a man has not improved them much." I gave him the doubt straight, because the doubt was the hook; a thing offered with honest doubt is a problem, and he could not leave a problem alone. "What I know is that it is there. Rome has reached east until the east threw her off, south until the sand stopped her, north until the cold and the trees stopped her, and the one direction she has never put a keel into is west, into the open sea, where the world's true size is written and no one has read it."

"Because no one comes back," he said. "Sailors who go west of the Pillars come back along the coast or do not come back. You cannot crawl along a shore that is not there."

"No, Caesar. You cannot." Now the turn, the one Marcia and I had built and rebuilt, the one that had to be his idea inside the hour. "Which is why no one will ever go for plunder. There is nothing out there a man would cross a year of open water to steal that he could not steal closer. A general would be a fool to want it, and it would eat his army. But you have just sold three provinces. You are the emperor who counts. The one thing out there worth a freedman's afternoon is the thing no army can carry home, which is the true shape of the world, measured, mapped, in Rome's hand, the first and last time it will ever be done, because there is only one first time, and the man whose reign does it owns it forever, the way Alexander owns the East in men's mouths though the East threw his heirs off inside a lifetime."

I had said it too fast at the end, and I shut my mouth and let the room hold it. Then he did the thing I had watched him do with the press, and I let him, and watched myself let him.

"You are describing," he said slowly, as if the thought rose in him for the first time, "a thing no general could be trusted with and no merchant would pay for, that returns nothing but knowledge. A thing only a state can do, and only a certain kind of master of a state would think worth doing." He looked up. "Alexander conquered what he could reach and wept that there was no more. A greater man would have measured the part he could not reach, and not wept." The two fingers scooped the air over the western blank, the gourd-drawing gesture, possession made with the hands. "I would be the man who knew the shape of the world."

"Caesar would be that man," I said. Let him be the one who knows.

"Do not agree so quickly. I have told you." But he was already gone into it, and I had lost him to the idea, and that was the winning of it, and it tasted of ash, because he had taken it whole inside a quarter-hour, exactly as I had feared a year ago, and the only difference between that fear and this triumph was that this time I had wanted him to.

---

Then he came back, because his hunger had teeth on both ends.

"Thule," he said. I had known it would come and still flinched under the skin.

"You drew the north too." He put his finger above Britain, on the blank where the boy's tablet had once made a physician say a flat two-syllable word. "There is your country, that writes in marks, that no one has reached and you alone have left. And here, west." The finger swung down to the great shore. "You will tell me Thule is out there, and that this is how you know there is a far side, because you crossed it once and forgot the way. Scouts, the report says. Thulean scouts who know the western water. Tell me about your scouts."

The whole lie stood up in the room and asked to be defended, and I had learned on this floor a year before that the way to die in front of this man was to defend a thing he had already half-seen through. So I gave him a third of it and let him keep the suspicion, because a man allowed his suspicion stops digging for the rest.

"Caesar, I will tell you what I tell no one, since you have read most of it." I let the wax leaf lie between us. "I do not know if any man living has crossed that water. I have said scouts because a frightened man says scouts, the way a frightened man draws a map. What I had, coming here with nothing, was the knowledge that the far shore is there. How I came by it is the one thing I have never made a Roman believe, the same as Thule. Write me down a liar about the road and you will not be wrong. The shore is still there. The marks still cannot be cooked. A man can be a liar about where he is from and a true map still be a true map. Strabo called Pytheas a liar, and Pytheas had still seen the north."

He held my eyes a long time. I had handed him permission to disbelieve the lie, in order to keep the truth, and he was clever enough to feel the trade and take it knowing it was a trade.

"I think you are a liar," he said, without heat, almost with pleasure. "I think there are no scouts and no Thule, and you came from somewhere you will carry to your grave, and I find I do not care, because the map is the map and a man's hidden country is his own affair until it is mine." He tapped the western shore. "But I will keep the matter of where you are from." He meant the ledger, and let me see he meant it. "Now. Tell me why no state has done it, since the why is always the true price."

---

This was the part I could not lie my way past, and did not try, because the wall was real and I had broken myself on it already and he would find that out the moment his own men touched it.

"Two reasons, Caesar, and the second has beaten me." I drew a hull on the wax, crude. "First, ships. Yours crawl from headland to headland and run for harbor when the sky turns. The open Ocean will break them. You would need hulls and rigging Rome does not build, fit to take a far sea for weeks out of sight of land and claw back up into a wind. That is years and money, and it is the easy reason." I set the reed down. "The hard reason is this. A ship out of sight of land does not know where it is. How far north or south, any pilot finds by the sun and the stars; that is old. How far east or west he has come, no man can find. There is no star for it. A fleet could sail a year toward your shore and not know on any day whether the shore was a week ahead or a month, or whether the storm in the night had set them a hundred miles off the line into open water forever."

He was following it exactly. "And the solution."

"Is a clock, Caesar." Here I had to be most careful, because for once the survival law and the truth pointed the same way. "Carry on the ship a perfect clock set to the hour of the home port, and read the true hour of the sun where the ship is. The gap between the two is your westing, exactly, every day, how far around the world you have come." I let it sit. "And I must tell Caesar, because his own men will tell him inside a season, that I have tried for years to build a clock that keeps true time standing still in a warm room, and failed. I have a water-clock in my tally-room that a girl corrects against the sun twice a day, because it cannot be trusted past a morning. The thing the voyage needs is a thing I cannot make. I know no living man who can."

The room was silent. I had told the emperor the gift was gated on a wall I had broken my own head against, on purpose. A thing offered as easy he would seize and squander and blame me when it failed; a hard problem the size of a reign he could not leave alone, would make his own, and would not blame me for, because I had warned him. Warning him was the price of surviving the day the wall held.

He stood a long while over the crude hull and the word *clock* scratched in the wax. "So you bring me," he said at last, "a far shore you cannot reach, in ships I do not have, steered by an instrument no man can build." Something in the beard was almost a grin and was mostly the cold appraisal under it. "It is the most honest thing anyone has tried to sell me in a year."

"It is the truest thing I have, Caesar."

---

He did not launch a fleet. I had not let myself believe he would, and he did not. He gave it the shape of a consolidator who counts. No armada, no year's voyage west, not now, not soon, perhaps not in his reign or mine, and he said so in those words, that he was no Trajan and would not feed an ocean what Trajan fed a desert. What he sanctioned, over that summer and into the next year as the orders worked down through the fiscus, was bounded and exact, and I learned it in pieces, from clerks.

A survey first. The world Rome already held was to be mapped properly, the true coasts taken and set down in the new figures to a single scale, beginning with the seas he already sailed, the African shore pushed south of where the grain fleets went, a season at a time, recording and returning, no man sent past where he could come home from. A school of pilots and shipwrights at a yard on the western coast, he had not settled where, to build and try hulls for the outer sea, slowly, at a cost he named and would not pass. And the clock made a standing problem of the reign, with a purse behind it, offered to any craftsman who could keep true time through a month at sea, and I was to write down everything I knew of why it was hard and where I had failed, so the men who came after would not waste their years on my dead ends.

It was a tenth of the thing in my chest, the cautious near edge of it, and it was real, it had imperial wax on it, and Rome had turned her face, by a few degrees and for the first time on my account, toward the water she had always run from.

"You will write the why of the clock," Hadrian said, gathering the leaves, mine among them now, the western shore folded into his keeping where I would never get it back. "And you will tell no one the part about your scouts, since you have admitted there are none, and I prefer my survey to rest on a true map and not a barbarian's tale. The far shore is mine to find. Thule is yours to keep lying about. We will each have what we want." He paused at the door of his own attention, half gone from me. "You brought it to me and not to a general, which tells me you were afraid of what a general would do with it, which tells me you are afraid of the right things. Go and do Belgica. And send up the clock."

---

They walked me down the slope at dusk, the smell of the city coming up to meet me, fish and lamp-soot and the day's heat off the stones, and the lie was loose now, going up the rolls and out to the pilots and into the reign, the uninhabited shore, the year's reachable sail, the country a frightened boy had drawn to stay alive. Men would die against it eventually, in good hulls, sailing toward an empty shore that was not empty and a year's water that would not be enough, trusting a map a kid had drawn from a screen, steered by no clock. Or they would never sail at all, the yard would rot, and the whole of it come to a line in some clerk's accounts. I could not tell which, and I had aimed it anyway.

At the wharf the lamp was lit and Marcia sat at the table with the Belgica tablets already squared, and she saw what I was carrying before I set it down, the way she saw a short load through a sealed crate.

"He took it," she said.

"A tenth of it. A survey. A yard. He would not feed the ocean." I sat. "He kept the map."

"He kept the map." She did not make it a reproach. She made a mark and slid the Belgica accounts across, and I should have taken them up. Instead I went down to the press-room where Tyche was banking the lamps, and stood a while at the cold press with the empty box open on the bench where the map had lived for twenty-three years. The box was the size and shape of the thing no longer in it. I closed the lid on the nothing inside, and went up, finally, to count Belgica, where the numbers stayed where I set them and did not have a far side.
