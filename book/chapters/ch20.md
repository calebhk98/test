# Chapter 20: Tyche

The trouble with buying a person you care about is that you have to do it the way you'd buy a mule, in front of a man who knows you can't walk away.

I went to Macer's about it in the spring after the contest, on a clear cold morning with the river smell coming up the streets, and I made the mistake of dressing well. I had a toga now, a citizen's toga, which I still couldn't drape without Pamphilus pinning the back fold and which I'd worn maybe six times, and I put it on because I thought it would make me look like a man Macer had to treat as an equal. He took one look at me in his atrium, with the leather smell thick around us and a clerk waiting at the side with a tablet, and he laughed the way he laughed when I'd done something he found genuinely funny, which was rare and which I'd learned to dread.

"You came to buy a girl in your wedding sheet," he said. "Sit down, Thulean, before you trip on it."

I sat. He turned the gold ring on his thumb and looked at me for a while, pleasant, taking his time, the way he took his time when he was about to do business he was going to enjoy.

I had rehearsed this. I'd told myself I would be light about it, that I would make it sound like tidying up, the freedman buying back the household furniture, of no more weight than the cloth tally she kept. I'd told myself not to let him see it mattered. I had been telling myself that for two years, since I'd said *Pamphilus first* and watched her take it without a flicker, and I had failed to think hard enough about what it meant that I'd needed to tell myself.

"The girl Tyche," I said. "She keeps my tally. I'd like to buy her, the way I bought Pamphilus. Name a price and I'll have the silver to you."

"Pamphilus," Macer said, "is a porter with a strong back and a collar I'd riveted on him because he'd run twice. I sold you a man who was worth four thousand because his legs were good and his head was empty and I was tired of feeding a thing that wanted to be elsewhere." He let that sit. "You paid over the odds for him because you wanted him free that week and I knew it. Four thousand for a porter. I told my wife and she didn't believe me."

"And Tyche."

"Tyche," he said, and smiled, "keeps your tally."

So he knew. I had spent five years hiding it, hiding her, telling Onesimus she was slow and let her do the donkey-work of the cloth count because she was reliable and dull, keeping her sums off any tablet that left the yard, teaching her in the back of the tally-room with the door shut and a story ready about why the door was shut, because of the thing he'd said the day he gave me the workshop, that he would sell the cleverer slave to a factor if I made his hands worth more than he could keep. I had built the whole architecture of her invisibility on that one sentence, and I sat there in my stupid toga and understood that he had seen through all of it, possibly from the start, and had simply been letting me think I was clever.

"How long have you known," I said.

He shrugged the big shoulders, the wool moving. "You hid a girl who does in her head what my counting-house does with an abacus and a freedman and an afternoon. You hid her by telling Onesimus she was slow. Onesimus has been keeping books since before you were born on whatever rock you crawled off, and he came to me three years ago and said your slow girl had caught two errors of his that the abacus missed and that you'd told her to stop. He thought you were protecting your own importance." Macer's mouth did something dry. "I let you keep your secret because the secret was working for me. A clever beast that nobody knows is clever stays cheap and stays put. I've owned a hundred slaves, Thulean. You think I can't smell the one that's worth money."

"Then name it."

"Forty thousand," Macer said.

I had brought, in a strongbox on a cart with two of Macer's old yard men I'd hired to guard it because I trusted them more than strangers, eight thousand sesterces, which was twice what I'd paid for Pamphilus and which had felt, loading it, like an obscene amount of metal to set against one person. Forty thousand was most of everything I had. It was the contest fund and the press and the next two years of the yard. It was a senator's furniture, a small farm, a frontier officer's pay for more years than Celer had served.

"She's a tally-keeper," I said, and heard my own voice climb and hated it. "She's a girl of eighteen who can do sums. Forty thousand buys a Greek tutor and a cook and a litter team and you know it."

"It buys what a man will pay for it." He wasn't enjoying it now, or he was enjoying it the way you enjoy winning a thing you'd rather not have had to win. "A Greek who can read Homer to your sons is worth what a Greek's worth, there are shiploads of Greeks. A girl who can audit a grain factor's books faster than the factor and won't run because she's got nowhere better, sold to the right house, to a man with shipping and contracts and a need to know which of his stewards is stealing, that girl is worth what you cannot afford to find out. I've had two offers I didn't go looking for. I told you. A clever beast doesn't stay secret in a city this size. People come into your yard. People watch a girl reckon."

"You said you'd sell her to a factor."

"I said I would if you made her worth selling. You did. I didn't. I held her for you, you ungrateful barbarian, against two men who'd have given me what I'm asking, because I knew you'd come for her eventually with your sheet on and your sad face, and I'd rather sell her to you and keep you sweet than sell her to Vibius Maximus and lose the man who flies." He leaned back. "Forty thousand. I'm not gouging you. I'm telling you what you let her become."

There it was. He had put the price on her, and the price was true, and the truth of it was the worst thing in the room, worse than if he'd been cheating me, because if he'd been cheating me I could have been angry at Macer. He'd made me look at the number she was, and the number was high because of everything I'd taught her, and so the bill, when it came, was a bill for her mind, which I had loved like the one piece of home I'd managed to grow in this place, and which I had now to buy back at full market rate from the man who happened to own the body it lived in.

I am not going to dress up what I felt sitting there. I wanted to break his face with the gold ring still on it. I wanted to tell him exactly what I thought of a world where a person was a line in a counting-house, and I had the words, I'd had the words for years, the whole speech, and I knew with total clarity that the speech would cost me the deal and her the freedom and would change precisely nothing about the law, and so I sat in my wedding sheet and did sums in my head about what I could sell.

"Thirty," I said, because you don't take the first number, even when you've already lost.

"Thirty-eight," Macer said, "and you bring your water-barrels and your foreign filter into my country house at Tibur, free, and you teach my steward's boys your shopkeeper's numbers, so that when you're dead or grand and gone I'm not the last man in Rome doing his accounts the slow way. Thirty-eight and a string attached. I'd rather have the string than the two thousand."

"Done," I said, before I could think about it, because I'd have given him fifty.

He had the clerk write it. A *bill of sale*, a tablet, one slave, female, called Tyche, of his household, sold to Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified, for thirty-eight thousand sesterces, and the clerk's stylus going through the wax and the figure written in Roman letters because Macer would not have his own sale recorded in my marks, and the leather smell, and Macer turning the ring, and that was how I came to own another human being for the length of an afternoon.

He saw my face when the clerk turned the tablet for my mark. "Don't," he said, almost kindly. "You're going to free her the moment you're out my door, I know you are, and you'll feel clean about it, and that's your business. But you bought her, boy. Whatever you do after. Sign your grand name."

I signed my grand name.

***

He couldn't let me leave without the joke. He walked me to the atrium door himself, which he didn't do for clients, his hand heavy on my shoulder over the toga, and at the threshold where his porter could hear and the morning's last few hangers-on could hear he said, loud and good-humored, "Thirty-eight thousand for an eighteen-year-old girl. Marcus Ulpius Danihel, the emperor's own freedman, who doesn't keep a woman, who's never been seen to keep a woman, and pays a small farm for one with short hair and a quick tongue." He patted my shoulder. "I'd have sold her cheaper if you'd told me what you wanted her for. Buy her a comb."

The hangers-on laughed. The porter kept his face still the way porters do.

I had known it was coming. I'd known since I started planning this that there is exactly one story Rome tells about a man who buys a young woman and keeps her in his house, and that the story does not change because the man is a freedman with a yard and a strange reputation, and does not change because the truth is that I thought of her as the closest thing I had to a sister in a thousand miles of strangers, because Rome did not have a slot for *the closest thing I had to a sister* and did have a very well-worn slot for the other thing. I'd thought I'd weathered it in advance. I hadn't. Standing in the door with men laughing about what they assumed I'd been doing to a girl I'd watched grow up, I felt the particular sick heat of being talked about in a way that was filthy and that I couldn't correct without sounding like exactly the kind of man who'd protest too much.

"For her sums," I said, which was true and which got another laugh, because of course it did, it was the thing the man in the story always said.

Macer grinned, not unkind, just a man who found the world funny because he'd given up being shocked by it decades ago. "Tell yourself stories, Thulean," he said. "Just don't put her name on a contract next to yours where a magistrate can see it, or the story tells itself."

That stopped me, on the step, and I think he meant it to. Because under the dirty joke there was a real warning, the kind Macer gave for free when he could afford to, and the warning was this. The gossip wasn't a danger to me. I was a man, and the worst Rome would think of me was the ordinary thing it thought of every man with money and a household. The danger was hers. A freedwoman living unmarried in the house of the man who'd bought and freed her, doing his books, going where he sent her, was a woman whose respectability was a thing other people got to decide, and the deciding had already started, in Macer's doorway, with men I'd never see again. I had been about to make her free, and I had not once, in two years of planning the silver, thought hard about what I was making her free *into.*

***

I freed her in the yard, that afternoon, badly.

I'd meant to do it properly, the full ceremony, *manumissio* before a magistrate, the rod laid on the shoulder, the turning, the whole legal machinery that makes a freedwoman a citizen of limited kind and not a Junian half-thing the way Pamphilus had come out, lesser-free, his children's status still a question. I'd looked into it. I had standing now to do it right, and I wanted it right for her, the real kind, the kind with the name and the cap and the law behind it. But the magistrate's court took days to get into and I had a tablet in my hand that said I owned her and I could not stand to own her for days, so I did the informal thing first, in the tally-room, in front of Pamphilus and Heras, who I'd asked to come, and I said the words, that she was free, that I freed her, *inter amicos,* among friends, witnesses present, and I told her we'd do the court part as soon as I could get the date, for her citizenship, for the proper kind.

I had imagined this moment for two years. In the version in my head she wept, or she didn't weep, or she said something I could carry around afterward, and in all the versions she looked at me a particular way, and I am ashamed of how much I'd wanted the way she'd look at me.

She stood by her table with the day's tally still open on it and listened to the whole thing with her arms folded, and when I finished she said, "How much."

"What?"

"How much did he take you for." Her voice was flat and clean and city-quick, the way it had always been, the way she'd never slowed it for me even when my Latin was broken and she was fourteen. "I keep your books. I'll find it in a month when I balance the year. You can tell me now or I can find it and know you didn't tell me."

"Thirty-eight thousand," I said. "And the filter at Tibur, and teaching the steward's boys the figures."

Something moved in her face and was gone, fast, like a fish turning under water. She looked at the open tally. "Thirty-eight," she said. "More than nine of Pamphilus." She was doing it in her head, I could see her doing it, the arithmetic of herself, and I understood I had told her the one number I should have lied about and that lying would have been worse. "You priced low on the porter and they laughed at you. You priced high on me and they laughed at you. There's no number where they don't laugh."

"Tyche."

"I'm not thanking you," she said.

It landed exactly where she'd aimed it. I'd told myself I didn't want her gratitude, and that had been a comfortable lie, because some part of me had absolutely been waiting for it, had bought it in advance with the silver, and she'd seen that, the way she saw the errors the abacus missed, and she'd refused to pay.

"I'm not asking you to," I said, which was the second lie of the afternoon.

"You hid me." She wasn't loud. Heras had gone very still by the door, and Pamphilus was looking at the floor. "Five years. You taught me the figures and then you told the old man I was slow and you told *me* to stop catching his mistakes so I'd stay cheap and stay in the yard. You did it to keep me from being sold to a factor, I know, I'm not stupid, I heard the threat the same day you did, I was carrying the bread. I know you were saving me." She put her hand flat on the tally, on her own neat columns. "But what it was, every day, was you deciding how much of me Rome got to see. You. A girl who can do this" - the hand on the columns - "had to spend five years pretending she couldn't, so that the man who'd been good to her could keep being able to be good to her. You don't know what it is to make yourself small on purpose every single day so that nobody clever buys you. You decided I'd be slow. For my own good. And now you've decided I'm free. For my own good. And the comb." Her chin went up. "I heard about the comb. The wharf heard about the comb before I did."

The Tibur joke had beaten the cart back to the yard. Of course it had.

"That's the part I got wrong," I said. "Macer's right that it's a danger and he's wrong about who it's a danger to. It's not on me. It's on you. A woman in my house, freed by me, unwed, keeping my books and walking where I send her. They've already started telling the story. I should have thought about that two years ago and I didn't, because the only thing I thought about was the silver, and I'm sorry."

She looked at me for a long moment. It was not the look from the versions in my head. It was the look she gave a column that didn't sum, working out where the error was.

"You think you freed me into freedom," she said. "You freed me into being a freedwoman, which is a citizen who can't hold office and owes you duties and whose word a magistrate weighs at a freedwoman's weight, and you freed me into being a woman, which Rome owns a different way, papers or no papers. I'm eighteen. With no man and no family and your bought name on me. You know what a free poor woman alone is in this city? She's a thing waiting to be married off by whoever takes an interest, or worse, and the worse is most of it." She said it without self-pity, like a sum. "Freeing me was the easy part. It was thirty-eight thousand and an afternoon. Now there's the rest of my life, in a city that has a story about me already, and I get to live it, not you."

Heras spoke for the first time, dry, from the door. "She's not wrong, you know," he said, to me, in Greek, which she half-followed now and pretended not to. "You solved a problem and bought yourself a harder one. Congratulations. It's what you always do."

***

So I let her set the terms, because she was right that they were hers to set, and because I had finally understood, two years too late, that the whole point was that they were hers to set.

She would stay at the yard, she said, because she had nowhere better and the work was real and she liked the work, which was as close to a kindness as she'd give me that day. She would be paid, a real freedwoman's wage, on a real reckoning, no peculium-fiction, no allowance, money she could keep and bank and that I could not take back. Her name would go on nothing of mine, no contract, no shared lease, nothing a magistrate or a wagging tongue could read as the other story. She would have her own room with a door that barred from the inside, away from mine, in the front building with Pamphilus and his wife between, so the geography of the yard itself argued for her. And the court date, the proper *manumissio*, the citizenship, I would get it within the month or she would know I'd lied about that too.

"And one more," she said.

"Anything."

"Don't say *anything* to me like a man in a story. You don't have anything. You have a yard and a borrowed name and more enemies than you count." She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile of the afternoon and it was at my expense. "Teach me the thing you won't teach anyone. The real one. Not the figures, everyone has the figures now, the fish-sellers have the figures. The other thing. The one you do at night when you think we're asleep, the marks that aren't Latin and aren't Greek and aren't the figures, the ones in the little book you keep against the dead glass."

I had thought no one had seen me writing English.

***

I'd kept it back from everyone for nine years. Heras had glimpsed a line of it once and called it a homeland script and I'd let him, because that was the cover I'd built for it, that the people of Thule wrote this way in their cold north, and it was useful precisely because no one in the world could read it but me, which made it the only truly private thing I owned, the only place I could put the things I couldn't say to anyone.

The encyclopedia was going to be in it. I'd known that for a while. Everything I carried that mattered, the ladder I wanted to leave so that someone, someday, could climb past the steel wall and the flux and the rest, all of it had to go down in a script that the priests couldn't read for blasphemy and the rivals couldn't steal and the magistrates couldn't subpoena, in a hand that was mine and dead and unfalsifiable, the script of a country no one could check. And it could not stay only mine, because I was one bad cut from dead and then it would all die behind my teeth, which was the fear that had grown, over the years, into the biggest one I had.

I got the little book and the dead phone fell out of the cloth onto the table, the black glass, and she looked at it the way she looked at everything, once, hard, and let it go without a question, which was its own kind of mercy.

I made a line of letters. *A B C D.* I said them. I made her name in it, the Roman way it sounded, T-Y-C-H-E, and turned the book so she could see, and watched her face do the thing I'd seen it do over the figures five years before, the stillness that meant the machine inside her had started turning and would not stop.

"It goes left to right," I said. "Like Latin. There are only these, look, twenty-six marks, the same twenty-six, and they make every word there is, you don't learn a new mark for a new word, you spell the sound. The figures were the sounds of numbers. These are the sounds of everything else."

She put her finger on her own name. "*Tyche.*"

"That's the *y* sound. We argue about that one even at home."

"At home," she said, and looked at me, because I never said *at home,* I had spent nine years not saying *at home,* and she filed it, the way she filed everything, and didn't ask.

She copied the four letters under mine, slowly, getting the *D* backward, which everyone does, and then again, right, and then her name, and then, unprompted, the way she'd taught herself long division in a week with no one watching, she made a mark I hadn't shown her, a fifth letter, *E,* having reasoned that the sound at the end of her own name probably existed and probably had a mark, and held it up.

"Is there one like this," she said. "I think there's one like this."

There was. I told her there was. I was handing her the only thing in the world that was entirely mine, the country I couldn't get back to written in the marks no one else could read, and she'd already, in the first lesson, started reaching past where I'd shown her toward the marks I hadn't, the way she always had, the way I'd spent five years hiding from a man who'd seen it anyway.

"Show me the rest," she said. It wasn't a thank you. It was an order, given by a free woman to the man who worked for her now in the one currency she'd decided to accept.

Outside, Pamphilus was getting the cranes quiet for the evening, and Heras had gone, and the lamp needed trimming, and Tyche bent over the little book in the bad light and made the letter *E* again, larger, until she had it the way she wanted it.
