# Chapter 5: Latin and the Little Ones

The first word I owned outright was *aqua*. Water.

I got it the way I got everything that winter, by being thirsty and stupid in front of people. The boy who carried water for the workshop, a knock-kneed kid called Stichus, maybe ten, who did not like me, set a jug down by my bench, and I pointed at it and made a noise, and he looked at me like I'd asked him to explain fire and said the word slow, the way you talk to a dog. *Aqua.* I said it back wrong. He said it again. I got it closer. He picked up the jug and walked off, and I sat there turning the one word over like a coin, because it was the first thing I'd taken whole instead of guessing at the front of it.

That was how it went, for months. No wise old man teaching me by lamplight. Just being thirsty and cold and humiliated, pointing at things, getting the word wrong, getting laughed at by a ten-year-old, trying again. The brain does it whether you want to or not, if you're drowning in the sound long enough and the alternative is staying deaf. The workshop ran on talk, the two men at the benches arguing about a joint or a price, the woman of the household, the boy, the people who came through the courtyard, a river of sound that ran over me ten hours a day, and I stood in it like a stone until the stone wore into the shape of words.

I built a grammar in the loft at night, on the inside of my own skull, because I had no tablet of my own and nothing to write with that wasn't theirs. I'm decent at patterns. That's the math talking. Two years of high-school French turned out to be the most useful thing my whole education handed me, more than the calculus, more than anything, and it had nothing to do with the French words. It was one idea: that a word could *bend*, that *amo* and *amat* were the same animal wearing different collars, and the collar told you who was doing the loving. Latin bent harder. It bent six ways and then bent the bending. I called myself by the wrong collar for weeks, used the form that meant someone was doing a thing *to* Daniel when I meant Daniel was doing the thing, so I went around announcing myself as the object of every sentence, which, fair.

The sounds were their own private joke at my expense. Their *c* was always hard, a *k*, no exceptions, so every soft *c* I'd ever read came out wrong in my mouth and right in theirs, and I had to unlearn a thousand words I'd never said out loud, only read. And the *v*. The *v* killed me. They said it like a *w*. I'd point at the wine, *vinum*, and say it with the buzzing *v* of home, and they'd squint, and Heras would say it back soft, *winum*, and the whole shape of the language tilted half a degree away from everything I'd assumed. I kept slipping the *v* back in for a year. It marked me the way an accent always marks you, a little flag that said foreign before I'd finished the word, and all I could do was flatten it down a degree at a time.

---

Heras came every few days. I had his name by the time the rains set in, and getting it was its own small idiot triumph.

He'd been "the physician" in my head for weeks, the clean-handed Greek who'd caught me lying about long division and decided he liked me for it. He came and went and I never had the language to ask the one thing you ask a person, which is who you are. Then one grey afternoon he was leaning over my bench correcting a figure I'd written, and the man who ran the workshop called across the courtyard to him by name, and I heard it land clean. Two syllables. I'd heard it a dozen times and never cut it out of the noise. This time I had enough words around it to know it was a name and not a thing.

I pointed at him. "Heras."

He straightened up and looked at me the way you look at a parrot that's said something true by accident, and then he put a finger on his own chest and said it again, properly, the Greek shape of it, and made me say it until I had the vowels close, and then he touched my chest and said my name, the mangled middle of it, *Danihel*, the version they could all pronounce, and we stood there formally introducing ourselves four months after we met. He found this funny. He had a way of finding things funny that never once involved laughing. The mouth went up a hair on one side and the eyes did something dry, and that was the whole performance, and I'd already learned to chase it, because it was the only applause in my life.

He talked to me in Latin mostly, slow and clean, because Latin was what I needed to survive in the street. But when a Latin word annoyed him he slid into Greek, and over the months I picked up Greek too, slower, sideways, the way you learn a thing standing next to someone who knows it. Greek had the roots I'd half-recognized from the start, the *therm* and the *photo* buried in the long words, the bones of a hundred English words I'd never thought about. I'd reach for a Greek word and find an English one wearing it as a mask. Heras never knew the language he was handing me had already gone ahead of him by two thousand years and come back wearing my clothes. There was no way to tell him. There was no way to tell anyone anything, and that was the whole problem, and it got worse as the words came, not better.

Nobody warns you about that. The first weeks, being mute is just survival, thirst and cold and pointing. Then the words come and you think it'll get less lonely, and instead it gets worse, because now you can almost reach people and the almost is a knife. I could ask for water. I could not say that home wasn't even a place yet, that it was a thing that hadn't happened. I could count to a hundred and haggle for bread. I could not make one joke that anyone alive would get. I'd think something funny, a line from a movie, a thing my mom said, and it would come up the back of my throat and die there, because the only audience for it was eighteen centuries off and didn't know it was missing me.

---

The first real joke I made in Latin, I made by accident, and it nearly killed me, I laughed so hard from pure relief.

It was the marketplace. Heras had started taking me into the town, with the optio's blessing or somebody's, on a loose leash I never fully understood, a foreigner walking a half-pace behind a freedman physician through a street that stank of fish and piss and the woodsmoke of a hundred braziers and the wet wool of everybody's cloaks in the rain. He'd name things and make me name them back, in the open, in front of people, which was harder than the workshop because the market didn't slow down for me and would cheat a foreigner who fumbled his numbers. I fumbled them constantly. Knowing *seven* in your head and saying *septem* fast enough that a fishmonger doesn't see you flinch are two different skills.

So there's a man selling eggs, and Heras is buying, and the man names a price, and I understand the price, which is itself a small miracle, and the price is robbery, double what eggs go for, because he's looked at my tunic worn wrong and my mouth that can't say the *v* and decided I'm the soft money walking behind the doctor. Heras starts to argue him down the normal way, and out of nowhere, out of four months of swallowed words, I hear myself say it. I point at the eggs. In my broken collar-wrong Latin, the case probably wrong, I ask whether the hen laid them or Caesar did. Because it was an emperor's price. That was the joke. It's barely a joke. But it lands, the case wrong and the *v* buzzing and all, because the fishmonger next door barks out a laugh and the egg man goes red, and Heras turns and looks at me with that dry uptick of the mouth gone wider than I'd ever earned. I got my eggs at the right price and laughed all the way back to the workshop like a man let out of a cell, because for one second in a market in a dead language I'd been funny on purpose and someone had heard it.

Up in the loft that night the high came off it and left the other thing, which was that the people I'd have called to tell about the egg man would never be born. I lay there with the dead phone against my hip out of habit and didn't take it out, because there was nothing on it anymore, and went to sleep counting eggs in Latin to keep from counting anything else.

---

The little ones nearly got me killed, and I walked into it on purpose, like an idiot, because a kid was dying and I thought I knew something.

It was deep winter, the wet kind, everyone coughing, and the boy who worked the next bench cut his hand badly on a chisel and the cut went wrong. A wound here was a coin flip with death on the other side, and this one came up death's face. The hand swelled and went red and the red ran up his arm in a thin angry line crawling toward the elbow, and the boy went hot and stupid with fever, and they sent for Heras, and I, with my four months of Latin and my half a memory of a science class, decided this was the moment.

I'd been trying to get the idea across for weeks, in pieces, badly. Now I had a sick boy and a reason. When Heras came I waited until he'd looked at the arm and done the things he did, the smelling, the pressing, the muttering about the humors, and then I got the wax tablet and tried to draw it. You can't draw what I was trying to draw. I drew dots. Crowds of them, swarming, going into the cut, into the red line, up the arm. I said the words I had. *Small. Living.* I made them with my fingers, tiny, crawling, walked my fingers up his arm like little legs, and said, in the Latin of a four-month-old, that they were small living ones, that they went in, that they climbed inside you and ate. I didn't have the word for invisible, so I said small past seeing. And because I'd spent four months listening to people swear by gods at every turn, I added the only cover I had. I pointed up. The gods send them. Tiny living things the gods send to climb inside you and eat you from the middle out.

Heras let me finish. He had the patience of a man watching a child build a tower of blocks so he can see exactly how it falls. And when I was done he took the stylus out of my hand, gently, and he took me apart.

I got maybe half the words and the whole of the contempt, which needs no translation. He pointed at the swollen arm and said the word I'd learned for bad air, the air of marshes, low and rotten. *This* is what sickens. You got fever from bad air and bad places and the wrong stars, ask anyone. He pressed his own four fingers to his own arm and named them, the wet and the dry and the hot and the cold, the humors, the balance of the body, and named the imbalance that made a fever, and this he said slowly and twice, because it was real to him, it was Hippocrates, the best medical thinking on the planet earth in that year, and he held it the way I held the quadratic formula. And then the gods angry, a curse, a thing done wrong at an altar. Air, humors, the gods' wrath. Three good frames, the frames every educated man from Britain to Syria used, and into the middle of them I'd dropped a story about invisible animals that crawl into cuts. He looked at my drawing of the swarming dots and said a word I didn't know, and the man at the bench snorted, and Heras said it at me again, and from the way he said it I understood it meant something near *witch-talk*, the thing old women in the hills believed. He was telling me, as a friend, that I'd just said the kind of thing that got people killed here, that invisible things sneaking inside you was the language of curses and the evil eye and the magic the law had teeth about, and I should, for my own sake, shut up about my little animals.

I'd known it was a risk. I hadn't known how much. I'd reached for the one true thing I had, the half-memory of a video, swarming dots under a microscope I'd seen once on a screen, and I'd reached for it because the kid was dying and I was *sure*, the way you're sure of a thing you've never seen and only half remember, and Heras had just shown me that being sure was worth exactly nothing. I had a story and no way on earth to make him see the dots, because the only thing that could show him the dots was a thing I could not build. I had the truth and no ladder to it. It was the loneliest I'd been since the phone died.

So I did the only thing left. I gave up the story and kept the chores.

---

I couldn't make him believe in the little ones. But I could make him *do the things*, because the things worked, and Heras, whatever else he was, would do a thing that worked even if he hated the reason.

I took the water he meant to wash the boy's arm with and I wouldn't let him use it cold from the jug. I made him boil it. I had no word for *sterilize* and there's no such concept here anyway, so I framed it the one way that didn't sound like witchcraft: hot water is clean, fire cleans, you wouldn't eat off a dirty plate, don't wash a wound with dirty water, the fire makes it clean past what the eye can judge. That, he could take. Cleanliness he understood. The Greeks were big on the regimen and the bath, and *clean* slotted in with no demons attached. He boiled it, let it cool, and we washed the arm properly and washed it again, and washed our own hands first, which I made a thing of, which he thought was fussy and did anyway because the fussy foreigner wouldn't stop until he did. We kept the wound open and clean instead of packing it with the muck they'd packed it with. He knew his own herbs, the ones that helped, and I didn't argue any of that, because he'd cut up more bodies than I'd seen and I wasn't about to play doctor at him. I owned the water and the hands. He kept everything else.

The boy lived. The red line hung at the elbow for two bad days and went back down. He kept the arm. I want to be honest about what that proves, which is nothing, exactly nothing, because boys with red lines up their arms sometimes live no matter what you do and sometimes die no matter what you do, and one kid is not a number you can stand on. I knew that even then, lying awake doing the math I couldn't do, no count, no control, no second boy untreated to set him against, just one kid and a coin flip and my story dressed up as cleanliness. I'd never, in this place, have proof. I'd have the thing that worked and the story that didn't, forever, and I'd have to learn to live in the gap.

But Heras saw the boy keep his arm. And weeks later, a different patient, he sent the household girl to boil the water before I'd said a word, and washed his hands first without being told, and when I caught his eye he gave me nothing, no credit, no concession, just went on as if he'd always done it this way. He never once said I was right about the little ones. To the end of that winter and probably the end of his life he thought my invisible animals were a superstition I'd carried down off the ice, a barbarian's ghost story. He just quietly started boiling his water and washing his hands, because he could *see* it helped, and the story he hung on it was his own business and the doing of it was mine. That was the only way anything was ever going to happen here. Give them a thing that worked and let them keep their own gods on it.

I told him the soldiers' *posca*, sour wine and water, washed a scrape cleaner than plain water, because I half-remembered the acid did something, and I couldn't tell him what and he didn't ask anymore. He'd stopped asking me why. He'd learned that *why*, with me, was where the witch-talk lived, and what to do was where I was worth keeping.

---

Spring came around again. I'd been here a year. Eighteen, now, by my own count off the seasons, though no one here cared and there was no candle and no cake and no one to tell.

I could talk, and I want to be precise about how much, because it would be a lie to make it more. I could hold a conversation about a thing in front of us, a tool, a price, a body, the weather, the work. I could follow Heras at his slow and lose him entirely when he got going at his real speed with another educated man, the Greek flying, the long words stacking, and I'd sit there catching one word in five and feel the deafness come back like a tide. I made errors that cost me, wrong cases that turned a sentence sideways, the *v* that buzzed and marked me foreign in the first three words. I could barely read, only the figures and a few words. A clever man behind a child's tongue is its own specific hell, because people hear the broken Latin and price your whole mind at the level of your worst sentence, and you have to claw their estimate back up joke by joke until they forget the accent and talk to the part of you that's actually there. Heras had skipped all that. He'd priced my mind off a long-division error in week one and never re-priced it down for the tongue. That was the whole of why I could stand it. One person on earth who heard past the accent.

But one person is not enough, and a year in I knew the shape of my problem with a clarity I hadn't had when I was just trying to live. I was lodged in a workshop off a household that wasn't mine, fed by people who watched the door, useful enough to keep and not free to leave, a foreigner with a homeland nobody believed in. Heras had a word for the place I claimed to be from. He'd said it that first day over the map, flat and skeptical, and over the year it had become a thing we both used, half a joke between us, the cold land at the top of the world past the last real coast. *Thule.* He'd read the men who called Thule a sailor's lie, and he'd as good as told me he thought I was a clever fraud about the where of me and a real thing about the what of me, and we'd both decided not to fight about it. I was the Thulean. It was a better cage than the cell and it was still a cage. The numbers had spread to the handful of people Heras showed them to and no further, and the map sat in a drawer somewhere as a curiosity, and that was the sum of my dent in the world after a year: a kid's arm, a fishmonger laughing, some accountants squinting at a zero.

It was never going to be enough this way, in pieces, useful to one doctor, owned by one household, talked down to by ten-year-olds. I needed a thing that wasn't an argument. Heras had taught me that cold with the little ones: you can't win them with a story, you can't win them with truth you can't show, you win them with a thing their own eyes won't let them call a lie. Something cheap, because I had nothing. Something mine, because everything else here belonged to someone. Something nobody could argue with, no haruspex, no skeptic, no fishmonger, a thing that just *happened* in front of them and ended the question of whether the Thulean was a fraud.

I was up in the loft when I landed on it. A draft came up under the tiles, the smoke off the household fire below, warm air pushing through the gaps so you could feel it on your face if you lay close to the floor, the heat rising, the smoke rising, always up. I'd known the why of that since I was twelve. Hot air is lighter. Hot air climbs. The gods don't send it up, it just goes, and nobody in this entire world knew the why and I did, I had that one for free, and I lay there with my hand in the rising warm and thought about a bag.

A bag full of hot air. Light enough, full enough of fire-warmed air that it would do what the smoke did, what I'd watched a thousand times over a campfire and a candle and never once thought about. It would go *up*. Off the ground, into the sky, in front of everyone, a made thing of cloth and fire rising on a warm wind the way the smoke climbed past my face in the dark. I didn't know how to build it. I didn't know what it cost or what it weighed or whether the cloth would hold or burn, and it would turn out I didn't know the half of how much I didn't know. But I knew it would *rise*, the way I'd known the eggs were an emperor's price, and this time the thing I was sure of was true, and better than true, it was visible, the most visible thing in the world, climbing into the open sky where every eye in the town would have to look up and watch the Thulean make the air carry fire, and not one of them would have a word for calling it a lie.

I lay there with my face in the warm draft a long time, doing the only math I had, not the engineering, that came later and cost me, but the simple animal arithmetic of a cornered man. Cheap. Mine. Impossible to argue with. The hot air gave me all three at once. I went to sleep with my hand still up in the rising heat, and for the first night in a year I didn't reach for the dead phone at all.
