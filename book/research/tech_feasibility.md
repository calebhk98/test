# Technology Feasibility Reference — Rome, c. 98-100 AD

A hard-nosed engineering and plausibility reference for a lone, non-expert
modern 17-year-old (Daniel) trying to bootstrap technology in the early Empire.
The brief is to be skeptical and puncture wish-fulfillment. For each item:
(a) known by 100 AD? (b) Roman materials/skills, (c) feasibility for a lone
non-expert, (d) real obstacles, (e) rough timeline, (f) most likely failure mode.

Sourcing note: written with Jan 2026 model knowledge plus a handful of targeted
web checks (saltpeter, distillation, stirrups, spinning wheel, verge escapement,
Roman glass/lenses). Web sources are listed at the end. Where I am leaning on
my own training rather than a confirmed source, I flag it inline as "(no source
checked)". Daniel, of course, would know far less than this document; this is
the truth against which his guesses should be measured.

## TIER 1 — The cheap, hard-to-dismiss unlocks (do these first)

### Math / notation (positional system, zero, Hindu-Arabic numerals, decimal point)
(a) **Not in Rome.** Romans used Roman numerals with an abacus for actual
computation; the positional decimal system with a true zero is Indian, reaching
the Mediterranean via Arab mathematicians ~9th-12th century. Greek geometry
(Euclid, Apollonius) and number theory existed; algebra in symbolic form did not
(Diophantus, ~250 AD, is rhetorical/syncopated). Calculus is 1600s.
(b) Materials: nil. This is pure notation. Skills: Roman accountants, surveyors
(*agrimensores*), and engineers are numerate and motivated.
(c) **Highly feasible — this is Daniel's single most realistic high-impact win.**
He almost certainly remembers digits 0-9, place value, the decimal point, and
long-multiplication/division algorithms. No furnace, no chemistry, no precision.
(d) Obstacles are social, not technical: scribal conservatism, the abacus lobby,
and the fact that the symbols must be taught and copied accurately. Zero is a
genuine conceptual hurdle for some, but merchants grasp "a placeholder for an
empty column" fast because the abacus already has empty columns.
(e) Demonstrable in week one; meaningful spread among accountants in 1-5 years;
generational to displace Roman numerals in monuments and law.
(f) Failure mode: it spreads as a fast "merchant's trick" but elite scribes keep
Roman numerals for prestige, so adoption is patchy for decades. Also realistic:
Daniel garbles a long-division algorithm under pressure and is briefly humiliated
before he reconstructs it. Algebra he can gesture at (unknowns as symbols);
calculus he can name and sketch limits/areas-under-curves but cannot rigorously
teach — flag that as honest struggle.

### Crop rotation / legumes / better plows / horse collar
(a) Mixed. Romans **already practiced** a two-field system and knew legumes
restored soil (Columella, Varro, and Pliny discuss alternating crops, lupins,
beans, vetch as soil-improvers — "green manure" is Roman knowledge). The
**three-field system** as a formalized northern-European rotation is early
medieval. The **heavy moldboard plow** for heavy wet northern soils is later
(spreads in NW Europe c. 6th-9th century); Romans used the light scratch-plow
(*aratrum*), which suits dry Mediterranean soils. The **horse collar** is the
big one: Rome used the throat-and-girth harness that chokes a horse under load;
the rigid padded collar is Chinese, reaching Europe ~9th-10th century. **Stirrups
did not exist** anywhere yet (earliest firm evidence China c. 322 AD); Romans
used the four-horned saddle, which actually works well, so stirrups are a smaller
"win" than fiction assumes.
(b) Materials/skills all present: iron, wood, leather, oxen, horses, skilled
farmers and smiths.
(c) Feasible for the **concepts**, not the execution. Daniel can describe a
collar and a moldboard; a wheelwright/smith builds the prototype. The honest
catch: Mediterranean soil and ox-based farming mean the moldboard's payoff is
muted in Italy itself; it shines in Gaul/Britain.
(d) Obstacles: the horse collar's payoff depends on shoeing and on horses being
worth more than oxen for plowing, which is a whole agronomic shift. Three-field
rotation needs the right legume/cereal mix for the climate.
(e) Plow tweak and legume rotation: demonstrable in 1-2 growing seasons. Collar:
1-2 years to a working prototype, a decade-plus to change farm practice.
(f) Failure mode: it works on a demonstration plot but spreads at the speed of
peasant trust and landlord incentive — i.e., very slowly, and Daniel never sees
the empire-wide effect. Stirrups underwhelm because the army's saddles are fine.

### The germ framing ("invisible creatures") + sanitation + boiling water + soap
(a) Germ theory: **absolutely unknown**, and genuinely centuries premature
(miasma theory dominates into the 1800s). But the *practices* are achievable
without the theory. Romans had aqueducts, public baths, sewers (Cloaca Maxima),
and used **oil + strigil** rather than soap to clean the body; **soap** as a
substance was known to Gauls/Germans, mentioned by Pliny as a Gaulish hair
pomade (*sapo*, tallow + ash), so the chemistry is at hand even if Romans don't
bathe with it.
(b) Materials: animal fat/tallow, wood ash (for crude lye/potash), olive oil,
wine, fire, fabric for filtering — all present. Skills: basic.
(c) **Feasible and high-value, with one giant caveat: framing.** Boiling water,
washing hands and wounds, wine/strong vinegar on a wound (genuine mild
antiseptic effect), and crude soap are all buildable. **Penicillin is rightly
near-impossible** — Daniel cannot identify the right *Penicillium* strain,
cannot purify or stabilize it, and would more likely poison someone with mold
toxins; correct call to never attempt it. The "tiny invisible creatures the gods
send to climb inside you" story is the smart workaround: it motivates boiling
and cleaning without a blasphemy or quackery charge.
(d) Obstacles are social/religious, not material: medicine is the doctors' and
priests' turf; a foreign scholar pushing unseen-creature dogma risks being
branded a fraud or worse. Crude soap is harsh (uncontrolled lye) and may burn
skin — getting mild soap needs trial and error on fat/ash ratios.
(e) Boiling/cleaning habits: spreadable in months to a few years via story.
Reliable soap: months of batches. Measurable mortality effects: invisible to
Daniel in his lifetime; he has no statistics.
(f) Failure mode: the framing half-works, gets absorbed into existing
god-of-pestilence belief in a distorted form, and a bad plague year either
"proves" or "disproves" him by coincidence. Soap stays a niche product; Romans
like their oil-and-strigil.

### Papermaking from rag/linen pulp
(a) **Not in Rome.** True paper (macerated fiber, screen-formed, pressed,
dried) is Chinese (Han, ~100 BC-100 AD) and reaches the Islamic world ~8th
century, Europe ~12th. Rome has **papyrus** (laminated reed strips, not pulped)
and **parchment/vellum** (treated animal skin). Daniel's instinct — "soak old
clothes for months and make sheets" — is the right family of idea but missing
the key steps.
(b) Materials: linen/hemp rag is abundant; water, a wooden vat, a wire or fine
cloth screen (mould-and-deckle), a press. All within Roman capability.
(c) **Feasible but harder than Daniel thinks.** The hard part is not the idea;
it is (1) reducing rag to true fiber pulp — Romans lack mechanical stamping mills
for this initially, so it is slow hand pounding; (2) the **screen mould**, which
needs an evenly woven fine mesh to form a uniform sheet; (3) **sizing** the sheet
(gelatin/starch) so ink doesn't feather. Get any of these wrong and you get a
fragile, blotchy, uneven mat, not writing paper.
(d) Obstacles: precision of the screen, consistency of pulp, sizing chemistry by
trial and error. Not exotic, just fiddly. A water-powered stamping mill (see
mills below) hugely accelerates pulping later.
(e) First crude, usable sheets: 6-18 months of iterated failure. Paper that
beats papyrus for cost at scale: several years plus a stamping mill.
(f) Failure mode: early sheets are weak and absorbent, scribes hate them, and
papyrus/parchment guilds have no reason to switch. Paper only wins once it is
*cheap at volume*, which needs mechanization Daniel hasn't built yet.

### Block printing → toward a press
(a) Rome **already stamps**: coins (struck dies), brick stamps, signet rings,
*terra sigillata* pottery stamps, branding. The conceptual leap to **inked
block/movable type on a screw press** is not made. Block printing is Chinese
(~7th century); movable type Bi Sheng ~1040; Gutenberg's metal type + press
~1440s. Crucially, **Romans already have the screw press** (olive/wine/cloth
presses) — the mechanism Gutenberg adapted.
(b) Materials/skills: the screw press exists; engravers, die-cutters, and
metal-casters are skilled (coin dies prove fine relief work); oil-based ink is
the missing chemistry (water ink beads on metal type).
(c) **Block printing is very feasible.** Movable metal type is feasible but
serious: it needs (1) a type-metal alloy that casts crisp and doesn't shrink
oddly — lead/tin/antimony is the historical answer, and **antimony is the catch**
(Romans have lead and tin; controlled antimony alloying is not a known practice,
no source checked on Roman antimony use); (2) a casting mould (matrix) for
uniform type height — a real precision problem; (3) oil-based ink. Daniel can
get there because he has the press and skilled engravers, but it is a multi-year
craft program, not a weekend.
(d) Obstacles: type-metal alloy and uniform body height (precision), oil ink
chemistry, and the fact that Latin's relatively small alphabet *helps* a lot
(versus Chinese), which is a real advantage Daniel should exploit.
(e) Inked woodblock books: ~1 year. Crude movable type, ugly but working: 3-6
years with a dedicated workshop. Reliable type for mass book runs: longer.
(f) Failure mode: type casts unevenly, lines print gray and crooked, and Daniel
falls back to block printing for years — which is honestly the *correct* and
still-revolutionary outcome. The Library-of-Alexandria "print 50 of every book"
dream is achievable via blocks far sooner than via type.

### Hot-air balloon (treated linen envelope, heat source) — the first big project
(a) **Not known.** No one flies until the Montgolfiers, 1783. The physics
(hot air is less dense) is not understood, but it does not need to be — it just
needs to work.
(b) Materials: **linen is the problem child.** Romans have excellent linen and
wool, rope, wicker. They do **not** have rubberized or tightly woven balloon
silk. To hold hot air, the envelope must be near-airtight and light; treating
linen (oil, wax, varnish, sizing) to seal it adds weight and flammability and
may never be light *and* tight enough. Heat source: an open flame (straw, wool,
charcoal) under a fabric bag — an obvious fire hazard.
(c) **The premise calls this his first win, and it is the riskiest "easy" item
in the book.** A small unmanned demonstration balloon (a few meters) is genuinely
achievable and is the honest version of the first success — a Montgolfier-style
paper/linen bag that rises on hot air and amazes onlookers. A **manned** balloon
is a different beast: lift scales with volume, so a person needs a very large
envelope (hundreds of cubic meters), which means a lot of sealed fabric, a lot of
sustained heat, and a serious fire/structural risk over an open flame.
(d) Obstacles: (1) envelope weight vs. lift — treated linen is heavy; (2)
permeability — leaks bleed your hot air; (3) sustained heat without torching the
fabric; (4) seams — hand-sewn seams leak and tear. Hydrogen is out (no practical
hydrogen production, and lethal). 
(e) Small tethered/unmanned hot-air model that visibly rises: realistically
achievable in the first 1-2 years and is a perfect "the man who made fire fly"
moment. A genuinely safe manned free flight: optimistically several more years,
and frankly may stay tethered, low, and dangerous.
(f) Failure mode (and the honest one to dramatize): the envelope catches fire,
or a manned attempt leaks/cools and drops hard, injuring or killing someone. The
plausible win is the **unmanned spectacle**; the manned flight should cost
crashes, burns, and at least one near-fatal accident. Do not let the first manned
flight be clean.

## TIER 2 — Real but slow, precision/chemistry-limited (defer 5-15 years)

### Better steel (carbon-iron, crucible/wootz, folding, bone/ash)
(a) Partly known. Romans make **wrought iron and some steel** (carburized
surfaces, pattern-welding appears in the era) but without understanding *why*:
they have no concept of carbon content as the controlling variable. **Wootz/
crucible steel** is Indian/South Asian (already being made in the era, but Romans
import the product, not the method). "Folding" (Japanese-style) homogenizes and
removes slag — it does **not** add carbon; three folds won't magically make
modern steel.
(b) Materials: iron ore, charcoal, bloomery furnaces, skilled smiths. The
critical lack is **reliable, controllable high heat** (no coke, limited forced-
air; they cannot easily reach/hold the temperatures for liquid steel) and the
*conceptual* lack of carbon control.
(c) **Feasible to improve, not to leap.** Daniel's genuinely useful contribution
is the *idea* that a specific amount of carbon (from charcoal) in iron, plus
quench-and-temper, controls hardness — pointing smiths at a variable they can
then optimize empirically. Crucible steel is plausible to *attempt* (clay
crucible, iron + charcoal, sealed, long high heat) and is the most promising
"better steel" path, but it is finicky and fuel-hungry. **Bone/ash additions** do
real things — bone ash and charcoal in a sealed crucible carburize iron (this is
the kernel of truth behind the "Viking bones" folklore) — but control is poor.
(d) Obstacles: temperature control and measurement (no pyrometer; smiths judge
by color), fuel (no coal/coke — correct that Romans lack practical coal use),
consistency batch-to-batch, and the absence of any theory to debug failures.
(e) Noticeably better blades/tools via better quench-temper and slag control:
1-3 years. Repeatable crucible steel in quantity: 5-15 years and arguably never
fully reliable at Roman scale.
(f) Failure mode: gorgeous results one batch, brittle garbage the next, because
nobody can measure or hold temperature. Steel is the **chokepoint** — Daniel
should understand that almost every Tier-3 dream (cannon, springs, clocks,
engines) dies here.

### Telescope / binoculars / microscope
(a) Not known as instruments. Romans had **burning glasses** and possibly simple
magnifiers (Nero's emerald is debated; ancient ground lenses exist, e.g., the
Nimrud lens). The compound telescope is 1608 (Netherlands); the microscope
roughly contemporaneous.
(b) Materials: Roman **soda-lime glass was reasonably clear** by the imperial
period and affordable; gem-cutters could grind and polish convex/concave shapes
against abrasives. So the raw capability for a *simple lens* exists.
(c) **A single-lens magnifier is feasible. A useful telescope is a stretch.** A
working telescope needs **two** decent lenses of controlled, matched curvature
and good optical homogeneity, plus a tube — and Roman glass, while clear, is
**full of small bubbles, striae, and color tints** that wreck image quality at
magnification. Grinding a true spherical curve by eye, repeatably, with no test
plate, is the killer. A crude 2-3x "spyglass" that shows a blurry, color-fringed,
slightly closer view is the realistic ceiling for a long time; a genuinely useful
military scope is years of optical trial and error and better glass.
(d) Obstacles: glass purity/homogeneity, repeatable curvature, polishing,
chromatic aberration (uncorrectable without compound optics he can't make). A
microscope that sees bacteria is **out** (needs far better single lenses than
he can grind — Leeuwenhoek-grade beads are a craft unto themselves).
(e) Hand magnifier: months. Crude low-power spyglass: 2-5 years of frustration.
Anything militarily useful: longer, gated on glass quality.
(f) Failure mode: every lens he grinds is slightly off, images are dim, blurry,
and rainbow-fringed, and the "telescope" underwhelms compared to the hype. The
microscope-sees-atoms claim is, per the premise, a deliberate **lie** he tells —
keep it a lie; it is physically impossible at any era.

### Water-powered mills, textile mechanization, spinning wheel, flying shuttle
(a) **Romans HAD water mills** — note this clearly. Barbegal (near Arelate,
early 2nd century) is a 16-wheel flour-milling complex, genuinely industrial.
Vertical undershot/overshot wheels are known. **Spinning wheel: NOT yet** (China
~1000 AD, Europe ~13th century) — Romans spin with **drop spindle and distaff**.
**Flying shuttle: 1733 (Kay)**, far future. The medieval water-powered loom/
fulling mill is later.
(b) Materials/skills: Romans build excellent wheels, gearing, and hydraulics.
The gap is applying water power **beyond grinding** — to bellows, hammers,
fulling, pulping.
(c) **High-value and very feasible direction.** Daniel doesn't need to invent the
water wheel; he needs to point it at new jobs (trip-hammers for metalwork, stamp
mills for paper pulp, fulling stocks for cloth) — all mechanically within reach.
The **spinning wheel** is one of his best textile wins: simple, transformative
(10x spinning throughput), buildable from a wheel, spindle, and band. The
**flying shuttle** is harder and less safe and offers less if spinning is still
the bottleneck — historically you fix spinning first.
(d) Obstacles: cams, trip-hammers, and reliable wooden gearing wear and need
maintenance; siting needs water. Textile guild/household resistance.
(e) Repurposed water mills (hammer, stamp, fulling): 1-3 years each, since the
base tech exists. Spinning wheel: under a year to a prototype, longer to spread.
(f) Failure mode: the machines work but threaten household spinners' livelihoods
and guild structures; adoption is social-friction-limited. Wooden gearing wears
fast without good lubricants and metal bearings.

### Rail/wagonways for mines (wooden rails, horse-drawn)
(a) The **Diolkos** (Corinth, ~600 BC) is the real precedent: a grooved stone
trackway for hauling ships across the isthmus — Romans know guided-track haulage.
Wooden mine wagonways are late-medieval (Germanic mining, ~1500s). Romans run
mines (Spain, Dacia soon) with brutal slave labor and no rails.
(b) Materials: timber, iron strapping, draft animals, carts — all present.
(c) **Very feasible and thematically perfect** (rails reduce the need for slave
porters — the quiet anti-slavery argument). Wooden rails with flanged wheels,
horse- or human-drawn, in a mine, is genuinely buildable with Roman materials.
(d) Obstacles: wooden rails wear and rot; iron strapping helps but eats iron;
gauge/switch standardization; and the social fact that slave labor is *cheap*, so
the economic incentive to mechanize is weak. That last point is the real enemy.
(e) A working mine wagonway demo: 1-2 years. Broad adoption: gated on whether
anyone wants to spend iron and money to replace free slave muscle.
(f) Failure mode: it works beautifully in demo and nobody adopts it widely
because slaves are cheaper than rails — an honest, bitter beat for the book.

### Clocks (mechanical, gear/escapement)
(a) Romans have **water clocks (clepsydra)** and the **Antikythera mechanism**
proves Hellenistic **gear-cutting skill** — but the **verge escapement** (the
thing that makes a weight-driven clock keep time) is not invented until c.
1280-1300. The Antikythera device models the heavens; it is not a regulated
clock.
(b) Materials/skills: bronze gears can be cut (Antikythera proves it); the
missing piece is the **escapement concept** and the precision to make it run
steadily.
(c) **Feasible to attempt, brutally hard to make accurate.** Daniel almost
certainly does **not** remember how a verge-and-foliot escapement actually works
(this is the honest gap — most modern teens couldn't draw one). He can build
geared *displays* and improved water clocks easily. A weight-driven, escapement-
regulated mechanical clock that keeps decent time is a multi-year precision
project even *with* the concept, and without remembering the escapement it may
never come. A pendulum (Galileo/Huygens insight) he might half-remember; a
pendulum clock still needs an escapement.
(d) Obstacles: the escapement concept (memory), precision gear/pinion cutting,
low-friction pivots, lubrication, temperature stability.
(e) Better water clocks and geared astronomical displays: 1-3 years. A real
timekeeping mechanical clock: 10+ years, possibly never to good accuracy.
(f) Failure mode: he builds an impressive geared automaton/water clock that
*looks* like a triumph but doesn't keep steady time, and the true mechanical
clock stays out of reach because the escapement never clicks (literally) in his
memory. Good place for honest failure: the "grandfather clock" he promised never
ticks right.

### Soap, water filtration, distillation, nitroglycerin
- **Soap:** see Tier 1. Known to Gauls/Germans (Pliny), tallow + wood-ash lye,
  feasible, harsh until ratios dialed in.
- **Water filtration (sand/gravel/charcoal):** materials trivially available;
  the **idea** is the only missing piece. Very feasible, low-cost, immediate.
  Failure mode: it clarifies and improves taste but does **not** sterilize, so it
  doesn't stop waterborne disease by itself — Daniel may overrate it. Pair with
  boiling.
- **Distillation (alcohol):** **Subtle and important.** Stills existed in
  1st-century Alexandria (alchemical apparatus), so the *apparatus* is era-
  plausible — but they **lacked water-cooled condensers**, ran too hot, and
  alcohol distillation specifically is not achieved until ~12th century
  (Salerno). So Daniel *can* plausibly introduce a proper **water-cooled coil/
  condenser** and get distilled spirits — a genuine, achievable, valuable win
  (concentrated alcohol = better antiseptic + a sellable product). It is one of
  his better chemistry-adjacent plays *because the gap is one clever part, not a
  whole science.* Failure mode: weak, foul, low-proof output until the still and
  condenser are refined; fire/explosion risk with alcohol vapor.
- **Nitroglycerin:** **Flag hard — near-impossible and lethal.** Requires
  concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids (Romans have neither in usable
  concentration), precise low-temperature control, and it detonates from shock/
  heat. A 17-year-old attempting this dies, full stop. Should be named as a thing
  Daniel *knows not to touch.* Correct to treat as off-limits.

## TIER 3 — Defer hard or treat as "impossible bait" (he names them, never builds)

### Gunpowder and a black-powder cannon
(a) Saltpeter (**sal petrae**) was **known to Greeks/Romans as a curiosity** —
the white efflorescent salt on cellar/cave walls, noted in meat-curing for its
reddening effect — but **never combined with fuel**, and they could not tell
natural from cultivated niter. Sulfur is readily available (volcanic Italy).
Charcoal trivial. China has incendiary/gunpowder knowledge only later (9th
century), so the "China had it by 100 AD" assumption in the premise is **wrong**
— good place for Daniel to be confidently mistaken.
(b) Materials: sulfur yes; charcoal yes; **potassium nitrate is the bottleneck.**
Wall-scrapings give mostly *calcium* nitrate (deliquescent, won't make good
powder); converting to potassium nitrate needs **niter beds** (composting
manure/urine + ash + lime over months to years) and wood-ash leaching. This is
doable in principle and is exactly how it was historically done — but it is slow,
smelly, and requires sustained, deliberate cultivation.
(c) **Discovering/making usable powder is feasible but slow; a working cannon is
not, early on.** Black powder requires the right ~75/15/10 ratio AND **corning**
(wetting, pressing, granulating) — uncorned "serpentine" powder is weak and
separates. Daniel can plausibly get *something that flashes/bangs* within a couple
of years given niter beds; reliable propellant powder is harder.
(d) The cannon itself: **Roman bronze and iron cannot reliably contain the
pressure.** Cast bronze is the best bet (Romans cast bronze well), but without
controlled metallurgy early barrels are porous, weak, and burst. This dovetails
**perfectly** with the premise's plan: build a cannon you *know* will fail with
Roman metal, demonstrate it from a safe distance, let it burst, and use the
explosion to argue for better steel. That is the honest, correct use of this tech.
(e) Niter beds to first usable powder: 2-5 years. A cannon that *fires* without
bursting: gated entirely on metallurgy — many years, and the dramatic point is
that it *bursts* on purpose first.
(f) Failure mode (and the intended one): the barrel ruptures. Realistically, the
bigger risk is that **niter production is the silent multi-year slog** nobody
sees, and a careless step blows up the powder shed and kills an assistant.

### Concrete
(a) **Rome already had outstanding concrete** (*opus caementicium*) with
**pozzolana** (volcanic ash) and lime, including **hydraulic (underwater)
concrete** — the Pantheon dome (a bit later) and harbor works prove it. This is a
case where **Rome is ahead of what Daniel could improvise.** He should *learn*
from them, not "introduce" it. Possible marginal contributions: reinforcement
ideas (but no good rebar without cheap steel), and the *concept* of testing mixes
systematically. Mostly: leave it; flag that Roman concrete is excellent.

### "Airless"/woven-spoke tires, leaf-spring suspension
(a) Not known. The Mars-rover-style woven metal tire and the leaf spring both
**depend on good spring steel** — exactly what Rome lacks.
(b) Romans have iron and bronze, neither of which gives reliable, fatigue-
resistant springiness.
(c) **Low feasibility until steel improves.** A woven-iron "airless tire" made of
soft Roman iron will deform, fatigue, and fail; bronze is worse for fatigue. Leaf
springs from Roman iron sag and crack. Daniel's own instinct in the source
material ("springs for suspension even with old copper") is **wrong** — copper/
bronze make poor springs. These are gated on Tier-2 steel.
(d) Obstacle: spring steel (carbon control + temper) — the steel chokepoint again.
(e) Plausible only **after** years of metallurgy progress; honestly maybe never
in his lifetime at useful quality.
(f) Failure mode: prototypes sag, crack, or go dead-soft; a good honest beat is
Daniel confidently promising better tires and being defeated by metal fatigue he
didn't anticipate.

### Steam engine, electricity, combustion engine, aircraft, microscope-sees-atoms
- **Steam engine:** Hero's **aeolipile** existed as a spinning toy, so the
  *phenomenon* is known — but a real engine needs **pressure vessels** (Roman
  metal can't safely hold steam pressure), **precise cylinders/pistons** (no
  boring machines, no tight tolerances), and the **concept of pressure as
  usable work** (absent). Correctly impossible in his lifetime. Good "bait": he
  shows the toy spinning and says *imagine this strong enough to pull a hundred
  carts.*
- **Electricity:** no practical generation, no insulated wire, no concept of
  circuits. Even a simple battery (Baghdad-battery-style cell) gives a feeble
  current with no use case he can build. Off the table; pure imagination fuel.
- **Combustion engine / car / train-with-engine:** needs steel, precision
  machining, fuel refining, ignition — every prerequisite missing. The premise's
  own narrator drifts toward "maybe a 4-piston engine if oil and better iron" —
  that is **exactly the overconfidence to puncture.** No.
- **Aircraft (heavier-than-air, rotary):** no engine, no light strong materials,
  no aerodynamics theory. Impossible. Balloon yes; airplane no.
- **Microscope seeing atoms:** **physically impossible at any technology level**
  (optical microscopes can't resolve atoms; that needs electron/STM tech). Per
  the premise this is a **deliberate lie** Daniel tells to seed ambition. Keep it
  a lie and never let it pay off as truth.

## RECOMMENDED REALISTIC ORDERING

### Do first (cheap, hard to dismiss, unlock others) — pick 4-6:
1. **Hindu-Arabic numerals + place value + decimal point.** Zero cost, instant
   demonstrability, spreads through accountants, builds credibility fast. His
   single best opening move.
2. **The germ "invisible creatures" framing + boiling/sanitation + crude soap +
   sand-charcoal filtration.** Cheap, saves his own skin (he fears dying of a
   cut), low materials, huge potential payoff, socially camouflaged as religion.
3. **Hot-air balloon — but the UNMANNED demonstration version first.** It is the
   premise's signature spectacle and credibility-maker; keep the manned flight
   costly and deferred. This is his "the man who flew" moment but should bleed.
4. **Crop rotation/legumes + plow & harness tweaks.** Nearly free, uses existing
   farmers, the horse collar is a genuine missing unlock, payoff is broad.
5. **Block printing (then push toward type).** He already has the screw press and
   skilled engravers; block-printing the existing canon (the "50 copies of every
   book" dream) is achievable early and seeds literacy — a core theme.
6. **Repurposed water power (trip-hammer, stamp mill, fulling) + spinning wheel.**
   Romans already have the wheel; pointing it at new jobs and adding the spinning
   wheel is high-leverage and buildable. Also feeds papermaking (pulp stamping).

### Defer 5-15 years (gated on steel, chemistry, or precision):
- **Better steel / crucible steel.** The master chokepoint; start the *idea*
  early (carbon control) but expect years and unreliability.
- **Gunpowder + the deliberately-bursting cannon.** Niter beds are a multi-year
  slog; the cannon's job is to *fail on purpose* and argue for steel.
- **Telescope/useful optics.** Gated on glass quality; a crude spyglass takes
  years and underwhelms.
- **Mechanical clock with escapement.** Likely never works right (escapement
  memory gap); a great honest failure.
- **Distillation of spirits.** Achievable (the condenser is the one missing
  part) but put it after the antiseptic/medicine arc matures.
- **Airless tires / leaf springs.** Gated entirely on spring steel; maybe never.
- **Papermaking at scale.** Crude sheets early, but cheap paper needs the
  stamping mill — sequence it after water-power repurposing.

### Never build (name as bait, let them inspire):
Steam engine (toy only), electricity, combustion engine, car, engine-driven
train, aircraft, submarine, the atom-seeing microscope (a lie). Nitroglycerin
(lethal — he refuses it). Concrete (Rome already beats him; he learns from them).

## THE THREE LEAST-REALISTIC WISH-FULFILLMENT ITEMS (write him struggling here)
1. **Manned hot-air balloon.** A small unmanned rise is fine; a safe *manned*
   flight with treated linen over an open flame is genuinely dangerous and
   probably crashes/burns first. Make the signature triumph cost blood.
2. **A working mechanical (escapement) clock.** Most modern teens cannot actually
   reconstruct a verge escapement; without it there is no timekeeping clock, only
   pretty water clocks and geared automata. Let the promised "grandfather clock"
   never tick right.
3. **Useful telescope / any high-power optics.** Roman glass is clear but full of
   bubbles and striae, and grinding repeatable matched lenses by eye with no test
   plate is brutal. Expect a blurry, color-fringed 2-3x toy, not a war-winning
   scope — and the atom-seeing microscope is an outright lie.

(Runners-up worth a humbling beat: the airless tire / leaf spring, defeated by
soft Roman metal, and the cannon, which is *supposed* to burst.)
