**Notes on tricky items:**

- **Lines 1–6** are all from a single extended scene (ch07, the balloon-and-counting exchange). Lines 2, 3, 4, and 6 belong to Heras; lines 1 and 5 to Daniel. The quiz tests whether readers notice Heras's dry, deflating wit vs. Daniel's flat, honest brevity.
- **Lines 7–9** are Celer: plain military Latin, no decoration, zero sentiment about death.
- **Lines 10–12** are Apollodorus: builder's language, structural metaphors, a grudging acknowledgment of something that "does one thing" — high praise from him.
- **Lines 13–15** are Hermes: terse, anvil-short, every sentence a blow.
- **Line 16** is Tyche (ch10, the collar scene): city-quick, factual, no warmth but not cruel.
- **Lines 17–18** are Marcia (ch22, first meeting): commercial, flat, runs a test before explaining herself.
- **Line 19** is Macer (ch20): contractor's contempt, working-class metaphor, enjoys the irony.
- **Lines 20–21** are Tyche (ch20, the freeing scene): refuses gratitude, lays out the legal reality as a sum.
- **Line 22** is Trajan (ch17): plain army cadence, prizes honesty over spectacle.
- **Lines 23–24** are Hadrian (ch35): quick, sets traps, enjoys self-awareness as a tool.
- **Line 25** is Theophanes (off-roster, ch37): a minor Roman clerk speaking with careful courtly caution — the only off-roster line in the set.
- **Line 26** is Apollodorus (ch37): uncompromising, first-person statement of principle, plumb-line honesty.
- **Line 27** is Ulpia (ch38): child-gravity, factual, no drama.
- **Line 28** is Procula (ch52): paraphrase of her insight rendered as the rule she derived — clean, principle-first, impersonal phrasing.
