# Voice Range Audit
### Competence / Class / Honesty / Intelligence range across the dialogue

**Question (distinct from `dialogue_distinctiveness.md`):** That review asked whether characters sound
*different* from each other (register variety). This one asks a harder, narrower question: is the cast's
*range of human competence* honestly distributed? Are some people dull, ill-educated, crude-mouthed, or
confidently wrong — or has the book (the catalogued AI failure) made everyone smart, articulate, perceptive,
and basically honest, just in different keys?

**Sources:** ch01-53 read widely, weighted to scenes with low-status walk-ons and crowds (ch04, 05, 07, 08,
09, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16, 17, 20, 22, 24, 26, 28, 30, 32, 37, 44, 48) plus `02_characters.md` and
`07_glossary.md`.

---

## 1. INTELLIGENCE / COMPETENCE RANGE

**Verdict: badly skewed toward the top.** With one structural exception (steel), nobody who *speaks* in this
book is dull, and there is no character who is foolish, gets things wrong, and *stays* wrong. The cast is a
gallery of the perceptive.

Worse: the book's most-quoted line about a "slow" character reveals the dullness was *fake*. Daniel made
Tyche **perform** stupidity to keep her cheap (ch20): "you told the old man I was slow ... You decided I'd be
slow. For my own good." The one apparent dim slave in the yard is secretly a numerical prodigy who taught
herself long division in a week (ch10). That is the failure mode *inverted into a plot point* — even the
"slow" one is brilliant.

Every walk-on who opens his mouth is sharp:
- The **baggage clerk** is "a sharp Greek-tongued boy who did the grain tallies" (ch30).
- The **nervous young clerk** instantly grasps the audit value of place-value notation that his 40-year
  veteran boss refuses — "leaned over it ... and traced them up the column" and "saw the thing Onesimus
  would not" (ch10).
- The **fish-seller's son** keeps a factor's book in the new figures off his own initiative (ch16).
- **Lucanus, age 10**, lands the chapter's closing thematic line: "Then he did all right," which Daniel
  flags as "the only thing said in the house all that day that I could not fold any smaller" (ch37).
- Even a **perfumer's wife** in a crowd is "too well-bred to say so" but "suspected, correctly, that she had
  been cheated" (ch26) — correct, perceptive, socially adroit.

The nearest thing to a genuinely limited mind is **Onesimus**, the abacus-clerk who refuses the new numerals
"out of spite" and "kept his board till he died" (ch10). But he is not dull — he out-computes Daniel on the
board ("his thumb was faster than my pen") — he is merely *stubborn*. Reactionary, not stupid. That is the
only "wrong and stays wrong" figure in the book, and even he is technically excellent at his craft.

The genuine incompetence in the book is **systemic, not personal**: nobody can hold or read a furnace
temperature (ch15), the steel works one batch in four, Demetrios's spyglass shows "nothing but colored fog"
(ch26). These are honest failures of *technology and method* — but they are never embodied in a dumb person.
The people are all sharp; only the tools and the era fail them.

## 2. CLASS / EDUCATION IN SPEECH

**Verdict: there is essentially NO grammatical or lexical class-marking in dialogue.** The book signals class
through *content, deference, and narration* — never through rougher grammar, thinner vocabulary, or halting
syntax. A slave, a smith, a tribune, and a senator all speak in clean, complete, well-formed clauses; they
differ in *attitude*, not in *facility with language*.

The author's own canon describes Tyche's speech as a "slave's survival caution," but on the page it is
fluent and pointed, not uneducated:
- Tyche, age 14, fresh from the kitchen: "It's not yours to take off. It's the master's collar. He had it
  riveted. You take it off him, you've broken the master's thing. They'll think he ran and you helped."
  (ch10) — four flawless conditional sentences delivered "city-quick."
- Tyche again: "He's careless with money but he's not a liar, watch the first and trust the second." (ch22)
  — a polished antithetical aphorism from a freedwoman.

Hermes the smith is "city Latin, fast, with the consonants bitten off short," and the author renders his
*rhythm* (hammer-broken clauses) beautifully — but never his *grammar*. His lines are terse by choice, not
limited: "Tell me how much black. Tell me how hot. Tell me how long. Tell me one number, Thulean, one I can
do twice." (ch15) That is a craftsman's brevity, but it is rhetorically perfect parallelism.

The clearest tell: when low-status people might speak crudely, **the book mutes them rather than render
rough speech.** Crowd hecklers are kept deliberately off-page — "A man in the crowd shouted something I did
not catch" (ch08); the witch-cry is reported as shapes "going past my ears like thrown stones" (ch08), never
quoted. The author reaches for *narrated impression* exactly where a rough idiolect would have to appear.

The two people who literally cannot speak well get no dialogue at all: **Pamphilus** (collared slave) is
characterized as patient and silent — "the patience of a man who has nothing waiting for him" (ch10) — and
"Pamphilus did not say anything" recurs (ch13, ch20). **Davus** is "a big slow household slave" (ch08) — and
gets zero lines. The book's solution to "how would a dull or uneducated man talk" is **to make him mute**, so
the clean register of everyone who *does* speak is never broken.

## 3. SWEARING / VULGARITY

**Verdict: the book is almost entirely clean-mouthed in dialogue — a clear authorial choice, not a period
constraint.** (Per the brief: Catullus, the Pompeii graffiti, and soldiers' slang prove Roman speech could
be filthy. None of that texture is here.)

A full search for oaths and crude terms turns up only:
- **Narration**, never dialogue: "one degree off pissing himself from nerves" (ch04); a knife that "would not
  hold an edge worth a damn" (ch15); the street "stank of fish and piss" (ch05). These are Daniel's
  retrospective *narrator* voice, not anyone's spoken line.
- **One reported, translated curse**, explicitly glossed as tone rather than quoted as speech: Macer's "you'll
  come to my door ... we'll see what the war's worth to us both," which Daniel renders as "*which was Macer
  for I'm glad, you bastard, in the only tongue he owned*" (ch17). The "you bastard" is Daniel's interpretive
  paraphrase — Macer does not actually say it.

No soldier swears. Celer, a career frontier officer who has buried men, never utters an oath; his roughest
on-page moment is rendered indirectly — he "said something low and soldier's-rough to him that I didn't
catch" (ch11) — again, *muted* rather than quoted. The dockworkers, baggage train, recruits, smiths, and
wharf-women never curse. No coarse insult, no obscene oath (no *mentula*, *futuo*, *irrumare*, no "by
Hercules' balls"), no crude sexual joke appears anywhere in quoted dialogue. For a book set among soldiers,
dockers, and slaughter-lot crowds, the total absence is conspicuous.

## 4. LIARS / UNRELIABILITY

**Verdict: liars exist, but they are all *competent, controlled, intelligent* liars — strategic deceivers,
never blusterers, fools, or the confidently-wrong.** The book has no empty braggart, no windbag, no person
who lies badly or believes his own nonsense.

The deceivers on the page:
- **Crispus** lies/slanders — but brilliantly, with Strabo citations and Greek tags, building the fraud charge
  into genuine legal danger (ch10, ch16). He is "clever and not always wrong" (canon). A *smart* liar.
- **Vibenius** is the master of strategic ambiguity: he reads Daniel's fire so that "whichever way it went
  now, he had read it true ... The haruspex is never wrong" (ch08). Calculated, never exposed as a fraud.
- **Scaeva** deceives through "a freedman of his who ... let me feel, through the warm words, the length of
  the leash" (ch26). Smooth, lethal, controlled.
- **Daniel himself** is the book's biggest liar — the spyglass claim "had been a lie I told to live" (ch40),
  the niter beds "I had to pretend belonged to a man in volcano country" (ch22) — but his lies are clever
  survival tactics.
- **Petty cheats** appear but are immediately *out-thought*: "the Helena one's short. She'll say you owe her
  two more asses ... and she'll be lying, it's here" (ch10). The carter "shaving" a tariff for a year is
  caught by Marcia (ch22). The cheats lose; the smart people detect them instantly.

What is **absent**: anyone who bluffs and is empty, who is wrong with confidence and exposed as a blowhard,
who rambles, who misunderstands and barrels on. Macer is blunt and exploitative but always *correct* about
money. Even the antagonists never miscalculate stupidly. There is no Roman equivalent of the loud fool who
talks past the facts.

## 5. THE "EVERYONE IS COMPETENT" SMELL

**Verdict: strongly present — and it is the book's signature tic.** This overlaps with, and confirms from a
different angle, the `dialogue_distinctiveness.md` finding of "a slight surplus of quotable wit distributed
across the upper cast." The problem is broader than the *upper* cast: it reaches all the way down to
ten-year-olds and walk-ons.

The recurring move is: **a side character lands the scene's smartest line, often completing or out-thinking
Daniel.** The worst offenders:
- **Lucanus, age 10**, delivers the thematic capstone of ch37 ("Then he did all right") — and the *narrator
  explicitly canonizes it* as the truest, most unfoldable thing said all day. A child out-philosophizes the
  protagonist.
- **Apollodorus** doesn't just disagree — he diagnoses Daniel's entire character arc in one speech: "you
  bend ... you fold your true things smaller and smaller until there is nothing left to fold, and you will
  die a rich man no one ever heard say a single thing straight" (ch37). The walk-on engineer reads Daniel's
  soul better than Daniel does.
- **Marcia** reads "the whole architecture of my enterprise" in the time it takes to drink a cup (ch22), then
  finishes Daniel's strategic thinking for him about Hadrian: "A way to be worth more without being bigger
  ... There is an emperor coming who reads." (ch32) — she completes his logic.
- **Heras** continually out-frames Daniel and gets the aphoristic mic-drop ("Now make it not ugly, and you
  will have done something, and not before," ch10; "I am too old to teach another barbarian his manners,"
  ch37).
- **Eudemus, Hadrian, Hermes, Tyche, Celer** — every one of them gets at least one perfectly-honed,
  quotable, perceptive line. The fish-seller's son, the nervous clerk, the perfumer's wife: all "correct,"
  all quick.

The cumulative effect is a world where *everyone is operating at the top of their intelligence at all
times,* and the only limits are technological. No one is ever simply slow on the uptake, gets the wrong end
of a stick and keeps it, or says something genuinely dim.

## 6. VERDICT + FIXES

**VERDICT: The book substantially commits the failure — with sophistication.** It dodges the *register*
version of the trap (narration is ornate, Daniel's spoken Latin is deliberately broken, voices are
mechanically differentiated). But on the **competence/class/honesty/intelligence** axis it is firmly inside
the failure: there is no dull person who speaks, no class-marked rough or halting grammar, **zero** crude
oaths in any character's mouth, no empty blusterer or confidently-wrong fool, and a pervasive habit of
letting side characters — down to a 10-year-old — out-perceive the protagonist and land the scene's smartest
line. The book's *handling* of class is honest about power (collars, ownership, deference) but dishonest
about *minds*: it makes everyone sharp and simply silences (mutes, narrates around) anyone who would have to
sound dumb or crude. The "slow slave" turns out to be a genius performing slowness — the failure mode written
into the premise.

**Five specific, existing characters to roughen / dumb / coarsen, and where:**

1. **Pamphilus (the collared slave) — give him rough, limited, *spoken* lines instead of muteness.** Right
   now the book's answer to "how would an uneducated laborer talk?" is to keep him silent (ch10, 13, 20).
   Let him speak in genuinely small, ungrammatical, concrete fragments — get a measurement wrong,
   misunderstand an instruction, swear tiredly at a dropped bolt. He is the single best vehicle for
   class-marked dialogue and currently carries none.

2. **Davus (ch08) and the recruit Naso (ch11) — let a soldier/laborer be coarse and a little dim on the
   page.** Naso is *narrated* as "stupid with the particular fearlessness of a boy who has not yet been
   hurt," and his only near-quote ("the Thulean being more of a mother than his mother") is reported, not
   quoted. Give him an actual crude, cocky, foolish line *before* the disaster — let the reader hear the
   stupidity the narration claims. Same for Davus, who has no lines at all.

3. **Onesimus (ch10) — promote the book's one reactionary into a fuller confidently-wrong figure.** He
   already refuses the new figures "out of spite." Give him a scene where he *argues* for the abacus, gets
   the reasoning wrong, won't be moved, and is plainly the lesser mind for it — the book's only candidate for
   "wrong and stays wrong," currently under-used and too dignified.

4. **The crowd / hecklers (ch08, ch16, ch26) — quote them instead of muting them.** The author repeatedly
   reaches for "shouted something I did not catch." Render two or three actual rough, dumb, superstitious,
   or obscene crowd lines (a Pompeii-graffiti register). This is the cheapest single fix: the crowds are
   already present and already characterized as frightened and crude — just let them *talk*.

5. **Macer or Celer — let one of the hard men actually swear, crudely, once.** Both are written as blunt
   soldiers'/contractors' men whose roughness is always either clean ("Smaller fire next time," ch11) or
   muted ("something low and soldier's-rough I didn't catch," ch11). A single genuine Roman oath in Macer's
   mouth at a lost contract, or Celer's after the burning, would crack the clean-mouthed seal and cost
   nothing in characterization — it would *deepen* it.
