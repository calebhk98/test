# Chapter 21: The Applications
Dates (from the chapter header and text): Header "October 2022 to March 2023". Essays in autumn 2022; applications land in November; thirty institutions have files by January; visiting representatives in winter; Nadia's job count tallied mid-March; decisions arrive across March 2023.
Chloe's age: Not stated; 17 for this period (she turns 18 in August 2023; the text says "a seventeen-year-old picks things" and ch22 gives 17).   POV: Mostly close third on Chloe, but drifts widely into omniscient: an admissions officer at Penn, a committee, the Caltech and Duke representatives, and Nadia's notebook are all told from their own side.
Setting(s): Third-floor common room; Ruth's room; admissions offices (Penn); a windowless reading room (Caltech rep); campus visitors' programs, dining hall; library; Chloe's dorm floor.

## What happens
The year of ninety-one applies to colleges. Chloe, ranked in the middle since she was seven, expects the state school. She applies to fourteen: eight Ivies, the state school, her mother's college, the community college, a Michigan school, Georgetown, and one other.

On a Sunday they write essays in Ruth's room. Chloe cannot cut her "challenge" essay (the year she taught the twelves and Marek would not hand anything in) to six hundred words and cuts it three times; Marek survives as half a line. Sam finishes in under an hour and complains a paragraph decides his future. Kavi finishes in twenty minutes, one word over. Odile submits five pages as a file. Ruth dithers over what "failed" means. Nadia is not applying to schools; she is applying for jobs (plumbing supply on Route 8, a dealership, a transmission shop, freight dispatch in Ambridge, an insurance trainee program). Chloe writes about the reservoir hill, and for her mother's college she writes about her mother's old sweatshirt.

Applications land in November. The legal transcript is read as a formatting error because of the number of classes. The essays are suspected of ghostwriting, until a committee checks birth years. A Penn officer reads Odile's essay, which mentions a self-defense assessment, and phones a colleague. By January over thirty institutions have open files on Halstead. Three applicants list research papers; the school allows reading them only in a windowless room under escort. Five accept. A Caltech rep asks for the third author, who is sixteen and in class until four; the answer to "four" is no.

Representatives from Yale, Stanford, Duke, Caltech, MIT and others visit. The Duke rep asks what the sports building is and is told "Sport", "Athletics", "Recreation". Stanford sits in the crypto elective, Yale in Hearn's seminar (ten pages a week), MIT in the radio elective. At dinner the Caltech rep asks about group projects; Bex gets up and tells the long field project, claiming the geometry. Chloe had drawn it on the back of an exam script in April, with Bex present. At breakfast Bex says it was true by the time she said it. Ruth says it is the second time that morning and stops saving Bex a seat. Chloe tells her mother only that Bex answered for the long field and got it right.

Sam tells a rep he is first in fighting; Odile is second. Ruth says her hardest thing was two years of Saturdays. Nadia has sent 190 applications against twelve replies, ten from software. Ruth advises sending each twice; the pasted-back copy draws a call.

Decisions arrive across March. Ruth gets MIT; Kavi MIT, Caltech, Chicago; Sam all but one; Odile everywhere; Chloe twelve of fourteen. Sam says the three percent acceptance rate lies. Chloe puts the letters in a drawer.

## Who knows what
- Chloe knows Bex took credit for the geometry; Ruth knows it too and withdraws her seat for Bex.
- Bex believes she is entitled to it ("true by the time I said it"); she does not apologise.
- Chloe's mother knows only that Bex answered for the long field and got it right.
- Nadia has not told her mother the true count; she answers the shop phone first.
- Nadia does not know why her applications fail; Ruth guesses the software is matching listing to itself.
- The admissions offices believe the transcripts are errors, then that the essays are ghostwritten, then check birth years.
- Sam believes the three percent figure is a lie by definition.
