## What happens
Chloe's blog grows over the year. People keep asking who works with her, including an editor at a paper asking about syndication; her answer is always that it is just her. Roughly a third of those conversations trail off. She adds each to the sheet she began in February; by winter it has a dozen rows, none naming an outlet. She writes most nights after midnight, working full course load and the translation job; none of it registers as a load.

She studies Hebrew in her second year (alphabet in a weekend, reads a Tel Aviv opinion column with a dictionary by March and without by June). Three weeks after she turns twenty she sits the Foreign Service exam: a four hour written section with a world affairs part she enjoys like a crossword. The result arrives by mail weeks later (the result is not stated).

A background investigation takes ten weeks. She fills out the electronic questionnaire for national security positions in one evening. Investigator Whitaker comes to her apartment in the second week of October at eight, with credentials, notebook and thermos. He explains the visit is a full accounting of her life, verified where possible. She asks whether any of it is released; he says it goes into a controlled file and nobody opens it without a reason on record.

He goes through her life. Residential school from the April after her seventh birthday, after a summer programme which is how they found her; her mother questioned the school at length and refused the free campus house so she could take Chloe back at will. Chloe lists languages: Spanish, French, Mandarin, Russian, Latin, Arabic, German, Japanese, Hindi, Korean, Swahili, then Polish and Hebrew from her transcript. He asks her to speak; he only notes that she did. She lists classes over twenty minutes. She taught mathematics from the seventh year: seven students in the first year, one failed; three years later he returned a stack of practice papers with only a better question written on the back of the top sheet; she kept it two years then threw it away. She describes the school's bar exam, sat at sixteen, two days in the long room on the second floor, proctored by the teachers who wrote it; she failed in April by a few points and passed in October. Whitaker's pen pauses and he asks nothing more. She names two published papers; a third is internal, she will not say its subject, asked by the lead researcher because other names are on it. Her condition: her name on the next agreement or she stops telling the researcher anything not public. Two other similar projects, same condition. Whitaker asks if she would tell him if it were necessary; she says she would want to talk to the researcher first.

He returns in the first week of November, with a thicker folder. He mentions the reference interviews; Chloe guesses the longest was Bex Alcantar and he neither confirms nor denies. He asks who did the geometry on the long field with the wire down the fence; she did (from April), with Nadia there for the run in March. He asks who the school's principal donor is; she does not know. At the door he jokes about writing it in triplicate for a form needing one copy. The clearance arrives in December; she calls her mother and leaves a message, files it in the kitchen drawer. The job starts in January as a trainee; she is sworn in that August, on her birthday.
