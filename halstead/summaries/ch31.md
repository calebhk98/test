# Chapter 31: Ruth
Dates (from the chapter header and text): Header "October 2023 - November 2025". Opens October 2023 (Ruth's first year at MIT); study-group joke in November 2023; "no fifth-grade" remark in February (2024); a year-long silence follows; April finals and the chat posts; the group-chat scenes at the end are undated (see Open threads).
Chloe's age: not stated. Chloe is in the final chat scene only.   POV: Ruth, close third. The chat scenes are bare transcript with no POV.
Setting(s): MIT / Cambridge (registrar, department office, a fourth-floor common room, a library study room, library carrel, a professor's fourth-floor office, a dining hall, a dorm); the group chat.

## What happens
Ruth spends her first year at MIT believing the school has put her in a remedial track. The first-year sequence covers material she had at eleven. She spends October chasing offices: the registrar is patient but unmoved and hands her a petition form that needs a faculty signature; advising and the department send her back and forth. She carries a folder of problem sets clipped in date order. A woman in the department office reads the whole folder and tells her the sequence she is in is the only one there is, and that she herself sat on the committee for two years before Ruth's. Ruth does not accept this; she decides the mistake is "upstream" and files it in the folder as a problem to solve later.

Classes fit what she remembers from the middle years at Halstead: a Friday problem set takes forty minutes on a Tuesday. A preceptor returns a perfect problem set with no comment and says he has not graded enough to know whether the class is easy for the room or just for her. She reads silence as confirmation.

In November she makes a small joke in a study group (it needs two things held together; it would have got a groan from Sam and a correction from Kavi). Three people look at her; someone says "wait, what?"; explaining kills it. In February she asks the study group when the course will get past fifth-grade concepts. A boy called Devansh laughs, then watches her face and stops. The table goes quiet and Ruth cannot work out why. That night she writes on the inside cover of her problem-set notebook that MIT tracks students by ability and keeps that quiet.

For most of a year she holds that theory. She hides it from her parents (her mother calls Sundays; Ruth twice starts "so the placement thing" and asks about the dog instead). In spring Devansh brings her a proof; she finds the dropped negative on line six in under a minute. He asks if she is ever stuck; she says "No."

A professor breaks the theory in office hours. He stops her mid-explanation and makes her write out the two skipped lines. At step three he puts his hand on the paper. She has to unpack an assumption she had folded into one line and finds the sentence for it: "The tail dies before the boundary does." He says it took her five passes, that third-year graduate students would not have got him there at all, and that she went and found the sentence. She leaves early and sits on a wall until the light changes twice. The realization is that an ordinary Halstead peer would have got there in two of his five passes.

Over following months she looks up national statistics herself: adults fluent in a second language, adult reading levels, a fractions benchmark. All are far worse than she assumed. She posts each into the chat as a bare fact, and each time someone jokes and moves on. In April she stops posting and mostly stops answering.

The final scenes are chat. Ruth tells the group she needs ten minutes without jokes, says she has had this for about a year because "i didnt want it to be true," and says they are not "the same kind of thing" as other people. She asks who picked her at six and why; Chloe says they do not know what was on the form; Sam says his grandmother nearly binned his letter.
