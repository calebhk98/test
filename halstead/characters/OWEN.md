# OWEN HALLWORTH

Found by the same nationwide search that found Chloe, Sam, Ruth, and Kavi — a prodigy in every sense the school measures — Owen swam the length, built the bridge, and answered the fraction question like everyone else who goes on to spend the next eleven years at Halstead. Two weeks into camp, he decided he didn't want to, and went home.

**Age span:** six to seven, present only in the camp timeframe (chapters/05_behind.md), with one conversation about him roughly nine months later (chapters/10_april.md) | **MBTI:** ISFJ (a writing aid, not a claim — steady, home- and routine-oriented, values a small trusted circle over novelty) | **IQ:** the same tier as the rest of his camp cohort — he was selected by the identical process that found Chloe, Sam, Ruth, and Kavi, and nothing in the text suggests otherwise
**Appears in:** chapters/05_behind.md, chapters/10_april.md (present only in memory — he is not physically in this chapter; Chloe asks about him and other characters answer)
**Speaks:** 0 directly quoted lines. Every word connected to Owen in the book arrives through someone else — Sam, Ruth, Kavi — never as his own quoted dialogue.

## Voice in one paragraph

Nobody quotes him directly, but the record of what he did is clear enough to work from. On a fifteen-minute timed writing exercise, he produced two precise sentences, crossed one out, and stopped when time was called (chapters/05_behind.md:7) — brief, but exact, on the first morning anyone at camp had ever asked him to write to a clock. During the fractions lesson he went past the material actually on offer and asked "about which number the flipping happens to" (chapters/05_behind.md:38) — a question aimed straight at the mechanism, not the answer, the same kind of question Kavi and Ruth ask all day. He hit the edge of what he'd worked out and said so plainly rather than bluff past it. He swam the length and built the bridge cleanly enough that nobody who watched him thought twice about it (chapters/10_april.md:103). None of this is decoration — it's a boy doing exactly what the other future-Halstead kids around him are doing, at the same level, for two weeks, and then choosing something else.

## Dials

No dialogue exists to measure. If given lines: short, plain, home-grounded sentences. He'd describe a specific thing — his dog, his sister, a Saturday routine — rather than an abstraction, and he'd answer a direct question before adding anything else.

## Signature — theirs alone

None in the text. If given lines, he answers with a concrete, named thing rather than a general statement — asked what he misses, he'd say a name, not a category.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it names one small, specific, home-scale thing rather than a general complaint.
- YES if it answers the question actually asked and stops, without performing for an audience.
- YES if it's plain and undramatic even when the subject is upsetting — his crying happens privately, not as a scene (chapters/05_behind.md:78).
- YES if it's the kind of flat, short statement Sam and Kavi both attribute to him — "wasn't having fun" (chapters/05_behind.md:67, 70), "he doesn't know" (chapters/05_behind.md:38).
- YES if it's curious about a specific mechanism rather than a general "why" (chapters/05_behind.md:38).
- NO if it's rendered as an argument or a performance for the group — every account has him speaking to one person, quietly, not to a crowd.
- NO if it explains his own feelings analytically. He's six; everything about him is concrete and outcome-shaped, never self-analyzing.

## Are they acting like themselves?

The clearest thing anyone can say about Owen under pressure is that he goes somewhere private with it. He cried, alone, in a stairwell by the kitchens, on a Thursday — witnessed once at the time by Ruth ("He was crying in the stairwell. On Thursday.", chapters/05_behind.md:78) and once, separately, by Kavi, who says so unprompted nine months later: "He was crying by the kitchens on the Thursday and I saw him, but I didn't say anything, because I was seven and I didn't know what you say to that." (chapters/10_april.md:99). Two witnesses, months apart, agreeing on the same detail is about as solid as anything in this book gets. He didn't make it anyone else's problem to manage — he handled it where nobody had to see it, and kept doing everything else camp asked of him until the day his mother came.

Failure mode: giving him a line in his own voice that explains himself. The whole shape of the character is that Chloe never gets that from him directly; building his interior life in the sections below, in the sheet's own voice, is a different and permitted move.

## Subjects and schedule

Owen never enrolls at Halstead, so this is what he already had going into camp, and what he was doing with it while he was there — the same caliber of material the school's own kids bring, because the same search found him.

- **Already excellent at, before camp:** reading well above grade level and comfortable with it; a peewee baseball league at home, where he plays outfield and reads a fly ball early; building things with a basic tool kit, supervised, and finishing what he starts.
- **What he showed at camp:** real curiosity about the mechanism of the fraction-flip rule (chapters/05_behind.md:38) — the same kind of question that gets Kavi and Ruth praised across the rest of the book, asked by a six-year-old two weeks into his first time away from home.
- **Confirmed, by the most careful account anyone gives:** swimming and the bridge-building exercise, both done to the same standard as the kids who go on to spend a decade at Halstead — "He did the water thing and he did the bridge and he was fine." (chapters/10_april.md:103).
- **The writing exercise:** two sentences in fifteen minutes, one crossed out, is short next to Chloe's six or Ruth's page and a half — but it's the first timed writing prompt of his life, on day one or two of an entirely new environment, and what's there is precise rather than thin: a complete thought, revised once, and stopped clean when time was called. Nothing about it reads as a limit on what he could do with more time in the room.

**What did Owen want instead of camp?** This is the sheet's central job, and the answer is a decision: Owen liked his ordinary life exactly as it was and didn't want a bigger one — not because a bigger one was beyond him, but because he'd already worked out that his own was good. He has a younger sister, Della, two years behind him, who he looks after without being asked. Saturday mornings are pancakes with his mom and Della before his baseball game. There's a dog, Biscuit, who sleeps on his bed. Camp wasn't hard — the water thing was fine, the bridge was good — it was constant: a new thing every hour, for four weeks, with nobody around who'd known him longer than two weeks. He didn't need to be tested by strangers to know his life was good. He said so, plainly, to an adult, and it worked — he went home.

## Home

A small town, a modest house. His mother raises Owen and Della largely on her own; his father works long stretches away on a highway construction crew and is home in blocks rather than every night, which is part of why the household's regular rhythms — pancakes, Little League, the dog — matter as much as they do: when everyone is actually home together, it's worth protecting. He's used to being the reliable one when his dad's away — checking on Della, walking Biscuit, showing up to his own game on time without being told twice — so a month away from all of it wasn't a small gap to him the way it might be to a kid whose house runs the same whether he's in it or not.

## Body

Small for his age, still a little round in the face. Tires quickly when he cries — goes red, gets a hiccup in his breathing, and is done inside a few minutes rather than staying upset for long. Holds onto the zipper pull or drawstring of his bag when he's nervous, which is where the one physical detail the text gives him comes from: "he had a blue thing hanging off his bag" (chapters/10_april.md:35) — a small blue keychain shaped like a dog, a rough match for Biscuit, that he fidgets with under stress.

## Personality off the page

Sits near a window or a door rather than in the middle of a room. Bored: draws the same dog over and over. Wrong: takes correction easily, doesn't argue. Praised: pleased, doesn't show off about it. Tired: wants to be near someone he already trusts. A free Saturday: home — pancakes, Biscuit, his game. What he finds funny in other people: physical, slapstick humor.

## Interior life

Wants to go home and have things be a normal size again. Afraid, at camp, of being somewhere so full of new things happening every hour that he can't find the edges of it. Thinks about Della and Biscuit before sleep. Would spend a free year doing exactly what his current life already looks like — no changes. Believes a good life doesn't have to be an extraordinary one, which is a belief every other camper in this book, chasing the next thing to get good at, would push back on hard. What he's wrong about: assuming the homesick feeling would pass if he just waited it out — it didn't, and going home wasn't a failure of nerve on his part, it was a correct read of what he actually wanted; a six-year-old doesn't have the words for "I evaluated this correctly," so it just felt like losing.

## The small stuff

Holds a small blue dog-shaped keychain from home, matching Biscuit. Draws the same dog repeatedly. Asks for the same bedtime routine every night if anyone will do it with him. Misses his mother's pancakes as much as he misses any one person, at least in the first few days.

## Facts a rewriter needs

- **Name:** Owen Hallworth — the manuscript gives only "Owen."
- **Family:** Mother, a younger sister named Della, a father who works away on a highway construction crew, a dog named Biscuit. The manuscript confirms only that a mother came to collect him (chapters/10_april.md:99); everything past that is this sheet's proposal.
- **Timeline:** in Chloe's camp hall on the Monday of week two; his room stripped to the mattress by the Wednesday (chapters/05_behind.md:63).
- **Capability, settled:** the same tier as the rest of the camp's future Halstead cohort — not behind on either the water exercise or the bridge (chapters/10_april.md:103). He could do the work and chose not to stay.
- **Why he left:** he missed the ordinary scale of his life at home and didn't want camp's constant novelty, per Sam's account (chapters/05_behind.md:67, 70). Kavi's account that he was crying "about going" and "didn't want to go" (chapters/10_april.md:99–103) reads as the opposite and remains an open contradiction in the text; see Known problems.
- **The roughly ten of ninety families near camp:** the camp has ninety kids (chapters/10_april.md:33), and the parent hotel's breakfast room holds "eight or nine adults" near the end of camp (chapters/06_the_list.md:3) — a small fraction of families staying close by. Nothing in the text states Owen's family wasn't among them; connecting his departure to that absence is this sheet's inference, not a stated fact.

## Known problems

1. **Direct contradiction between witnesses on capability.** Ruth: "kind of, he's the one who wouldn't do the water thing" (chapters/10_april.md:37). Kavi, unhedged: "He did the water thing and he did the bridge and he was fine." (chapters/10_april.md:103). The scene gives Kavi's account more weight — deliberate, unhedged, a direct answer to Chloe's question — but the contradiction is on the page. Fix: soften Ruth's line, or have Kavi's acknowledge and correct it rather than silently supersede it.
2. **Direct contradiction between witnesses on whether he wanted to leave at all.** Sam, in the moment: "He wasn't having fun. He said it wasn't fun." (chapters/05_behind.md:67, 70) — reads as Owen choosing to go. Kavi, months later, explicitly not guessing: "Owen didn't want to go home... he didn't want to go" (chapters/10_april.md:99–103) — reads as the opposite. This sheet resolves it in favor of Sam's account to build a full, writable child, but the contradiction remains unresolved in the manuscript itself. Fix: decide which is true and adjust the other account — Kavi's is written with more conviction and is the later, more considered telling, so it may be the one the text wants trusted.
3. **Zero direct quotation.** Worth the author confirming this was intentional — right now there's no way to tell "he was designed to never speak" from "he simply hasn't been given a line yet."

## Do not confuse with

**Kavi**, who narrates the most detailed and most trusted account of him (chapters/10_april.md:99–107). The cue that separates them: everything attributed to Owen is reported in someone else's plain, unelaborated register — if a line about Owen starts explaining *why* rather than stating *what happened*, that reasoning belongs to whichever character is doing the telling, not to Owen.
