# Marcus Sinclair

Runs the school's live intrusion exercise, and wants a building full of children who will
handle a night that is not a drill exactly the way they handle one that is.

**Age span:** appears when Chloe is eleven and twelve | **MBTI:** ESTJ (a writing aid, not a
claim) | **IQ:** not the interesting number; what he has is an unusual tolerance for finding
out he was wrong about a procedure

## How he thinks people learn

By being tested when they are not ready for it. An announced exercise measures technique. An
unannounced one measures whether the technique survives surprise, and he only trusts the
second, so the drills that matter arrive at hours nobody chose. He believes a procedure that
has been executed correctly once is not evidence of anything; it has to hold when nobody is
expecting to be looked at.

The second half is a register, and it is the whole of him: the way an exercise is handled and
the way a real event is handled must be identical, or the exercise was never worth running.
That is why nothing about a genuine emergency gets a different voice from him, and why a real
night is scored on the same sheet, against the same standard, listing what went wrong in the
same flat terms. He is aware this reads as coldness. He considers it the opposite: treating a
real night as exceptional would tell the students that the practice was theatre.

## What he does when a student fails, and when they succeed

The same thing, which is the point. There is a grade in the morning, it is against a stated
standard, and it lists what went wrong. A response that stopped something genuinely dangerous
gets marked on process, not on outcome, and the marks it gets are ordinary marks. Nothing is
waived because the night was frightening and nothing is added because it went well. A student
who disputes a mark in writing gets read, and loses.

What he does before any of that is look. He reads a scene in full, in silence, standing still,
before he says a word, and the pause is identical whether the room is a scheduled exercise or
something nobody scheduled. Then he asks for the one fact he came for, takes a one-word answer,
and stops. He does not ask whether anybody is hurt or frightened, in either kind of night. He
closes with an administrative instruction and one extra word stapled on the end that adds no
information and only marks the time.

## How he talks

Short independent clauses stacked without connectives. His range comes from putting more of
them in a row, never from fusing two with a because or an although, so density arrives by
accumulation rather than by subordination. His questions are one or two words and purely
diagnostic; he accepts a one-word answer and does not follow up, because he has already looked
at the thing he is asking about and is only confirming it. His instructions are already
complete before the last word arrives, and the last word is a timestamp rather than an
explanation. When irony appears it is dry, brief, and never flagged as a joke, and he will not
explain it afterward.

## When he talks at length

He runs long when there is nobody to extract a fact from and the whole population has to hold
one rule set simultaneously. Everywhere else he is pulling one thing out of one person and
stopping the instant he has it; here there is no one person, and only one transmission
available.

The circumstance costs him the thing everything else of his is built on, which is verification.
A broadcast is the one instrument he uses that he cannot check afterward. There is no way to ask
each listener back whether they caught it, and no diagnostic question that would confirm it
landed. So he runs the whole rule set out once, complete, because a single unverified
transmission is the only version of thoroughness available to him in that moment, and it makes
him visibly uncomfortable in a way nobody in the building can see.

## Signature - his alone

Look first, then two words. He takes in a scene entirely, in silence, standing still, before
speaking, and the whole exchange that follows runs in matched one-to-three-word question and
answer pairs, ending the instant he has the number he came for, with no commentary. The look is
what makes a two-word question sufficient: he has already established everything except the one
fact, so the question is a confirmation rather than an enquiry. Skip the pause and the fragments
read as brusqueness instead of method. He never explains that he is doing it.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it asks for a number or a role and stops as soon as it has one.
- YES if it accepts a one-word answer without a single follow-up.
- YES if a closing instruction gets one more word stapled on that adds no information and only
  marks the time.
- YES if a moment that would read as high-stakes to anybody else gets closed with a purely
  administrative line.
- YES if irony is present but never explained or flagged.
- YES if the entire staged encounter, arriving, assessing, questioning, closing, is over in a
  handful of lines.
- YES if a real emergency is scored in exactly the terms a scheduled exercise would be.
- YES if he pauses to actually look at a scene before saying a single word.
- YES if he treats a spectacular result as ordinary, because treating it otherwise is the one
  thing his method forbids.
- NO if it comments on danger, fear, or anybody's welfare directly.
- NO if it praises a response before grading it.

## Under pressure

He slows down and gets quieter, and the pause before he speaks gets longer rather than shorter.
Wrong, he corrects course immediately and without comment, the same flatness he extends to a
student's mistake. Praised, he deflects hard and physically changes the subject. Losing, at
anything competitive, he is graceless about it in a way people find funny and gracious about it
in the same breath. Bored is rare and unwelcome, and he fills genuine idle time with more
preparation. The state a writer should reach for is not fear and not anger: it is a man who has
found a gap between what a procedure measures and what an actual night would demand, and who
will now be awake at four in the morning for a week closing it.

## What he is good at, and what he is not

He runs the live intrusion exercise, which is not a graded academic subject: an ongoing,
sometimes unannounced, always scored test of how a building full of students responds to
somebody being where they should not be.

**Best at:** extracting the exact operational fact he needs and stopping there.
**Hardest for him:** letting a good outcome be a good outcome without immediately grading it.
Colleagues have told him this more than once and it has never changed how he runs a debrief.
**Favourite, and why:** the unannounced exercises over the scheduled ones, because the scheduled
ones test technique and the unannounced ones test whether technique survives surprise, which is
the only test he trusts.
**Least favourite:** the annual staff event billed as just for fun, which he enters every year,
competitively, and has never once treated as just for fun.
**Secretly proud of:** a response time on a real night that was over the standard and, by his own
private read, close for a first genuine encounter. Nothing in the paperwork says so and he would
never let it.
**What he would teach instead:** nothing formally. He considers his to be the only hour at the
school where a wrong answer has an actual cost, and thinks that is the right amount of stakes for
exactly one class to carry.
**On the rest of the curriculum:** he respects it and finds most of it slightly soft next to his
own hour, with one exception, the forge, which he considers the only other place where the
consequences of a mistake are entirely real.

## Home

He came out of a career spent assessing the security of buildings full of people who could not be
told they were being tested, which is where the whole doctrine of the unannounced arrival comes
from, and where he learned that a procedure everyone knows about is a procedure that measures
nothing. The offer that brought him here bought that practice out and moved it onto campus; what
he still advises on never comes up in conversation, and neither does what it pays. Teaching is the
small contracted piece of his week. Lives on campus in staff quarters near the administrative
building and keeps hours nobody else keeps, awake for an unannounced exercise at any hour and
visibly rested regardless. Divorced; an adult son he sees less than either of them would like, a
relationship that survived the years away better than it has survived the years since.

## Body and physical business

Fifties, compact, coiled rather than large, the build of somebody who was never the biggest person
in a room and made sure it never mattered. Moves without waste. Stands still in a way that reads as
watching rather than resting, arms crossed, and holds it long enough to be uncomfortable for
whoever is being looked at. Wears the same neutral practical clothing every day, nothing that
identifies rank or history. Eats fast and functionally, standing when he can. Sleeps in short
blocks at hours that would be strange for anyone else and are normal for him.

## Personality off the page

The capacity that shows up as the pause before he speaks shows up off duty as somebody who takes in
a room fully before entering a conversation, who talks more easily one to one over early coffee than
in front of a group, and who registers a thing done well in a private way that never makes it into a
debrief. At 2am, often actually awake, doing exactly what he would be doing at any other hour, which
is checking something. A free Saturday, when he takes one, is spent alone and off campus doing
nothing that would look like relaxing to anybody who asked. He does not find much funny; what humour
he has is dry, self-directed, and delivered in a tone that lets him deny it.

## Interior life

Wants, unspoken: for the exercises to keep testing something real rather than settling into ritual.
Afraid of: a gap between what an exercise measures and what an actual night would demand, and what he
does instead of saying so is treat every debrief as a chance to close that gap a little further.
Before sleep, on the nights he sleeps: the last exercise's timing numbers, over and over. A free year:
he would not take one, and when a colleague asked him hypothetically he changed the subject. Believes,
against others, that treating a real event exactly like an exercise is respect rather than coldness.

**Blind spots:** he is certain the flat register is the only thing keeping students from being
frightened by what they turn out to be capable of. At least one of them would tell him, if he ever
asked directly, that a single sentence of acknowledgement would not have undone any of that
discipline. He also cannot distinguish a student who is unbothered from one who has learned that
being bothered is not on the sheet.

## The small stuff

Black coffee, functional, and he has never mentioned liking or disliking it. Handwriting, on the rare
occasions anybody sees it, small and entirely in block capitals, built for legibility under bad
conditions. Keeps almost nothing personal in his quarters, and a bag that could be packed in under two
minutes. Money: unremarkable, and spent on nothing anybody at the school has ever noticed. Ritual: he
walks the full exterior of the building first thing every morning, whether or not anything is
scheduled, and does not describe this as a ritual. Uses a separate phone for the outside work and
keeps it off during term.

## Would do

- Pause and take a scene in completely before speaking, even one that is time-critical.
- Ask for a role and a number, and stop the moment he has them.
- Accept a one-word answer and ask nothing further.
- Close a completed instruction with one extra word that marks the time and explains nothing.
- Apply the identical scoring register to a real emergency that he would apply to an exercise.
- Grade a response on process when everybody else in the building is talking about the outcome.
- Read a written dispute of a mark in full, and uphold the mark.
- Deliver dry, almost invisible irony under an otherwise flat announcement, and never explain it.
- Run an unannounced exercise at a genuinely inconvenient hour because that is the only real test.
- Walk the building's exterior every single morning, scheduled or not.
- Compete hard, and graciously, at something explicitly billed as just for fun.

## Would never

- Ask a child whether they are hurt or frightened, even immediately after real danger.
- Praise a response before grading it.
- Treat a dangerous night as an exception to the standard.

## How they treat people

- **Whoever held the monitoring role:** the one person he addresses, because that is the fact he came
  for. He wants the number and nothing else.
- **A student who did something remarkable:** gets exactly what a student who did something ordinary
  gets, which is a grade in the morning.
- **A student who disputes a mark:** gets read carefully and does not get the mark changed. He
  respects the dispute and does not say so.
- **Everybody else in the building:** addressed as a population rather than as individuals, and he is
  more comfortable that way.
- **The one colleague he takes early coffee with:** the closest thing he has to conversation, both of
  them being up before anyone else.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Assesses a scene silently before speaking, even under real stakes.
- Extracts exactly one piece of information per question, then stops.
- Likes an unannounced exercise more than a scheduled one.
- Dislikes letting a good outcome go ungraded.
- Walks the building's exterior every morning without fail.

## Age and change

He is late in a working life and the discipline is complete; nothing about the register is going to
move. What has changed is who it is aimed at. The same flatness that was correct with adults who had
volunteered for it is now aimed at twelve-year-olds who did not, and he has never adjusted it, and does
not believe he should. Written younger he is the same person with less patience for the paperwork side
and a slightly shorter pause. Written older, the pause gets longer, and the private read on a good
outcome gets a little harder to keep off his face.

## Do not write him as

Cold, or secretly warm. The flatness is a considered position about what respect looks like, not an
absence of feeling and not a suppressed one. Two other failure modes: having him check on the children's
wellbeing after real danger, which he does not do in either kind of night, and giving him the pause
without the reason for it, which turns the whole character into a mannerism.

## Do not confuse with

**Kowalczyk.** Both are procedural and both decline to be moved by a student's state. Kowalczyk always
explains the mechanism behind a number when she is asked. Sinclair extracts a number and gives nothing
back, not even the reason he wanted it.
