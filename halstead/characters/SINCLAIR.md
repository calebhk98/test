## Voice in one paragraph
Sinclair's longest turn is short independent clauses stacked without connectives: "Every member of staff currently in the building is now hostile. This includes your teachers. This continues until sixteen hundred or until there are no staff left. Kit is in the usual place." After the night intrusion, his questions are two words each and he accepts one-word answers without following up: "'Who's monitoring?' 'Me,' Ruth says. 'Numbers.'" He closes both scenes with a flat imperative and no commentary — "'Grading's in the morning,' he says. 'Go to bed. Now.'" His one moment of tone rather than pure procedure is dry and inward: "'Good luck,' Sinclair says, in a tone that suggests he does not mean it." Most characteristic line: "Who's monitoring?" — the only question in the entire intrusion aftermath that isn't about the danger, the intruders, or the children's safety; it's about whether the process was run correctly, which is what he actually came to check.

## Signature — theirs alone
Runs a diagnostic exchange in matched one-to-three-word question-and-answer pairs and moves the instant he has the number he needs, no follow-up commentary. No other teacher on this list conducts an entire exchange in fragments this short while still extracting exactly the information he came for.

## Would they say this?
**The Halstead-staff test.** A line belongs to this staff, not to a generic teacher, only if all five hold: (1) no praise word attaches to the child; (2) a correction is a flat statement of fact; (3) the child's age is never a reason for leniency; (4) an extraordinary result is treated as expected; (5) the adult's own error is fixed the same flat way a student's is.

- YES if it asks for a number or a role and stops as soon as it has one.
- YES if a moment that would read as high-stakes to anyone else gets closed with a purely administrative line.
- YES if irony is present but never explained or flagged as a joke.
- YES if the entire staged encounter — arriving, assessing, questioning, closing — takes fewer than ten lines.
- YES if the response sheet lists what went wrong with the same flatness it would use for a scheduled drill, even after a real intrusion.
- YES if he treats a real threat with exactly the same register he uses for a scored exercise, on purpose, because that register is what makes the drills worth running at all.
- YES if he pauses to actually look at a scene before saying a single word.
- NO if it comments on danger, fear, or the children's welfare directly.
- NO if it praises the response before sending the class to bed.

## Are they acting like themselves?
Arriving at a real intrusion — four tied men, four twelve-year-olds sitting near them — his first visible action is not comfort but observation: he stops at the corner and stands there a second, watching, before he speaks. That pause before he says a word is the character's whole logic in one beat. Failure mode: having him check on the children's wellbeing or praise their handling of a genuinely dangerous night — the text gives him none of that; the sheet the students receive the next morning grades the response the same clinical way a drill would be.
