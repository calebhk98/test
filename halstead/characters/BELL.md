# COACH BELL

Runs marksmanship (nerf, paintball, archery, later rifles): teaches by handing a student the object that will hurt them and making them look at it instead of flinching from it.

**Age span:** appears throughout Chloe's ages 8-12 (nerf at 8, paintball ~10-11, archery/rifles ~11-12) | **MBTI:** ISTP (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** no textual basis; not assessable
**Appears in:** chapters/11_eight.md, chapters/13_ten_pages.md, chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md, chapters/15_twelve.md
**Speaks:** 9 turns, all four chapters. Not front-loaded — one or two turns per chapter, spread evenly. No line outside these four files.

## Voice in one paragraph
Bell opens on a physical prop, not a claim: he holds the dart, the arrow, his own flat hand, up where the line can see it, and describes what it is doing before he says what to do about it. Sentences run long when he is teaching a mechanism (three or four clauses stacked with commas, rarely "and") and short when he is closing a decision ("Right, then nothing gets pointed at a person."). He never softens a demand with a reason aimed at feelings — the reason is always physical ("that's the only thing you've got going for you and you're going to want all of it"). He does not ask questions; students ask him questions and he answers them as statements, sometimes conceding the student's smaller point while overruling the larger one in the same breath. Most characteristic line: "Look at the size of the thing. Look how slowly it comes at you. You can watch the entire flight from one end to the other, and there is nothing in this building easier to hit than that." (chapters/11_eight.md:141) — because it is honest in a way that makes the task easier rather than harder, which is the whole of his teaching method in one breath.

## Signature — theirs alone
Holds the demonstration object while he talks, and does not let go of it until the point is made: "Bell holds a dart up between two fingers and turns it around" (chapters/11_eight.md:141); "Bell keeps hold of the arrow while he says it" (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:13); "Bell puts a hand out flat and holds it" (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:21). Three instances across two chapters — this is a genuine, textual tic, not a proposal.

## Would they say this?
- YES if it names why a hard-looking thing is actually easy, using its physical properties, not encouragement. ("A foam dart is fat and slow.")
- YES if it concedes a narrower objection and then restates the actual rule unchanged. ("That's fine, and you're still doing it my way.")
- YES if it ends a debate procedurally rather than emotionally. ("Right, then nothing gets pointed at a person.")
- NO if it praises a student's aim, hand, or effort — no praise word attaches to a person anywhere in his lines.
- NO if it asks the student how they feel about the drill.
- NO if it uses a simile or metaphor — his comparisons are always literal physical description, never invented imagery.
- YES if it withholds the answer on purpose and says so ("who is not saying" — chapters/13_ten_pages.md:65 is narration, not dialogue, but it is consistent with how he runs a silence).

## Are they acting like themselves?
Under a nuisance objection (Kavi's "we don't do that with the paintballs," chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:11) he does not argue the merits, he restates the rule and moves the line forward — behavior, not persuasion. He runs paintball for two weeks while the whole year is bad at it and volunteers nothing (chapters/13_ten_pages.md:65), which is the same discipline as the metronome scene: he lets the counting problem be a problem for as long as it takes. Failure modes a writer would fall into: (1) having him praise a hit or a good shot — he never does, not once, in nine turns; (2) having him explain the safety rule with an appeal to danger or fear rather than as a flat procedural line; (3) softening his refusal to answer a question ("who is not saying") into an apology or a "you'll figure it out!" — the text plays it completely flat.

## Likes, dislikes, habits
- Runs drills in a fixed progression, easy version to hard version, and does not skip ahead even under pressure to. [text: chapters/11_eight.md:135, chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:5]
- Physically demonstrates before he explains. [text: three instances above]
- Dislikes being asked to justify a rule about pointing something at a person — refuses to litigate it. [inferred, from the flatness of "Right, then nothing gets pointed at a person."]
- Comfortable with silence as pedagogy — will let a class struggle without giving them the answer. [text: chapters/13_ten_pages.md:65]
