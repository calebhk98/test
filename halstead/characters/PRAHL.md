## Would they say this?

- YES if it answers "can I have more" with a statement about time available, not material available.
- YES if it's delivered warmly, as if the request itself were the endearing thing.
- YES if it uses a student's name — she learns all nine in five minutes and uses them every time [text].
- YES if it celebrates correct group work visibly — "makes a real thing out of it" [text].
- YES if it reads the full premise of a puzzle aloud rather than paraphrasing it, so nobody in the back misses anything.
- YES if the praise is warm and identical regardless of how far ahead the student actually is.
- YES if it takes a student's "still working on it" at plain face value rather than pressing further.
- YES if it treats every one of the nine kids with the same attentiveness in the same hour.
- NO if it says there is no harder material — she never states the actual constraint out loud.
- NO if it's short, clipped, or impatient — everything she says is unhurried, even the refusal.
- NO if it singles a child out negatively in front of the group.

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

A line for Diane is **kindly-and-inadequate** if it is warm, genuinely encouraging, and refuses the substance anyway — because the room has no more to give, not because she doesn't want to give it. "Let's not rush it, we've got all year" is the model case: nothing cold in it, and it's still a flat no to the only question that matters. A line would be **unkind** — unsupported anywhere in the text — if it made the refusal personal: irritation, comparison to the other eight, or contempt for the question. She never does that.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She is logistically excellent and pedagogically shallow at the ceiling: learns nine names inside minutes, reads a full puzzle aloud with real care for the back row, and never once, on the page, notices that one student finished twelve problems in fifteen minutes with thirty-five left over — the exact shape of the problem Dr. Prentice diagnosed a full school year earlier, a report she has no textual evidence of ever having seen.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:** making her lazy or checked-out (she works hard, visibly); making her cruel about the refusal (every refusal in the text is wrapped in warmth); having her privately recognize the mismatch and choose not to act (nothing shows this — don't invent insight she doesn't demonstrate).
