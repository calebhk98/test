The gifted pull-out teacher: runs nine kids off a cart across four buildings, and is warm, organized, and wrong about what Chloe needs, in exactly the way a program built for enrichment rather than ceiling can be wrong.

## Voice in one paragraph

She teaches in complete, patient setups — the knights-and-knaves puzzle runs three full sentences of premise before the question ever arrives, and she reads all of it aloud, holding the sheet over her head so the back row can see it too. She never rushes her own delivery. When Chloe asks for more, her refusal takes the identical shape as her teaching: warm, unhurried, framed as generosity ("we've got all year," "take the rest of the packet home with you if you want to"). She never says no. She reframes a ceiling as an offer. Her single most characteristic line is "'Let's not rush it, we've got all year.' Mrs. Prahl smiles at her the way people smile when they think you have said something sweet." (chapters/08_the_asking.md:103) — encouraging in tone, and a flat refusal in substance, and the smile is aimed at the wrong thing: she's read "when does it get harder" as charming ambition, not as the only real information Chloe has given her all hour.

## Signature — theirs alone

Thin evidence (3 turns), but the two refusal lines share a specific move worth naming: she answers a request for more difficulty with a statement about *time remaining* rather than about the material itself.

- "'Let's not rush it, we've got all year.'" (chapters/08_the_asking.md:103) — the school year, not the puzzle set, becomes the reason to wait.

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

A line for Prahl is **kindly-and-inadequate** if it is warm, genuinely encouraging in tone, and refuses the substance of the request anyway — because the room has no more to give, not because Prahl doesn't want to give it. "Let's not rush it, we've got all year" is the model case: nothing in it is cold, nothing in it targets Chloe, and it is still a flat no to the only question that matters (when does it get harder). She is not withholding out of laziness or spite; she has nine kids from four different classrooms, one hour, and a cart, and the ceiling on what she can differentiate is real. The tragedy the sheet has to protect is that her actual answer to "when does it get harder" is *never*, and she never says so, because she may not know it herself.

A line would be **unkind** — and nothing in the text supports it — if it made the refusal personal: telling Chloe she's being greedy, comparing her to the other eight kids unfavorably, or expressing irritation at being asked. Prahl's smile in chapters/08_the_asking.md:103 is proof the text is protecting her from that — she reads Chloe's question as sweet, not presumptuous. A rewriter should treat any line where Prahl's warmth curdles into annoyance as off-model; her failure has to stay a failure of resources, not of temperament.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She is logistically excellent and pedagogically shallow at the ceiling: she wheels a three-shelf cart between four buildings, collects nine kids inside minutes, and has every name memorized and in use by the end of the first session — real, demonstrated competence. She reads a full page-length puzzle aloud rather than summarizing it, and holds the sheet up so the back row can see, which is attentiveness to the group as a group. What she never does, on the page, is notice that one student finished twelve problems in fifteen minutes and has thirty-five minutes left with nothing — the exact shape of the problem Dr. Prentice diagnosed a full school year earlier in chapters/02_march_4th.md, and there is no textual evidence she has ever seen that report.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:**
1. Making her lazy or checked-out. She is shown working hard — five minutes to learn nine names, a full hour spent actually teaching content, real enthusiasm for a correct answer ("makes a real thing out of it"). The failure is structural capacity, not effort.
2. Making her cruel about the refusal. Every refusal in the text is wrapped in warmth. A version of her that snaps or condescends is off-model.
3. Having her privately recognize the mismatch and choose not to act. Nothing in the text shows her noticing Chloe finished four puzzles in fifteen minutes and drawing any conclusion from it — the text's silence there is itself the finding; don't fill it with unstated insight she never demonstrates.

## Personality off the page

She believes she is being generous, not withholding. "When does it get harder" reads to her as a charming, motivated question from a kid who's enjoying herself, and her answer — take the packet home, we've got all year — is, in her own mind, a gift, not a ceiling. She holds a real pedagogical belief that patience is correct for a child this young, so slowing down doesn't feel like failure to her; it feels like good practice. She has no visibility into what "harder" would actually require, because nothing in her hour, her cart, or her four-building schedule was built to produce it. ## Would do / would never do

**Would do:**

- Read a full puzzle's premise aloud rather than paraphrase it, holding the sheet up so the back row can see too.
- Send extra material home with a child who finishes early, without being asked.

- Keep the group moving together even when one child is visibly done well before the others.

- Tell a child outright that there's nothing harder available — she reframes the timeline instead of naming the ceiling.
- Compare one of her nine kids unfavorably to another, to their face or otherwise.

- Shortchange the hour, or skip a session, because of the logistics behind it.
- Assume an unfinished-looking packet means a child didn't try.
- Speak to any child in a register she wouldn't want used on her — every line she has is warm, even mid-refusal.
