# PRIYA

Chloe's camp roommate, still at the same dinner table nine years later, who wants horses,
loyalty, and to be left alone about the rest of it.

**Age span:** ~7–18 (camp through a one-line post-graduation mention) | **MBTI:** ESFP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** UNDEFINED-leaning-average (reader inference; the text never quantifies her the way it quantifies Kavi or Odile — this is the weakest-evidenced number on this sheet)
**Appears in:** chapters/04_pluto.md, 05_behind.md, 06_the_list.md, 07_the_same_room.md, 10_april.md, 12_nine.md, 13_ten_pages.md, 14_sixty_degrees.md, 15_twelve.md, 16_thirteen.md, 17_fourteen.md, 18_fifteen.md, 19_sixteen.md, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md (one mention, no line)
**Speaks:** ~15 quoted lines across 10 speaking turns in 6 of the 13 chapters (04, 10, 12, 15, 17). Not front-loaded the way it first looks — she is present and referenced in narration in every chapter on the list, and named as a fixture of the daily dinner table (16_thirteen.md:121), but only about a third of her chapters give her a quoted line.

## Voice in one paragraph

She never opens with a greeting or a setup — the first thing out of her mouth assumes you were
already in the conversation. Once she's going, a turn tends to keep adding clauses instead of
stopping at the first complete thought, chained with "and," "which," or a trailing qualifier,
until the thought runs out on its own. Her short lines (three words or fewer) are not complete
statements the way Sam's are — they're launches, immediately followed by the longer thing they
were opening for. She states even trivia as settled fact, no hedge, no "I think." She demonstrates
rather than argues: shown a doubt, she walks up to the horse instead of explaining further. Most
characteristic line: "There's a girl at my barn who says hers is a horse and it is absolutely not
a horse, it's fourteen even, I've seen the paper." (chapters/04_pluto.md:17) — three clauses
chained by "and," delivered as flat fact, about a horse, to a total stranger she has known for
under a minute.

## Signature — theirs alone

  "Oh, sure, that's fine," she says, and keeps working oil into a bridle she already had out for

## Would they say this?

- YES if the line opens with no greeting, as if answering a question the reader didn't hear —
  "Do you ride?" (chapters/04_pluto.md:13); "At breakfast." (chapters/17_fourteen.md:131).
- YES if a single turn keeps adding clauses with "and" or "which" instead of stopping at the
  first complete thought — "There's a girl at my barn who says hers is a horse and it is
  absolutely not a horse, it's fourteen even, I've seen the paper." (chapters/04_pluto.md:17).
- NO if the line is one flat, single-clause declarative with nothing added after the first full
  stop — that belongs to Sam or Kavi.
- YES if a short opener (three words or fewer) is immediately followed, same turn or next beat,
  by a longer explanation. Her short lines are never left to stand alone.
- NO if the line hedges — "I think," "maybe," "probably." She states horse trivia and everything
  else as settled fact.
- YES [new, untested] if a disagreement is voiced as agreement plus an unrelated action
  continuing in the background, rather than a correction or a refusal.
- NO if a disagreement corrects someone's wording or challenges them by name — that's Ruth's or
  Kavi's move.
- YES [new, unused] if an animal shows up in a comparison about a person or situation and the
  line never flags it as a comparison — no "like," no "as."
- NO if the figurative language is about weather, machinery, or anything that isn't an animal —
  that belongs to Chloe or to nobody.
- YES if she is shown doing something physical — reaching for the bread mid-sentence, rolling
  onto her front, walking straight up to a horse — instead of pausing to make an argument.

## Are they acting like themselves?

Falls asleep within a minute of finishing a thought, mid-scene, played completely straight —
immediately after nine minutes of continuous talking she is asleep before Chloe can respond
(chapters/04_pluto.md:20–21). She demonstrates instead of arguing: told Chloe doesn't believe a
horse has a blind spot, she walks directly up to the horse's nose to prove it rather than
explaining further (chapters/12_nine.md:111). She renegotiates space physically instead of
verbally — bangs the wall instead of starting a conversation about the noise
(chapters/17_fourteen.md:93); tells Kavi to eat instead of engaging with his math
(chapters/18_fifteen.md:9). She reaches for what she wants without pausing the sentence she's
mid-way through — "reaching past her for the bread" while still delivering a line
(chapters/10_april.md:49). She takes physical possession of a room instead of sitting upright
with the group — lying across the end of a bed, then rolling onto her front
(chapters/15_twelve.md:53, 65). She tracks a friend's physical growth by hand, in pencil, on a
door frame, rather than saying anything about it (chapters/17_fourteen.md:121). Under a financial
loss, she shows no visible reaction on the page at all — the only thing reported is that she can
name exactly which paragraph she misread (chapters/18_fifteen.md:123).

**Failure modes — the three easiest ways to get her wrong:**
1. Playing her as ditzy or scatterbrained because the horse monologue runs long. The text keeps
   her competent throughout — best rider in the group, able to take apart other people's plans
   under scrutiny for forty straight minutes (chapters/15_twelve.md:67), and precise enough to
   name her own reading error after losing money. Long-winded is not the same as unintelligent.
2. Giving her a stake in winning, ranking, or being praised. Every scene that touches
   institutional reward — the essay-topic swap, the class lending exercise, the post-graduation
   year abroad — has her decline it or absorb the loss without visible cost. A version of her that
   competes hard is off-model.
3. Trimming her lines to match the cast's dominant economical register. She is the built
   exception; cutting her run-ons to sound tighter erases the one trait the cast needs her for.

## Known problems

1. **The "badly underused" / "mentioned once in ch10, then gone" claim does not hold against the
   text.** She appears in all 13 chapters of the reading list, speaks in 10 separate scenes across
   6 chapters, and is named outright as one of the fixed core group: "Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Priya,
   Odile, Eli and Nadia are always there" at the daily dinner table
   (chapters/16_thirteen.md:121) — the same sentence that anchors several characters who did get
   profiles. Likely mechanical cause: `voice_separation.py`'s `TAGGED` regex, the tool that
   produced the measured dials backing the other characters' sheets, does not include "Priya" in
   its pattern at all — she is structurally invisible to that measurement regardless of how often
   she actually appears. **Fix:** add her to `TAGGED` and re-run before concluding anything further
   about her presence from the numbers; don't add scenes to solve a presence problem the text
   doesn't actually have.
2. **The run-on shape is present but inconsistent.** chapters/04_pluto.md:17 and
   chapters/15_twelve.md:65 already tumble the way the signature wants. chapters/17_fourteen.md:131
   ("At breakfast." / "In the queue." / "Holding a tray." / "In front of about forty people.") is
   built as clipped fragments instead, which currently reads like Odile's assigned register, not
   hers. **Fix:** rewrite that beat as one running clause instead of four fragments.
3. **The Axis 2 disagreement move has no example anywhere in the reading list.** Her one clear
   disagreement — "Do it how?" (chapters/15_twelve.md:65) — is a direct factual objection, the
   opposite of "agrees out loud and carries on doing what she was doing." **Fix:** needs a new beat
   or a rewrite; there is nothing to extend yet.
4. **The animal-figurative permission is unused.** No invented image, noticed or unnoticed,
   appears anywhere in her lines in the reading list. **Fix:** one unmarked animal comparison
   about a person, example given above under Signature.
5. **"Still doing the horse thing years later" overreaches what the text shows.** It's true from
   camp through age 9 (chapters/04–12) and then drops out completely — no horse, stable, or
   riding word appears in chapters 13 through 19, or in either v2 file, including the one line
   that covers her post-graduation year. **Fix:** either add one later beat that actually shows it
   continuing, or stop asserting "years later."

## Do not confuse with

- **Odile** — Odile's signature is fragments and single words, the shortest delivery in the cast.
  Priya's short lines ("At breakfast.", "Do it how?") are launches into a longer turn, never a
  complete stand-alone statement the way Odile's are. If a clipped line stands alone with nothing
  after it, it's Odile's; chapters/17_fourteen.md:131 currently reads like a collision and should
  be lengthened, not trimmed further.
- **Chloe** — Chloe builds a concrete parallel scenario and argues the parallel ("If you...").

  an argument or an analogy. If the line is being used to make a point by comparison to a
  different situation, it's Chloe's move, not hers.
