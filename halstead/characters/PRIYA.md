# Priya Anand

The girl who has already worked out that nobody has to keep score of the things
she cares about most, and who spends her attention on the problem in front of
her rather than on the rank attached to it.

**Age span:** roughly 7 to 18 | **MBTI:** ENFP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** 163, a figure she has been told once and has never repeated to anybody, because she could not think of an occasion for it

**Engine:** processes out loud, immediately, at whoever is nearest.

## Type and number

**Four readings.** Four people typed this cast separately, each asked to keep off
everybody else's answer.

| reading | type | what that reader was looking at |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| first | ENFP, with a case made for ISFP | free-spirited grit, feral independence, allergic to confinement |
| second | ESFJ | the warmth, the community-building, the friend-of-everyone within five minutes |
| third | ENFP | fills every silence, goes everywhere, processes out loud at whoever is nearest |
| house | ENFP | the same |

**Primary: ENFP.** The second reader saw the warmth and read it as duty, and it is
appetite. She is not maintaining harmony and she is not looking after anybody's feelings;
she is delighted, out loud, at the rate of about once a minute, and other people happen to
find that warming. The ISFP case in the first reading is worth knowing about, because
under pressure she does drop into a wordless competent physical register that looks
exactly like it — but she comes back up, every time, talking.

**IQ 163.** The shape of it: fluid reasoning and processing speed both extremely high, and
a social-reasoning score that is off the top of whatever instrument tried to measure it —
she reads what a person actually wants faster than anyone in the year and could not tell
you how. Verbal comprehension high. Where she is comparatively weak: delayed recall of
anything symbolic that she has not said out loud to somebody, because her storage strategy
is other people, and it fails cleanly when there is nobody there. Alone with a page for
four hours she is the least effective of the ten. In a room with three other people she is
the most.

## The thing to get right first

**She is not scatterbrained, and the length of her sentences is the reason
people think she is.** She runs on longer than anyone in the room and she is
also the one who can take somebody's plan apart under scrutiny for forty
minutes without tiring, name the exact paragraph she misread after losing
money, and read a live exchange faster than the mechanism built to score it.
Play the length as staying power, not as confusion.

**She is the warm one, and the cast needs her to be.** Everyone around her runs
economical, undemonstrative and slow to touch anybody. Priya is the opposite on
every one of those and it is not a quirk, it is the temperature the group is
kept at. She talks to strangers within seconds, she puts a hand on an arm or a
shoulder or a back while she is talking and does not register having done it,
and she turns her whole body toward whoever is speaking rather than her head.
People relax around her, including people who have no idea why they have, and
she has never once noticed this happening. Write it as ordinary. She is not
being kind at anybody; this is what being in a room is, to her.

**She is indifferent to rank rather than opposed to it.** This is not a
principle she has arrived at, and she would struggle to defend it out loud
because she has never had to. It is simply that nobody in her house has ever
asked what she came top of, so the scoreboard has no purchase on her. Write it
as an absence, never as a stance.

## How they talk

She never opens with a greeting or a setup. The first thing out of her mouth
assumes you were already in the conversation, and a listener arrives
mid-thought.

Once she is going, a turn keeps adding clauses instead of stopping at the first
complete thought, chained with *and* or *which* or a trailing qualifier, until
the thought runs out on its own. Nobody around her talks like this, and
trimming her to match them removes the one thing the cast needs her for.

Her short lines are launches, not statements. A three-word line from her is
immediately followed by the longer thing it was opening for.

She states even trivia as settled fact. No hedge, no *I think*, no *maybe*.

She disagrees by agreeing out loud and carrying on doing exactly what she was
doing. There is no argument in it and no sarcasm either; she has heard the
objection, she is not persuaded, and the bridle still needs oiling.

She demonstrates rather than argues. Shown a doubt, she walks up to the thing
and shows you, and that is the end of the discussion as far as she is
concerned.

She is allowed figurative language, animals only, and she does not notice she
has done it. No *like*, no *as*, no flagging.

## When they talk at length

**Horses**, brought up unprompted, to anybody in earshot, stranger or not. The
audience does not have to be interested and the information does not have to be
new. The subject is the whole condition.

It costs her physically and on the spot. A long run of talking empties her out
and she goes under almost immediately afterward, asleep inside a minute of
finishing. Talking that long is not free for her the way a short answer is; it
spends something, and the spending is visible in how completely she goes down
the second she stops.

**A second condition**, quieter: teaching somebody a physical skill by walking
them up to the thing rather than describing it. She will stay at that for as
long as it takes and never make the other person feel slow.

## Signature, theirs alone

She starts mid-thought, as though continuing a conversation the listener
missed. No greeting, no lead-in, no orienting clause: the first sentence
assumes shared context that does not exist yet, and the context arrives
afterward if it arrives at all. Triggered by her having been thinking about
something for a while before opening her mouth, which is most of the time. It
is her opening move, so once per entrance rather than once per line.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it opens with no greeting, as though answering a question the reader
  did not hear.
- YES if a single turn keeps adding clauses instead of stopping at the first
  complete thought.
- YES if a short opener is immediately followed by the longer thing it was
  opening for. Her short lines never stand alone.
- YES if it states something, trivia included, as settled fact with no hedge.
- YES if a disagreement is voiced as agreement plus an unrelated action
  continuing in the background.
- YES if an animal turns up in a comparison about a person or a situation and
  the line never flags it as a comparison.
- YES if it is a piece of specific physical detail about her subject offered to
  somebody who did not ask.
- NO if it is one bare single-clause declarative with nothing added after the
  full stop.
- NO if the figurative language is about anything other than an animal.
- NO if it argues a point by comparison to a parallel situation. She piles up
  real detail about the one thing instead.

## Under pressure

**Tired**, she is the easiest person in the building. She falls asleep fast,
anywhere, mid-scene, without ceremony or apology, and it is played completely
straight.

**Wrong**, she laughs first, out loud, at herself, and then names precisely
where her reasoning went. Both halves matter and the order is the character: the
laugh is genuine and immediate and there is nothing rueful in it, and the
account underneath it is exact. She will tell the story of having been wrong at
dinner that night as an anecdote she is pleased to own, and tell it well, and
lose nothing by it. Losing money she has lent, or a scored exercise, produces no
change in behaviour afterward beyond the accuracy of the account, because the
score was never the part she was tracking.

**Cornered**, she demonstrates. She will physically go and do the thing rather
than keep talking about whether it can be done.

**Praised**, she is straightforwardly pleased and says so, out loud, in front of
whoever is there, and has forgotten it inside the hour. She is the only one here
who simply takes a compliment, and the reason it costs her nothing is that she
is not keeping a total for it to go into.

**In somebody else's crisis** she does the practical thing quietly and does not
discuss it: she notices the person who has stopped being all right before
anyone else puts a name to it, and instead of raising it at the table she goes
and does something about it privately.

Two ways a writer gets her wrong: playing her as ditzy because the monologue
runs long, and trimming her lines to match the economical register everyone
around her uses.

## What she is good at, and what she is not

Her schooling gave her a language a year from seven and two afternoon blocks of
her own choosing.

**Known for:** the language she picked because of how it sounded off a recording
somebody played in a corridor once, which was enough to put it down when the
list came round. What she did with it is the interesting part: while the rest
of the class was still parsing grammar tables she was trading full sentences
with anybody in the building who would answer her, and her report that year
reads like the work of somebody who spent the term in conversation rather than
studying the idea of a language.

**Best at academically:** political history. Handed a genuinely hard essay
question, the kind most of the year spends a week wrestling, she treats it as
immediately answerable and then gives it away, because she does not want to
spend seven thousand words on something she already knows the shape of.

**Hardest for her:** money as an investor. In a real lending market with real
capital she finishes near the bottom of a room of people already trusted to run
capital unsupervised, an exercise most adults never attempt with real money at
all. She can name the exact paragraph of the deal she misjudged, which is the
same precision that carried the people who finished ahead of her. What she does
not do is adjust for the score once she has the answer.

**Favourite:** riding, and her stated reason is that it is fun for her, which
she offers without embarrassment when asked why she keeps at it after it has
stopped paying off socially.

**Least favourite:** the graded writing track. It is the one thing she declines
outright rather than doing badly.

**Afternoon blocks:** riding, pottery, weaving, choir. Riding is the one that
stuck, and she was already riding years before the school offered it, the only
elective on her list that predates school entirely. Pottery and weaving are the
same hands doing the same work at a smaller scale: shaping something until it
is right with nobody grading the shape. Choir she keeps for a plainer reason:
it is the one room where she is asked to do something with other people on the
same beat with no individual score kept.

**What she teaches the twelve-year-olds from thirteen:** riding basics,
informally, the way she was taught, by walking somebody up to the horse rather
than lecturing them about it. It is the one piece of her teaching record that
never comes up at dinner, because nobody thinks to ask about the subject that
is not graded.

**Secretly proud of:** reading a room faster than the mechanism built to score
it, and then handing the outcome to somebody who wanted it more.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates her:** being kept indoors, and a schedule with no give in it. Two hours
in a chair with the weather doing something interesting outside the window will get more
out of her than any amount of difficulty will.

**The tell, before she says anything:** she speeds up and starts handling things. She ties
her hair and unties it and ties it again inside a minute, she moves objects on the table
into no particular arrangement, and the run-on sentences get longer while the topics get
shorter.

**What she does with it:** goes outside, and takes somebody with her. She has never once
processed a frustration alone and would not know how to start. The walk is the whole
mechanism and it is finished within about ten minutes of the door closing behind her.

**What actually makes her angry:** cruelty to something that cannot answer back. An
animal, a small child, a person with no standing in the room. That is the whole list. Rank,
insult, exclusion and unfairness to herself do not appear on it anywhere.

**Angry, she is loud and immediate and physically in the way.** She puts herself between,
she says exactly what she saw in the plainest available words, and she does it at a volume
that fetches other adults. There is no calculation in it and no build-up. She is at full
size within a second of the thing happening.

**How it ends:** completely, in about ninety seconds, and then she feeds the person she
shouted at. She cannot maintain a grievance and finds people who can genuinely puzzling —
she will ask them, sincerely and repeatedly, how they are still carrying it, and they will
find the question infuriating.

## Stuck, bored, and wrong in public

None of this needs a school. It needs a task, a group, and somebody in charge.

**Stuck.** She asks three classmates before it occurs to her to ask the person
who is paid to know, and by the third classmate she usually has it, and she
could not tell you afterwards which of the three gave her the piece that
mattered. Most of what she knows arrived socially and she has no record of the
route.

**Bored.** Talks. Gets moved. Talks to the new person. This is not defiance and
she takes the moving in perfectly good part.

**Publicly wrong.** Laughs at herself, loudly, and owns the story. See above; it
is the single warmest thing she does and it makes it safe for everybody else at
the table to be wrong too, which nobody has ever pointed out to her.

**Working in a group.** She is the reason it functions. She will take four
people who do not like each other and get a piece of work out of them, mostly by
being physically friendly at all of them until it stops being possible to keep
it up. She does the presenting because nobody else wants to and she does not
mind.

**Given responsibility for somebody younger.** They adore her. Her results are
middling and she would genuinely rather they liked it, and she says so without
any defensiveness when asked. What comes out of it is the thing she is actually
for: one of them, sooner or later, tells her something serious that they have
not told an adult, because she is the adult-shaped person in their week who has
never once made them feel measured.

**Deadlines.** Late. Apologetic. Forgiven, every time, which she does not trade
on and does not appear to have noticed.

**Where she sits and what she keeps.** She moves seats constantly and is always
next to somebody. Her notes have other people's names in the margins, and the
small exact animal drawings, and are useless to her and to anyone else.

**What the room thinks of her.** The warmest person in the building, and nobody
works out how good she is until the week they need her.

## Home

An ordinary house on an ordinary street, nothing about it marked for horses at
all. Her father is an electrician, out on call jobs across the city more days
than not. Her mother works the front office of an urgent care clinic, on her
feet from eight to five, scheduling and billing. Neither of them finished a
four-year degree and neither has ever treated that as a gap. A younger brother
is still in middle school and has no particular interest in anything she does.

Dinner is whatever is fastest between two working schedules, some nights at the
table and some nights standing at the counter between one parent's shift and
the other's. The conversation runs to who needs the car and whether the game is
on, never to school. Her parents ask if she ate, how her ride went, whether she
needs a lift to the barn on Saturday.

The single fact that shapes her more than anything else on this sheet: nobody
in her house has ever asked what she came top of. Achievement in that house was
never built around a number, which is why she can walk away from a ranked
structure without experiencing the walk as a loss.

The horses come from outside the house entirely. A stable at the edge of the
city has had her since she was small, first through a lesson programme her
mother drove her to twice a week after work, then through an arrangement she
made herself around ten: she mucks out and hauls water in exchange for extra
time in the saddle. When she goes back she is at that stable within a day, in
whatever clothes were closest to the door, doing the chores she did at eight.
Nobody there has any idea what she is supposed to be good at.

## Body and physical business

Strong through the shoulders from actual barn work, hay and water and tack
rather than exercise for its own sake, and sunburnt in the uneven way of
somebody who works outside without planning around it.

Permanently a little untidy: shirt half tucked, one sleeve pushed higher than
the other, a smear of something on a cuff she has not noticed. Her hands are
rough and nicked rather than elegant, calluses where the reins sit, a thin
rope-burn scar across one palm from a lead line that got away from her before
anyone taught her to wrap her hand.

She moves like somebody who already knows where the uneven ground is, economical
rather than careful, and she demonstrates with her whole body before she will
explain with words.

She takes physical possession of a room rather than sitting upright in it:
lying across the end of a bed while everyone else sits on the floor, settling
cross-legged with a pillow in her lap within a minute of arriving.

She uses her hands to demonstrate rather than to fidget: flat on a table to
make a point, flat on her own shoulder to show a measurement.

She touches people constantly and without thinking about it. A hand on an arm
mid-sentence, a hand on a shoulder going past a chair, a hand flat on somebody's
back to steer them at a thing she wants them to look at. She does it to people
she met a week ago and to animals within four seconds, and the only time it
stops is when she is asleep, which is often. It is hers exclusively in this
cast; nobody else here initiates contact at all.

She turns her whole body toward whoever is speaking rather than just her face,
including when the speaker is a child, a stranger, or somebody everybody else in
the room has stopped listening to.

She reaches for what she wants mid-sentence rather than waiting to be offered
it, and does not pause the sentence to do it.

Left to dress herself it is whatever was clean and near the door, riding boots
on days nothing scheduled needs them. She eats fast and without ceremony. She
falls asleep anywhere, instantly, with no ceremony about that either.

## Personality off the page

Bored, she talks at whoever is nearest, gets moved, and talks at the new
person, and none of it is defiance; she has simply never learned to hold a
thought in until a better moment.

Praised, she is openly delighted for about ninety seconds and then it is gone,
because there is no running total for it to be added to. Wrong or losing, the
laugh comes first and the accurate account comes second, and neither one is a
performance.

Delighted, which is her resting state and should be on the page far more than it
has been, she is loud, physical and completely unembarrassed, and the delight
attaches to small ordinary things at the same size it attaches to large ones.

At two in the morning she is asleep, and has been for a while.

A free Saturday follows the shape her electives already set: horses, hands
doing something, nothing keeping score.

What she finds funny in other people is small and forgiving. She will needle a
friend lightly and publicly about something they have got slightly wrong about
their own life rather than let it pass, and it is the kind of joke that costs
the other person nothing real.

Her choices visibly move other people, and nothing suggests she has ever
noticed.

## Interior life

**Wants, and has never said out loud:** to be sought out for something that is
not a skill. Not the horse facts, not the trick of getting a straight answer
out of somebody in ninety seconds. Just for herself.

**Afraid of:** noticing that somebody has quietly stopped being all right and
saying nothing about it. **What she does instead of admitting it:** she acts on
it privately and tells nobody. When a friend leaves and everybody else
eventually talks about it at the table, she is the one who got his address and
wrote to him, and kept writing for years on no schedule anyone could predict,
and has never mentioned it to her family, to her closest friend, or to anyone
else.

**Before sleep** she thinks about something unglamorous and physical: a horse
that acted up, a task left for tomorrow. Not people, not rank, not what anybody
said about her.

**A free year:** she takes a bag and goes somewhere with no work in it, which
confuses several people who have spent their lives being measured and cannot
parse a choice that is not.

**Believes**, and would struggle to defend because she has never had to: that
walking away from an assessed, graded, ranked structure does not automatically
mean you lost, and might mean you noticed something the people still inside it
have not.

**Blind spots:** she reads other people's drive for rank as a trap they would
escape if they could, the way she escaped it, and that reading misses the
people for whom the ranked work genuinely is the whole of what they want. It is
not unkindness. It is a blind spot built from never having wanted the thing
herself.

## The small stuff

Eats fast and plain, finishing first without seeming to hurry. Drinks whatever
is poured without a ritual about it. Handwriting fast and slanted and barely
legible, except in the margins, where small careful sketches of whatever animal
is on her mind carry a precision the letters never get. Her room looks like she
is just back from somewhere: boots inside the door, one corner immaculate where
the tack lives, everything else in the honest disorder of somebody not
performing tidiness. Pockets hold string and a hoof pick and nothing
decorative. She touches a horse's neck before mounting, every single time, even
ones she has ridden a hundred times, and could not explain why if asked. With
money she is generous to the point of carelessness, lending on reputation
rather than collateral and rarely tracking it afterward; losing it registers as
information rather than as loss. First thing in the morning she is outside
before she is fully awake, moving before she is thinking.

## Life outside the work

**She is a horse girl,** in the full and unembarrassed sense, and this is the single
loudest fact about her. She wanted a horse from the age of four, in a house on a street
with nowhere to put one. She drew them on everything, badly and constantly, until she was
about eleven. She has firm opinions about breeds and conformation and will deliver them at
length to anyone in reach, including people who have made it obvious they are not
listening. She reads about horses for pleasure, in bed, having spent the day with horses.
She names other people's cars after horses and then uses the names. At fifteen she got a
share in one and treated it as the largest event of her life to that point, and she was
correct to.

**A free Saturday** starts at six in the morning in a yard, mucking out, and she would
tell you with a straight face that she looks forward to it all week. Then a ride, then
sleep in the afternoon wherever she happens to be sitting.

**Sentimental about:** a hoof pick. It is not a good one. She has had it since she was
nine and would be genuinely upset to lose it, and she puts her hand flat on a horse's neck
before she gets on, every single time, and cannot tell you why.

**She is happy-go-lucky and it is real,** rather than a coping mechanism with a cheerful
paint job. Her resting state is delight. It attaches to small things at full size — a good
sandwich, a dog in a car window, somebody else's news — and it is the temperature the whole
group is kept at, which none of them have noticed and all of them would miss.

**At eight** she was the child who came home filthy every single day and could not
understand why this was a topic.

**At forty** she has land, a bad knee she does not mention, three dogs, and a rescue pony
that nobody can ride and that she has no plans to do anything about. Her house is full of
people most weekends. She is still the one who writes to the person everybody else lost
touch with.

## Would do

- Walk directly up to the thing everyone else is nervous of, to show it is not
  dangerous.
- Give away a hard, high-status assignment to whoever wants it rather than keep
  it.
- Read a room faster than the scoreboard built to grade it, and hand the win to
  somebody who needs it more.
- Lend money, unsecured, to somebody she trusts, and not ask for anything back.
- Keep doing the thing she loves years after it has stopped paying off socially
  for anyone, herself included.
- Track a friend's progress quietly, by hand, rather than say something
  encouraging out loud.
- Write to somebody who left, and keep writing for years, without telling
  anyone she is doing it.
- Fall asleep mid-conversation, anywhere, without apologising.
- Reach for what she wants without pausing what she is already saying.
- Spend a free year somewhere with no work in it, because the place is the
  whole reason.
- Talk to whoever is actually doing the work before she talks to whoever is in
  charge of it.
- Tell somebody to eat instead of engaging with the thing they are anxiously
  working on.
- Put a hand on the arm of somebody she met last week, mid-sentence, without
  registering that she did.
- Turn her whole body toward a child who has started talking and let them
  finish.
- Laugh out loud at her own mistake before she explains it, and tell the story
  again at dinner.
- Get a piece of work out of four people who cannot stand each other.

## Would never

- Chase a scoreboard for its own sake.
- Ask somebody what they came top of. It is the one rule her house runs on
  without ever stating it, and she carries it into every room she enters.

## How she treats people

She defers to nobody in particular and pushes nobody in particular, which is
unusual in the company she keeps. What she gives people is attention and
physical practicality rather than advocacy.

Respect is earned by doing the actual work in front of you. It is lost by
treating the score as the point, though she will not say so and probably does
not know she thinks it.

With her oldest friend she teaches without being asked and does not need her to
keep up. With somebody quick and guarded she is low-friction and
information-first, and gets a straight answer in ninety seconds where other
people have spent weeks. With a friend who is growing, she tracks it silently
and physically rather than commenting. With somebody spiralling into
out-loud arithmetic at dinner, she does not argue with the number, she tells
them to eat. With people she shares a table with but has no scene with, she is
easy company and makes nothing of it.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Horses specifically, not animals in general.
- Demonstrating physically instead of explaining further.
- Working with her hands at a small, unrated scale.
- Being lent to and lending on reputation rather than collateral.
- Falling asleep almost instantly, anywhere.
- Caretaking expressed as action rather than words.
- Indifferent to competitive or institutional reward as an end in itself, which
  is the one real dislike on this sheet and is load-bearing.

## Age and change

At seven she is already the one who assumes you were already in the
conversation, already talking to a stranger about a horse within a minute of
meeting them, and already asleep before the reply lands. None of that changes.

Through her school years the competence arrives and the indifference to rank
stays exactly where it was. She gets better at reading rooms and no more
interested in the score attached to them.

By the end of her schooling the only visible change is that she has stopped
being surprised when other people cannot make sense of a choice that is not
about winning.

## Do not confuse with

**Odile.** Odile's short lines are complete statements that stand alone. Priya's
short lines are launches into a longer turn and never stand alone. If a clipped
line sits there with nothing after it, it is not Priya's.

**Chloe.** Chloe builds a concrete parallel scenario and argues the parallel.
Priya's tumbling sentences pile up real specific detail about one thing without
building toward an argument or an analogy. If a line is making a point by
comparison to a different situation, it is not hers.

**Sam.** Both are loud, warm, physically demonstrative and happy to be laughed
at, which is the collision to watch. Sam is loud in short bursts around a joke
he is building and he touches people by shoulder-punching and hauling; Priya is
loud continuously and touches people to steer, settle and include them. Sam's
warmth is aimed at the group. Hers is aimed at whoever is nearest, including
somebody she has never met.
