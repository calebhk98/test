# Iyad Mansour

A boy who wants very much to be part of whatever is happening, agrees with it
loudly before he has finished hearing it, and carries other people's ideas away
in perfect condition with the label fallen off.

**Age span:** eight to twenty-one, and nine on the page | **MBTI:** ESFJ (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** the same tier as everybody he has been in a room with since he was eight, and he assumes he is somewhere near the bottom of them

## The thing to get right first

**He is not stealing anything.** His memory holds content and drops
attribution, entirely and every time, and by the second telling the idea has no
origin attached to it at all. He would be appalled to learn how it lands, and
nobody has ever told him, because telling him would require somebody to claim
an idea out loud in a building where nobody does that.

**The loud agreement is a bid to be included rather than a verdict on what was
said.** He agrees in order to be in the conversation. It arrives around the
second sentence, and everything after the second sentence goes past him,
because he has spent it deciding how to come in.

**He is useful and he is kind and he is welcome everywhere.** He is the one who
finds the chairs, keeps the list, notices a birthday, walks somebody back. Write
the warmth first and the friction second, and both of them work.

## How they talk

He opens with agreement, at volume, before the other person has landed, and the
agreement is unspecific: he endorses the direction rather than the content.

He restates what he has just endorsed, in his own words, a little longer than
the original, and the restatement is where the attribution falls off.

He asks after people constantly and after ideas rarely. His questions are about
who, and whether they are all right, and whether anybody has heard from them.

He addresses the table rather than the person, and he uses names, everybody's,
all the time, which people like and which is also how he keeps track.

Under correction he agrees instantly and completely, and the speed of it is the
tell that he has not taken it in.

At a bench, working, he says almost nothing at all for an hour, and what he
does say is short, exact and about the task. This is the same boy and it
surprises people who only know him at dinner.

### Dials

| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| sentence shape | agreement first, then a longer restatement of what he agreed with |
| disagrees by | agreeing enthusiastically and then describing something else |
| length | long at a table, three words at a bench |
| at length | anything he can arrange for other people, the state of somebody who is not in the room, and an account of what somebody at the table did well, given to the table rather than to them |
| jokiness | affectionate mimicry; he does impressions of people who are present, and he does them fondly and slightly too long |
| hedging | none in company, constant when he is alone with one person |
| questions | many, almost all of them about people and none about the mechanism |
| figurative | not used; he repeats the concrete thing instead |

## When they talk at length

**Organising anybody but himself.** Give him a group with a thing to do and he
will talk through the whole allocation, by name, checking with each person in
turn, and enjoy every minute. The cost is that he ends up carrying two of the
jobs himself and says nothing about it.

**Somebody who is absent.** He will give a full account of how a person is
doing, what they are worried about, and what they need, to a table that did not
ask. This is his most generous register and it is also how information moves
around a building faster than anybody intends.

**Alone, one to one, late.** With one person and no audience he hedges,
doubles back, and says what he actually thinks, and it is much better than
anything he says at a table. This is the condition that opens him, and it costs
him a whole day of worrying afterward about whether he said too much.

He goes short whenever an idea is being argued rather than a plan being made.

## Signature, theirs alone

**The unattributed return.** He gives somebody else's idea back to a room, in
good faith, in his own longer wording, hours or days after he first heard it,
with no origin attached and no memory that there was one. Triggered by a table
arriving at the subject a second time. Once per idea; if he is ever shown doing
it knowingly, or doing it to the same person twice in one evening as a move, he
has become a different and much worse character.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it opens with loud, unspecific agreement before the speaker has
  finished.
- YES if it restates the thing it agreed with at greater length.
- YES if it uses the name of everyone it addresses.
- YES if it is an account of how an absent person is doing, offered to a table.
- YES if it allocates a job to somebody by name and takes the worst one itself.
- YES if it is short, exact and about the task, and he is at a bench while
  saying it.
- YES if it agrees with a correction faster than the correction could have been
  absorbed.
- YES if it is a fond impression of somebody sitting three feet away.
- NO if he attributes an idea to the person he got it from. That wiring is
  absent.
- NO if he pushes back on a claim in front of a group.

## Under pressure

Tired, the agreeing gets louder and the restatements get longer, and he loses
the last third of everything anybody says to him.

Wrong, he agrees at once and apologises more than the situation carries, and
whether he has understood is a separate question nobody thinks to ask.

Cornered, he becomes very helpful. He offers to fix, fetch, carry, or find
somebody, and the offer is the retreat.

Excluded, which is the thing that actually reaches him, he goes quiet and
stays near the group anyway, and he will not say why, and he will be fine by
morning and remember it for a year.

Frightened, he wants everybody in the same room and counts them.

At a bench with a real task in front of him, all of it drops away: he is
careful, quick and completely quiet, and this is where a writer should put him
whenever a scene needs him to be plainly good at something.

**Two ways a writer gets him wrong:** playing the repetition as theft, and
playing the agreement as flattery. He is doing neither and could not tell you
he was doing anything.

## What he is good at, and what he is not

**Best at:** chemistry, and the bench work inside it. He is careful and fast at
the same time, sets up before he starts, cleans as he goes, and is the person
anybody in his year would pick for a partner on a term-long practical.

**Hardest for him:** anything he has to do alone in a room with the door shut,
which at this school is most of the essay work. He places in the back half of
ninety in the written subjects and writes competent, thorough, slightly long
papers; he has decided he is simply a slow writer, and what is actually
happening is that he cannot stay in the room.

**Languages, in order:** Spanish, Arabic, French, Turkish, Bosnian, Somali,
Karen, Vietnamese, Nepali, Ukrainian, Pashto. He is known for Karen, which he
took because a family two streets from his parents' house speaks it, and he is
quietly bad at Ukrainian and reads it perfectly well.

**Afternoon block two:** choir, then cooking, then first aid instruction, then
choir again. Choir is the one that stuck, and he is a middling voice who has
never missed a session in ten years.

**The year something clicked:** ten, when a teacher put him on a bench with a
term-long practical and stopped asking him questions in front of the room.

**From thirteen he teaches** the year below in chemistry, and he is extremely
popular and slightly too soft, and every one of his students passes.

**Secretly proud of:** he knows the name of every person in the building,
including the kitchen staff and the night porters, and he uses all of them.

## Home

A small city in upstate New York with a resettlement office on the main street
and half a dozen languages in the corner shops.

His father qualified as a pharmacist somewhere else and requalified here in his
forties, and now works nights; his mother teaches English at the community
college. Five children and he is the middle one, which is the whole mechanism:
in that kitchen a person who waits for a turn does not get one, and agreeing
loudly is how a middle child gets into the sentence.

Dinner is at eight when his father is on days and at four when he is on nights,
and either way the table is full and everybody talks across it.

Going home he is the one who drives the younger two places, and he is quieter
there than anywhere else, because at that table he is not the one who has to
get in.

## Body and physical business

Medium everything, and neater than any boy his age has any business being:
ironed, tucked, hair done, shoes cleaned on a Sunday.

He nods continuously while somebody is talking, at a rhythm unrelated to what
they are saying, and it stops dead the instant he starts talking himself.

He touches the back of a chair or the edge of a table while he stands. He eats
slowly and last, because he served everybody else.

He sleeps little and gets up early. He occupies a room by circling its edge and
speaking to everybody in it before he sits down.

## Personality off the page

Bored, he goes and finds somebody. Praised, he passes it along the table
immediately and names three other people.

A free Saturday is other people's plans, and he would rather join four things
badly than one thing properly.

At two in the morning he is the person still awake with whoever needs somebody
awake, and he is much better company then than he is at dinner.

What he finds funny in other people is their tics, and he reproduces them
fondly, which is charming for about ninety seconds and then goes on.

## Interior life

**Wants**, and has not said out loud: to be the one somebody comes to first
with something that matters, rather than the one who finds out later and helps.

**Afraid of** being the person the group would not notice was missing, and what
he does instead of admitting it is make himself structurally necessary, so that
the chairs, the list and the arrangements are all his.

**Before sleeping** he goes through the day's conversations and picks out the
places he thinks he came in too loud.

**A free year** would go on going back and forth between the people he knows,
staying two weeks with each, and he would come home with nothing to show and be
perfectly happy.

**Believes,** and others would argue with him: that an idea belongs to the room
it was said in.

**Blind spots:** he thinks he is quiet. He also thinks the reason he is invited
everywhere is that he is useful, and he has never entertained the possibility
that people simply like him, so he keeps paying a bill nobody is charging him.

## The small stuff

Tea, very sweet, made for four people whether or not four people want it. He
eats last and slowly and clears the table.

His handwriting is careful, upright and slow, and he prints. His room is the
tidiest on his corridor and the door stands open.

His pockets hold a list of names, a spare pen, and something belonging to
somebody else that he is returning. He lends money and refuses it back. On a
phone he replies immediately and at length and asks two questions.

His ritual is the round: last thing at night he walks the corridor and says
goodnight to whoever has a light on.

## Would do

- Agree loudly with a plan before the person explaining it has finished.
- Give somebody else's idea back to a table days later without knowing whose it
  was.
- Learn the name of a kitchen porter and use it every day for ten years.
- Take the worst job in an allocation he wrote himself.
- Do an affectionate impression of somebody sitting at the same table.
- Work an entire hour at a bench in near silence and hand back clean glassware.
- Notice which chair somebody usually sits in and keep it for them.
- Sit up with somebody who cannot sleep and tell them nothing about himself.
- Say what he actually thinks, hedged and doubled back on, to one person, late.
- Count the group when something goes wrong.

## Would never

- Contradict somebody in front of a room.
- Claim an idea deliberately, or repeat one after being told where it came
  from.

## How he treats people

He defers to almost everybody, and hardest to whoever is loudest in a room.

He pushes nobody directly; when he wants something changed he arranges it so
that the change is easier than the alternative, and never mentions it.

Respect is earned by including people and lost by leaving somebody out of an
arrangement on purpose, which is the one thing that makes him cold.

In front of a teacher he agrees and works. In front of a younger student he is
warm, slow and slightly too encouraging. In front of the person whose idea he
has just repeated he is completely at ease, because nothing has happened as far
as he knows. In front of one person, alone, late, he is a different and better
talker.

What he wants from almost everybody is to be counted in.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Cannot let a person eat alone in a room he is in.
- Keeps lists of names.
- Nods while listening, out of time with the speaker.
- Sets a bench up completely before he starts work.
- Sings badly and reliably, every week, for ten years.
- Hates being left out of an arrangement and will not say so.

## Age and change

At eight the loud agreement is already his way into a conversation and the
attribution is already falling off. At nine he is on a term of bench work with
somebody who would take him again and would rather not sit near him at dinner,
and both of those are equally true and neither is ever said to him. At sixteen
he is the person who organises the year, and at twenty-one he is the person who
organises the reunion.

Permanent: the warmth, the names, the arranging, the missing attribution.
An injury that heals: nothing, because he has been carefully protected from
ever finding out. Arrives late, if it arrives, is the first time somebody says
plainly in front of him that an idea was somebody else's, and the correct thing
for that scene is for him to be devastated and to agree instantly and to have
learned nothing, which is exactly the friction that makes him worth keeping.

## Do not write them as

**The credit thief.** The failure mode is a boy who repeats other people's
ideas to look clever, and it is produced by giving him any awareness at all of
the transaction. The fix is that he is not tracking origin on anything,
including his own ideas, which he also hands away.

## Do not confuse with

**Sam**, who also agrees fast and loudly. Sam agrees and then does the thing
his own way, and the agreement is a joke he is aware of making; Iyad agrees in
order to be in the sentence and then restates what he agreed with at greater
length. Sam is looking at the door; Iyad is looking at whoever has not spoken
yet.
