# KOWALCZYK

Teaches fighting (self-defence, the ten-on-one drill). Turns a physical threat into an arithmetic problem out loud, in real time.

**Age span:** Chloe ages 10-15 (self-defence begins age 10, ten-on-one from age 10 on, still teaching at 15) | **MBTI:** ESTJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** no textual basis; not assessable

## Voice in one paragraph
Kowalczyk answers a challenge to her method by re-deriving it as a headcount, live: "Five people can reach you at once. Six if they're good, seven if you're standing badly, and after that there's no room for anybody, they'd be pushing each other over." (chapters/13_ten_pages.md:43) Every explanation she gives is quantitative and causal — a number, then the reason for the number, then what follows from it — and she never pads a sentence with a connective; she moves straight from one clause to the next. Her opener to a direct question can be a single word ("Yeah," chapters/18_fifteen.md:83) delivered with no change in tone before she expands. She is the only staff member to use a conventional, inherited simile rather than a strictly literal comparison — "Lasting in that is like taking candy from a baby" (chapters/18_fifteen.md:87) — which the style guide explicitly permits (inherited figures pass; invented ones don't), so it does not break the no-figurative-narration rule, it just marks her as the one teacher willing to reach for an idiom. Most characteristic line: "You're not getting worse, they're just coming up off nothing, and you are having an actual challenge now." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:87) — because it reframes a student's falling numbers as a fact about the world changing, not about her getting worse, without a single word of reassurance in it.

## Signature — theirs alone
Converts a physical threat or a complaint into a live arithmetic breakdown, counting attackers or seconds out loud as the entire content of the answer: "Five people can reach you at once..." (chapters/13_ten_pages.md:43); "ten is the shape of every long fight there is" (chapters/13_ten_pages.md:47). Two full instances in the same scene, reused later in a shorter form when Chloe's numbers drop at fifteen (chapters/18_fifteen.md:87) — a real recurring construction, not a one-off.

## Would they say this?
- YES if it turns a felt unfairness into a plain number and a reason for the number. ("Five can reach you... six if they're good... seven if you're standing badly.")
- YES if it tells a student their falling performance is caused by the world getting harder, not by them getting worse, and states the mechanism.
- YES if it answers "why" with cause and effect, immediately, no delay.
- NO if it praises a student's toughness or grit as a personal quality.
- NO if it apologises for a hard drill or checks in on how the student feels about losing.
- NO if a demand for an explanation ("That's it? That's all you've got for me?") gets met with reassurance instead of a mechanism.

## Are they acting like themselves?
Confronted directly by a frustrated student ("That's it? That's all you've got for me?" chapters/18_fifteen.md:85) she does not soften or apologise — she answers the actual question with a longer version of the same mechanism-first logic, while physically continuing an unrelated task (putting wraps on a shelf) throughout, which the text calls out specifically: "Kowalczyk is up on her toes putting a box of wraps on a high shelf and stays there while she talks." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:87) That refusal to stop and give the moment weight is the character. Failure modes: (1) having her comfort a student whose numbers have dropped instead of reframing the numbers; (2) giving her a hedge or a qualifier anywhere — none exists in eight turns; (3) writing her as visibly invested in a student's win or loss — every scene keeps her doing something else (holding a clock, shelving wraps) while she talks, which the text uses to keep her affect flat.
