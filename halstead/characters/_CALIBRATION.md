# Calibration — how good these people actually are

Read this before touching a sheet. Several sheets currently write these people as ordinary
students.

## The students

**Every one of them is a genius.** Not "bright". They were found young, from across the
country, and brought to a school built to push them to the limit of what they can do.

**Their weakest subject would be exceptional anywhere else.** Most sheets currently miss this. A "bad" subject at this school means:

- Chloe is ranked ninetieth of ninety in drawing. She can draw a realistic hand.
- Chloe is bad at Mandarin. She reads it, and writes papers in it, on a few hundred hours.
- Ruth is doing Portuguese "badly". She is reading novels in it a month after starting.

Write a weakness as a genius outperformed by ninety other geniuses in a subject they are
excellent at. A reader of the sheet should be able to see what the person can actually do
in that subject, stated plainly, alongside where they place among the ninety.

**They do not know any of this.** They have no outside comparison. Everyone they talk to
all day is a genius, so what they do looks ordinary to them. Ruth and Odile are the
furthest out of anyone in the book and both would describe themselves as somewhere around
a B+. Nobody in this cast thinks they are remarkable. Keep it that way.

**The school runs so many subjects that nobody can be best at all of them.** Whatever a
student is strongest at, they can see several people better than them at something else.
The ranking reads to them as a number next to other numbers.

**The work ethic is real and it compounds.** They have been pushed daily for years, so
their endurance, study habit and tolerance for difficulty are far past what raw ability
alone would give. Write that in. It is a large part of who they are.

## The teachers

**Every one is a world leader in their field, top one percent, and still actively doing
research.** The research is the job. Teaching is a part-time obligation written into the
contract — a handful of hours a week out of forty, the rest spent on their own work.

They were sought out and offered far more than they could earn anywhere else, with better
benefits, and they signed strict non-disclosure agreements. Do not invent a personal
crisis, a failed career or a wound that drove them here. They came because the offer beat
anything else available to them and because the work is real.

**They never let a student know they are exceptional.** This is the mechanism behind
everything a teacher says on the page. A student solves something a teacher put up as
rhetorical, and the teacher can be pleased, but treats it as the expected thing: yes, that
is how you get to the formula, that is the same route somebody took five hundred years
ago. Nothing is ever remarked on as impressive.

Asked why an Olympic swimmer is teaching swimming, the honest in-world answer is that any
school hires people who have done the job, and children are the future of the country.
It is presented as ordinary, because to the school it is.

## Where they come from

An IQ near 160 runs about one in thirty thousand. That puts roughly a hundred and twenty
children in a whole national birth year, which is about two per state. Two of them do not
grow up in the same county, and they do not grow up in the same town.

The manuscript says this directly. Meg, on the phone to the school: "And they're not from
around here. Not one town, not one state. She said they come from all over, and then she
said we're one of the closer ones." Her husband answers, "Four hours is one of the closer
ones." (chapters/03_the_letter.md:71)

So every student comes from somewhere else. Some are thirty minutes away and some are ten
hours. They met at the school and nowhere before it. Meg knows Ruth's mother by name
because she has been driving to that campus for eleven years, not because they live near
each other.

Give each student a different part of the country. Two of them in one town, school
district or county contradicts the arithmetic and the manuscript both.

## Owen

Owen is the exception to everything above, and the sheets currently run the other way.

The search that found the others found him in error. He is somewhere near 140, which is
exceptional in an ordinary school and well under the floor of this one. He could not do
the work. It was too hard for him, in the plain sense, and that is why he was crying in
the stairwell on the Thursday, and it is why he left.

Write him as a bright child who was brought somewhere he could not keep up, and who knew
it. Do not write him as a genius who chose to leave. Do not write him as the one who saw
through the place. He is the evidence that the selection is not perfect and that the
floor here is real.

## How big the school is

The school takes in **roughly a hundred children a year**, and **Chloe's cohort is the oldest
in the building**, so there is no year above her and the place grows with her. Every year
of her time there is one more year-group on the roll than the year before.

| Chloe's stage | Roughly on the roll |
| :---- | :---- |
| around third grade | ~200 |
| the middle years | growing by about a hundred a year |
| her final year | close to 1,000 |

Both figures the manuscript states sit on that curve rather than against it. The Watch in
`chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:199` puts "two hundred students out of eleven rooms" into the
stairwells, and Ruth's bread argument in `chapters/15_twelve.md:31` says "two hundred of us,"
both from the lower half of the ramp. Graduation in `chapters/23_the_first_one.md:14-17` puts
"eight hundred children who are not graduating today" on the grass behind ninety-one who are,
which is the top of it. **This has been logged as an inconsistency; it is not one.** The
school is a different size in chapter 14 than it is in chapter 23 because it is a different
size, and a sheet or a scene that needs a number should take it off this curve rather than
off whichever chapter it happens to be near.

One consequence worth holding onto: her year is the smallest cohort's worth of company the
building ever has, and the younger years she teaches into keep getting bigger under her.

## Banned constructions

These assert a verdict instead of showing the thing, and they smuggle in an assumption
that has not been earned. Keep them out of every sheet:

"that is correct", "that is right", "I was wrong", "that is the best", "that is the
worst", "that is a better form", "which is the good part", "correctly", "rightly",
"the strongest", "the weakest", and every close variant.

Write the substance instead. Rather than "her reading of the scene is correct", write
what she read and what was there.

## No negative framing, no minimising

Writing that spends its force denying a reading is weaker than writing that states the
thing. "An absence of comparison data, never as modesty" tells a reader what to rule out.
Write what is true: she has never met anyone outside the school and has no idea what an
ordinary sixteen-year-old can do.

Do not undercut a statement in the same breath that makes it. Say the thing.

## Rules for writing a sheet

**Write the character, not the plot.** Do not build anything as setup for a later event.
Do not write that a choice at twelve pays off at nineteen. A twelve-year-old picking a
language is picking a language.

**No tags.** Do not mark lines [text], [inferred] or [invented]. The sheets are read by
people writing scenes, and the tags get in their way. Write the character as a person.
Where the manuscript shows something, quote it.

**Names may repeat.** One or two shared names across a cast is normal and needs no fixing.

**Secondhand becomes firsthand.** The book is Chloe's point of view, so most of what
exists about anyone else arrives through her: reported, overheard, secondhand. The job of
a sheet is to take that material and turn it into the character's own action — what they
did, why they did it, what they were doing when Chloe was not in the room.
