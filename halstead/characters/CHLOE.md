## 2. Voice profile — by age band

**Age 6 (ch1–2).** Two registers running side by side, not blended: in public/institutional spaces (Aldana, Kayleigh, the librarian) she gives short, safe, often monosyllabic answers ("I don't know," "It's good," "Water"), while in the private dinner-table or testing-room space she produces long, structurally complete, adult-grade explanations (the locker-key thesis about the runaway book; "an hour isn't really a thing, it's just where we cut it"). The gap between the two is the chapter's whole argument, and it is well executed as *behavior*, not narrated. The librarian argument ("The rule is about *at a time*, though") is the most characteristic line of this band: legalistic, uses "though" as a hinge word, restates the opponent's own rule back at her — a rhetorical move that recurs at every subsequent age.

**Age 7 (ch3, 7–9).** Under stress the voice regresses to pure repetition and incomplete syntax — the February breakdown ("please," "I can't do it anymore," "This, this, all of this") is a genuine register the six/seven-year-old voice hasn't shown before, and it's earned by four chapters of escalating asking. Outside of stress, the "asking" chapters show a second recurring pattern: she can sustain an extended, structured request/argument (four-books-in-four-weeks logic to the librarian) but cannot make a plain, single unadorned ask — the biggest ask of her life ("I can't do it anymore... will they please just let her go") only surfaces after full collapse, never as a simple sentence. This is a genuinely well-built throughline.

**Age 8–9 (ch11–12).** First fully comic set-piece (the hot-dog "hinge" argument) — willing to argue for the pleasure of it, not just to get something. The curfew argument reuses the age-7 device of turning an abstract disagreement into a parallel concrete scenario (library-at-eleven vs. curfew-at-nine, later echoed by the age-7 moon/table-walk analogy) — this is the closest thing the book has to a genuine, load-bearing Chloe verbal tic, and it's consistent for years.

**Age 10–12 (ch13–15).** Full technical/political register arrives (Thanksgiving tariffs, the area-bombing essay, the Latin-is-cheating argument, the intrusion). The sentences get longer and load-bearing, and she starts using terms of art fluently and dismissively ("It's the whole first term"). The intrusion chapter (age 12) shows the voice under real physical pressure for the first time and it stays clipped, functional, almost affectless ("That's the point." / "It's still going to be slow"). This is the point where her explanatory dialogue starts to sound structurally identical to Ruth's and Kavi's (see swap test).

**Age 13–16 (ch16–19).** A didactic, adult teaching-voice emerges with Marek that is flatter and more clipped than anything before it ("Because that's what the hour is for"). The bar-exam material (age 16) is the most aphoristic she ever gets: "There's an answer, and there's why. After that you're padding." This is the most syntactically compressed she becomes — short main clauses, almost no subordination, no hedging. It reads as the voice arriving at its final adult shape roughly four to five years before the book's timeline says she's an adult.

**Age 17–19 (v2 ch16–20).** Spoken dialogue drops sharply in volume and length — she becomes mostly reported ("Chloe applies to fourteen places") rather than heard. What survives is compressed to single lines ("It's my school. You don't stay at your school."). The chat register begins here: lowercase, dropped apostrophes, deadpan, functionally identical in cadence to Sam's, Kavi's, and Eli's chat voices (this is a book-wide flattening the beta notes already praises for naturalness but that also erases character distinction — see Section 3).

**Age 20–21 (v2 ch25, 29–30).** The Whitaker interview (Ch25 Cleared) is the clearest adult articulation of her defining trait *in behavior*: short, factual, compartmentalized answers, no self-justification, one moment of genuine hesitation rendered plainly ("I'd want to talk to her first"). This is the single best-written Chloe scene in the whole book for voice — restrained, precise, gives away exactly as much as the character would and no more.

**Is the progression credible?** Mostly yes, and better than most of the ensemble: the "collapse into short safe answers under scrutiny, full sentences in trusted company" pattern and the "concrete analogy for an abstraction" tic both persist from age 6 through 21 and genuinely earn the compression of the adult voice. The weak point is the youngest end: several age-6 lines (the librarian argument, "Chloe doesn't believe the last part. Everybody says that") already carry an adult's rhetorical polish and cynicism, which is the same "literary reach" problem BETA_NOTES flags for Icarus and the clouds line — it undercuts the credibility of six specifically, not the arc as a whole.

**Most characteristic line:** "The rule is about *at a time*, though. So six is fine, because it's still four at a time, it's just four and then four again, and two of them are ones you already gave me." (chapters/01_before.md) — because it contains, at six years old, the exact rhetorical structure (restate the opponent's own rule, extend it past where they meant it) that recurs unchanged through the Latin-is-cheating argument at eleven and the padding line at sixteen. It is the one line that could not be swapped to anyone else and could not have been written for any other age without adjustment — everything else competes with it for that title but loses on the "unswappable" test.

## 3. The swap test

This is the most important section, and the honest count is not small. A large share of Chloe's *explanatory* and *tactical* dialogue is functionally interchangeable with Ruth's or Kavi's — the book has an ensemble tic ("precocious kid explains the mechanism clearly") that is distributed across all three of them without a distinguishing marker for who's talking. Rough count from the material read: of the roughly 230 individually-attributed Chloe lines above, at least 30–40 would survive a swap to Ruth or Kavi unchanged (no reader would notice), concentrated in the explanatory/tactical mode from age 10 onward. That is a meaningful minority, not a majority — her *stress-register* lines (age 6–7) and her *loyalty/asking* lines are much harder to swap and do read as specifically hers.

**Two representative swap-passing examples:**

1. "We're all reacting and you can't react to it, there isn't time to react to it, you have to already be there. ... It's a barrel, Ruth. It's a barrel on the end of a person, and it's pointing where the ball's going before the ball goes anywhere, and they all shoot at the same speed." (chapters/13_ten_pages.md, paintball) — this is pure "kid explains the trick" delivery. Nothing marks it as Chloe rather than Kavi (who does the identical move with volcanoes at camp and the AES-256 explanation at twelve) or Ruth (mass vs. weight, fraction division past its domain). Put in Kavi's mouth with "Ruth" swapped for "Chloe," nothing reads wrong.

2. "It's a question about who carries the risk while a thing is sitting in a shed. There's an answer, and there's why. ... After that you're padding." (chapters/19_sixteen.md) — terse, correct, allergic to excess. This is close to indistinguishable from how Kavi talks throughout (the pencil-timetable scene two pages earlier, "That's not what any of the maths says, Kavi" notwithstanding). A reader given this line cold, without the "Chloe" tag, would have no textual reason to prefer her over Kavi.

Other strong swap candidates: "It's still going to be slow... Alarm to contact. That's got to be seven, eight minutes." (ch15, tactical assessment — could be Ruth or Kavi verbatim); the chat-register tech jokes in CHAPTERS_16_22_v2 ("sam the storage is 1tb. the MEMORY is meant to be 1tb" — this deadpan is the ensemble's shared chat voice, not Chloe's specifically); "Because they're criminals... That's what's left after everything else didn't work." (ch20 — cold-analytical judgment equally at home in Nadia's or Ruth's mouth).

**What does NOT swap:** the compressed-collapse register under emotional pressure (age 7 asking/breakdown chapters — nobody else in the book gets this register at all, so it's safe by default); the librarian argument and its later echoes (unique because of the "restate the rule back at them" structure); the Sandoval/Whitaker loyalty scenes (unique because they combine the interrogation-of-both-sides habit with an outcome of compliance, which is specifically her defining trait in action). The finding for the author: Chloe's *personality* (what she wants, how she treats outcomes, her loyalty) is well differentiated. Her *sentence-level explanatory voice*, especially from age ten onward, is not yet distinct from Ruth's or Kavi's — it is currently a shared "smart kid" register rather than a Chloe register.

## 4. Out of character

**(a) Genuine inconsistency / mismatch between brief and text.** The synopsis and STYLE_GUIDES both list "the room-shaped object" as one of Chloe's own protected invented images, alongside Icarus and the moon-as-liar. In the actual text (chapters/04_pluto.md) the line is spoken by **Sam**, immediately after Chloe's own line ("They put us in the one that isn't a planet anymore"): *"'It's not even a room,' he says. 'It's a room-shaped object.'"* This is a real discrepancy between the character material and the manuscript — either the line needs to actually become Chloe's at some point (she could be shown reusing it later, the way Icarus and the moon get reused), or the reference material's attribution needs correcting. As written, one of her four signature images is not textually hers.

**(b) The author's own framing put in her mouth/head ("narrator in costume," Chloe's channel specifically).** The clearest instance: *"She does not read the result as information about herself. She reads it as having sat an exam."* (CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md, Ch25 Cleared) — this restates the synopsis's own "Defining trait" line almost verbatim, as narration, rather than dramatizing it. It is a textbook case of the author's thesis about the character appearing in the character's own narrated perception instead of being shown through her behavior — the same problem the shared style guide flags elsewhere in the book ("Chloe explaining her own behavior to the reader" is explicitly listed as a checklist FAIL). The scene doesn't need it — the flat, compliant behavior in the same scene (giving Whitaker exactly, only, and no more than the truth) already carries the trait without the sentence.

A milder instance in the same channel: *"Chloe doesn't believe the last part. Everybody says that."* (chapters/02_march_4th.md) — a level of institutional cynicism about being tested that reads more like the sixteen-year-old bar-exam voice than a six-year-old's, even allowing for close-third access to her thought.

**(c) Credible growth / pressure — explicitly NOT a problem, flagged for contrast.** The jacket tantrum at seven ("You hate me, that's what it is, you actually hate me") is a genuine and well-earned regression under sustained deprivation — it does not contradict the controlled, compressed voice she has everywhere else, it explains why that control gets built. Likewise, the final Whitaker/Sandoval material (age 15 and 20–21) is a clean, uninterrupted throughline from the age-6 mechanism (say the safe, minimal, compliant thing to adults; keep the real answer for people you trust) all the way to withholding from the federal government — this is the arc the synopsis promises, and the text delivers it. Worth telling the author this is working; it's the strongest structural thing in her characterization.

**Testing the defining trait against the text — does it hold everywhere?** Largely yes; no clear scene was found where she takes visible credit for an outcome or performs author-underlined self-deprecation about her own ability (contrast the forge chapter's "your heat's wrong" — she reacts to correction, not to praise, by immediately re-examining the work, e.g. rejecting Sam's compliment on the hook in ch15 and pointing out its own flaw). The one place the trait is *stated* rather than shown is the Ch25 line addressed above — that's a register problem (telling), not a consistency problem (the trait itself does hold).

## 5. Personality, likes, dislikes, habits

- **Turns disagreements into concrete parallel scenarios rather than arguing in the abstract.** [textual] — table-walking vs. the moon (ch5), library-at-eleven vs. curfew-at-nine (ch11), the hot-dog "hinge" (ch11), Latin as "the year paying for itself twice" (ch14).
- **Cannot make a plain, unadorned ask; escalates instead.** [textual] — the four-weeks-of-four-books negotiation (ch3), the entire "asking" arc (ch7–9) culminating in bargaining away her birthday rather than simply asking (ch9).
- **Gives the full, true answer only to people she trusts (Ben Prentice's testing room, her father over fractions, Sam, Ruth); gives the minimal safe answer to everyone else (Aldana, Ammons, Whitaker on withheld topics).** [textual] — consistent from age 6 to 21.
- **High personal craft standard independent of external grading; rejects her own work before anyone grades it.** [textual] — drops three finished hinges in the scrap bin unprompted (ch17); criticizes her own hook to Sam's face after he's praised it (ch15).
- **Dislikes being told to slow down / "let's not rush."** [textual] — Mrs. Prahl's pull-out group (ch8); explicit in the synopsis profile and confirmed repeatedly in text.
- **Likes difficulty for its own sake and dislikes plateaus that don't move — but tolerates a *known* plateau if she chose it (riding, sung choir) by simply quitting rather than complaining.** [textual] — quits riding and choir cleanly, no fight, once they stop being hard in a way she can use (ch11–12).
- **Loyal past the point of self-interest, and this loyalty overrides her habitual interrogation of arguments.** [textual] — Sandoval scene: she builds the case against complying, then complies anyway (ch18); repeats the identical move for Whitaker three years later.
- **Weak axis: processing speed / clerical speed.** [textual, shown as behavior, not told] — bad at the timed symbol-copying test while it's happening (ch2); "the mathematics thing" needing a night on the floor while Ruth and Kavi get it in the room (ch5, camp).
- **Weak axis: drawing, dance.** [textual] — ranked 90th of 90 in both, in the same week, explicitly on a posted sheet (ch11).
- **Weak axis: the forge.** [textual] — four years, effectively one kept piece; the hook survives as a callback object on her door in ch19.
- **Weak axis: lying with her face.** [asserted by narration, not demonstrated failing] — ch15 states it directly ("she is bad at it, just like drawing and dance") in the same beat where the lie in fact works perfectly; unlike drawing and dance, this trait has no on-page failure to point to. Flagged in Section 4/6 as under-shown.
- **Habitually pivots away from vulnerability with a fact or a joke.** [textual] — "Anyway, that isn't the bit they got us for" (ch15); "It's like clays but harder" (ch17).
- **Likes Deb and Deb's son's life story; genuinely invested, evidence against misanthropy.** [textual] (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch20).
- **Reads for pleasure compulsively as a young child; this specific behavior (not "loves reading" as a trait-label) mostly disappears from the text after Halstead absorbs the impulse into assigned material** [inferred from absence — the unlimited library at Halstead is used functionally, not described with the same hunger as the home library scenes].

## 6. The name

**Chloe Kessler.** No hard contradiction was found in usage — the name is spelled and used consistently across all twenty-plus chapters read, with no drift (unlike Dr. Sandoval's pronoun, already flagged elsewhere).

One soft tension worth noting: "Kessler" reads as a plausible Ashkenazi/German-derived surname, and the family is otherwise given no ethnic or religious specificity — except a Christmas ham at the grandmother's house (chapters/13_ten_pages.md: "I'm doing the ham at four and you're carrying it"), which mildly cuts against a Kessler household being identifiably Jewish without being an actual contradiction (plenty of secular or interfaith Kesslers exist; nothing else in the text leans either way). Given the author's own note that some names were coin-flipped, this is exactly the kind of soft mismatch worth a deliberate look — either it's nothing, or the surname was picked without the Christmas material in mind. No other mismatch — nothing about "Chloe" or "Kessler" clashes with the family's otherwise unmarked, generic-American register (Dad's job, Mom's accounting degree, the grandmother, the Thanksgiving table).

## 7. Fix list — five highest-value changes, most valuable first

1. **Cut or dramatize the theme-statement sentence in Ch25 Cleared** (CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md): *"She does not read the result as information about herself. She reads it as having sat an exam."* This is the synopsis's own "Defining trait" line, restated as narration. The scene already proves it through her flat, compartmentalized answers to Whitaker — the sentence is redundant and is the clearest single instance in her whole arc of the author's framing appearing in her head instead of her behavior.

2. **Resolve the "room-shaped object" misattribution.** It is spoken by Sam (chapters/04_pluto.md) but treated everywhere else (synopsis, style guide) as one of Chloe's own protected conceits. Either give her an explicit beat of picking the phrase up and reusing it later (the way Icarus and the moon get reused across years), so it becomes hers by adoption, or correct the character material — right now a quarter of her signature-image list isn't textually hers.

3. **Give her explanatory/tactical dialogue from age ten onward its own tic**, distinct from Ruth's and Kavi's identical "smart kid explains the mechanism" delivery. The paintball-barrel explanation (ch13) and the intrusion tactical read (ch15) currently read as generic ensemble competence-signaling. Her established early tic — recasting an abstraction as a concrete parallel scenario (the moon/table-walk, library/curfew) — largely disappears from her dialogue exactly where the swap-test risk is highest. Reintroducing it there would make roughly a third of her currently-swappable lines unswappable.

4. **Dramatize a failed face-lie at least once.** Right now "bad at lying with her face" is narrator-asserted in the one scene (ch15) where the lie in fact works flawlessly, unlike drawing and dance, which are both ranked and repeatedly shown failing/improving on the page. A single scene of the lie almost or actually failing would convert this stated weak axis into an earned one and match how the other weaknesses on her list are handled.

5. **Tone down the rhetorical polish of a few age-6 lines** ("The rule is about *at a time*, though..."; "Chloe doesn't believe the last part. Everybody says that.") so the youngest register reads more credibly six and less like the sixteen-year-old bar-exam voice transplanted backward — this is the same "literary reach" problem BETA_NOTES already flags for Icarus and the clouds line, but it shows up in her plain dialogue and cynicism too, not just the invented images.
