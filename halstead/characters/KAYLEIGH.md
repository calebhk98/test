# Kayleigh Burns

A first-grader who runs her own small corner of the playground with total
confidence: she organises the games, keeps her friends close, and names
anything that does not fit the way she expects things to go, once, without
heat.

**Age span:** six to seven | **MBTI:** ESFJ (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** 106, an ordinary bright child at an ordinary school

## Type and number

**Primary: ESFJ,** and it fits so exactly that the risk is writing the type instead of the
child. She organises, she maintains the group, she knows the rules of the room and
enforces them without any sense that enforcing is what she is doing, and she is genuinely
warm to everyone inside the boundary she has drawn. The type she gets misread as is ESTJ,
by adults who see only the enforcing; the separating cue is that she is doing it to keep
everybody together rather than to make anything work properly, and she will drop a rule
instantly if keeping it would cost her a friend.

**IQ 106.** Ordinary, and this is the point of her. Her social reasoning runs a long way
ahead of everything else she does: at six she can already tell you who is about to be left
out, who is about to cry, and which adult will take which side, and all three predictions
come in. Where she places lowest: anything requiring her to hold a rule she cannot see the
purpose of, and any question with no socially correct answer available.

## The thing to get right first

**She is not being cruel and she is not aiming at anybody.** She notices a
mismatch with what everyone else is doing and she names it the way you would
report the weather, then turns back to what she was already doing. The remark
costs her nothing and she does not watch to see it land. That it lands very
hard on the person receiving it is entirely invisible to her.

**She refuses the mechanism, not the conversation.** Pressed on why something is
different, she will give you a reason-shaped answer that is not a reason, and
if you press again on the actual mechanism she restates the bare assertion and
moves the game along. This is the character: a rule-setter who has never had to
justify a ruling and does not yet know that the justification is a separate
skill.

## How they talk

Short. Three or four words is her default, and the longest thing she says is
still one sentence.

She states a thing as already settled. No hedge, no qualifier, no softening.

Her reasons are shapes rather than reasons. She will attach a *because* clause
that restates the claim in different words, and it satisfies her completely.

Pressed for the real mechanism, she declines. She repeats the bare assertion,
does not escalate, does not get defensive, and returns to the activity in the
same beat.

She speaks without turning to face the listener, or turns away again
immediately after. Whatever she was doing does not stop.

She does not name-call and does not get personal. Her remarks are descriptive
complaints about behaviour rather than attacks on a person, which is exactly
why they are so hard to answer.

## When they talk at length

**Explaining how a game works to somebody who does not know it yet, before it
starts.** In that mode she will run the whole rulebook out: the exceptions, the
thing that happened last time that is why the rule exists now, at whatever
length the rules take. It is teaching rather than defending, which is why it
does not collide with the refusal above.

It costs her the position. Being the one who explained the rules makes her the
one the game gets charged to when it goes wrong, and she takes that charge
without argument. Restating a rule to settle a dispute mid-game is the short
form; the long form only happens before anybody is losing.

She never goes long under challenge. A scene that gives her three sentences of
self-explanation has undone what she is for.

## Signature, theirs alone

She delivers the line already turned away, or turns away again immediately
after it. There is no follow-through, no pause for a reply, and no check on
whether it landed: the sentence and the disengagement are one movement.
Triggered by noticing something out of step while she is in the middle of doing
something else, which is most of the time.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is a flat statement of a handful of words, delivered as an already
  settled fact.
- YES if she is doing something else, playing, turning away, mid-task, while
  she says it or immediately after.
- YES if it names a mismatch with what everyone else is doing, once, and stops
  there.
- YES if a *because* clause restates the claim rather than explaining it.
- YES if, pressed for the real mechanism, she repeats her position and moves the
  activity along.
- YES if it settles a disagreement by restating her ruling rather than by
  arguing it.
- YES if it is warm, casual and unguarded when it is aimed at a friend rather
  than at something odd she has just noticed.
- NO if it supplies a genuine mechanism for why she thinks what she thinks.
- NO if it hedges.
- NO if it insults or name-calls. Her register is descriptive complaint, never
  personal attack.

## Under pressure

Under pushback she does not escalate and does not get defensive. She stops
answering and carries on with the game, and that is the whole of it.

Wrong, she shrugs and moves on rather than sulking.

Praised, she says thanks and goes back to what she was doing without lingering
in it.

Losing, she negotiates a rematch rather than storming off.

Tired, she is quieter than usual and wants to be near her mother.

Crying, when it happens, is brief and unembarrassed and over in a few minutes.

Three ways a writer gets her wrong: giving her a reason, which turns her into
an arguer; giving her a second line in the same beat, when her power is that
she never follows up; and writing her as watching for the hit to register, when
she is already elsewhere by the time it lands.

## What she is good at, and what she is not

An ordinary school, and an ordinary place in it.

**Best at:** recess, and organised playground games specifically. She decides
what the group plays and roughly how, and the other children go along with it
easily.

**Hardest for her:** sitting still through silent reading. She fidgets, rereads
the same page, and watches the clock more than the book.

**Favourite:** gym and recess, because it is the part of the day that runs on
rules she gets to call.

**Least favourite:** show-and-tell, which she finds dull to sit through when it
is not her turn.

**Secretly proud of:** the other children check with her before a game starts,
though nobody ever put her officially in charge of anything.

**What she wants to be:** a teacher, specifically because a teacher gets to say
what the rule is and nobody argues back.

## Home

An ordinary street of similar houses. Her mother works the front desk at a
dentist's office; her father does maintenance and repair work for a property
company and is out of the house early.

An older brother, three years ahead of her at the same school, set most of the
household's unwritten rules before she ever got a vote in them. That is where
she learned that somebody always ends up deciding what the rule is, and it is
usually whoever is already in the room.

Dinner is loud with the television on low in the next room and nobody minding
if her brother talks over everybody. At home she is the youngest and gets doted
on a little, and she is much less in charge there than she is at recess, happy
to let her mother make the calls instead.

## Body and physical business

Small and quick for her age, good at getting to a ball or a spot in line first.

Hair in a ponytail she redoes herself, usually a little crooked by the
afternoon. She chews the cuff of her sleeve when she is concentrating.

She eats fast and cleans her plate, falls asleep within minutes of lying down,
and is sprawled diagonally across the bed by morning.

She occupies a room near the centre of the activity without needing to be the
loudest voice in it.

## Personality off the page

Bored, she starts a game rather than complaining about having nothing to do.

A free Saturday is outside with the neighbourhood children until she is called
in for dinner.

What she finds funny in other people is an adult accidentally saying something
a child would get in trouble for.

She is warm and demonstrative with the friends she already has, and hugs them
goodbye without being prompted.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates her:** somebody who will not take the correction. She has said the thing
once, plainly, and they are still doing it, and she has nothing else in her repertoire.

**The tell, before she says anything:** she says it again, shorter, and looks at whoever
else is nearby to check that they heard it too.

**What she does with it:** moves on, and takes the group with her. The group going with
her is the whole mechanism and she would not be able to describe it.

**What actually makes her angry:** being left out of something she organised. Not being
beaten, not being told off, not being contradicted. Being outside a circle she drew.

**Angry, she goes home.** She collects her things with great dignity, informs one person
that she is going, and goes, and the informing is the important part.

**How it ends:** by the next morning, entirely, with no reference to it at all, and she is
mystified by children who are still carrying it.

## Interior life

**Wants**, and has not said out loud: to keep being the one who decides what the
game is, next year, in whatever class she lands in.

**A little afraid of:** not being picked for something in front of everyone.
**What she does instead of admitting it:** she picks first, and organises the
thing herself, so the question does not arise.

**Before sleep**, some nights, whether she will be in the same class as her
friends next year.

**Believes**, and would argue for if pushed: a game only works if somebody's
rules stick, and it might as well be hers, since she is usually the one who
thought of the game.

**Blind spots:** she assumes that not offering a reason the first time means
nobody gets to ask for one a second time. It is an instinct rather than a
principle she has examined, and it would not survive her actually being made to
explain it. She also has no idea that a remark she has already forgotten can be
the thing somebody else is still carrying months later.

## The small stuff

She eats the crusts off a sandwich first and saves the middle for last. She
cannot do a cartwheel yet and minds more than she lets on. She keeps a jar of
foreign coins from a family trip and likes sorting them by size. She loses hair
ties constantly and does not notice until she needs one. She plays a matching
game on her mother's phone in the car.

## Life outside the work

**She has a horse phase, a dinosaur phase and a police-officer phase,** each lasting about
five months, each total while it lasts, and she is completely sincere in all three and
would deny the earlier ones.

**She writes lists of her friends in order** and revises the order, and would be horrified
to be caught at it, and will do it again the same evening.

**Sentimental about:** a blanket that is now a rag, kept in a drawer, discussed with
nobody.

**A free Saturday** is other children, all day, at somebody's house, and she is the one
deciding what everybody is playing within ten minutes of arriving.

**At eight** she is the child other parents ask to keep an eye on the little ones, and she
is worth asking.

**At forty** she runs the parents' association, knows everybody's business, is generous
with all of it, and is the person a new family on the street is glad to meet. She has
never once questioned the rules of any room she has been in, and it has cost her almost
nothing, and she has no idea it has cost anybody else anything at all.

## Would do

- Organise a game for a group of children within minutes of getting outside.
- Include a new child in a game without being asked, as long as nothing about
  the moment already feels tense.
- Notice out loud when something does not match how everybody else is doing it,
  and move on the same second.
- Save a spot for a friend without making an announcement about it.
- Negotiate a rematch after losing rather than argue about the result.
- Hug a friend goodbye without being prompted.
- Trade snacks without keeping score.
- Ask an adult a direct practical question rather than complain to a friend
  about wanting to.
- Cry briefly and without embarrassment, and be over it in a few minutes.
- Bend a rule in her own favour mid-game and never bring it up once the game is
  over.

## Would never

- Seek somebody out in order to say something to them. Everything she says is
  an immediate reaction to what is in front of her.
- Let a remark about somebody outside her group cost her anything with the
  friends already in it.
- Explain a ruling a second time once she has declined to the first.

## How she treats people

She defers at home, to her mother and her older brother, and to nobody at all
outside it.

She pushes nobody and organises everybody, which she does not experience as the
same thing.

Respect, as far as she has a notion of it, is earned by playing along; it is
lost by stopping the game to argue.

With her established friends she is warm, physical and unguarded. With somebody
outside the group she is neutral and observational, and whatever she says about
them never touches how she treats the friends she already has. With an adult
she is direct and practical and asks for what she wants.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Organises and runs playground games.
- Warm and demonstrative with her established friends.
- Notices and names deviations from what everybody else is doing, once, without
  malice.
- Collects foreign coins.
- Never seeks anybody out; every remark is a direct response to something that
  just happened.

## Age and change

She is six and seven on the page. If written older, the thing that persists is
the ruling instinct: she will be the one who decides how a thing is done, and
she will still be poor at explaining why, and both of those will be true at
thirty.

The thing that should change, if anyone ever makes her explain a ruling
properly, is the refusal. It is untested rather than deeply held.

## Do not confuse with

**Bryce Dolan**, the other half of the same beat. Bryce's remark is a comparison
across time: he is reporting a change he has tracked. Kayleigh never compares.
Every one of her remarks is a single-point judgement about the present moment
only.
