# KAYLEIGH BURNS

A first-grade classmate who runs her own small corner of the playground with total confidence. She organizes the games, keeps her friends close, and calls out anything that doesn't fit the way she expects things to go, once, without heat.

**Age span:** six to seven (chapters/01_before.md through chapters/09_february.md; ordinary elementary school, never Halstead) | **MBTI:** ESFJ (a writing aid, not a claim; sociable, organizes her group, comfortable being the one who says how a game works) | **IQ:** average, an ordinary bright kid at an ordinary school, not measured against anyone
**Born:** November 2004, an ordinary classmate on the same school-year cycle Chloe was on before Halstead.
**Appears in:** chapters/01_before.md, chapters/02_march_4th.md, chapters/07_the_same_room.md, chapters/09_february.md (name-check only, she is not present in this scene)
**Speaks:** 5 lines, all one short sentence each. Four of the five are in chapters/01_before.md; the fifth and last is in chapters/07_the_same_room.md.

## Voice in one paragraph

Three to four words is her default; her longest recorded line is seven words. She never explains a claim. When Chloe presses her for the mechanism behind one ("How was it different, though?") she repeats the bare assertion instead of supplying a reason: "It just was." She never hedges, never uses a subordinate clause, never reaches for an image. She says her piece and returns to whatever she was already doing in the same beat, without watching for a reaction. The most characteristic line: "You talk weird," she says (chapters/01_before.md:56). The sentence right after it does the character work: "She isn't being mean about it. She says it, turns back around, and for her that's the end of it." (chapters/01_before.md:57). That's Chloe's own read of the moment, a six-year-old delivering a correction the way you'd point out an untied shoe, with no follow-through.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is a flat statement, three to seven words, delivered as an already-settled fact.
- YES if she is doing something else, playing, turning away, mid-task, while she says it or right after. It never stops her.
- YES if it names a mismatch with what everyone else is doing, once, and stops there.
- YES if it's warm, casual, and unguarded when it's aimed at a friend rather than at something odd she's just noticed. Her recorded lines are all the second kind, but nothing about her rules out the first with people she already trusts.
- YES if it settles a disagreement about a game by simply restating her position and moving the game along.
- NO if it contains a "because," a qualifier, or any explanation of why she thinks what she thinks. Asked for a reason, she declines to give one.
- NO if it hedges, "maybe," "I guess," "kind of."
- NO if it directly insults or name-calls. "You talk weird" and "why do you talk like that" are descriptive complaints about behavior, not slurs. A line that gets personal, "you're stupid," "nobody likes you," is a different register than anything on the page for her.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She occupies space by continuing whatever she was already doing, the ball game, turning back around, being one of three people she hugs at the classroom door on the last day of school (chapters/02_march_4th.md:122). She is ordinarily social, embedded in a group, not isolated or watching for effect. Under pushback, Chloe's "How was it different, though?", she doesn't escalate or get defensive. She stops answering and carries on with the game.

Failure modes:
1. Giving her a reason. The instant she explains why something is weird or unfair, she stops being Kayleigh and starts being an arguer.
2. Giving her a second line in the same beat. Her power is that she never follows up.
3. Writing her as watching for the hit to register. She isn't. She's already elsewhere by the time the line lands.

## Subjects and schedule

Ordinary public elementary school, not Halstead. `CURRICULUM_GRID.md` and `_ALLOCATIONS.md` don't apply to her; she never receives a Halstead letter and this sheet does not invent one.

- **Best at:** recess and organized playground games (chapters/01_before.md:43–47). She decides what the group plays and roughly how, and the other kids go along with it easily.
- **Worst at:** sitting still through silent reading time. She fidgets, re-reads the same page, and watches the clock more than the book.
- **Favourite, and why:** gym and recess, because it's the one part of the day that runs on rules she gets to call.
- **Least favourite:** show-and-tell, which she finds boring to sit through when it isn't her turn.
- **Secretly proud of:** the other kids check with her before a game starts, though nobody ever put her officially in charge of anything.
- **Would want to be when she grows up:** a teacher, specifically because a teacher gets to say what the rule is and nobody argues back.

**How she feels about a classmate who reads ahead:** this is the one thing the text actually shows. Chloe reading further than everyone else, or answering with more than the expected one word, registers to Kayleigh as worth a single remark, not a threat, not something to admire, just something that doesn't match. She says so once, plainly, and goes back to her own game. It's the same reflex that makes her call an inconsistent ruling at the ball: notice, name it, move on.

## Home

Same town as Chloe, an ordinary street of similar houses. Her mother works the front desk at a dentist's office. Her father does maintenance and repair work for a property company and is out of the house early. An older brother, three years ahead of her at the same school, set most of the household's unwritten rules before Kayleigh ever got a vote in them. That's where she learned that somebody always ends up deciding what the rule is, and it's usually whoever's already in the room. Dinner is loud, the TV usually on low in the next room, nobody minding if her brother talks over everyone. At home she's the youngest and gets doted on a little, less in charge there than she is at recess, happy to let her mother make the calls instead.

## Body

Small and quick for her age, good at getting to a ball or a spot in line first. Hair in a ponytail she redoes herself, usually a little crooked by afternoon. Chews the cuff of her sleeve when she's concentrating on something. Eats fast, cleans her plate, falls asleep within minutes of lying down, sprawled diagonally across the bed by morning.

## Personality off the page

- Occupies a room near the center of activity without needing to be the loudest voice in it.
- Bored: starts a game rather than complaining about having nothing to do.
- Wrong: shrugs and moves on rather than sulking.
- Praised: says thanks and goes back to whatever she was doing, doesn't linger in it.
- Losing: negotiates a rematch rather than storming off.
- Tired: quieter than usual, wants to be near her mother.
- A free Saturday: outside with the neighborhood kids until she's called in for dinner.
- What she finds funny in other people: an adult accidentally saying something a kid would get in trouble for.

## Interior life

What she wants that she hasn't said out loud: to keep being the one who decides what the game is, next year, in whatever class she lands in. What she's a little afraid of: not being picked for something in front of everyone. What she thinks about before sleep, some nights: whether she'll be in the same class as her friends next year. What she believes, and would argue for if pushed: a game only works if somebody's rules stick, and it might as well be hers, since she's usually the one who thought of the game. What she's wrong about: she assumes that not offering a reason the first time means nobody gets to ask for one a second time. It's an instinct, not a principle she's examined, and it wouldn't survive her actually being made to explain it.

## The small stuff

Eats the crusts off a sandwich first and saves the middle for last. Can't do a cartwheel yet and minds more than she lets on. Keeps a jar of foreign coins from a family trip and likes sorting them by size. Loses hair ties constantly and doesn't notice until she needs one. Plays a matching game on her mother's phone in the car.

## Would do

1. Organize a recess game for a group of kids within minutes of getting outside.
2. Include a new kid in a game without being asked, as long as nothing about the moment already feels upset or tense.
3. Notice out loud when something doesn't match how everyone else is doing it, and move on the same second.
4. Save a spot for a friend without making an announcement about it.
5. Negotiate a rematch after losing rather than argue about the result.
6. Hug a friend goodbye without being prompted (chapters/02_march_4th.md:122).
7. Trade snacks at lunch without keeping score.
8. Ask a teacher a direct, practical question, can we do it outside today, rather than complain to a friend about wanting to.
9. Cry briefly and without embarrassment, and be over it within a few minutes.
10. Bend a rule in her own favor mid-game and never bring it up again once the game's over (chapters/01_before.md:43–47).

## Would never

1. Would never seek Chloe out to say something. Every one of her five lines is an immediate, in-the-moment reaction; she has no instinct to go looking for a reason to speak to her.
2. Would never let a remark about someone outside her group cost her anything with the friends already in it. Whatever she says about Chloe never touches how she treats Maddie or the rest of her circle.
3. Would never explain a ruling a second time once she's declined to the first. She disengages rather than argues (chapters/01_before.md:48–47).

## Known problems

The frame can be read as covering both, but the text only states it about him.
