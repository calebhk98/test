# KAYLEIGH

A first- and second-grade classmate who polices whatever is in front of her — a rule at recess, a sentence Chloe just said — states it once, flatly, and considers the matter closed.

**Age span:** six to seven (chapters/01_before.md through chapters/09_february.md; pre-Halstead only) | **MBTI:** ESTJ (a writing aid, not a claim — decisive, present-tense, unbothered by justifying herself) | **IQ:** average (reader inference; the text gives no signal of exceptional or deficient ability in either direction — she is written as an ordinary foil to Chloe, never tested or scored)
**Appears in:** chapters/01_before.md, chapters/02_march_4th.md, chapters/07_the_same_room.md, chapters/09_february.md (name-check only — she is not present in this scene)
**Speaks:** 5 lines, all of them one short sentence each. Four of the five are in chapters/01_before.md; the fifth and last is in chapters/07_the_same_room.md. She never appears again after that — chapters/09_february.md:25 is Chloe listing her name internally, not a scene she is in.

## Voice in one paragraph

Three to four words is her default; her longest recorded line is seven words. She never explains a claim, and when Chloe presses her for the mechanism behind one ("How was it different, though?") she refuses to supply it and repeats the bare assertion instead ("It just was"). She does not ask real questions — her one interrogative line is closed and rhetorical, not an actual request for information. She never hedges, never uses a subordinate clause, never reaches for an image. She says her piece and immediately returns to whatever she was already doing, in the same beat, without watching for a reaction. The most characteristic line is the one both the author's own notes and this book's differentiation problem centre on: "You talk weird," she says. (chapters/01_before.md:54) — and the sentence right after it is doing the character work: "She isn't being mean about it. She says it, turns back around, and for her that's the end of it." (chapters/01_before.md:55). That is Chloe's own read of the moment (Chloe is the POV character, so this is permitted interiority, not narrator intrusion) — a six-year-old delivering a correction the way you'd point out an untied shoe, with no follow-through and no investment in the reaction.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She occupies space by continuing whatever she was already doing — the ball game, turning back around, being one of "three people" she hugs at the classroom door on the last day of school (chapters/02_march_4th.md:120) while Chloe gets no goodbye from anyone. That detail matters for how a rewriter should picture her: she is ordinarily social, not isolated or friendless, which is part of why her lines read as unweighted rather than pointed — she has nothing riding on Chloe's reaction. Under any kind of pushback (Chloe's "How was it different, though?") she does not escalate or get defensive; she just doesn't answer and moves on.

Failure modes:
1. Giving her a reason. The instant she explains *why* something is weird or unfair, she stops being Kayleigh and starts being an arguer like Ruth.
2. Giving her a second line in the same beat. Her power is that she never follows up — a rewriter tempted to "land the point" with a second jab has broken the character.
3. Writing her as watching for the hit to register. The text is explicit that she isn't; she is already elsewhere by the time the line lands.

## Known problems

1. **chapters/01_before.md:41–47 vs. chapters/01_before.md:54–55.** Her first appearance is self-interested rule-bending with evasion ("It just was," refusing to justify staying in after a catch that should have put her out); her defining trait per the reference documents is norm-*enforcement* ("you talk weird" policing Chloe's speech). These are not strictly contradictory — a six-year-old can both dodge a rule and enforce one — but a rewriter handed only "she enforces norms matter-of-factly" could easily flatten the ball-game scene into the same register as the Icarus scene, when the text currently plays them as two different moves (self-serving evasion vs. disinterested correction). Worth a line distinguishing them if she is expanded.
2. **chapters/07_the_same_room.md:135–137.** The explicit "she isn't being mean about it" defense is written onto Bryce's line, not hers, even though her line ("Why do you talk like that?") comes first in the same beat. It is reasonable to read the frame as covering both, but the text only says it about him. If the author wants Kayleigh's second line to carry the same non-cruelty as her first, add a clause — right now a rewriter has to infer it by analogy rather than read it on the page.
