# KAYLEIGH BURNS

A first-grade classmate who runs her own small corner of the playground with total confidence — she organizes the games, keeps her friends close, and calls out anything that doesn't fit the way she expects things to go, once, without heat.

**Age span:** six to seven (chapters/01_before.md through chapters/09_february.md; ordinary elementary school, never Halstead) | **MBTI:** ESFJ (a writing aid, not a claim — sociable, organizes her group, comfortable being the one who says how a game works) | **IQ:** average (reader inference; the text never scores her — she is written as an ordinary classmate, not a foil measured against Chloe)
**Appears in:** chapters/01_before.md, chapters/02_march_4th.md, chapters/07_the_same_room.md, chapters/09_february.md (name-check only — she is not present in this scene)
**Speaks:** 5 lines, all one short sentence each. Four of the five are in chapters/01_before.md; the fifth and last is in chapters/07_the_same_room.md.

## Voice in one paragraph

Three to four words is her default; her longest recorded line is seven words. She never explains a claim, and when Chloe presses her for the mechanism behind one ("How was it different, though?") she repeats the bare assertion instead of supplying a reason ("It just was"). She never hedges, never uses a subordinate clause, never reaches for an image. She says her piece and immediately returns to whatever she was already doing, in the same beat, without watching for a reaction. The most characteristic line is the one this book keeps circling back to: "You talk weird," she says. (chapters/01_before.md:54) — and the sentence right after it is doing the character work: "She isn't being mean about it. She says it, turns back around, and for her that's the end of it." (chapters/01_before.md:55). That is Chloe's own read of the moment — a six-year-old delivering a correction the way you'd point out an untied shoe, with no follow-through and no investment in the reaction.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is a flat statement, 3–7 words, delivered as an already-settled fact.
- YES if she is doing something else (playing, turning away, mid-task) while she says it or right after — it never stops her.
- YES if it names a mismatch with what everyone else is doing, once, and stops there.
- YES if it's warm, casual, and unguarded when it's aimed at a friend rather than at something odd she's just noticed — her recorded lines are all the second kind, but nothing about her rules out the first with people she already trusts.
- YES if it settles a disagreement about a game by simply restating her position and moving the game along.
- NO if it contains a "because," a qualifier, or any explanation of why she thinks what she thinks — asked for a reason, she declines to give one.
- NO if it hedges ("maybe," "I guess," "kind of").
- NO if it directly insults or name-calls — "you talk weird" and "why do you talk like that" are descriptive complaints about behavior, not slurs; a line that gets personal ("you're stupid," "nobody likes you") is a different register than anything on the page for her.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She occupies space by continuing whatever she was already doing — the ball game, turning back around, being one of "three people" she hugs at the classroom door on the last day of school (chapters/02_march_4th.md:120). That detail matters: she is ordinarily social, embedded in a group, not isolated or watching for effect. Under pushback (Chloe's "How was it different, though?") she doesn't escalate or get defensive; she just stops answering and carries on with the game.

Failure modes:
1. Giving her a reason. The instant she explains *why* something is weird or unfair, she stops being Kayleigh and starts being an arguer.
2. Giving her a second line in the same beat. Her power is that she never follows up.
3. Writing her as watching for the hit to register. She isn't; she's already elsewhere by the time the line lands.

## Interior life

[Invented, consistent with everything above.] What she wants that she hasn't said out loud: to keep being the one who decides what the game is, next year, in whatever class she lands in. What she's a little afraid of: not being picked for something in front of everyone. What she thinks about before sleep, some nights: whether she'll be in the same class as her friends next year. What she believes, and would argue for if pushed: that a game only works if somebody's rules stick, and it might as well be hers, since she's usually the one who thought of the game. What she's wrong about: that not offering a reason the first time means nobody gets to ask for one a second time — it's an instinct, not a principle she's examined, and it wouldn't survive her actually being made to explain it.

## The small stuff

Eats the crusts off a sandwich first and saves the middle for last. Can't do a cartwheel yet and minds more than she lets on. Keeps a jar of foreign coins from a family trip and likes sorting them by size. Loses hair ties constantly and doesn't notice until she needs one. Plays a matching game on her mother's phone in the car.
