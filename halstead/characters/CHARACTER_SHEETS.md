# Character Sheets

A working reference, one screen per character. Use it to answer two questions while
drafting: *would this character say this line?* and *are they acting like themselves here?*

The long per-character files in this folder are the evidence. This is the summary.

## How to read the dials

Rated 1 to 10 against the other eight, not against people in general. Everyone here is
terser and less figurative than an average person, so a 5 for wordiness means average
*for this cast*.

Dials marked **(m)** are measured from the text by `voice_separation.py`. The rest are
judgements from the per-character audits.

MBTI is a working label to keep the voices apart while writing. It is not a claim about
psychology, and it is not in the manuscript. IQ figures come from
`SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md`, which states they are a reader's inference from
performance, not from anything in the text.

## Chloe Kessler — INFJ, IQ 160-168, ages 6-21

**Talks like:** two channels that never merge. To institutions (teachers, doctors,
Whitaker) she gives the shortest safe answer that ends the question. To people she trusts
she produces long, structurally complete explanations. Her tic, running from six to
twenty-one: she recasts an abstract disagreement as a concrete parallel scenario, then
argues the parallel instead. "If you walked around this table..." / library-at-eleven
versus curfew-at-nine / "Latin is the year paying for itself twice."

**Would she say it?** Yes if it converts an argument into a physical example, or if it is
four words to an adult with a clipboard. No if it evaluates her own performance — she
files outcomes as things that happened, not as evidence about her.

**Jokiness 3.** Rarely initiates. Her humour is deadpan agreement or a literalism.

**Figurative 7** — highest in the cast, and deliberately so. Her invented images are
protected: Icarus, clouds as the ocean going somewhere else, the moon as a liar.
*Note: the room-shaped object is on that list in `STYLE_GUIDES.md` and should not be —
Sam says it, `04_pluto.md:45`.*

**Likes** difficulty for its own sake, being argued with, reading, Deb's son's entire
life story. **Dislikes** repetition, waiting for the class, being told not to rush, being
handled, being told things are fine when they aren't.

**Acting out of character if:** she treats a result as proof of her own worth; she lies
successfully and easily; she asks for something directly without a run-up.

**Known problem:** her explanatory register from age 10 on is the least distinct thing
she does — it swaps cleanly with Ruth or Kavi. Her stress and loyalty scenes never swap.

## Ruth — INTJ, IQ 175+, ages 6-21

**Talks like:** opens with a flat assertion, never a question. Corrects mid-sentence and
uses your name while doing it: "It is not better, Sam." / "That is not what any of the
maths says, Kavi." Refuses outcomes as evidence for process — "Five out of eight is what
a coin does. Come back to me when it's eight." Scales unbroken from probing whether
fraction-flipping holds for zero at six, to the forensic proof at twenty-one.

**Would she say it?** Yes if it distinguishes a fact from an inference, or refuses credit
that hasn't been earned. No if it accepts a conclusion because the result came out right.

**Jokiness 4.** Dry, delivered as fact, never performed. "So, it's rude."

**Longest-winded of the students in chat** (9.3 words/line, 72 messages — she posts more
than anyone). **Lowest lexical diversity (43%)**: she hammers the same precise vocabulary
rather than reaching for variety. That is in character, not a defect.

**Likes** correctness, being argued with, Latin (contentiously). **Dislikes** surprises
without notice, arguments from outcome, being alone with a true thing.

**Acting out of character if:** she lets an error pass; she argues from a result; she
sits on a conclusion. *That last one is the Ch26 problem — six months of silence with no
earlier precedent for her keeping anything to herself.*

**Best differentiated voice in the book** — only 12-14% of her lines would survive a swap.

## Sam — ESTP, IQ 155-165, ages 7-21

**Talks like:** the shortest lines of anyone (5.1 words in chat, and the only student who
never writes a long message). Blunt declaratives and direct questions. Disagrees by
restating louder rather than producing evidence — the exact inverse of Ruth. Acts first,
explains after. The only one who regularly asks questions in chat (7%).

**Would he say it?** Yes if it is a flat fact, a plan, or a refusal to be impressed. No
if it is built as an aphorism with mirrored halves — he does that exactly once, in the
Ch21 rifle speech, and it is the book's thesis in his mouth rather than him talking.

**Jokiness 9** — the cast's comic register. But his comedy is *stubbornness*, not wit:
repeating "This is the worst mistake of my life" four times, not landing a clever line.
Write him funny by making him immovable, not quick.

**Likes** schedules, Sergeant Ruiz, being told to do it again, waffles, shopping
trolleys, having his window open. **Dislikes** being at forty percent (mildly), being
laughed at (acutely).

**Acting out of character if:** he builds a metaphor; he defers to rank; he lets a number
stand without asking what it measures.

**Known problem:** 35-40% of his lines swap out, almost all of them content-free chat
reactions. And "no internal ceiling" is asserted in Ch21 but contradicted by his
two-year plateau at forty percent, which he does not mind.

## Kavi — INTP, IQ 165-175, ages 6-21

**Talks like:** front-loads the conclusion, then either stops dead or runs one long
unbroken technical explanation and *then* stops dead. Nothing in between. **The most
clipped speaker in the cast — 44% of his chat messages are three words or fewer** — and
simultaneously the longest-winded in prose (6.5 words/line, highest of anyone). That
whiplash is his signature. Corrects rather than argues. Downplays himself pre-emptively:
"It's four seconds of compute. It's not even interesting."

**Would he say it?** Yes if it fixes a mechanism or refuses a compliment. No if it is
figurative — he produces no invented image anywhere in the book, and that is worth
keeping.

**Jokiness 2** — the lowest. **Highest lexical diversity (69%)**: he reaches for the
precise word.

**Likes** the lab, the problem being the interesting thing. **Dislikes** being told
something is impressive when it was easy, letting an unjust mark stand.

**Acting out of character if:** he accepts praise; he speaks in an image; he lets a wrong
mechanism pass without correcting it.

**Three known problems.**
1. **The camp setup doesn't hold.** `04_pluto.md:96` establishes him silent for six days,
   then breaking out over volcanoes and "keeps going. Sam tries twice more to get back
   into it and doesn't manage it either time." That is the whole payoff. He has **zero
   lines in ch7 and ch13**, and outside ch4 and ch15 averages under 1.5 lines a chapter.
   The trait exists in one scene.
2. **He has no surname.** `10_april.md` gives Ruth hers in full — "Aymar. A, Y, M, A, R."
   — then asks Kavi "What's your last name," fourteen lines later and never answers.
3. **The name does no work.** No food, language, holiday, or family detail anywhere. His
   mother's one line is reported secondhand. Free to change, or free to make real.

## Nadia — ESTJ, IQ 160-170

**Talks like:** short independent clauses, statements not questions, no hedging, flat
correction instead of argument. Deadpan agreement rather than invented jokes. Zero
figurative language across every line she has. Her one line built on rhythm rather than
flat statement is the one that defines her: "If you're going to come at me, come at me.
Don't insult me with a cleaner."

**Would she say it?** Yes if it is about a concrete object, person, or decision, and
proportionate to the offence. No if it reasons abstractly — the Ch18 labour speech is the
only place she does, and it reads as the author rather than her.

**Jokiness 5.** Agrees deadpan; doesn't invent.

**Likes** being come at properly, building a thing a person uses, running things.
**Dislikes** being underestimated, cheap attacks, wasting a police officer's afternoon.

**Acting out of character if:** she escalates disproportionately, or theorises.

**Known problem — the worst score in the cast. 59% of her 41 lines would swap, and the
profile above rests on 13 lines, nine of them in one chapter.** Her fix is already in
your text: `14_sixty_degrees.md:213` has her taking the third floor and splitting it in
four minutes, "eleven people take four instructions from her in a row before any of them
thinks about it" — her defining trait, in narration, with no dialogue. Give her one
spoken command there.

## Eli — ENTP, IQ 160-170

**Talks like:** short, unhedged, assertive. Treats every institution as a system with a
findable mechanism — "a rule with a known enforcement mechanism and you learn where the
cameras are." Jokes by gamifying stakes: "guys im speedrunning retirement."

**Would he say it?** Yes if it reframes a risk as a solvable constraint. No if it
expresses doubt about whether something *can* be done.

**Jokiness 7**, and the only playful register in the cast that isn't Sam's stubbornness.

**Acting out of character if:** he defers, or treats a rule as a wall rather than a
mechanism.

**Known problem:** "i have never once been at the top of a room in my life" is unearned —
every prior appearance shows him winning. And he has **one scene in chapters 1-20**
(`16_thirteen.md`, the cipher on a napkin).

## Theo — INFP, IQ 165-175

**Talks like:** the longest messages of anyone (10.6 words/line) and the fewest short ones
(12% terse — half the cast average). Multi-clause, briefing-shaped. **The only one who
visibly hedges while being the least uncertain** — "apparently... i genuinely cannot see
where." Objects procedurally, then complies: "i want it on record," and then signs off
anyway. Ends threads on open questions; everyone else ends on assertions.

**Would he say it?** Yes if it registers an objection without preventing the thing. No if
it is a flat unhedged assertion — that is Eli's register, and the two blur badly.

**Jokiness 3.**

**Acting out of character if:** he acts unilaterally, or states a conclusion without a
qualifier.

**Known problem — the worst structural gap in the book. Theo has zero dialogue in
chapters 1-20.** One narration mention, `13_ten_pages.md:162`. He arrives with his own
chapter at Ch22. A blind test could reassign only 11 of 20 of his and Eli's chat messages
correctly, barely above a coin flip, and the failures are the short filler both share.

## Meg — ESFJ, IQ 110-120

**Talks like:** institutionally competent, emotionally avoidant. Escalating three-clause
questions aimed at schools, districts, and gas ovens; then a joke or a flat lie the moment
the subject turns to what Chloe is feeling — "So you're all good... Everything's great."
**The most clipped speaker in the book at 54% terse**, which is the avoidance showing up
as measurement. Uses Dave's name when she is pushing him, all four times.

**Would she say it?** Yes if it is a question with a follow-up already loaded, or a
deflection. No if it names a feeling directly.

**Best line, verified exact:** "I'm doing my best guess," she says. "Okay?"
(`02_march_4th.md:13`). Best constructed: the refusal of the house in ch9.

**Acting out of character if:** she sits calmly with a hard thing, or accepts a first
answer.

**Two known problems.** She loses her own story: 10-20 turns a chapter through ch1-9, then
4 in ch10 (all secondhand by phone), 0 in ch13, and **3 lines at the graduation her ch9
scene sets up**. Neither parent appears at all in the final eight chapters. And **"She got
polite" is not in the manuscript** — `BETA_NOTES.md` cites it four times and the synopsis
twice, including as a timeline anchor, but neither the line nor the hallway speech around
it exists in any chapter file.

## Dave (Chloe's father) — ISTJ, IQ 120-130

**He is named.** `SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md:233` heads his section "Dad (unnamed)";
Meg calls him Dave in `06_the_list.md` twice, `09_february.md`, and `11_eight.md`. The
narrator never does.

**Talks like:** two registers. Clipped domestic — "Cool," "Huh" — and a recurring
analytical triple-question aimed at documents and money: "So how does a four-year-old
school get that guy. What does it pay him. With what." (ch3), repeated almost identically
about a vendor contract in ch12, which makes it a stable trait rather than one showcase.
**Asks far more questions than anyone else (15%).**

**Would he say it?** Yes if it interrogates a document or a number. No if it pushes on an
emotional point — his defining failure is that he asks every time and never presses.

**"Huh." as a low-commitment closing beat is his one unshared verbal tic.** Meg never
says it. Build on it.

**Acting out of character if:** he pushes past a deflection, or stops fact-checking.

**Known problem:** zero lines at graduation, against a defining trait of always asking.

## Telling them apart

The blind-attribution scores, worst first. A high number means a reader cannot tell them
apart from dialogue alone.

| pair | reassigned to the wrong one | what actually separates them |
| :-- | --: | :-- |
| **Meg / Dave** | **17 of 20** | Only *what they investigate* — she chases danger, he chases money — and stage direction: she touches, he photographs. Both vanish in a transcript. Dave's "Huh." is the sole diction tic. |
| **Eli / Theo** | 9 of 20 | Theo runs long and hedges; Eli runs short and asserts. Theo's vocabulary is institutional ("on record", "classified", "supervisor"), Eli's is adversarial ("cameras", "pipeline"). Both collapse into identical filler under low stakes. |
| Sam / anyone | — | Shortest lines in the cast, the only frequent question-asker among the students, never writes long. Easy to spot. |
| Kavi / anyone | — | The clipped-then-essay whiplash. 44% terse and the longest prose lines simultaneously. Nobody else does both. |
| Ruth / Chloe | — | Ruth opens with an assertion and names you while correcting. Chloe opens by building a parallel scenario. Reliable. |

**The text demonstrates the problem on itself twice.** `CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md:465` and
`:468` give Kavi and Ruth the identical six words back to back — "thats a person getting
older." Lines 844 and 847 do the same to Ruth and Eli with "thats the only thing we have."

**The good news, measured.** In the chat sections your seven span 5.1 to 10.6 words per
message with genuinely separable habits. In prose dialogue the same seven collapse to
4.0-6.5 and roughly half of everyone's lines are three words or fewer. The voices exist —
you have already written them — they just aren't reaching the prose.
