## 2. Does "silent six days, then never stops" pay off?

**Short answer: no, not beyond the one scene that creates the reputation.**

The line "silent for six days and then never stops" is not the manuscript's own wording — it's
the reader-inference synopsis's summary (SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md, Kavi profile). What the
manuscript actually says is narrower: "There's a fourth one in Pluto who doesn't say anything for
six days... Then nothing." (chapters/04_pluto.md), followed by the volcano explanation, which ends
"'How thick it is,' Kavi says, **and keeps going**." That one sentence — a single unbroken
technical monologue on day seven — is almost certainly the entire textual basis for "never stops."

Counted per chapter across the reading list (a "turn" = one uninterrupted speaking beat, whether
one sentence or several):

| Chapter | Turns | Chapter | Turns |
|---|---|---|---|
| 04 Pluto (his intro) | 7 | 16 Thirteen | 2 |
| 06 The List | 5 | 17 Fourteen | 4 |
| 05 Behind | 1 | 18 Fifteen | 1 |
| 07 Same Room | 0 | 19 Sixteen | 3 |
| 10 April | 5 | v2·16 Applications | 0 |
| 11 Eight | 1 | v2·17 The Offer | 2 |
| 12 Nine | 2 | v2·18 First One | 0 |
| 13 Ten Pages | **0** | v2·19 The Chat | 7 |
| 14 Sixty Degrees | 4 | v2·20 Nineteen | 0 |
| **15 Twelve** | **15** | v2·23 Nadia | 1 |
| | | v2·26 Ruth | 2 |
| | | v2·27 The Money | 2 |
| | | v2·29 The Files | 3 |
| | | v2·30 Nine Minutes | 2 |

That's roughly 61 turns across 21 chapters — not "fewer than 10," but the distribution gives away
the setup. Two chapters (04's introduction scene and 15's overnight intrusion + bread test) supply
**36% of everything he ever says** in the reading list. Strip those two out and the remaining 19
chapters average under 1.5 lines each, with two chapters — 07 and, notably, 13 — at zero, despite
Kavi sitting at the dinner table for the whole of chapter 13 while Chloe, Sam and Ruth carry every
exchange. The special instruction's threshold ("if a character established as never stopping talks
speaks four times in a chapter, that is a finding") is worth inverting here: in most chapters he
speaks *fewer* than four times, several at zero, and the "never stops" reputation survives only
because readers remember the two loud chapters and generalize from them. On the page, Kavi is one
of the quietest members of the core four in ordinary ensemble scenes — he only reliably speaks up
when there is a technical or security fact on the table.

## 3. Voice profile

Derived only from the lines above.

- **Sentence shape:** short declaratives building into one long explanatory run, then stopping
  dead. The volcano speech (ch4), the AES-256 sequence (ch15), and the arrow/rifle progression
  speech (ch17) are all the same shape: two or three short factual sentences, then one longer
  sentence that carries the whole payload and ends the turn.
- **Opens with the fact, not the person.** He almost never opens with "I think" or a hedge. "It's
  not hollow." "It's AES-256." "That's a heat exchanger problem." He states the conclusion first
  and only backfills the reasoning if pressed.
- **Disagreement is correction, not argument.** He doesn't build a case the way Ruth or Chloe do
  (compare Chloe's essay-length arguing). He states what's wrong and stops: "That's not how it
  works though." "That's worse than not knowing." He rarely escalates a disagreement into a
  multi-line back-and-forth the way Ruth/Sam do (compare ch5's moon argument, which runs for a
  full page of Ruth-Sam-Chloe crossfire — Kavi gets exactly one line in it).
- **Downplays his own competence before anyone can call it impressive.** "It's four seconds of
  compute. It's not even interesting." This is not modesty — he's pre-empting a compliment he
  doesn't want, which the text later confirms is a live dislike (Sam mocks him with it seconds
  later: "'Four seconds,' Sam says.").
- **No figurative language of his own.** Per the style guide's rule that figurative language
  belongs in characters' mouths, Chloe, Sam and Ruth are each given at least one invented image
  (Icarus, the moon as a liar, "room-shaped object," "tidally locked" as an argument). Kavi never
  produces one. Everything he says is literal, mechanical, or a number. This is consistent and
  could be read as a genuine, deliberate trait (a character who deals only in what's
  falsifiable) — but it also means he's the one character with no verbal signature beyond subject
  matter, which the swap test below tests directly.
- **Physical staging around his lines is unusually consistent:** arms folded in a doorway (ch10),
  back against the radiator (ch15), chin on his forearms (ch17), one-shoulder shrug (ch06). He is
  physically economical the way he is verbally economical — this is good, working characterization
  that doesn't need a dialogue tag to land.
- **Most characteristic line:** "That's worse than not knowing. If you don't know then it's
  nobody's fault, but he knew and he forgot, and that's a decision about how much it mattered."
  (chapters/10_april.md.) It's the purest expression of his defining trait — applying a precise,
  slightly punitive logical instrument to something as small as Sam forgetting to mention Chloe had
  arrived — and it's the only line in the corpus that shows him running his scepticism on a person
  rather than a system, for free, unprompted. That's "second-order defences nobody asked for" in
  miniature.

## 4. The swap test

Roughly a third of Kavi's lines are functionally interchangeable with another character's. Two
examples the author should look at first:

**The duplicate line.** CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md, Ch27 "The Money":
> ruth: the band doesn't move when the money moves countries. it moves about forty minutes total, gradually, over two decades
> kavi: thats a person getting older
> ruth: thats a person getting older

Ruth repeats Kavi's line back verbatim, unchanged, one beat later. This isn't a case for inferring
whether the line would survive a swap — the manuscript performs the swap for you, in the text, with
no distinguishing marker between the two deliveries. If the line were reassigned entirely to Ruth,
or entirely to Kavi, nothing about the scene would need to change.

**The marking scene.** chapters/19_sixteen.md, the bar-practice-paper exchange ("Two out of four...
You have the rule. You have where it comes from... Write me the shed burning down.") reads as
generic exacting-teacher voice rather than anything specific to cryptography or networks — it could
be handed to Hearn or Amberg (who both mark this exact way, on the exact same exam, two pages
later in the same chapter) without a reader noticing the register had changed.

**Other lines that would likely survive a swap unchanged**, because they carry information the
scene needs but no voice markers:
- "Nope." / "server room" (ch15) — pure content words, no character signature.
- "First one's always short. You'll have it back by the third." (ch18) — could be Sam or Odile
  comforting Chloe; nothing in the phrasing is Kavi-specific.
- "who though. you need a reason to care and the money to chase it" (v2 Ch27) — generic strategic
  reasoning any of the seven could produce.
- "ruth" / "read that back" / "no. read it back" (v2 Ch29) — could be any of the seven insisting
  Ruth reckon with her own words.
- "Nah, They have moved everything, every year, school doesn't stay the same, it gets harder over
  time." (ch17) — this observation ("the school escalates difficulty on a schedule") is made by
  nearly every character across the book in nearly identical flat delivery; it's a systemic
  under-differentiation across the whole cast, not a Kavi-specific problem, but it means the line
  does nothing to individuate him.

**Lines that would NOT survive a swap** — his strongest, most load-bearing material:
- "It stopped being school for me a while ago. It's a lab that also has children in it."
  (v2 Ch17) — unique to his arc; only he stays.
- "the only reason i built that is because im me" (v2 Ch30) — an explicit, rare moment of
  first-person self-branding; none of the other six talk about their own capability this directly.
- "That's a heat exchanger problem and I want to know which part of it is the limit." (ch15) — the
  bread test is about taste; Kavi's only interested in the mechanism. This redirection-toward-the-
  interesting-problem is specific to him and to no one else in the ensemble.
- "That's worse than not knowing... that's a decision about how much it mattered." (ch10) — see §3.
- "I got it by nine. ... That was not the thing to say." (ch19) — the hand-over-mouth self-catch is
  the single clearest piece of *shown*, non-narrated interiority he gets in the whole book, and it's
  specific: it shows he has a working, if slow, model of when he's landed badly, which nobody else's
  parallel scene provides for them.

## 5. Out of character

- **"They did it because we've been getting good."** (chapters/15_twelve.md, immediately after the
  4am intrusion). Ruth's response is "That's not a reason. It's four in the morning." This is the
  one place Kavi extends charity to an institutional decision instead of scrutinising it — the
  opposite of "correct scepticism applied indiscriminately." Read generously, it's credible growth-
  under-pressure (he's just fought off four armed men and is offering the calmest available
  explanation); read less generously, it's the narrator's own thesis about Halstead ("the school
  only pushes because it respects you") arriving through Kavi's mouth rather than being demonstrated.
  Given how flatly the line lands with no follow-up or pushback beyond Ruth's one retort, this reads
  closer to (b) than (c) — the author's framing in a character's mouth.
- **The bar-paper marking scene** (chapters/19_sixteen.md) is not an inconsistency so much as a
  missing opportunity — see the swap test above. It's "in character" only in the loosest sense
  (Kavi is rigorous), not in the specific sense the brief asks for (a line only he would say).

## 6. The MIT test, specifically

The special instructions ask for the two moments where Kavi's "correct scepticism applied
indiscriminately" is shown getting one call right and one call wrong, using the same instrument.

**The call he gets right** — CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch19 "The Chat":
> Somebody posts a screenshot of a performance review. Strongest hire in a decade.
> ruth: mine says something almost identical
> kavi: thats a technique
> kavi: you tell people theyre exceptional early so they work harder and dont leave. its in the same chapter as the four percent thing
> eli: so its fake
> kavi: its not fake exactly, its just not information

This is real, on the page, and correctly attributed to Kavi.

**The call he's supposed to get wrong — but doesn't.** The synopsis's Kavi profile says: "He is
right about the performance reviews and wrong about MIT, using the same instrument." I checked the
only scene in the reading list where the MIT four-percent claim appears — CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md,
Ch16 "The Applications" — and the "it's marketing" misreading belongs to **Sam, not Kavi**:
> "MIT says four percent," Ruth says, in the common room in October.
> ...
> "Right, and if you were running a school and you wanted people to think it was hard to get into,
> what number would you put on your site." He doesn't look up. "It's a signal. You read the same
> chapter I did." [Sam]
> ...
> "It said four percent." [Chloe]
> "They lie," Sam says. "We established this." ... "Ninety for ninety. That's not four percent,
> that's a hundred percent, so either they're lying or the number means something else." He shrugs.
> "It's marketing. It's the same thing the watch companies do." [Sam]

Kavi has **no lines in this scene at all** — he's mentioned once, in passing, as having gotten
into MIT, Caltech and Chicago, and says nothing about the odds.

Kavi's own line even references "the same chapter" as the four-percent lesson ("its in the same
chapter as the four percent thing," v2 Ch19), which confirms the *idea* is shared classroom
material — but the specific misapplication of that idea, the moment where the instrument
misfires, is dramatized through Sam, not Kavi. As written, the text does **not** let a reader see
one instrument produce two results in one person. It reads as two different characters, one lucky
and one not, which is a different and much weaker effect than the one the character notes describe.
This is worth flagging to the author directly, since it undercuts the single most interesting claim
made about this character in their own profile.

## 8. The name

**Verdict: the name does essentially no work, and the text goes out of its way to avoid
answering the one question that would let it.**

Three separate pieces of evidence, strongest first:

1. **He is the only member of the core four never given a surname**, and the text specifically
   sets up and then withholds it. chapters/10_april.md gives Ruth's surname in dialogue one page
   before Kavi arrives ("'What's your last name, spelled out?' 'Aymar. A, Y, M, A, R.'"), which
   Chloe writes down and which recurs later (Voss calls her "Aymar" in chapters/17_fourteen.md).
   The identical beat is then set up for Kavi — "'What's your last name,' Chloe says." — and the
   chapter cuts to the next scene without ever supplying the answer. In roughly 21 chapters he is
   never once referred to by a surname, initial, or patronymic. This isn't restraint; it's a
   promise made and not kept, in the same chapter, for the character it would matter most for.
2. **His family gets a single scene, with a dead-end detail.** chapters/06_the_list.md (outside the
   assigned reading list, but the only chapter that answers this question): "My parents already
   asked... They liked the food... They liked a lot of it. The food was one thing... My dad talked
   to Mr. Baptiste for an hour." *What* food, and what his father and the math teacher discussed
   for an hour, are never specified anywhere else in the manuscript. This is exactly the kind of
   detail a coin-flip name would produce — a gesture toward specificity (food, a parent, a long
   conversation) that never cashes out into anything culturally legible. Beyond this one scene, his
   mother appears exactly once, at graduation, and even then only in report: Meg tells Chloe
   "Kavi's mother said the same thing" (v2 Ch18) — the reader never hears Kavi's mother's own words
   directly, only Meg's paraphrase of them. There is no father beyond the one unspecified hour with
   Mr. Baptiste. No siblings. No home city, no language spoken at home, no holiday, no religious or
   dietary reference, ever.
3. **No physical description ties him to any background.** I searched the full manuscript for any
   physical description of Kavi (hair, skin, build) and found none. His electives are electronics
   and cryptography/networks (chapters/11_eight.md, chapters/16_thirteen.md) — nothing steers him
   toward Hindi, Sanskrit, or any other marker that would connect to the name's most obvious real-
   world association. (Chloe, notably, is the one who studies Hindi, in chapters/18_fifteen.md —
   one line, unconnected to Kavi, and never remarked on by either of them.)

Compare this with Ruth, whose surname is established, reused, and even becomes a running joke
(other students addressing her by it during archery); or with the specificity Chloe's family gets
(a bachelor's-in-accounting mother, a father whose department circulates papers, a named
grandmother scene). Kavi has none of that scaffolding. This is the character where "some names
were coin flips" is most visible in the text itself, not just in principle: the name carries a
strong cultural association that the manuscript sets up exactly one hook for (the family scene in
ch06) and then drops completely, while simultaneously declining — twice, in explicit,
attention-drawing ways — to give him the ordinary identifying detail (a surname) every other core
character receives without a second thought.

## 9. Fix list (highest value first)

1. **Fix the MIT/performance-review split.** The character notes claim Kavi is "right about the
   performance reviews and wrong about MIT, using the same instrument" — but the actual "it's
   marketing" misreading in CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md Ch16 belongs to Sam, and Kavi has no lines in that
   scene. Either give Kavi a line in the Applications chapter where his scepticism about
   institutional numbers gets something wrong (ideally about a number he was confident in), or stop
   describing this as his defining trait, because as written it isn't demonstrated in one person.
   This is the single most valuable fix because it's the trait the author's own notes lead with.

2. **Answer "What's your last name."** chapters/10_april.md asks the question of Kavi in the exact
