graduation, because the work is the only thing he's ever wanted.

## Voice in one paragraph

He opens with the conclusion, never a lead-in. A line is either three words and done, or one
unbroken technical run that keeps going until the explanation is finished — nothing in between.
He disagrees by attacking the mechanism, never the person, and gives no sign he's noticed a
correction can sting. He states outcomes as settled fact: no hedges, almost no questions. When
something he did would read as impressive, he names the actual cost instead of accepting the
compliment. He never produces a simile, a metaphor, or any invented comparison — not once, in any
chapter. Most characteristic line: "It's four seconds of compute. It's not even interesting."
(chapters/15_twelve.md:161) — praise arriving, and he defuses it by naming the number instead of
taking the compliment.

## Signature — theirs alone

Pre-empts praise by naming how easy something actually was, instead of accepting the compliment.

- **Real:** "You just do a few billion codes and see which one gives you something that isn't noise. It's four seconds of compute. It's not even interesting." (chapters/15_twelve.md:161) — said before anyone has had the chance to call it impressive.
- **[PROPOSED]:** A teacher or peer tells him a piece of work was excellent; he answers with the actual time or resource it cost him instead of "thank you" — e.g. "Twenty minutes. It's not the hard part." Use only where the scene needs him praised and deflecting; do not repeat the exact wording above, or it becomes a catchphrase rather than a trait.

## Would they say this?

- YES if the line is three words or fewer, stating a fact with no softener — "Nope." / "The lava, there's a word for it."
- YES if the line is one unbroken run that keeps going until the explanation is finished, not until the listener reacts — compare the volcano speech in full (chapters/04_pluto.md:100).
- NO if the line sits at ordinary conversational length — one or two medium sentences, six to fourteen words, with nothing else marking it. That middle length is the one range his voice may never occupy.
- YES if a disagreement attacks the claim or the mechanism directly ("That's not how it works though," "The box always says AES-256") with no reference to the other person's feelings about being wrong.
- NO if a disagreement addresses the other person's ego, comfort, or reaction ("don't take this the wrong way," "I know that's annoying") — he corrects the fact and doesn't register the social cost.
- YES if, offered praise or asked to confirm something was hard, the line names the real mechanism, time, or cost instead of accepting the premise that it was impressive.
- NO if the line accepts a compliment outright, says thank you, or shows visible pride or pleasure at being told he did well.
- NO if the line contains a simile, a metaphor, or any invented comparison ("it's like," "it's basically"). He gets none, ever — this is the fastest test for a line that isn't his.
- NO if the line hedges with "I think," "maybe," "probably," "I guess," or similar. He states even his inferences as settled fact.
- NO if the line is a question posed to open discussion or invite opinion from the room. When he does ask something, it names one specific unresolved technical fact, not a general "what does everyone think."

## Are they acting like themselves?

Under pressure he's already working the problem before anyone has asked him to — first on
comms during the 4am intrusion, already running the crack before the others reach the corridor
(chapters/15_twelve.md). He occupies a room by handling the actual object in it — a thermometer,
a piece of equipment, a keyboard — rather than participating in the group's social back-and-forth.
Physical tags that recur: arms folded in a doorway, chin on his forearms, back against a
radiator, a one-shoulder shrug. One genuine break in the pattern is worth keeping: in
chapters/19_sixteen.md, after saying "I got it by nine" to a friend who failed the bar, "his hand
comes up over his mouth a half second behind the words" — the only moment in the corpus where he
visibly catches himself having landed badly. That's earned; it should stay rare.

**Failure modes — the three easiest ways to get him wrong:**
1. Writing him visibly proud or gloating over success. His signature is the opposite: naming the
   cost, not celebrating the win.
2. Giving him a simile or metaphor to make a line sound smart. He is not permitted one, ever.
3. Having him soften a correction to spare someone's feelings, or notice that a correction stung.
   He doesn't register the social cost of being right — that's the trait, not a bug to fix.

## Known problems

1. **The "never stops talking" setup doesn't hold across the book.** chapters/04_pluto.md:96
   establishes him silent for six days; the entire payoff is the volcano exchange ending "'How
   thick it is,' Kavi says, and keeps going. Sam tries twice more to get back into it and doesn't
   manage it either time." (chapters/04_pluto.md:106–107). Outside that chapter and
   chapters/15_twelve.md, he averages under 1.5 lines a chapter, and chapters/13_ten_pages.md gives
   him zero despite his presence being referenced twice. Note: the manuscript itself never uses the
   words "never stops" — that framing does not appear on the page anywhere in the reading. **Fix:**
   either give him more presence in ordinary ensemble scenes across the book, or drop "talkative" as
   a trait and lean into the one the text actually supports — silent except when a mechanism is on
   the table.
2. **No surname, anywhere.** chapters/10_april.md:19 gives another character's surname in full
   ("Aymar. A, Y, M, A, R.") twelve lines before the identical question is put to Kavi
   (chapters/10_april.md:31, "'What's your last name,' Chloe says.") and never answered. **Fix:**
   answer it in that scene, the same way the other answer lands immediately.
3. **The name currently does no work.** No food, language, holiday, or family detail survives
   past the single scene in chapters/06_the_list.md, and that scene's two concrete hooks (which
   food, what the hour-long conversation was about) are both left blank. His mother's only line is
   secondhand. **Fix:** see Facts above — either specify the existing scene or change the name; do
   not invent detail to paper over the gap.
4. **Collision:** CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md:465 and :468 give him and one other character the identical
   six words back to back, "thats a person getting older," with no distinguishing phrasing between
   the two deliveries. **Fix:** reword one of the two lines so it can't be swapped.

## Do not confuse with

He currently blurs into two other characters, in different scenes.

- **Ruth.** She corrects and uses the other person's
  name while doing it — "It is not better, Sam." Kavi corrects without naming anyone and without
  registering that it landed on someone; he states the mechanism and stops. If a correction uses
  the addressee's name as part of the jab, it isn't his.
- **Eli.** His move is to reframe the terms of
  the question before answering — "That's not X, that's Y" — then repeat the assertion minus the
  contraction. Kavi never reframes the premise; he explains the mechanism underneath it, in the
  clipped-or-run-on shape above. If a line's job is to relabel what's being asked, it isn't his; if
  its job is to explain how the thing actually works, it is.
