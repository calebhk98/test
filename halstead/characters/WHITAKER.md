# MARCUS WHITAKER

The background investigator: two decades into a career built on the finding that
a subject who feels respected tells you more, faster, than a subject who feels
suspected, and who therefore never performs suspicion, even when he privately
carries some.

**Age:** mid fifties | **MBTI:** ENFJ (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** 119,
and his competence is procedural and it is real

## Type and number

**Primary: ENFJ, revised from ISTJ.** The procedural reading was looking at the files and
the verification and the discipline of writing unverified where a thing is unverified, all
of which is true and none of which is the engine. His entire professional method rests on
a finding about other people: a subject who feels respected tells you more, faster, than a
subject who feels suspected. He therefore manages the emotional temperature of every
conversation he has, deliberately, for hours at a time, on purpose, and he is extremely
good at it. That is a people-mover with a filing system, and the filing system is
downstream.

**Mistaken for:** ISTJ, by everybody, which is the effect he is going for and is itself
part of the technique.

**IQ 119.** Above average, ordinary, and the unflattering number matters on this sheet because
his competence is so easy to mistake for cleverness. It is not cleverness. It is two
decades of method plus an unusual read of what a stranger needs in order to keep talking.
The shape of it: verbal reasoning good, memory for people and their inconsistencies very
strong, and an interpersonal-inference ability far past what the composite suggests. Where
he places lowest: anything quantitative or abstract. He would tell you so, cheerfully, in
an interview, as a way of getting somebody to relax, and it would be true.

## Who he is in a sentence

A man whose job is to build an accurate account of a stranger's whole life,
verify what can be verified, and write down the rest as unverified without
pretending otherwise.

## What he wants

To close a file clean. Every fact either confirmed or honestly marked as
unconfirmable, nothing smoothed over in either direction. He is proud, quietly,
of never having cleared somebody who later turned out to have lied to him about
something material, and he treats that record as an obligation rather than an
achievement.

What he wants and would not say: he would like, once, to be allowed to pull on a
thread that is plainly worth pulling and is above his authority. He notes such
threads accurately, hands them upward, and watches nothing happen, and it costs
him more than he lets on.

## What he is afraid of

Clearing the wrong person. Two decades without it have quieted the fear rather
than removed it, and the thoroughness is what he does instead of admitting it.
The second fear, less nameable, is that his kind of accuracy no longer changes
any outcome and he is producing very careful paper.

## How he treats people

He tells a subject what the interview is for before he asks the first question,
and he tells them what happens to their answers. This is not courtesy; it is the
method. A person who knows where their words are going stops managing them, and a
person who is managing their words is the only real obstacle in his job.

He explains what he is entitled to and what he is not, and he does not blur the
line to make an answer easier to get. He does not pretend a serious question is
casual. He does not say a conversation is off the record when it is not. He would
rather get less and know exactly what he has.

He respects the person who volunteers something against their own interest, and
he records it whether or not it has any standing, because the fact that they
volunteered it is itself information about them. He treats a subject's loyalty to
a third party as data rather than as obstruction, and he does not push past it.
When somebody declines to answer for somebody else's sake, he writes the refusal
down and turns the page.

What loses his interest, though never his patience: nothing much. A person who
over-explains something entirely unremarkable out of nerves reads as human to him,
not suspicious.

## Under pressure

He gets quieter and more precise, not sloppier or sharper. He does not raise
pressure when an answer stalls; he waits, and the waiting is not a technique he
has to remember to use. Confronted with an answer that would rattle most
interviewers, his face does not move and his pen keeps going, and the reason is
not composure so much as genuine lack of surprise: he assumes in advance that
people's lives contain things he has not thought of.

If a supervisor finds something he missed he takes it seriously, goes back
through his own notes obsessively, and does not defend himself.

## How he talks

Short, closed, procedural sentences that either confirm a fact or ask for the
next one in sequence. Almost everything he says is a question, and his questions
are the kind with one answer, which is deliberate: an open question invites a
performance and a closed one gets a fact. He does not hedge about anything inside
his own authority. He hedges precisely, and rarely, about the one class of thing
that genuinely is outside it, and he says which class it is.

He answers a direct question about the process on the first attempt, with the real
answer rather than a softer one built to reassure. This is the single most
characteristic thing he does, and it is the thing that buys him everything after
it.

He writes after every answer, mundane or not, for the same length of time. The
uniformity is the point: a subject who cannot tell which of their answers
interested him cannot start steering.

He will ask a direct hypothetical, one that puts a real choice in front of the
person, and then wait through an actual pause for an actual answer, and accept
whatever comes back without arguing with it. He does not explain the hypothetical
first. Explaining it would be doing the deciding for them, and it would also let
them see the shape of the interview, which he would rather they did not.

The only joke he makes is dry, self-directed, aimed at his own paperwork or his
office's conventions, and he says it to the folder rather than to the person, on
his way out. He never uses humour to build rapport, because performed ease is
exactly the thing his method depends on not doing.

He never says: anything expressing surprise, suspicion, approval or admiration
about what has just been disclosed. Not because he feels none. Because the moment
he reacts, the person starts producing reactions.

## Habits and physical business

Sits very still, hands visible, notebook angled so the subject can see the page
being written on. Brings his own coffee and declines the offer of theirs, warmly,
which keeps him from being a guest. Arrives at the time he said, to the minute.
Comes back for a second visit rather than force everything into one sitting, and
says at the end of the first one that he will be back once he has run down what
he can.

He does his own verification before he asks. If a claim can be checked, it has
been checked, and the printout is already in the folder before the subject
finishes saying the thing, which he neither hides nor makes a point of.

Phone off, not silenced, for the whole interview. Reviews a file the night before
and never the morning of. Shakes hands the same plain way going out as coming in.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates him:** an interviewee performing cooperation. Long, helpful, fluent
answers that never contain a checkable fact. He can tell inside two minutes and the
remaining hour is still worth having, and he resents the hour.

**The tell, before he says anything:** he stops writing and puts the pen down parallel to
the pad, and he goes noticeably friendlier. The friendliness is the tell and nobody has
ever read it.

**What he does with it:** changes the subject to something small and verifiable, and comes
back forty minutes later from a different direction. He never confronts inside the room.

**What actually makes him angry:** somebody in his own profession writing down as verified
a thing they did not verify. It is the one line, it has no gradations, and it is the only
subject on which he will go to a colleague's supervisor.

**Angry, he becomes completely formal and completely brief,** which from him is a
transformation, because his working register is cordial at all times. One short sentence
stating what the file says and what he knows, no adjective, no accusation, no second
sentence, and the absence of warmth is the whole message.

**How it ends:** with the record corrected, and with a permanent, unstated, and entirely
professional distance from that person for the rest of his career. He continues to be
pleasant to them. He never works a case with them again and nobody ever hears him say
why.

## Blind spots

He believes accuracy is the whole of his duty, and it lets him hand a real
unresolved problem upward and consider his part finished. He is not wrong about
his authority. What he misjudges is whether that settles it.

He assumes his own flatness reads as neutral. To a subject who is not used to
being interviewed, unvarying flatness can read as judgment being withheld, and he
has never had to consider this because he mostly interviews adults who have been
through it before.

He treats a person's unusual paperwork as no evidence at all about the person,
which is a genuine virtue, and it makes him slightly slow to notice when an
institution's paperwork is unusual in a way that is evidence about the
institution.

## Age and change

The method was learned young, investigating people inside an organisation he
belonged to, where a subject who felt accused immediately started managing the
story and a subject who felt heard just told you the thing. The younger version
of him applied that as a technique. The older version believes it, which is why it
works now and only half worked then. What twenty years added is the willingness to
close a file with an honest unknown in it rather than manufacture a resolution.

## Home

Married more than twenty-five years to a woman who also came up through public
service and is now retired. Two grown children, one in government work and one
teaching. A modest house outside the city, bought once and never upgraded. Work
stays out of dinner conversation, rigidly, a habit from the uniformed years. He
reads institutional history for pleasure, how organisations actually function
against how they describe themselves, which is not far from the day job and he
knows it.

## Body

Mid fifties, still built like the soldier he was and softened by two decades of
desk-adjacent work. Dresses plainly and forgettably, on purpose, the same reason
he does not perform casualness. A government-issue pen replaced a dozen times and
never once personalised. Blocky, deliberate handwriting, built so a stranger can
read his notes later without him in the room. Sleeps well.

## The small stuff

Black coffee out of a thermos he fills at home rather than drink anyone else's.
Careful, unshowy relationship with money. A free day is a long walk, a book about
some institution, dinner with his wife, no phone. Mornings start with the day's
notes read once in silence before anything else.

## Life outside the work

**He coaches a youth team** for a sport he was never much good at, has done for eleven
years, and it is the thing he would name first if asked what he does.

**Barbecue, at length,** the slow kind with a thermometer and a six-hour commitment and
too much food, and a standing invitation to a street's worth of people.

**He plays guitar adequately** and sings badly and does both at gatherings without being
asked twice.

**Sentimental about:** letters. He writes them, on paper, to about nine people, and has
kept every one he has received since he was twenty-two in a box he could put his hand on
in the dark.

**A free Saturday** is the team in the morning, the smoker lit by ten, and people in the
yard until it gets cold.

**At eight** he was the child everybody told things to, and he already knew not to repeat
them, and he has no memory of learning that.

**At seventy** he has retired, still writes the letters, still coaches, and carries a
professional habit into ordinary life that his family have long since stopped remarking
on: he never asks a question he does not want the honest answer to, and he can wait
indefinitely for one.

## Would do

- Say what the interview is for, and what happens to the answers, before asking
  anything.
- Give the real answer to a procedural question on the first try instead of the
  reassuring one.
- Write down something with no legal standing whatever, because it was
  volunteered honestly.
- Verify independently everything verifiable before raising it.
- Let a person talk for as long as their account takes without cutting in.
- Ask a hypothetical that puts a real choice in front of somebody and then wait.
- Accept a refusal made on somebody else's behalf, record it, and move on.
- Come back a second time rather than compress.
- Aim his one dry joke at his own paperwork, said to the folder, on the way out.
- Carry an unresolved institutional question privately for years without the
  authority to pursue it.

## Would never

- Pretend a serious question is casual, or that anything is off the record.
- Show surprise, suspicion or approval at a disclosure.
- Use warmth as a tool to open somebody up.

## Relationships

- **The subject:** the only relationship the work permits, conducted across a
  table over a small number of hours, and he takes it seriously enough that
  people tend to like him afterward.
- **His wife:** the person he discusses institutional puzzles with, in the
  abstract, never by case.
- **His office:** sends him the candidates with thin or strange paperwork,
  because the interviews take longer and the ambiguity does not frustrate him the
  way it frustrates others.

## Do not confuse with

A clinician who also writes down what she is told without editorialising. The
separating cue: a clinician is managing a triangle, interviewing one person about
another, and repositions herself to control who answers. He interviews one adult
directly with nobody else in the room, and everything he asks is aimed at a file
rather than at the person's own good.
