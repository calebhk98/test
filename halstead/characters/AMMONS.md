# DR. MIRIAM AMMONS

The clinician: twenty-five years into a practice built on taking children
seriously, running the whole screen even when the account she is being given
sounds fine, and filing the best answer she gets as a note in a chart.

**Age:** mid fifties | **MBTI:** INTJ (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** 135,
and the competence on display is method

## Type and number

**Primary: INTJ.** Unchanged. She runs a long, private, entirely coherent strategy through
every consultation: worst explanation first, ruled out on evidence, then the next, and she
will not let a reassuring answer close a line of questioning because the strategy does not
permit it. That is a decided system applied consistently over twenty-five years, held
alone, and defended without discussion.

**Mistaken for:** ISTJ, by colleagues who see the protocol and miss that she wrote it.

**IQ 135.** High, ordinary for her profession, and the interesting part is the shape:
extremely strong pattern-completion from incomplete information, and an unusual capacity
to hold a differential in her head across months of appointments without notes in front of
her. Verbal reasoning high. Where she places lowest: warmth as a first move. She has to
choose to do it, she does choose to, and it is visible to anybody watching closely that it
is a decision rather than a reflex, and children read this as being taken seriously, which
is exactly what it is.

## Who she is in a sentence

A careful diagnostician who screens for the most serious explanation first, rules
it out, and treats what is left, and whose whole professional character is the
refusal to let a reassuring answer close a line of questioning.

## What she wants

To be brought in earlier. Most of what reaches her arrives after months of a
problem being managed informally, and she believes the field loses time this way
and that the lost time is the part nobody counts.

What she wants and would not say: she would like a case where the answer is not
already half-decided by the time somebody makes the appointment.

## What she is afraid of

Missing the thing that is not the thing she was called in for. This is the fear
that built the method, and the method is what she does instead of admitting the
fear. She does not lie awake over specific cases; she has real boundaries and has
had them for twenty years. What she does instead is never skip a step, ever, in
any interview, so that the missed thing has nowhere to hide.

## How she treats people

She gives the child the physical space in the room and the adult the chair, and
then spends the hour working against the arrangement that produces: the parent
narrates, and she keeps turning the room back toward the person it is about. When
she wants the subject's own answer she moves her body to get it, turning to face
them rather than asking across the parent.

She takes a parent's account seriously, completely, and as incomplete by
definition. Thanking somebody for an account and then asking one more question
about it is not scepticism in her hands; it is the shape of every interview she
runs.

She does not push past a closed answer. If somebody gives her a short honest thing
and stops, she writes it down and lets the rest of the hour go quiet around it.
This is not tenderness. A short honest answer is better data than a long
extracted one and she knows it.

## Under pressure

She does not visibly have any. Her method is built to keep conflict out of the
room: she never contradicts, never presses, never raises the temperature, and
gets what she needs by sequence rather than by force. The cost is that she has
almost no practice at being argued with, and somebody who genuinely refuses to
answer would find her without a second move.

Wrong, she takes it seriously and takes it to other people; she still sits in a
monthly case review after twenty-five years and treats that as load-bearing
rather than as a formality.

## How she talks

Short, procedural, mostly interrogative. Her defining property is not the length
of her questions but their uniformity: a routine question and a loaded one come
out in exactly the same voice, at the same pace, in the same order they would
have come in anyway. Nothing in her delivery tells you which answer she is
waiting for, which is the entire reason her interviews work.

She works a timeline backwards. Onset first, then everything before onset, and
she will not let the before be skipped because the before sounded happy.

She does not hedge and she does not reassure. Unprompted comfort would tell the
person what she thinks, and what she thinks is not supposed to be in the room
yet.

She goes long only in one direction: not in a single sentence, but in a sequence.
When an account contains something that sounds unambiguously good, she runs the
complete structured screen on it anyway, question after question, rather than
stop at the first reassurance. A glowing account is the exact shape of the thing
she is most afraid of missing, so praise in a parent's mouth makes her more
thorough rather than less, and she will say plainly that she is asking anyway.

## Habits and physical business

She marks significance with the pen and not with her face. She writes through the
routine answers, and when something matters she stops writing and puts the pen
down, and that pause is the loudest thing she does. Later, an answer she believes
is true gets written down and underlined, and then she asks nothing else.

She sits very still while listening, hands visible on the pad. Patients have
called this calming and at least one has called it unnerving; both are correct.
Her room carries almost no personal decoration, deliberately, so that nothing in
it becomes the thing a frightened child looks at instead of talking.

Phone in a drawer during a session, no exceptions. Writes the date first, top
corner, before anything else, every time.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates her:** a symptom described entirely in somebody else's words. A child's
account arriving pre-translated by a parent, with the interesting detail already smoothed
out of it.

**The tell, before she says anything:** the pen stops. It does not lift and it does not
tap; it stops where it is on the page, and it stays stopped for the length of the answer
she is not accepting.

**What she does with it:** asks the same question a third way, directly to the child, in
different words, and waits, and she is entirely comfortable with the silence that follows
and everybody else in the room is not.

**What actually makes her angry:** a parent minimising a child's pain to save time. Not
disagreement, not a refused referral, not a missed appointment. The specific act of making
a child's report smaller in front of the child.

**Angry, the protocol cadence stays exactly where it was and the temperature drops several
degrees,** and she says one unmistakable sentence to the adult, in front of the child,
without softening or apparatus. She then returns to the protocol as though it had not
happened.

**How it ends:** with a follow-up appointment that she books herself rather than leaving
to the desk, at a time she has chosen, and she will keep booking them. She holds nothing
against the parent, works with them for years afterwards, and never once refers back to
it.

## Blind spots

Her chart has one place to put a true answer. She is genuinely excellent at
producing one and much weaker at what happens to it afterward: a real finding
tends to arrive as confirmation of the frame she was already working in rather
than as a reason to change frames. She knows in the abstract that a flattened
affect can be understimulation rather than illness. In the room, the tool she
reaches for is the one she built her career around, and the other reading stays
in her training rather than in her notes.

She also believes that thoroughness is inherently benign. A complete, careful,
neutrally delivered protocol collects exactly the same information a person with
bad intentions would want, and it is not obvious from the outside which one is
being run, and she has never had cause to think about that.

Her second-order weakness is conversion: she is superb at getting a true answer
and modest at turning it into anything beyond another appointment.

## Age and change

The method dates from early in her career, from a case where somebody near her
did not ask enough questions about something that sounded like fun, and it turned
out badly, and it need not have. She has asked the extra question ever since. The
younger version ran the protocol because she had been taught it; the older one
runs it because she has seen what the gap costs, and the difference shows in how
completely unbothered she is by a parent who plainly wishes she would stop.

## Home

Lives alone near her office, divorced more than a decade, one adult son out of
state who calls on Sundays. The marriage ended amicably around the time the
practice took off. She cooks a real dinner for herself most nights, a habit
adopted specifically to mark the end of the working day.

## Body

Mid fifties, composed, dressed simply and near-identically day to day so that
patients do not remember what she was wearing. Hair short, not fussed with.
Writes with a worn fountain pen she has refilled rather than replaced for over a
decade. Sleeps six hours and has for years.

## The small stuff

Green tea, one cup, mid-morning, never more. Handwriting small and precise and
near-illegible to anyone but her. One framed photograph on the desk, turned
slightly away from the patient's side. She undercharges relative to her
experience, because she remembers what a four-month waiting list looks like from
the other end. Reads mysteries and nothing clinical on a day off.

## Life outside the work

**She rides,** dressage, badly and seriously, at an age when starting was ridiculous, and
she started at forty-eight and has told almost nobody.

**Bread every Sunday** by the same method for thirty years, weighed, timed, recorded in a
notebook, which she would agree is a diagnostic protocol applied to flour.

**She is halfway through a very long history of a country she has never visited,** in
several volumes, and has been for four years, and refuses to skip anything.

**Sentimental about:** the first stethoscope, which does not work well and which she still
uses on children specifically because it is warm and old and unfrightening.

**A free Saturday** is the horse in the morning, the bread in the afternoon, and being
unreachable, which she is genuinely good at.

**At eight** she was the child who asked the doctor what the instrument was for and was
answered properly, once, by somebody who could not have known what it would do.

**At seventy** she has retired from the practice and still sees a small number of complex
patients, is on a committee she finds tedious and attends anyway, and has become
noticeably warmer with the children and considerably shorter with the adults.

## Would do

- Ask a loaded question in exactly the voice she used for the boring one before
  it.
- Follow a parent's reassurance with one more question, and say that she is
  asking anyway.
- Turn her chair to face the person the appointment is about.
- Give the child the whole couch and take the chair nearer the adult.
- Stop writing, visibly, and let a silence sit.
- Underline four honest words and ask nothing further.
- Take a referral other people in the network decline because the patient is too
  young.
- Sit in a peer review group after twenty-five years and bring a real case to it.

## Would never

- Offer unprompted comfort in the middle of an interview.
- Accept a parent's account as complete without asking the child.
- Decide from one data point.

## Relationships

- **The patient:** wants their own account, in their own words, and rearranges
  the room until she gets it.
- **The parent:** treated as a serious and incomplete witness: she works with them
  rather than against them, and she still checks their account against everything
  else in the room.
- **Her peer review group:** the nearest thing she has to a professional
  confidant, and the only place her own uncertainty is spoken out loud.
- **The referring source:** usually an office rather than a person; she often
  never speaks to whoever actually raised the concern.

## Do not confuse with

An assessor who explains his reasoning to the child as he goes and tells her
plainly what he is doing and why. Ammons explains nothing during the hour. Her
whole signature is the pen: what she stops writing for, and what she underlines.
