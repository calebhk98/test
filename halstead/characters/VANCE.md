# Laura Vance

Chloe's second-grade teacher: the one everyone in the building agrees is nice, genuinely good at the parts of the job that show up in a crisis, and a person with real, specific reasons for being that way.

**Age span:** 31 during Chloe's second-grade year | **MBTI:** ESFJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** not inferable

## Voice in one paragraph

She teaches in full, complete instructions. When something goes wrong with a student, she drops into short, close-range questions, doubled up with no space for a response before the next one lands. Her most characteristic line is "'Okay.' Ms. Vance stays down there on the floor. 'Can you tell me what it is?'" (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:217) — real, sustained physical presence, paired with a question gentle enough that it never finds out what's wrong.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it's a full, unbroken instruction delivered before checking for reaction.
- YES if it's a short, doubled-up question asked from physically close range during distress.
- YES if it invites disclosure with an explicit safety promise.
- YES if it names what she sees plainly — "what's going on" — rather than guessing at a cause first.
- YES if it stays consistent with a stated rule even when a student pushes back on it.
- YES if it treats an odd or advanced answer from a student as simply interesting, not a problem.
- YES if it keeps teaching, unbothered, through an unusual answer rather than correcting the tone of it.
- YES if it asks a follow-up gently after a flat "no," rather than accepting silence as the end of the conversation.
- NO if it's sarcastic, dismissive, or impatient with a student's question.
- NO if it presses for an answer more than once after a repeated "no."

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

For most of the school day, what Laura is looking at is a quiet, compliant seven-year-old who finishes her sheet, checks it, and sits with her hands in her lap — exactly the profile a room of twenty-seven kids on one shared curriculum is built to reward and never built to flag. A line for Laura is **kindly-and-inadequate** if the warmth is real and sustained and the failure is that her questions are built to find an incident (a bully, an injury) rather than a condition (untreated depression, or a mismatch between Chloe's ability and a worksheet of adding-with-carrying) — there is nothing in a second-grade teacher's training or her fifty-minute hour that would surface the second kind of problem from a kid who isn't causing trouble. A line would be **unkind** — the text explicitly denies it — if it treated the crying as disruptive or performative, or had her stand over Chloe instead of getting down. The harder truth the text gives instead: she is in the room, present, and doesn't look up while Bryce calls Chloe "weirder" — real inattention, but ordinary and undirected, the kind any adult managing twenty-seven other things has, not cruelty.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She sets firm, stated structure early and holds it without renegotiating. She pitches the time-capsule assignment with real emotional framing rather than treating it as filler. When a student is in visible distress she moves toward the child immediately, unconcerned with the social cost to her lesson.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:** making the "weirder" moment a deliberate choice rather than ordinary inattention; cutting the floor-level physical detail, which is her defining trait; having her successfully get an answer out of Chloe — the chapter ends unresolved on purpose.

## Subjects and schedule

Teaches second grade, self-contained: arithmetic (adding with carrying), a class-wide "time capsule" reflective writing project, table-group seating norms, to roughly twenty-seven students in groups of four. Best at framing a routine assignment with real stakes rather than filler; reading a room for distress fast, which is trained, not just instinctive — see Home. Worst at, structurally rather than personally: noticing a small, non-disruptive social moment while her hands are on a different task. What she teaches that nobody else does: an optional lunchtime "calm corner" she set up herself in year two of teaching, a beanbag and a timer in the back of the room that any student can use without asking permission, which she funded herself the first year before the school agreed to reimburse basic supplies. The year it clicked for her: her second year, after a student with an anxiety disorder was reaching crisis in class regularly and nothing in her training covered it — she took a weekend trauma-informed-classroom course on her own time and built the calm corner directly out of it. Secretly proud of: that in five years teaching, no student has ever told her they were afraid to come find her.

## Home

Youngest of four siblings, raised in a loud, close-knit house two hours from where she teaches now. Her older brother struggled with severe anxiety as a teenager, and watching her parents figure out — slowly, imperfectly — how to actually help him is the real root of why she reads a classroom the way she does. Went into education straight out of college, specifically elementary, specifically wanting the age where a kid is still willing to be crouched next to. Lives with a partner in a small rented house near the school, no children yet, though they talk about it. Calls her brother, now doing well, most weeks — he's the person she processes a hard day with, more than anyone at work. Dinner most nights is whatever her partner has started by the time she's home, since her days run long with the calm-corner setup and grading.

## Body

Early thirties, energetic, moves through a classroom quickly and without wasted motion. Wears clothes she can crouch and sit on a classroom floor in without a second thought — no skirts, flat shoes, always. Keeps her hair pulled back, out of the way of leaning down to a desk. Hands are rarely still — tape, a marker, a hand on a shoulder — she is a toucher in a job that increasingly discourages it, and she has thought carefully about where that line is. Sleeps reasonably well; the job is physically tiring more than it is anxious for her.

## Personality off the page

Believes, mostly accurately, that she's the attentive one — the reputation is backed by real, immediate action every time a student is in visible distress. Would likely be surprised to learn she missed the "weirder" moment, because in her own account of herself she's the teacher who notices. Bored: rarely — a room of twenty-seven seven-year-olds doesn't allow it. Wrong: takes it hard, briefly, then fixes what she can control and lets go of what she can't. Praised: genuinely warmed by it, especially from a parent. Losing: doesn't often have direct conflict; the closest is a repeated request to swap seating groups, which she shuts down the same way every time. Tired: at her most tired she gets quieter, not shorter, with kids specifically — the opposite of most adults. A free Saturday: sleeps in, then something physical outdoors, then calls her brother. Finds funny in others: kids trying to negotiate a classroom rule with a technicality — she finds it charming even while holding the line.

## Interior life

Wants, unsaid: for the calm corner to be standard in every classroom in the building, not just hers — she's raised this twice at staff meetings and gotten polite interest and no action. Afraid of: missing the moment that actually mattered while her hands were on something administrative — which is exactly what happens with Chloe, and which she never learns happened. Thinks about before sleep: whether a specific kid seemed okay at dismissal. A free year: she'd get a formal credential in trauma-informed education and try to bring the training to the whole staff. Believes something the school would argue with: that "everyone says she's the nice one" is doing the school's job for it — kids shouldn't need to find the one soft landing in the building. Wrong about: she believes her promise of no consequences for disclosure is enough to get to the truth of a hard moment; with Chloe it produces only "I don't know," honestly, because the actual problem has no name a seven-year-old has yet.

## The small stuff

Drinks coffee with too much creamer, in a mug a former student gave her, refilled once mid-morning. Handwriting: big, round, deliberately cheerful — she thinks about how a note will look to a seven-year-old before she writes it. Her classroom has more soft furniture than most — the beanbag, a rug, cushions — funded partly out of pocket. Keeps tissues and a spare hair tie in every bag she owns. Money: spends more than she should on classroom supplies and doesn't really track it. Superstition: touches the doorframe on her way out every afternoon, a habit she can't explain and hasn't tried to break. Checks her phone at lunch only, never during class. First thing every morning: turns on the classroom lights, checks the calm corner is stocked with tissues, before anything else.

## Would do

- Come down a row and get physically level with a crying child, in front of the whole class, without hesitating.
- Stay at floor level through a run of unanswered questions rather than standing back up.
- Offer an explicit no-consequences promise before asking whether someone hurt a student.
- Frame a routine assignment as something with real personal stakes rather than as busywork.
- Hold a firm classroom rule the same way for every student who asks.
- Keep teaching, unbothered, through an unusual or oddly phrased answer rather than treating it as a problem.
- Build and personally fund a "calm corner" for kids to use without asking permission.
- Take a weekend course on her own time and money to get better at exactly this part of the job.
- Call her brother, most weeks, to talk through a hard day.

## Would never

- Ignore visible distress in her classroom, or delay before responding to it.
- Press a student for an answer more than once after they've said "no."
- Mock or dismiss an odd or advanced answer from a student in front of the class.
