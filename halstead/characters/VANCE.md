# Ms. Vance

Chloe's second-grade teacher, and the one everyone in the building agrees is nice — which the text uses to show that "nice" and "attentive" are not the same thing.

**Age span:** adult, one school year (Chloe age 7, second grade) | **MBTI:** ESFJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** not inferable — no cognitive content in her lines, only classroom management and comfort-giving
**Appears in:** chapters/07_the_same_room.md (all her scenes) and chapters/08_the_asking.md (referenced only — she calls Chloe's house, reported in indirect speech, no quoted line of hers in that chapter). Not present anywhere else.
**Speaks:** 10 quoted lines, all in chapters/07_the_same_room.md, split across two scenes: the time-capsule lesson (4 lines) and the crying-at-her-desk scene (6 lines). The brief's working estimate of "~2 lines" undercounts what's on the page — see Known problems.

## Voice in one paragraph

She teaches in full, complete instructions — the time-capsule lesson runs three unbroken statements about what the box is, when it opens, and who it's really for, delivered before any student reacts. When something goes wrong with a student, she drops immediately into short, close-range questions, repeated until she gets an answer: "Are you hurt anywhere? Is something hurting?" (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:209), one right after the other, no space between them for a response before the next one lands. She is the only adult in this batch who is shown physically closing distance with Chloe on her own initiative — crouching, getting down to floor level, putting a hand flat on her back and leaving it there. Her most characteristic line is "'Okay.' Ms. Vance stays down there on the floor. 'Can you tell me what it is?'" (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:217) — real, sustained physical presence, paired with a question gentle enough that it never actually finds out what's wrong, because the chapter ends before Chloe can answer it.

## Signature — theirs alone

Her distinguishing move is physical: she is the only adult in this group of eight who lowers herself to Chloe's level and stays there through an entire exchange, rather than delivering a line and leaving.

- "Ms. Vance comes down the row and crouches all the way down beside the desk, in front of everybody, and puts a hand flat on Chloe's back and leaves it there." (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:203)
- "'Okay.' Ms. Vance stays down there on the floor." (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:217) — she doesn't just crouch and ask; she remains at floor level through a run of unanswered questions.

**Proposal** [inferred]: if she gets more scenes, this — get down to the child's physical level and stay there through the silence, rather than standing over her — is the trait that should recur. It's the one thing in her characterization that isn't shared with any other adult in this batch.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it's a full, unbroken instruction delivered before checking for reaction — her teaching voice doesn't pause for questions mid-explanation.
- NO if it's sarcastic, dismissive, or impatient with a student's question — the one flat "no" she delivers (to swapping groups) is stated once and not repeated or elaborated, not snapped.
- YES if it's a short, doubled-up question asked from physically close range during a moment of distress ("Are you hurt anywhere? Is something hurting?").
- NO if she asks a distress question and then walks away before getting an answer — every instance shown has her staying in place, at floor level, through the silence.
- YES if it invites disclosure with an explicit safety promise ("You can tell me if somebody said something and nothing happens to you for telling me," chapters/07_the_same_room.md:213).
- NO if it presses for an answer more than once after a flat "no" — she asks "is something hurting," gets "no," and moves to a different question rather than repeating the same one.
- NO if it uses a metaphor or invented image — entirely literal.
- NO if she is present in the room, watching, during a peer conflict aimed at Chloe — chapters/07_the_same_room.md:143 places her "over at the cabinet with the tape" and explicit that she "doesn't look up for any of it" during the exact four seconds Bryce Dolan tells Chloe "you're weirder." A rewrite that has her intervene in that moment would contradict the text as written.

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

A line for Vance is **kindly-and-inadequate** if the warmth is real and sustained — she crouches, she stays down at floor level, she offers an explicit no-consequences promise for disclosure — and the failure is that her questions are built to find an *incident* (a bully, an injury) rather than a *condition* (untreated depression, or a mismatch between Chloe's ability and her sheet of adding-with-carrying). "Can you tell me what it is?" is asked with total sincerity and gets "I don't know" back, honestly, because Chloe genuinely doesn't have the word for what's wrong — and the scene ends there. Vance's failure is a failure of the available vocabulary, not of effort or attention in that specific moment.

A line would be **unkind** — and the text explicitly denies it — if it treated the crying as disruptive, performative, or attention-seeking, or if she stood over Chloe instead of getting down to her level, or if she left the moment unresolved out of impatience rather than because the scene simply ends there. The text's harder indictment of her isn't in that scene at all — it's the earlier one where she is in the room, physically present, and doesn't look up while Bryce calls Chloe "weirder." That is real inattention, but it is inattention to an ordinary classroom moment that gave no other adult in the book any reason to flag it either — not cruelty, and not aimed at Chloe.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She sets firm, stated structure early (seating groups until Thanksgiving, no swapping, "helping means you talk about it with them, it does not mean you do it and let them copy it off you," chapters/07_the_same_room.md:105) and holds it without renegotiating once it's set. She teaches with genuine care for the lesson's emotional framing — the time-capsule assignment is explicitly pitched as a message to a future self, not busywork, and she gives real thought to how she introduces it. When a student is in visible distress, she is shown moving toward the child immediately and without hesitation, in front of the whole class, unconcerned with the social cost of the interruption to her lesson. In the other scene that defines her, she is doing ordinary maintenance (taping foil to a box at the cabinet) and simply isn't looking at the room during an unremarkable-seeming social exchange.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:**
1. Making the "you're weirder" moment a choice — having her deliberately ignore it, or having her notice and decide not to intervene. The text is specific that she "doesn't look up for any of it"; she is occupied, not withholding.
2. Cutting the floor-level physical detail. The crouch and the hand on the back are her defining trait; a version of her that responds to Chloe's crying only verbally, from standing, loses the one thing that separates her from every other adult in this batch.
3. Having her successfully get an answer out of Chloe. The chapter ends on "I don't know," repeated, with Chloe unable to stop. Do not resolve this scene for her — the lack of resolution is the point.

## Would do / would never do

**Would do:**
- Come down a row and get physically level with a crying child, in front of the whole class, without hesitating.
- Stay at floor level through a run of unanswered questions rather than standing back up.
- Offer an explicit no-consequences promise before asking whether someone hurt a student.
- Frame a routine assignment as something with real personal stakes rather than as busywork.
- Hold a firm classroom rule (no swapping seat groups) the same way for every student who asks.
- Keep teaching, unbothered, through an unusual or oddly phrased answer from a student rather than treating it as a problem to manage.

**Would never:**
- Ignore visible distress in her classroom, or delay before responding to it.
- Press a student for an answer more than once after they've said "no."
- Make a struggling or crying child feel watched-as-a-spectacle rather than cared for — she gets down to their level instead of standing over them.
- Mock or dismiss an odd or advanced answer from a student in front of the class.
- Blame a student for a classmate's comment about them.
- Leave a distressed child mid-conversation to attend to something administrative.

## Known problems

- **The brief's estimate of "~2 lines" is a significant undercount.** She has 10 quoted lines across two scenes in chapters/07_the_same_room.md — closer in volume to Ammons (7 lines, one chapter) than to Prahl or Whitaker. Any revision plan built on "she barely speaks" should be corrected against the actual page count.
- **No line of hers survives into chapters/08 in quoted form.** The Friday phone call that sets the Ammons referral in motion is entirely reported through Meg's side of the conversation (chapters/08_the_asking.md:3–5); Vance's own words in that call are never given. A rewriter wanting a Vance line in chapters/08 has to invent one.
- **The "weirder" scene and the crying scene are never connected on the page.** Both happen in chapters/07_the_same_room.md, but nothing in the text has Vance draw a line between the incident she missed and the breakdown she responds to weeks or months later — worth flagging to the author as a possible missed beat rather than assuming it's implied.
