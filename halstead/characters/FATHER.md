Scope: chapters/01 through chapters/20, plus CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md and CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md. He appears — speaks or is present on the page — in chapters/01, 03, 06, 09, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, and CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md Ch18 (The First One, non-speaking). He does **not** appear at all in chapters/02, 05, 07, 08, 10, 20, or anywhere in CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md.

**On the name.** SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md and BETA_NOTES.md both label him "Dad (unnamed)." That's not quite accurate as a claim about the text: Meg calls him "Dave" twice in dialogue — chapters/06_the_list.md ("Four hours away, Dave. And free. And they wrote to her.") and chapters/09_february.md ("She said take my birthday, Dave, and then she said it a second time."). The "unnamed" framing describes the narration's choice accurately but overstates what the dialogue does — worth being precise about, because it changes the fix (see Fix List).

## 2. Voice profile

**Sentence shape.** Two registers, cleanly split by context. At home, casual and clipped: "Cool." "Huh." "I know." Under analysis — a document, a proposal, a website — he produces tight, escalating three-part questions: "So how does a four-year-old school get that guy. What does it pay him. With what." / "There isn't a version of this where they've failed to do it. That's why it reads so well. This is four pages of the same trick, isn't it." Both instances end on a short, almost rhetorical final clause. This is his most reliable structural signature and it recurs at least twice (ch3, ch12) in near-identical shape, which argues it's a genuine, stable trait rather than a one-off good scene.

**How he opens a line.** Often with a bare acknowledgment token — "Cool," "Huh," "I know," "Okay" — that undersells whatever's coming, or that is, in fact, the entire response. He is the only tracked character who says "Huh." as a complete turn (at least twice: ch1, ch15).

**Questions vs. statements.** Two very different question modes: analytical (aimed at documents/money — always structured as escalating triples) and curious (aimed at Chloe's friends — always structured as a short, immediate follow-up: "How does it not touch him?" "Why does she do it?"). He almost never asks Meg a genuine question; his lines to her are mostly assertions or her name used as a stop-word ("Meg." / "Meg, I've had it for ninety seconds.").

**Jokes / humor.** Reads as genuinely delighted more often than Meg does, but the delight is usually contained rather than voiced: a suppressed laugh into a glass (ch15), a "small thing with his eyebrows" instead of finishing a sentence (ch13), laughing "for a good deal longer than she expects" at Sam's line (ch19). When he does joke aloud it's dry and short: "Cool, I bet they teach driving at school next year." His laughter is more often staged as physical action than as a quoted line — which means a transcript-only reading of him understates how much he actually enjoys his daughter.

**Figurative language.** None. Like Meg, his register stays completely literal — no simile, no built image — consistent with the style guide's restriction of figurative language to Chloe alone.

**Most characteristic line:** "So how does a four-year-old school get that guy. What does it pay him. With what." (chapters/03_the_letter.md). Three short questions, each shorter than the last, each one a real red flag — and then, within the same chapter, he overrides it. The line is characteristic not because of what it says but because of what happens to it: it's his best piece of reasoning in the book, and it's also the reasoning he abandons.

## 2a. Register vs. the students — do the adults sound older?

*(This section is identical in MEG.md.)*

Yes, early — genuinely, measurably — and the gap closes or reverses later, which is itself the finding.

**Ages 6–7 (ch1–9): real separation.** Child dialogue in this band is short and repetitive by design: Kayleigh Burns's "You talk weird" (three words, no clause), Bryce's "That was different." / "How was it different, though?" / "It just was," (ch1) — flat assertion, no elaboration, no willingness to defend the claim. Against that, Meg's institutional register is unmistakably adult: "I'm asking whether anybody has ever made an exception to it, for any reason, in any year" (ch7) has a subordinate clause and a hedge ("for any reason, in any year") no child in the book produces at six or seven. This is real and it's the clearest evidence in the manuscript that the author can write more than one register.

**But Chloe herself complicates it immediately.** At six, arguing library policy: "The rule is about at a time, though. So six is fine, because it's still four at a time, it's just four and then four again, and two of them are ones you already gave me." (ch1) That's a longer, more tightly reasoned sentence than most of what Meg says in the same chapter — a six-year-old out-arguing the adult register around her. So the "adults sound older" finding is true on average, not true as a hard rule, and it's true despite Chloe rather than because the book withholds complexity from children categorically.

**Ages 8+ (ch10 onward): the gap closes, then reverses.** Once Halstead training starts, the cohort's diction gets denser and more technical while the parents' does not grow with them. Compare Dad's longest sustained arguments — "So how does a four-year-old school get that guy. What does it pay him. With what." (ch3); "This is four pages of the same trick, isn't it." (ch12) — against Chloe at eleven: "It's bread with a filling in it, so it's a sandwich, and I don't think there's a way out of that... The only thing you've given me is a hinge, and a hinge is a fact about bread, not a fact about food." (ch11) or Ruth at nine, unprompted: "Because it's already in everything... That's not learning a language. That's collecting on one you did four years ago." (ch14). By the tariffs chapter (ch13), Chloe is constructing multi-step economic arguments ("If you're wrong about one of those you can't get it back. The factory goes, then the people who knew how to run it get other jobs, and then in fifteen years they're sixty and there's nobody left who's ever done it.") that are longer and more structurally ambitious than anything her father says in the same scene — he's asking short, genuine follow-up questions, and she's the one doing the sustained reasoning.

**The honest summary:** the adults sound older than the students only in the first third of the book, when the students are genuinely young. Once Halstead's curriculum takes over the children's syntax, the parents' register stays essentially flat — plain, short, undecorated, the same "Cool" / "Huh" / short-declarative register at sixteen that it was at six — while the children's complexity keeps climbing. That flatness may be the right choice (ordinary adults staying ordinary against extraordinary children is arguably the point), but as a test of "can this author write more than one adult register," the honest answer is: one clear adult register early, largely overtaken by the children's own trained register later, with no second or evolving adult register to compare it against.

## 4. Out of character

- **"Cool," after the twenty-seven-day recitation (ch6).** This reads, on a first pass, like flatness or disengagement — the opposite of "he asks every time." It isn't a contradiction once you read the stage direction around it: "He looks past her at the room, at the other tables, long enough that she turns around to see what he's looking at. It's people eating." He's not failing to react; the narration shows him overwhelmed and unable to produce anything bigger than a monosyllable, then folding the napkin she gave him into his shirt pocket. This is the single clearest instance in the book of "no interiority for non-POV characters" being handled correctly — his emotional state is entirely inferable from action, never stated. Credible, not a violation.
- **"She said something." then "Meg. Leave it." (ch17).** This is the one place he is genuinely bad at what the synopsis says he's bad at ("pushing when it matters") — and it's presented as a real failure, immediately visible to the reader in the silence that follows and Meg's retreat to the kitchen. Consistent with the profile, well executed, and it's the direct dramatization of "Her dad says yeah, and doesn't push it" from the synopsis, which I can confirm the text supports.
- **No line found where he states the book's thesis or otherwise reads as "the narrator in costume."** His analytical lines (the website, the vendor proposal) stay grounded in the specific object in front of him — he never generalizes about institutions or gifted education the way a beta-note-flagged "narrator in costume" line would. This is a genuine strength: he sounds like a person reasoning about a document, not like the author making a point through him.
- **Absence from ch1's institutional scenes, presence at its dinner table.** Beta note 1.2 says he "does not exist" in ch1–2. That's true for ch2 (total absence — not mentioned once) but is an overstatement for ch1: he's at dinner, at Christmas, in the car. What's actually true and more precise is that he is absent specifically from every scene where Meg is doing institutional work (the doctor, the school, the district) and present only in domestic ones. That's a meaningful and probably intentional distinction the beta notes slightly blur.

## 5. Personality, likes, dislikes, habits

- **Genuinely strong analytical reasoning, recurring** [textual] — the ch3 anomaly ("with what") and the ch12 vendor-proposal scene are structurally the same move, five chapters apart, which supports this as a stable trait rather than a single showcase scene.
- **Overrides his own analysis under emotional pressure, and the text does not show him naming this** [textual for the override; not textually confirmed that he "knows it"] — see ch3 entry above. The synopsis's framing is the correct *reading*, not a verified *line*.
- **Bad at pushing when it matters** [textual] — "Meg. Leave it." (ch17); "Her dad says yeah, and doesn't push it" is a synopsis paraphrase but the underlying scene supports it.
- **Curious about his daughter's friends specifically, more than about her achievements** [textual] — ch19's unbroken run of questions about Sam, Ruth, Priya, Nadia. He never once asks a follow-up question about her grades, marks, or rankings in any scene I found.
- **Delight expressed physically, not verbally** [textual] — suppressed laugh into a glass (ch15), the eyebrow thing (ch13), extended real laughter (ch19) contrasted against short verbal responses ("Cool," "Huh.").
- **Makes things with his hands and involves Chloe in the process** [textual] — the back steps (ch16), teaching her to drive (ch18). Notably, these are the two chapters where he gets the most unshared one-on-one time with her, and both are wordless-labor-adjacent activities rather than conversations.
- **"Huh." as a closing verbal tic** [textual] — recurs at least twice (ch1, ch15) as his complete response to information he's still processing.
- **Does not banter with Meg** [textual] — see MEG.md; no scene of the two of them joking together was found anywhere in the material read.
- **Likes being right in a low-stakes argument and says so with real pleasure** [textual] — "Nobody's ever put the hinge to me. Twenty years and nobody's thought of the hinge." (ch11) — genuine delight at being beaten, not competitive frustration.

## 6. The name

See the note at the top of this file. He is not, strictly, "unnamed" — "Dave" appears twice, both from Meg, both in charged moments (ch6, the car-speaker recitation; ch9, the birthday-offer scene). But the narration itself never uses his name even once across every chapter I read, always falling back to "her dad" / "her father" / "he," and no other character (not Chloe, not any teacher, not any Halstead staff member) ever addresses or refers to him by name. The felt effect on the page is closer to "unnamed" than the two "Dave" instances would suggest, because they're easy to miss and never anchored by narration — a reader who skims past ch6 or ch9 could reach the end of the book without ever learning his name. **Whether the unnamedness is felt as a gap:** mostly no — "her dad" carries the functional load fine throughout, and the absence doesn't read as a withholding the way, say, the founder's namelessness does. The inconsistency (two real uses of "Dave" against an otherwise total narrative avoidance) is more of a loose thread than a deliberate effect right now; it reads like an artifact of drafting rather than a choice.

## 7. Fix list

1. **Give him at least one line at graduation.** Zero dialogue in the chapter that closes his arc, against Meg's three lines and a physical gesture, is the most concrete, single-scene fix available. Since his defining trait across the book is asking follow-up questions, the natural fix writes itself: give him one question to Chloe on the grass, in contrast to "takes about four hundred photographs and gets none of her face."

2. **Either commit to "unnamed" or stop almost-doing it.** Two uses of "Dave," both from Meg, both easy to miss, is the worst version of this choice — not deliberate enough to read as a statement (compare the founder, who is genuinely, consistently, meaningfully never named), not absent enough to make "unnamed" true. Pick one: cut the two "Dave" instances and let the unnamedness be real and noticeable, or let the narration use his name at least once so it doesn't depend on a reader catching two isolated dialogue tags.

3. **Dramatize the override the synopsis credits him with.** The text supports him having the best financial red flag in the book ("with what") and then abandoning it for an emotional reason ("she's been flat since Christmas") — but it never shows him registering that he's done this, and it never shows Meg naming it either ("Kids say it was good" minimizes rather than confronts). One added beat — a look, a pause, a single line where he half-acknowledges the trade he's making — would convert strong reader-inference into an actual character moment, and would make "and knows it" true on the page instead of only true in the synopsis.

4. **Un-share the exact-repetition tic with Meg.** He does it too ("I thought you knew. I thought you knew and it was fine.") and it's currently indistinguishable from her version of the same device. Since his other patterns (the analytical triple-question, the curious single-follow-up, "Huh.") are genuinely his, this one repetition tic is the thing most actively damaging his separability from Meg — cut it from one of them.

5. **Let the ch12 vendor-proposal scene pay off later.** It's the strongest, cleanest demonstration that his ch3 reasoning is a stable trait and not a one-scene flourish, and it currently sits in a chapter (Nine) that isn't on anyone's reading list and easy to miss. A short callback later — Chloe using the same "how would you find out if they hadn't" move on something of her own, and crediting it to him — would connect a real strength across the book instead of leaving it as an isolated, slightly hidden scene.

1. **Target, not tone.** Both parents run the identical rhetorical move — an escalating, multi-clause interrogation of a problem — but Meg always aims it at an *institution or a danger* (the district, the school, the gas oven, the rifle range) and Dad always aims it at a *document or a number* (the faculty roster, the vendor proposal, tariffs, the website's production budget). Swap the target and the sentences are near-identical in shape.

2. **The exact-repetition tic is shared, not distinguishing.** "Usually pretty good. Usually pretty good." (Meg) and "I thought you knew. I thought you knew and it was fine." (Dad) are the same device. If the goal is separable voices, this needs to belong to only one of them.

3. **Physical vs. verbal register.** Meg touches — a hand flat on the back, holding Chloe on the floor, fixing a collar. Dad photographs, drives, builds (the back steps), and once suppresses a laugh into his glass rather than voicing it. Neither parent's dialogue alone signals this; it's almost entirely stage direction, which means it's invisible in a transcript-only swap test and only visible in full scene context.

4. **He says her name; she says his.** "Meg" (Dad, twice) and "Dave" (Meg, twice — ch6: "Four hours away, Dave"; ch9: "She said take my birthday, Dave"). This is the single most reliable tag separating them in the charged scenes, and it's a name-drop, not a voice marker — which means if a reader ever loses track of who's talking, the text's actual fallback is proper nouns, not diction.

5. **One small, real, and unremarked tic: Dad says "Huh."** as a low-commitment closing beat at least twice (ch1, ch15 — "'Huh,' her father says, and holds his hand out for the ham"). I found no instance of Meg saying "Huh." It's minor, but it's genuine, and it's the kind of thing that should be built on rather than left as a coincidence.

**Verdict:** the two parents are reliably distinguished by *what they investigate* (danger vs. money) and by *stage direction* (touch vs. photograph), not by *how they talk*. On the page, stripped of tags, they are close to one voice wearing two roles.
