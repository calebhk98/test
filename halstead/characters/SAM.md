## 2. Voice profile

**Sentence length and shape.** Overwhelmingly short declaratives and short questions. Rare subordinate clauses; when they appear, it's to state a tactical reason ("Because the other way's open ground, Ruth, I just said —"), not to qualify or hedge. He almost never uses a comma to soften a claim — flat assertion is the default mode ("That's not real." "It worked." "Four seconds.").

**Register.** Casual, colloquial, no vocabulary reach. He is the only one of the core four who never produces an invented image or a classical reference (contrast Chloe's "clouds are the ocean going somewhere else," Ruth's tidally-locked lecture, Kavi's viscosity explanation). His most "built" line is the ten targets aphorism (see §4 — flagged as likely not his).

**Question style.** Opens conversations with direct, often mechanical questions: "Why ten?" "Why a minute?" "What are you doing?" "How long till this bit's boring?" These are curiosity-as-impatience questions, not analytical ones — he wants to know when the boring part ends, not why the rule exists.

**Disagreement.** He disagrees by restating his position louder or by declaring the argument over ("Noted. It's still the plan."), never by producing new evidence. This is the clearest differentiator from Ruth (evidence, precision, will not concede) and Kavi (reframes the whole problem). Sam wins arguments by being first to act, not by being right on paper — textually borne out by "the plan takes Sam ninety seconds and he is defending it before he's finished."

**Comedy.** Physical, deflating, often self-effacing, and built from bluster that gets punctured rather than from wit. "This is the worst mistake of my life" (about losing an argument to Kavi, repeated four times) is not a clever line — it is funny because it is stubborn and repeated. "You're agreeing at me" is his one genuinely verbal joke (a real coinage). Where the book's best jokes tend to be observational (Ruth, Nadia) or dry (Kavi), Sam's comedy is about not backing down ("I liked dance and I was good at dance, and I'm going to keep saying it").

**What he never does.** Never uses figurative language. Never produces the book's thematic/thesis lines in his own register — when he approaches one (the accuracy speech), it reads as un-Sam-like polish (see §4). Never hedges an opinion. Never asks for help with an idea (he asks "why" questions about rules, not "how do I" questions about problems).

**Most characteristic line:** *"Because I'm on forty and I do other stuff. Go and be miserable at Ruth. She's on ninety-something."* (chapters/18_fifteen.md, Fifteen). This is the single line the synopsis itself quotes as his defining stance, and it earns that status on the page: it is short, funny, redirects rather than dwells, and demonstrates "opted out of the ranking without opting out of the work" better than any narration could — he's still on the range, still shooting, just not miserable about the number.

## 3. The swap test

Sam has a real voice in the tactical/physical material and in a handful of specific jokes. But a large fraction of his lines — especially the one- and two-word chat reactions — carry no marker that would be lost if reassigned. Rough estimate: of roughly 100+ attributed lines, on the order of 35-40% would survive a swap to another character's mouth unchanged or nearly so. That is not a fatal count, but it means the character leans on his tag ("sam:") to do work his word choice isn't doing.

**Lines that would survive the swap (representative, not exhaustive):**

- "sam: oh" / "sam: oh thats horrible" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch19 The Chat) — pure reaction, no distinguishing content. Could be Eli, Nadia, or Theo reacting to the same gigabyte joke with identical effect.
- "sam: WHAT" (CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md, Ch27 The Money) — a bare reaction to Theo's revelation; any character could produce this all-caps beat.
- "sam: yeah" (used at least three separate times across the chat chapters as a closing beat) — content-free agreement.
- "That's worse," the boy says. (chapters/04_pluto.md, Pluto) — a straight-man reaction to Ruth's correction; nothing marks it as specifically his rather than any bystander's.
- "Why ten?" / "Why a minute?" (chapters/13_ten_pages.md, Ten Pages) — generic clarifying questions. Kavi is the character established elsewhere as the one who interrogates mechanism ("We don't do that with the paintballs... we watch the person," chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md), so handing Sam the "why" question here blurs rather than sharpens the two of them.
- "I got nothing out of that." (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md) — plausible from any of the four in that scene once Ruth's rebuttal follows; the line itself carries no Sam-specific texture.
- "sam: are you ok" (CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md, Ch23 Nadia) — a bare check-in; interchangeable with Eli's or Kavi's concern elsewhere in the same chat.

**Lines that would NOT survive the swap (Sam-specific):**

- "It's not even a room. It's a room-shaped object." (chapters/04_pluto.md) — the joke lands specifically because it is instant, disarming, and physically demonstrative (Chloe laughs "until she has to bend over"), in direct contrast to Ruth's clinical correction two lines later in the same scene. The scene is built as a contrast between the two of them; swapping the joke to Ruth would erase the point of the pairing.
- "Because I'm on forty and I do other stuff. Go and be miserable at Ruth. She's on ninety-something." (chapters/18_fifteen.md) — no other character in the book is written as comfortable with a middling number; this is load-bearing for his whole profile.
- The night-intrusion planning sequence (chapters/15_twelve.md) — "ninety seconds and defending it before he's finished" is dramatized, not just claimed, and the rapid-fire commitment to an unproven plan under Ruth's real-time objections is distinctly his mode of operating versus Ruth's evidence-first mode.
- "You're agreeing at me." (chapters/04_pluto.md) — a genuine, specific verbal coinage, rare for him.

## 4. Out of character

### (b) The narrator's framing in his mouth — Ch21 Ten Targets

This is the scene the special instructions flagged, and the audit confirms it. The captain speech runs in two parts. The first half is in register:

> "Right, but that's not accuracy. The target's not doing anything. It's just sitting there. That's not a measurement of anything, that's a measurement of whether the rifle works."

Flat, blunt, structurally simple — consistent with everything else he says.

The second half is not:

> "Accuracy is whether you can put a round into another round. In flight. On the count, opposite lanes, you both fire on the same beat. I'm at about forty percent and that's poor, there are people I went to school with at ninety. That's the number that means something." "A gun you can't stop a projectile with isn't a tool. It's a weapon. Anyone can carry a weapon."

The tool/weapon line is a mirrored, epigrammatic construction (STYLE_GUIDES flags mirrored halves and anaphora as narrator tells even though this instance is in dialogue, which is technically exempt). More telling: the phrase is close to verbatim to how the book's own synopsis document describes the entire marksmanship curriculum's rationale — "a weapon you can't stop a projectile with isn't a tool" (SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md, PART ONE). That is the author's own summary of the curriculum's thesis, relocated almost word for word into Sam's mouth. Nowhere else does Sam produce a constructed aphorism like this — every other line he has is a flat fact, a number, or a joke. This reads as the author using her least analytical character to deliver the book's analytical point, which is exactly the "narrator in costume" failure mode BETA_NOTES §8 and STYLE_GUIDES §8 both name. Recommend cutting the tool/weapon coda and ending the speech on "That's the number that means something," which is already earned and already his.

### (b) "No internal ceiling" asserted, not dramatized

The defining trait — "maxes a test in week two, does it again better in week three, because nobody told him to stop" — is introduced by narrator summary in CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch21 Ten Targets: "There is no version of a task in Sam's head where you reach a number and then don't try for a higher one." Nothing in chapters/04 through chapters/20 (ages 6-17) dramatizes this pattern. His persistence in that material is a different shape — he keeps failing at the overhang and keeps trying (chapters/12_nine.md), which is grit against failure, not re-maxing a passed test for its own sake. The trait arrives fully formed for the Army chapters with no earlier scene to point back to.

It also sits in unaddressed tension with his own established Halstead-era number: "He's on forty since he was fifteen and it slides straight off him" (chapters/19_sixteen.md, reported by Chloe) and his own "I'm on forty and I do other stuff" (chapters/18_fifteen.md) — a plateau he is visibly content with, not one he keeps re-attacking. A reader can reconcile this (he doesn't chase relative rank against people, but does chase an absolute number once it's just him against a stopwatch) but the text never makes that distinction explicit; right now it reads as two different Sams depending on which chapter you're in.

### The laughing-face moment does not earn its billing

chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md, The Parking Lot, ends: "Somebody in the year above has sent one laughing face and nothing else, and Sam won't discuss it, and won't for so long that it becomes the funniest part of the whole thing."

The special instructions describe this as landing "hard enough that he won't discuss it for months" and call it "the only instance in the book of anyone caring what someone else thinks." The text does not support that reading. This is the entire treatment of the moment — one sentence, at the very end of a chapter, and its own narration immediately reframes the silence as comedy rather than hurt ("becomes the funniest part of the whole thing"). There is no scene of Sam declining to discuss it, no later chapter where it resurfaces, no line of dialogue showing discomfort. It is asserted, not shown — a straightforward violation of the book's own house rule that a non-POV character's inner state has to appear as action or speech (STYLE_GUIDES §1). As written, the moment reads as a running joke about Sam being touchy, not as the book's one instance of social vulnerability.

It also inherits a continuity problem for free: "somebody in the year above" contradicts CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch18 The First One, which establishes Chloe's cohort as the first graduating class — there is no year above them (already flagged in BETA_NOTES §1.5, noted here because it sits inside the one scene doing the heaviest emotional lifting for this character).

### Credible growth, not inconsistency

His Army-era sincerity and total lack of self-consciousness with a captain (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch21) is a believable extension of the Halstead-era Sam rather than a break — he has always taken institutional structure at face value and answered exactly the question he thinks was asked (see the "obviously" exchanges as a child, chapters/06_the_list.md). Moving from a school that ranks everyone to an institution that praises him without irony plausibly reads as more of the same trait in a new context, not a rewrite of the character.

## 6. The name

No mismatch found. "Sam" carries no implied ethnicity, family background, or culture that the text contradicts. Unlike Ruth (mother, father who sells insurance, a brother) and Kavi (mother, father who "talked to Mr. Baptiste for an hour"), Sam is given no family at all across the entire reading list — the single exception is one throwaway line in chapters/07_the_same_room.md: "He has a sister or maybe a cousin who plays soccer and she never worked out which one it was," which is never resolved or returned to. This isn't a name problem, but it is the same category of issue BETA_NOTES §1.14 raises for other characters — Sam has main-cast weight (POV chapters, the parking lot set piece, the Army arc) with essentially no household or backstory, which will read as a gap once a reader notices Ruth's and Kavi's families are sketched in and his is not. The name itself is used consistently throughout with no drift or error.

## 7. Fix list

1. **Cut or re-ground the "tool/weapon" coda in the Ch21 accuracy speech** (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch21 Ten Targets). End the speech on "That's the number that means something" — already earned, already his register. The mirrored "It's a weapon. Anyone can carry a weapon" line duplicates the synopsis's own framing of the curriculum's thesis almost verbatim and reads as the author speaking through him. Highest-value single fix: it's his one big set-piece speech and it currently breaks his voice at the exact moment it should be showcasing it.

2. **Dramatize the laughing-face reaction instead of asserting it** (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md). One additional beat — Sam changing the subject, leaving a conversation, or a later chat message he deletes and re-sends — would let "won't discuss it for months" actually land instead of being undercut by its own punchline framing in the same sentence. As written it reads as a running joke, not the book's one moment of someone caring what others think.

3. **Replace the generic chat one-liners with something built from his existing toolkit.** "sam: oh" / "sam: oh thats horrible" / "sam: WHAT" / repeated "sam: yeah" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md Ch19; CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md Ch27) carry no voice and are the largest bucket of swappable lines. He already has a working tic — leading with a bare number ("About forty," "Four seconds," "Fifty-one seconds") — that could extend into chat as a habit (answering a group question with a number before anyone asks for one), giving even his shortest lines a fingerprint.

4. **Give him a successor to "and a half."** The verbal tic that marks him as a specific seven-year-old (chapters/04_pluto.md, chapters/07_the_same_room.md) evaporates after camp and is never replaced. By chapters/13 onward his line-openings ("Why ten?" "Why a minute?") are interchangeable with a generic curious classmate. A consistent adult-Sam habit — even something as small as always answering "why" with a fact rather than a reason — would close that gap.

5. **Either plant "no internal ceiling" earlier or reconcile it on the page with his Halstead-era plateau.** Right now the Army-era Sam who re-maxes a passed fitness test contradicts, without comment, the Halstead-era Sam who plateaus happily at forty percent on the range. One line distinguishing "I don't care about beating people" from "I do care about beating a number, once it's just me" would turn a latent inconsistency into a stated piece of characterization — and it's exactly the distinction the synopsis's own "opted out of the ranking without opting out of the work" phrase is reaching for but the text never says.
