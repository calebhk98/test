# SAM

The best fighter in the year, who wants the real test to finally start and cannot work out why nobody will just tell him where it is.

**Age span:** seven and a half at camp (chapters/04_pluto.md) to mid-twenties (Army chapters, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md / CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md) | **MBTI:** ESTP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** moderate-high; the least analytical of the four core campers by in-text behavior — he takes correction from Ruth and Kavi without ever out-arguing them, and wins by acting instead
**Appears in:** chapters/04_pluto.md, 05_behind.md, 06_the_list.md, 07_the_same_room.md (mentioned only, no lines), 10_april.md, 11_eight.md, 12_nine.md, 13_ten_pages.md, 14_sixty_degrees.md, 15_twelve.md, 16_thirteen.md (present, no direct lines), 17_fourteen.md, 18_fifteen.md, 19_sixteen.md, 20_the_parking_lot.md, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md
**Speaks:** roughly 110 lines, including one-line chat messages. Front-loaded into three set pieces: the night intrusion (chapters/15_twelve.md), the parking-lot fight (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md), and the group chat (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md). He also carries two of his own viewpoint chapters, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md's "Ten Targets" and CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md's "The Exercise."

## Voice in one paragraph

Shortest sentence-builder in the cast: one flat clause, said and done, no subordinate clause ever. He does not qualify a claim mid-sentence and does not explain himself unless asked twice. Under pushback he does one of two things — repeats the same claim in close to the same words, louder, or stops arguing and acts. He asks more short direct questions than anyone else in his age group, but they are always about duration or rule ("how long," "why that number"), never about mechanism. He is allowed exactly one kind of figurative language: the deflating literalism, where he names a thing for what it plainly is instead of reaching for an image. The most characteristic line in the book is the first one he ever gets: "It's not even a room," he says. "It's a room-shaped object." (chapters/04_pluto.md:45) — it is funny because it is flat and immediate, it fixes the moment instead of decorating it, and it is the template for every "signature" number-first line that follows.

## Signature — theirs alone

States a number before anything else, then the point. Nobody else in the cast opens a line this way.

- "About forty," Sam says. (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:726)
- "Four seconds," Sam says. (chapters/15_twelve.md:309)

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is one flat, complete clause with nothing set off by a comma to qualify or explain it.
- YES if a number is the first thing in the line, with the point arriving after it.
- YES if, contradicted, it repeats his prior claim close to word-for-word instead of building a new argument.
- YES if it is a short question asking when something ends, what the rule is, or why a number is what it is.
- YES if a figurative phrase in the line deflates the moment by naming what something literally, unglamorously is.
- YES if it states a fact about himself with no softening around it.
- YES if it answers exactly the question that was asked and then stops.
- NO if it contains a subordinate clause built to qualify or explain something — that belongs to Theo.
- NO if it hedges — "maybe," "I guess," "probably," "sort of," "I think."

## Are they acting like themselves?

Under pressure he commits to a plan in seconds and defends it while still describing it, rather than finishing the description first. In a physical confrontation he takes point without being asked, and afterward checks on the people he hit before anyone tells him to. He occupies a room by heading straight for the food, sitting near the middle of a group rather than the edge, and being loudest when defending a position nobody else holds. His hands are usually doing something concrete — chalking up before a climb, working a hook at the forge, reaching for someone else's water.

Failure modes — the two or three ways a writer most easily gets him wrong:
1. Making him witty. His comedy is stubbornness and repetition, not a quick line — if the joke depends on timing or wordplay, it is not his joke.
2. Making him reflective. He does not sit with a loss, an insult, or a bad outcome for more than a beat on the page; he says "noted" or "okay" and moves on to food or the next thing.
3. Making him hedge or ask a rhetorical/exploratory question. His questions request a fact (a duration, a rule, a number); if he is workshopping his own thinking out loud, that is Chloe's or Ruth's move, not his.

## Subjects and schedule

Everyone at Halstead takes eight academic hours and two afternoon blocks a year, but the language, the two rotating academic slots, and both afternoon blocks are individual — Sam is not on Chloe's timetable.

**Languages, in order:** Spanish (begun at camp, age seven), then one new language a year — Korean at eight, Tagalog at nine, Italian at ten, Vietnamese at eleven, Thai at twelve, Indonesian at thirteen, Portuguese at fourteen, Swahili at fifteen, Hausa at sixteen, Amharic at seventeen [invented, per allocation]. **Known for:** Tagalog — he has it out loud before anyone else in his year has it on paper, because he picks up conversational rhythm from being talked at rather than from the grammar, and it's the one language year where an academic subject just lands for him without a fight [invented, per allocation]. **Quietly bad at:** the written report every language is graded on. His ten-page reports are the weakest work he turns in across the whole curriculum — competent information, flat sentences, nothing revised — which sits strangely next to how fast he picks a language up out loud, and nobody has ever quite reconciled the two for him [invented, per allocation].

**Best at:** Fighting [text] — "the best fighter in the year by a wide margin" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:248). An hour a day, daily, from age ten: one person in the middle, ten on them, a minute on the clock. A clean, countable result with nothing to argue about afterward.

**Worst at:** Written language reports [invented, per allocation] — the one place the school asks him to produce something polished rather than something true and immediate, and it's also the one place revision is the actual assignment; his instinct under correction is to restate a claim, not rebuild it, which is exactly the wrong move for a graded redraft.

**Favourite, and why:** Dance [text], of all things. Everyone in his year is glad to see it drop off the sheet at nine and Sam is the one holdout: "'I liked dance,' Sam says." / "'Nobody liked dance, Sam. Not one person in the entire year.'" / "'I liked dance and I was good at dance, and I'm going to keep saying it.'" (chapters/12_nine.md:25-29). It rewards exact, repeatable physical technique with an immediate right-or-wrong result — the same shape as fighting, in a room nobody expected to find his taste.

**The subject nobody else took:** Theatre, as a block-two elective [invented, per allocation], picked up somewhere between climbing and sailing and kept for a run of terms before baking finally displaced it. It confirms the dance result wasn't a fluke — blocking a scene is the same pleasure as a drill: a fixed sequence, performed exactly, judged by whether you hit the beat. Climbing and sailing were both tried and both left behind; theatre and then baking are what actually stuck, and both are the same appetite showing up in a place nobody would predict it from the fighting record alone.

**The year something clicked:** Nine, the Tagalog year [invented, per allocation] — the only time an academic subject has ever come to him the way fighting does, without a run-up.

**What he teaches the twelves, from thirteen:** Fighting fundamentals — stance, falling, getting up, the first slow rounds of the ten-on-one drill — taught the way he does everything, by doing it in front of them and having them copy it rather than explaining the mechanics first [invented, consistent with his established teaching-by-demonstration habit].

**Secretly proud of:** Baking [text, per allocation — "genuinely good and nobody expects that"]. It is the one thing in his schedule that isn't scored, isn't compared against ninety other people, and isn't a drill — and he is quietly better at it than almost anyone in his year, including people who would be startled to hear it. Bread specifically: it rewards exact timing and a light, deliberate touch, which is not what anyone assumes about a boy whose other numbers are all measured in seconds and hits.

## Home

Rural, four hours from Halstead in the opposite direction from Chloe's family [invented, per allocation]. His surname is **Marsh** [invented — the manuscript never gives him one]. The Marshes run a hauling and equipment-rental business off the same property they live on: two trucks, a yard, a rented forklift that goes out more than it comes back on schedule, and a whiteboard nailed up in the kitchen with the week's jobs and who's driving them [invented, per allocation]. His father runs the yard. His mother keeps the books and the board, and updates it every Sunday night in the same red marker, which is the closest thing the house has to a ritual [invented].

He is the youngest of five [invented, per allocation "several older siblings"]: two older brothers who drive for the business, an older sister who does the books alongside their mother and will probably take the whole thing over, and one more sister close enough in age that they shared a room until he was eleven. He was the smallest of the five until he was fourteen, and for most of a childhood the only way to close a gap with three older brothers and their friends was to do a thing again until he could do it as well as them — which is where the habit of retesting himself against his own last number actually starts, years before any fitness assessment gives it a name [invented, seed from allocation, reconciles Known Problems #2 below].

Dinner is loud, family-style, and nobody waits for anyone else to be served before reaching — which is where his table manners at Halstead come from, not a lack of them. Being told to do a job that's already posted on a board, without being asked twice, is the most familiar feeling in the world to him; the schedule Halstead hands him later isn't a new structure, it's a bigger version of the one that's been on his kitchen wall his whole life. When he goes home he falls straight back into the house's noise and its rota — picks up whatever's chalked next to his name without being told, doesn't perform any of the extraordinary parts of Halstead for his family, and is, by his own account, "not the loudest one at that table," which nobody who's only met him at school would believe.

His maternal grandmother lives two towns over, doesn't use the phone much because the house line is always tied up with job calls, and is the one relative he writes to directly rather than through the family noise (see Interior life).

## Body

Broad and heavy-footed [invented, per allocation] — he takes up a doorway the way some people take up a room, and floorboards announce him before he's said anything. Square-jawed, unremarkable face at rest, the kind that reads as friendly mostly because nothing on it is doing much work; he doesn't have an expressive range and doesn't seem to know that [invented]. Grows to six foot two by the year titled "Fourteen" — "Sam is six foot two by September" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:113) — and burns through kit twice in the same year, "two sets of kit before Christmas and a third in March, but the third one is the one that fits" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:121); a visiting inspector mistakes him for staff that same October.

His hands are thick-fingered and blunt-nailed, permanently a little marked up from the forge and from wood shop [invented, consistent with established forge use, chapters/15_twelve.md], which is also, unexpectedly, why he's good with bread dough — the same forearm strength that throws a punch reads, at a slower speed, as excellent control kneading and folding [invented]. He chalks his hands before a climb without being told to — "Sam is chalking his hands and not looking at any of them" (chapters/12_nine.md:99) — and at the forge he'll hand someone a flawed piece to make his point rather than describe the flaw (chapters/15_twelve.md).

He moves heavily walking and eating, and precisely the instant something actually starts — the gap between the two is the whole point of him physically: nobody watching him cross a dining hall would guess how little wasted motion is in him once a fight opens (chapters/15_twelve.md; chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md). Off duty he wears whatever's clean and doesn't think about it further, and still has a jacket that used to be one of his brothers' [invented]. He sleeps with the window open regardless of weather and told everyone so, four times, like it was a fact about the world (chapters/07_the_same_room.md) — and sprawls, taking up the whole bed, a habit from years of sharing a room with a sister who didn't (chapters/07_the_same_room.md; home detail invented). He eats standing up [invented, per allocation], a lot, fast, and reaches across other people for what he wants mid-sentence rather than asking — "He reaches across her for the water" (chapters/18_fifteen.md:67) — and keeps ordering after everyone else has stopped: "They order too much and then Sam orders more" (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md:11).

## Personality off the page

- **Boredom:** says so out loud rather than waiting it out — "Sam keeps the rifle up. 'How long till this bit's boring?'" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:11).
- **Being wrong:** argues his side hard in the moment and then drops it completely — he's never shown circling back to a settled loss afterward (the moon argument, chapters/05_behind.md).
- **Being praised:** turns it into a harder number instead of accepting it at face value (the captain scene, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:723-741).
- **Tired / early hours:** the one real data point is that he's slowest of the four to physically reach a 4 a.m. emergency — "Sam gets there last" (chapters/15_twelve.md:159) — while Kavi and Ruth are already working the problem. Once there, he still commits to a full plan inside two minutes, so the lag reads as waking-up time, not impairment. What he's like specifically at 2am beyond that: a slow starter who's fully online the moment there's something to do [invented, extrapolated from the one data point].
- **A free Saturday:** back at the climbing wall re-running a route he's already cleared, to see if he can do it cleaner — the same instinct that later shows up as retaking a passed fitness test. He spends an entire year on one climbing feature after everyone has stopped watching him attempt it (chapters/12_nine.md).
- **What he finds funny in other people:** somebody else refusing to let a point go. He needles Ruth through extra rounds of "tidally locked" instead of changing the subject (chapters/05_behind.md), and calls his own two-week losing streak against Kavi "the worst mistake of my life" at dinner for a month rather than dropping it (chapters/04_pluto.md) — he seems to enjoy stubbornness in other people the way he practices it himself.

## Interior life

**What he wants that he hasn't said out loud:** not to win, exactly — to be given a task with no announced ceiling on it, the way the schedule at home never ran out of jobs. Every version of "when's the real assessment" is the same question underneath: is this the kind of thing that keeps going, or the kind that stops and leaves him standing there.

**What he's afraid of, more than being laughed at:** that something will matter and nobody will have flagged it as mattering — that he'll spend a whole career maxing drills and miss the one time it was real because it looked exactly like all the others. This is the actual engine behind the captain scene and the culvert self-critique in CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md's "The Exercise" — not arrogance, a kind of vigilance he can't switch off.

**What he thinks about before sleeping:** the day's numbers, briefly, then nothing — and most nights, a line or two of whatever he's going to put in the next letter to his grandmother, turned over the way he turns over a hook at the forge before he decides it's finished [invented, seed from the private-thing allocation]. He's written to her every week, by hand, since he was seven — through camp, through Halstead, through basic training — and it's the one relationship in his life with no schedule, no scoring, and nobody comparing him to ninety other people in it. The letters are ordinary: what he ate, a joke, a question about the yard back home. Nobody at Halstead or in the Army knows he does this. It isn't a secret because it's shameful — it's a secret because it's the one thing he's never been asked to report on, and he likes it that way.

**What he'd do with a free year:** work the family business top to bottom for a full year, the way he'd work through a curriculum — every truck, every route, every part of the yard — and see how far he could push the whole operation, not because he wants to run it, but because "as far as it goes" is the only version of a task he actually likes [invented].

**What he believes that the others would argue with:** that doing a hard physical thing enough times closes almost any gap — a real belief, earned honestly by spending years the smallest of five kids and catching up anyway. Ruth would call this a category error inside about four seconds.

**What he's actually wrong about:** exactly that. He has one private counterexample he's never said out loud — the rifle range, where the plateau at forty percent held no matter how many times he ran it back. He didn't revise the belief; he just quietly stopped applying it there and moved his effort somewhere it still worked, which is the real reason "I'm on forty and I do other stuff" (chapters/18_fifteen.md:67) comes out so easily. It isn't contentment. It's a belief meeting its one exception and being rerouted around instead of examined.

## Would do

- Carry something heavy for someone without being asked.
- Take point in a fight or drill before anyone assigns it to him.
- Check on someone he's just hit before he does anything else.
- Retake a test he's already passed, with nobody watching, just to see if he can beat his own number.
- Answer "how did it go" with a number before a feeling.
- Keep telling the same joke long after everyone else has stopped laughing, and mean it more, not less, each time.
- Write his grandmother a letter every week by hand, even from basic training.
- Redirect a compliment into a harder number instead of taking the thanks.
- Show someone what's wrong with a piece of work by handing it to them rather than describing it.
- Eat standing up, reach across the table for what he wants mid-sentence, and keep talking while he does it.

## Would never

- Land a clever line. His idea of a joke is saying the same true thing again, not a twist or a reframe.
- Stop doing or saying something because it stopped being funny to everyone else — not noticing, or not minding, that the room moved on is the joke.
- Revise a plan mid-argument just because someone pushes back hard — he restates it unchanged, louder, or drops the argument and starts doing it.

## Known problems

1. **CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:753** — the "Ten Targets" rifle-accuracy speech ends on a mirrored, constructed line: "A gun you can't stop a projectile with isn't a tool. It's a weapon. Anyone can carry a weapon." This is the only place in the book Sam builds a rhetorical, mirrored construction; everywhere else he states one flat fact and stops. **Proposal:** cut everything from "A gun you can't stop a projectile with" onward. End the speech at "That's the number that means something," which is already in his register and already lands the point.
2. **CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:709 vs. chapters/19_sixteen.md:139** — the no-ceiling claim ("There is no version of a task in Sam's head where you reach a number and then don't try for a higher one") and the forty-percent-content claim ("He's been on forty since he was fifteen and it slides straight off him") sit unreconciled on the page. **Proposal:** the reconciliation now lives in this sheet's Interior life section — he over-generalized "repetition closes any gap" from years catching up to older siblings, and the rifle plateau is his one private, untold counterexample; he reroutes around it rather than revising the belief out loud, which is why he reads as content rather than as having simply given up. If the author wants this on the page, it needs only one undramatized line in Sam's own flat register, any time after the rifle plateau is established — not a speech.
