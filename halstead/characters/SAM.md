# SAM

The best fighter in the year, who wants the real test to finally start and cannot work out why nobody will just tell him where it is.

**Age span:** seven and a half at camp (chapters/04_pluto.md) to mid-twenties (Army chapters, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md / CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md) | **MBTI:** ESTP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** moderate-high; the least analytical of the four core campers by in-text behavior — he takes correction from Ruth and Kavi without ever out-arguing them, and wins by acting instead
**Appears in:** chapters/04_pluto.md, 05_behind.md, 06_the_list.md, 07_the_same_room.md (mentioned only, no lines), 10_april.md, 11_eight.md, 12_nine.md, 13_ten_pages.md, 14_sixty_degrees.md, 15_twelve.md, 16_thirteen.md (present, no direct lines), 17_fourteen.md, 18_fifteen.md, 19_sixteen.md, 20_the_parking_lot.md, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md
**Speaks:** roughly 110 lines, including one-line chat messages. Front-loaded into three set pieces: the night intrusion (chapters/15_twelve.md), the parking-lot fight (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md), and the group chat (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md). He also carries two of his own viewpoint chapters, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md's "Ten Targets" and CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md's "The Exercise."

## Voice in one paragraph

Shortest sentence-builder in the cast: one flat clause, said and done, no subordinate clause ever. He does not qualify a claim mid-sentence and does not explain himself unless asked twice. Under pushback he does one of two things — repeats the same claim in close to the same words, louder, or stops arguing and acts. He asks more short direct questions than anyone else in his age group, but they are always about duration or rule ("how long," "why that number"), never about mechanism. He is allowed exactly one kind of figurative language: the deflating literalism, where he names a thing for what it plainly is instead of reaching for an image. The most characteristic line in the book is the first one he ever gets: "It's not even a room," he says. "It's a room-shaped object." (chapters/04_pluto.md:45) — it is funny because it is flat and immediate, it fixes the moment instead of decorating it, and it is the template for every "signature" number-first line that follows.

## Signature — theirs alone

States a number before anything else, then the point. Nobody else in the cast opens a line this way.

- "About forty," Sam says. (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:726)
- "Four seconds," Sam says. (chapters/15_twelve.md:309)

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is one flat, complete clause with nothing set off by a comma to qualify or explain it.
- NO if it contains a subordinate clause — a "which," an "although," a "because" that explains rather than just answers a question. That construction belongs to Theo.
- YES if, contradicted, it repeats his prior claim close to word-for-word instead of adding new support for it.
- NO if it responds to pushback by conceding ground, adding a qualifier, or producing a reason he hasn't already given — he restates or he acts, he does not negotiate.
- YES if a number is the first thing in the line, with any point or explanation arriving after it.
- NO if it hedges — "maybe," "I guess," "probably," "sort of," "I think," a trailing "or something." Sam never softens a claim.
- YES if it is a short question asking when something ends, what the rule is, or why a number is what it is, asked to get a fact fast.
- NO if the question is rhetorical or exploratory, or if a joke in the line depends on wit, wordplay, or a twist rather than on him repeating himself unmoved — that is someone else's kind of funny.
- NO if the line accepts a compliment or praise at face value; he redirects it into a harder number or corrects the premise of the compliment instead.
- YES if a figurative phrase in the line deflates the moment by naming what something literally, unglamorously is, rather than reaching for a poetic or emotional comparison.

## Are they acting like themselves?

Under pressure he commits to a plan in seconds and defends it while still describing it, rather than finishing the description first. In a physical confrontation he takes point without being asked, and afterward checks on the people he hit before anyone tells him to. He does not sulk after losing an argument — he says "noted" or "okay" and moves on to food or the next thing inside a beat; nothing sits with him on the page for more than a line or two. He occupies a room by being first to the food, first to volunteer, and loudest when defending a position nobody else holds. His hands are usually doing something concrete — chalking up before a climb, working a hook at the forge, reaching for someone else's water.

Failure modes — the three ways a writer most easily gets him wrong:
1. Making him witty. His comedy is stubbornness and repetition, not a quick line — if the joke depends on timing or wordplay, it is not his joke.
2. Making him reflective. He does not sit with a loss, an insult, or a bad outcome for more than a beat on the page; if he is shown dwelling on something for a scene or longer, that is out of character unless the text stops to justify it as new.
3. Making him hedge or ask a rhetorical/exploratory question. His questions request a fact (a duration, a rule, a number); if he is workshopping his own thinking out loud, that is Chloe's or Ruth's move, not his.

## Subjects

Halstead runs eight academic hours plus two afternoon blocks, every year, from age seven or eight. Everyone takes nearly everything, so this is about what he reaches for and what he endures, not about a special curriculum built around him.

- **Best at:** Fighting [text] — "the best fighter in the year by a wide margin" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:248). An hour a day, daily, from age ten: one person in the middle, ten on them, a minute on the clock. A clean, countable result with nothing to argue about afterward — the shape of task he's built for.
- **Worst at:** The weekly writing track [inferred]. That subject is graded on revision — draft it, get notes on what's wrong, rebuild the same argument better — and his instinct under any correction is to repeat a claim unchanged rather than reconstruct it, which is exactly the move that fails on a track built around redrafting.
- **Favourite, and why:** Dance [text]. The one subject he defends after everyone else is relieved to drop it: "'I liked dance,' Sam says." / "'Nobody liked dance, Sam. Not one person in the entire year.'" / "'I liked dance and I was good at dance, and I'm going to keep saying it.'" (chapters/12_nine.md:25-29). It rewards exact, repeatable physical technique with an immediate right-or-wrong result — the same shape as fighting, just somewhere nobody expected to find his taste.
- **Least favourite:** Politics [inferred]. Per the curriculum it "never ends. No final. Updated weekly" (CURRICULUM_GRID.md) — the one subject with no pass condition and no moment where it's over. That's the exact shape of the thing he spends his twenties complaining is being kept from him: a test that tells you when you're done.
- **Secretly proud of:** Medicine, from first aid on a clock at age nine [inferred]. The skill actually being graded there is staying calm, deciding fast, and not needing a second attempt while somebody stands over you applying pressure (CURRICULUM_GRID.md) — which is exactly what he already has for free, and exactly what he shows, unremarked on by anyone, in both real emergencies he's actually in (chapters/15_twelve.md; chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md). Nobody in the text calls him good at this, because we only ever see the subject itself through someone else's eyes.
- **Would drop if allowed:** Mathematics, from the year it turns into proof, age thirteen on [inferred]. The demand stops being "produce the answer" and becomes "justify it to somebody else's satisfaction" — and having to keep explaining a true thing after he's already said it once is the one demand he doesn't accept anywhere else in the book either.

## Personality off the page

- **Boredom:** says so out loud rather than waiting it out — "Sam keeps the rifle up. 'How long till this bit's boring?'" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:11).
- **Being wrong:** absorbs a correction without defending himself further and doesn't reopen it later — he keeps arguing his side in the moment (the moon, chapters/05_behind.md) but is never shown circling back to a settled loss afterward.
- **Being praised:** redirects it into a harder number instead of accepting it at face value (the captain scene, CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:723-741).
- **Tired / early hours:** mostly UNDEFINED, but the one real data point is that he is slowest of the four to reach a 4 a.m. emergency call — "Sam gets there last" (chapters/15_twelve.md:159) — while Kavi and Ruth are already up and working the problem. Once there, he still commits to a full plan in under two minutes, so the lag reads as waking-up time, not impairment. What he's like specifically at 2am: UNDEFINED.
- **A free Saturday** [inferred]: back at the climbing wall, re-running a route he's already cleared to see if he can do it cleaner — the same instinct that later shows up as retaking a passed fitness test. He spends an entire year on one climbing feature after everyone has stopped watching him attempt it (chapters/12_nine.md).
- **What he finds funny in other people** [inferred]: somebody else refusing to let a point go. He keeps needling Ruth through extra rounds of "tidally locked" instead of changing the subject (chapters/05_behind.md), and narrates his own two-week losing streak against Kavi as "the worst mistake of my life" at dinner for a month rather than dropping it (chapters/04_pluto.md) — he seems to enjoy stubbornness in other people the way he practices it himself.

## Would do / would never do

**Would:**
- Carry something heavy for someone without being asked.
- Take point in a fight or drill before anyone assigns it to him.
- Check on someone he just hit before he does anything else.
- Retake a test he's already passed, with nobody watching, just to see if he can beat his own number.
- Answer "how did it go" with a number before a feeling.
- Keep repeating a joke or a claim long after everyone else has stopped finding it funny — and mean it more, not less, each time.

**Would never:**
- Land a clever line — no wordplay, no timed punchline, no double meaning. His idea of a joke is saying the same true thing again.
- Stop doing or saying something because it stopped being funny to other people. That's the joke: he doesn't notice, or doesn't care, that the room moved on.
- Revise a plan mid-argument because someone pushes back hard — he restates it unchanged, louder, or drops the argument and does it.
- Accept a compliment at face value — he corrects the premise or names a harder number instead of saying thank you.
- Sit with a loss, an insult, or an embarrassing result for more than a beat on the page.
- Ask a question to invite discussion or think out loud — his questions request one fact and stop there.

## Body and habits

- **Build:** physically the most dominant student in his year — "the best fighter in the year by a wide margin" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:248).
- **Growth:** reaches six foot two by the year titled "Fourteen" — "Sam is six foot two by September" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:113) — and outgrows his kit twice in one year: "two sets of kit before Christmas and a third in March, but the third one is the one that fits" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:121). A visiting inspector mistakes him for staff that same October.
- **Hands:** chalks them before a climb — "Sam is chalking his hands and not looking at any of them" (chapters/12_nine.md:99); works metal at the forge well enough to hand a finished piece to someone rather than describe the flaw in it (chapters/15_twelve.md); reaches across people for food or water mid-conversation without asking (chapters/18_fifteen.md:67).
- **Eats:** a lot, visibly, and it's remarked on by others — "'I've been eating a lot.' 'You've been eating like a man with a farm.'" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:117-119) — and keeps ordering after everyone else has stopped: "They order too much and then Sam orders more" (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md:11).
- **Sleeps:** with the window open, and says so — established only in another character's memory of him, never dramatized directly on the page (chapters/07_the_same_room.md).
- **Speed to mobilize:** slowest of the four to physically arrive at a 4 a.m. emergency, fastest to commit to a plan once there (chapters/15_twelve.md:159 and following).
- **In a physical crisis:** disarms and restrains without visible hesitation, then methodically checks everyone he's put down before doing anything else (chapters/15_twelve.md; chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md).
- **Face, hair, handedness, voice, everyday clothing:** UNDEFINED. The manuscript gives none of this.

It's a room-shaped object," chapters/04_pluto.md:45), and their exchanges stay light and unguarded at every age the book shows them together.

## Known problems

1. **CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:753** — the "Ten Targets" rifle-accuracy speech ends on a mirrored, constructed line: "A gun you can't stop a projectile with isn't a tool. It's a weapon. Anyone can carry a weapon." This is the only place in the book Sam builds a rhetorical, mirrored construction; everywhere else he states one flat fact and stops. **Proposal:** cut everything from "A gun you can't stop a projectile with" onward. End the speech at "That's the number that means something," which is already in his register and already lands the point.
2. **CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md:709** — "There is no version of a task in Sam's head where you reach a number and then don't try for a higher one" asserts a no-ceiling trait that is directly contradicted by **chapters/19_sixteen.md:139** — "He's been on forty since he was fifteen and it slides straight off him," where he is shown content to plateau rather than keep re-attacking a number. **Proposal:** add one line distinguishing "won't stop retesting himself against a fixed, objective standard" (the fitness test) from "does not care about beating other people" (the rifle range, which is scored against peers) — or move the no-ceiling claim out of narration and into a dramatized scene so it isn't only asserted.

## Do not confuse with

- **Kavi.** Both ask short direct questions, but Kavi's questions chase mechanism and he pursues the technical answer once he has it; Sam's questions chase duration or rule ("how long," "why that number") and he drops the subject once he has the fact. The collision to fix: chapters/13_ten_pages.md, "Why ten?" / "Why a minute?" reads like Kavi's mechanism-interrogation move written in Sam's mouth — a corrected Sam question would ask when the drill stops being boring, not why the structure is built the way it is.
- **Nadia.** Both are terse. Nadia's short lines are commands or near-commands aimed at what should happen next; Sam's short lines are self-referential facts or a repeated stance about himself, and when he wants something to happen he acts instead of directing someone else to do it.
