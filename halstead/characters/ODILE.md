# Odile

The most physically developed student in the cohort — first to intercept a falling arrow, longest hold ever recorded against ten opponents — who has no way to know either of those things is remarkable, because everyone she trains with all day is exceptional too.

**Age span:** ~11–21 (on-page presence chapters/14–18, roughly ages 11–15; one later mention, narration only, in the 17–21 band)
**MBTI:** ISFP, a writing aid — private, physical, protective of the people closest to her, and slow to put anything about herself into words
**IQ:** reader inference only — high enough to be admitted to every school she applied to and to hold the best physical numbers in her year, though she would put herself around a B+, because everyone she trains and eats dinner with all day is also exceptional and nothing in her daily life gives her another comparison
**Appears in:** chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md, 15_twelve.md, 16_thirteen.md, 17_fourteen.md, 18_fifteen.md; CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch16 "The Applications" (two sentences of narration, no scene, no dialogue)
**Speaks:** 3 lines in the entire book. That's a note on the manuscript's current draft, not a description of who she is — use this sheet to write her, and treat her dialogue as ground still to be built.

## Voice in one paragraph

Two of her three lines are short and flat: "Ruth was bored in the briefing," (chapters/17_fourteen.md:17) and "That was nearly a whole minute," (chapters/17_fourteen.md:53). The third and earliest is longer and warmer: "Sunday. My brother's here Sunday, he's been three times and he's never seen anybody do it. I'm making him stand at your end." (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:49) — the one time the text lets her want something for its own sake, and it comes out as a small run of sentences, not a fragment. That's the real pattern: brief and observational about other people, expansive about her brother.

## Signature — theirs alone

She answers a direct question with a bare number and stops:
- "How long did you hold it?" / "Sixty-nine."
- "How many schools?" / "Twelve."

She disagrees by doing the thing rather than arguing it. Real anchor: when Priya and Sam are still arguing about who ended a relationship and when, Odile — who was actually in the room — settles it flatly: both of them have got the day wrong (chapters/17_fourteen.md:141). She states the fact once and moves on rather than debating either version down.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She runs toward the sound of trouble rather than waiting to find out what it was — seventy metres in about forty seconds, first on the scene when two arrows come down wrong (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:47). She intercepts more arrows than anyone else in the year, four days out of five by March (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:53). She keeps working with a fresh burn rather than favouring it, holding the scarred palm up across the breakfast table for weeks rather than hiding it (chapters/17_fourteen.md:21). She gets a hand under Chloe's arm and hauls her upright by the wrist mid-sentence rather than waiting for her to get there herself (chapters/17_fourteen.md:53).

Her one large set piece is the minute-and-nine hold against ten opponents, the longest in the reading list. She doesn't call it, and her body ends it for her: she goes to her hands and knees, breathing in pieces against the mat, arms shaking (chapters/18_fifteen.md:93-97). She doesn't experience that round as a record. She has spent a year training in a group where three other people also do the minute; sixty-nine seconds is nine more than the number she already hears named on the mat every week, which is a margin, not a miracle, from where she's standing. Up seven seconds on her own result from the year before (chapters/18_fifteen.md:79) reads to her as steady, expected improvement — the return on daily work, not a discovery about herself.

Two ways a writer most easily gets her wrong: giving her a speech about what a result meant to her — she has never once done this, and the fix is a gesture or a number, not a monologue; and writing her as modest or falsely humble about her own numbers — she isn't being modest, she genuinely cannot see the gap between herself and the room, because the room is full of people who are also exceptional and nobody has ever told any of them otherwise.

## Subjects and schedule

CURRICULUM_GRID.md is Chloe's timetable, not the school's — Odile's own languages and electives:

**Languages, in order:** French, Spanish, Mandarin, Russian, Arabic, German, Norwegian, Danish, and Swedish taken together in a single year, then Finnish, then Estonian. French is the one that came first and free — she grew up speaking it at home, so she arrived at Halstead already bilingual, and everything since has gone into a mind that already knows how to run two languages at once. The three Scandinavian languages landed in one year less because of any special arrangement and more because the grammars sit close enough together that once she had the first, the second and third moved fast — she worked the overlap herself, without waiting to be shown it, and finished the year with three where the syllabus expected one.

**Best at:** getting to a working spoken register fast. The ear and the switching reflex were already built in before Halstead ever taught her a language on purpose.

**Worst at, and still remarkable:** the annual ten-page report. Every year's language ends in one, written in the target language, and hers come back shorter and plainer than most of the room's — three or four clean pages of analysis on a legal document or a week of newspapers, in a language she's had for well under a year, done and handed in before anyone else has finished padding theirs to length. At an ordinary school that is fluent professional writing in a foreign language on a few hundred hours of study. At Halstead, marked against a room that treats Hamilton's fifty-one Federalist Papers in eight months as a reasonable comparison, it reads to her — and to Halstead — as merely adequate.

**Favourite, and why:** archery and interception, which isn't a language at all — it's the one thing she is shown wanting an audience for, telling Chloe her brother is coming specifically to watch and where to stand him for the best view (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:49). Nothing else she does gets that kind of staging.

**The one nobody else took the same way:** the combined Scandinavian year — every other student in the reading list who takes a language takes one at a time; she is the only one shown taking three inside a single year's worth of hours.

**What she teaches the twelves, from thirteen:** Arabic, by the time she's had four years of it — a full working fluency well ahead of what the weekly one-hour session with a twelve-year-old actually needs. She is a more patient teacher of a language's structure than she is a writer of a report in it, and has never once connected the two.

**Secretly proud of:** the interception, past the point any grade requires it. Doing it four days out of five and bringing outside witnesses in more than once is effort in excess of anything the school's own scoring asks for.

**Block two:** climbing, fencing, film, sculpture. Climbing is the one that stuck. She solos routes she hasn't been cleared for, alone, because she trusts her own hands and feet on rock more than she trusts being watched while she works something out.

## Home

French is the language of the house and has been her whole life — she is the only one of the nine who walked into Halstead already speaking two languages, and part of why the school's yearly-language machine has never quite impressed her the way it impresses everyone else. The family is first generation: her parents built a life somewhere new before she was born, and French at the dinner table is one of the few rooms where nothing has to be translated for anybody.

Her mother is a physiotherapist — someone whose entire working vocabulary is what a body can bear, whether it's loading correctly, and how to fix it when it isn't. That's the household fact that shaped Odile: she's been physically competent since before she could read, and it was never remarkable at home, because her mother's whole professional frame treats a strong, well-used body as ordinary maintenance, not achievement. Her father works in construction, on his feet all day, hands rough by the time he's home. Between the two of them, a kid who can run seventy metres flat-out or hold a position under load is doing what the family already does; nobody claps for it.

Her younger brother, Léo, is about nine — four years behind her — and worships her. He's the one she plans a whole visit around: "My brother's here Sunday, he's been three times and he's never seen anybody do it. I'm making him stand at your end." (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:49) At home he follows her from room to room and angles to be the one she practices a hold or a stance on, and she lets him, patiently, in a way she isn't patient about much else. He is the one person in her life for whom what she does still looks like something.

Dinner at home is loud and French and physical — people reach across each other, and a correction is as likely to be a hand resetting someone's shoulder as a sentence, her mother's clinic habits leaking onto the family table. Nobody there is measuring Odile against ninety other kids, so she doesn't perform for her parents the way she sometimes does for peers who are counting; she sleeps late on visits home, eats more than anyone expects, and spends most of a Sunday with Léo attached to her arm.

## Body

The fullest section on this sheet, and the one place the manuscript hands her something concrete to build every other section from.

She is the most physically developed student in her year — broader through the shoulders and forearms than the register the rest of the cohort reads at, and it shows fastest in her hands. A scar runs across her palm from an ejection-port burn taken during marksmanship training, and she doesn't hide it: she holds the burned palm up in plain sight across the breakfast table for weeks after, "because the scab pulls every time she opens her fingers" (chapters/17_fourteen.md:21). Those same hands are what she uses to move other people — she gets one under Chloe's arm and hauls her upright by the wrist rather than offering a palm to take (chapters/17_fourteen.md:53). Years of rope, bowstring, and tools have left them calloused and marked, and rarely still.

She cannot be still, and stands rather than sits given any choice at all. In a room she is against a wall, on the balls of her feet, or already moving toward whatever just made a noise — she covers seventy metres at a dead run in about forty seconds toward the sound of two arrows coming down wrong, arriving before anyone else on the field (chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md:47). Sitting through a class or a meal is the closest thing to discomfort she shows; she takes it by putting her hands to something — resetting her own wraps, checking a knot — rather than fidgeting loosely.

Dressed, she runs practical over anything else: kit that won't catch, sleeves pushed up past the scar rather than pulled down over it, hair kept back and out of the way. She eats fast and a lot, standing when she can get away with it, and finishes before people who started ahead of her. She sleeps hard and doesn't stir easily once she's down, and drops off late on nights she's working something out physically — a climbing sequence, a hold, a count — rather than lying still and thinking in words.

None of this reads as unusual to her. Years of daily training from age ten have compounded past what raw ability alone would explain — she can absorb a punishing physical day and be ready for the next one in a way that would be the whole story at any other school, and here it's just Tuesday.

## Personality off the page

She occupies a room by moving through it rather than settling into it — leaning on a wall, angled toward the door, already weighing whether whatever's happening needs her hands. Bored, she finds something to do with them: resetting a knot, turning an object over, correcting someone's stance without being asked. Wrong, she doesn't argue it — she does the corrected version once, silently, and lets that be the correction. Praised, she has no particular reaction, the same way nobody at home ever made one of being strong. Losing, or hitting a hard limit — the clearest data point is the ten-on-one she can't hold past sixty-nine seconds, and the answer is entirely physical: hands and knees, shaking, no words (chapters/18_fifteen.md:93-97), and then dinner as usual an hour later.

Tired, she gets quieter rather than short-tempered, and lets Léo or Chloe do more of the talking. At 2am she is usually still moving something with her hands — a knot, a hold sequence, a piece of kit — rather than lying still. A free Saturday goes to climbing, further out than she's supposed to go alone. What she finds funny in other people is competence done casually, without anyone announcing it — she confirmed Sam's read on Ruth being bored in a briefing (chapters/17_fourteen.md:17) rather than building her own joke, which is the model for her humour generally: agreement, sharpened, delivered flat.

## Interior life

She would put herself around a B+ if anyone made her grade herself, and it isn't modesty — she genuinely has no way to see otherwise. The ten people who come at her in the fighting hour are the same ten she's spent years learning to read, and three of them can also hold the minute; her own sixty-nine seconds sits on a board next to their numbers, not above the room. Nothing in her day supplies the outside comparison that would tell her what it actually means.

What she wants and hasn't said out loud: for Léo to keep being the person who's never seen anybody do it. Being watched by ninety classmates or a scoring sheet does nothing for her; being watched by her brother, once, on a Sunday, is the whole reason she does anything extra. She is quietly afraid of the version of him that stops being amazed — not of losing at anything the school scores.

What she believes that the others would argue with: that none of it is talent, only hours, and a house that never once made a big deal out of any of it. She thinks anyone could do what she does if they'd started at four instead of eleven and had a parent who thought a strong body was as ordinary as a clean plate.

What she's actually wrong about: the hours are real and they matter, and she is also simply better at this than nearly everyone she will ever train beside, and no amount of hours explains the whole gap. She has no way to find that out, because nobody around her — student or teacher — is in the business of telling anyone the truth about where they stand.

Given a free year, she'd want somewhere with real rock and nobody timing her.

## The small stuff

She takes coffee if it's offered and doesn't ask for it otherwise. Her handwriting is quick and small, more like a list than a letter — the same economy that keeps her reports short. Her pockets, dressed for anything but training, usually hold a hair tie and nothing decorative. Money doesn't interest her much as a subject; she'd rather spend a free hour moving than deciding what to buy. Her one small ritual is checking a knot or a strap twice before trusting it, even when she checked it a minute ago. On a phone she's fast and short, texts the way she talks — flat, factual, done. First thing in the morning she's up before most people, already dressed for whatever the first block is.

## Known problems

1. **Almost no dialogue to build from.** Three quoted lines in the whole book. Everything in Home, Body, and Interior life above exists to give a writer a person to extend, but her voice on the page stays thin until new scenes give her more of it.
2. **Don't let her react to her own record as if it were a discovery.** The instinct will be to give the sixty-nine-second hold a beat where she registers what she's done. She can't — she has spent years around three other people who also do the minute, so the number sits in a crowd from where she's standing. Any scene of her being told or shown that it's the best in the school needs an outside voice doing the telling; it can't come from her.
3. **The earlier draft of this sheet gave her a line about arguing for and winning the combined language year, and framed her as the only student who'd successfully renegotiated a school rule.** Cut. It doesn't fit a person who settles things by doing rather than arguing, and it read as a distinction manufactured for its own sake rather than something that follows from her. What's kept is smaller and more consistent: she worked out the overlap between the three Scandinavian languages herself.
4. **An earlier draft also gave her a private, unspoken fear of water, built specifically to contradict how fearless she reads elsewhere.** Cut. It had no textual anchor, and it was constructed as a reveal waiting for a future scene rather than something true of her now. Her actual private worry — that Léo will eventually stop being amazed — does the same job without needing a future payoff to justify it.

## Do not confuse with

**Ruth** — both are physically capable and comfortable correcting people, but Ruth corrects with a sentence and Odile corrects with her hands; a line where the correction is spoken and reasoned belongs to Ruth, a line where it's just done belongs to Odile.

**Nadia** — both are short, declarative, and undemonstrative, but Nadia names people directly mid-sentence as her signature move; Odile's default is naming what someone is doing or feeling ("Ruth was bored") rather than addressing them by name.
