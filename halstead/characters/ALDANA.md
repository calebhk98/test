# Carmen Aldana

Chloe's first-grade teacher: nineteen years into a job she is still good at, running a room of twenty-nine on a fair, exhausted, one-speed system that has no setting for a kid who finishes in ninety seconds and has already learned that the safe thing to do about it is nothing.

## Voice in one paragraph

She talks in verdicts, not sentences. Four words is her ceiling for anything that isn't a direct question. She never explains a decision, and she never raises the stakes of an infraction past the infraction itself: the book is confiscated "for the rest of the day," and it comes back. Her most characteristic line is "'That's what I thought.' Mrs. Aldana doesn't say it mean. She says it tired." (chapters/01_before.md:16). Four words of verdict carry the whole indictment, sitting in the one word the narration uses for how she says it.

## When they talk at length

Carmen goes past her own four-word ceiling when she is with one family rather than twenty-nine children, and has to give a full, mixed read of a child instead of a flat line about one worksheet. No scene stages it yet; all seven of her quoted lines hold to the verdict-and-move-on shape, and the one place the text puts her in that position is a parent conference that happens off the page, in writing: she "writes both a real strength and a real concern on the same conference report, honestly, knowing they might read as contradictory to a worried parent." A conference is the one setting structurally different from her classroom, one family instead of twenty-nine kids waiting on the same lesson, so it's the one place explaining wouldn't cost the rest of the room anything. The manuscript still doesn't stage her saying any of it aloud.

A conference on the page is the scene this condition is waiting for, and it would cost her something real: the flat, identical rule she holds for all twenty-nine kids is her whole definition of fairness, and giving one family a fuller account than the room ever gets, even for good reason, is exactly the kind of exception she otherwise refuses to make.

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

Carmen runs a room of twenty-nine children. She waits for every one of them to finish a worksheet before she moves the lesson forward, and that wait, fair to twenty-eight of them, empties the twenty-ninth kid's morning of anything to do. What she is looking at, most days, is a compliant six-year-old who has already worked out that the safe answer is the short one, sitting still, hands in her lap, giving Carmen nothing that reads as a problem. "That's what I thought" lands "tired" because Carmen has watched this exact pattern hundreds of times and has no second track to offer a kid who finishes early: the curriculum gives her one worksheet, one pace, one classroom of twenty-nine, and nothing else. A line for Carmen belongs on the page if it is a small, fair, exhausted response to that math. It falls off the page if it turns the verdict into a judgment of Chloe specifically, singling her out to the class or letting a confiscated book run past the day it was taken. She never does either.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She keeps a folder on Chloe that predates her own year with the class, but never opens it in front of anyone. She reads papers aloud on Fridays anonymously and lets the class guess. She writes both a real strength and a real concern on the same conference report, honestly, knowing they might read as contradictory to a worried parent.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:** making her cruel or sarcastic, the text specifies "doesn't say it mean"; giving her insight into why Chloe is inattentive that she never shows; writing new dialogue for her outside chapters/01 without flagging it as new invention.

## Subjects and schedule

Teaches first grade, self-contained: reading, spelling, arithmetic, the water cycle, weekly writing, to twenty-nine students. She paces a whole room toward the same idea at once, the green-marker box for carrying, the water-cycle arrows everyone recites back in order. She has no second track for a child who finishes before the lesson has properly started, because the school has never given her one to offer. She runs a free two-week pre-reading camp at the public library every August for incoming kindergartners, unpaid beyond a small stipend, and has done it for eleven years. Her fourth year teaching, a boy who had been silent since September read his own sentence out loud on a Friday without being asked twice, and that afternoon she understood the read-aloud ritual was doing real work. She is proud that in nineteen years she has never once lost her temper in front of a class. She has been tempted plenty. She goes quiet and tired instead.

## Home

Grew up two hours away in a smaller town, the daughter of a mechanic and a school cafeteria worker, the first person in her family to finish a four-year degree. Married young, took the name Aldana, divorced amicably in her early thirties, and kept the name because several hundred children had already learned to spell it. She has no children of her own; she has thirty new ones every September and has made her peace with that. She lives eleven minutes from the school in a rented two-bedroom with a cat named after a state capital she once visited on a teaching conference. Her mother, retired now, lives forty minutes away and calls every Sunday at six. Her father died when Carmen was thirty-one, and she still sometimes reaches to call him on the drive home before she remembers. Dinner on a school night is whatever's fastest, eaten standing at the kitchen counter while she grades. On Sundays she cooks a proper pot of something, a stew, a chili, that she eats off of all week, the one domestic ritual she actually enjoys.

## Body

Mid-forties, average height, a little soft through the middle from eight hours on her feet and none of it sitting. She started needing reading glasses two years ago and wears them on a beaded chain, patting her own chest to find them. She keeps her hair short and practical, having learned in year two that long hair and thirty six-year-olds do not mix. She wears cardigans with deep pockets year-round, even in warm months, because the pockets hold the classroom: chalk, a spare pencil, hand sanitizer, a rubber band she keeps meaning to use. She wears comfortable, unglamorous flat shoes, replaced twice a year. She moves through the room in short, economical steps. She sleeps well, and credits the physical exhaustion of the job for it.

## Personality off the page

She holds one rule for all twenty-nine kids, the same way, every day, with no exceptions carved out for anyone; that consistency is her definition of fairness. She is rarely bored; the day leaves no room for it. When she's corrected, by a parent or an administrator, she takes it quietly, turns it over privately, and slowly changes something. Praised, she deflects it onto the class: they did the work, and she means it. Losing an argument, she goes flat and short, the same register she uses on a misbehaving six-year-old. Tired is her one confirmed emotional register in the text: flat, short, unangry. At two in the morning, if she's up, it's report-card season and she's at the kitchen table with a stack of folders. A free Saturday runs library, then her mother's, then home with a book she picked for herself. She finds earnest overconfidence in someone half her age funny, and has a soft spot for it because she remembers having it herself.

## Interior life

She wants one year with a class small enough to build a second track for the two or three kids who finish everything early. She has never had the staffing or the time, and has stopped saying so out loud because nothing changes when she does. She is afraid she is quietly losing a kid to the system's slowness every year without finding out which one, because the ones who go quiet don't make noise on the way down. Before sleep she runs through tomorrow's read-aloud pick and whether a particular grade was fair. Given a free year, she would spend it reading everything she's never had time for and running her library camp full-time. She believes a mandatory single pace for an entire room is a policy failure dressed up as fairness, though she has never said this in a staff meeting because she has no alternative that's fair to the other twenty-eight. She is wrong that there's no room in her classroom for an early finisher: the room exists, she has simply never had the minutes to build it, and she has stopped believing they're coming.

## The small stuff

She drinks black coffee from a thermos, refilled in the teacher's lounge at 7:10 sharp, every day, without variation. Her handwriting is small, fast, correct cursive she was taught in the 1980s and never updated. Her desk runs on a color-coded folder system nobody else in the building uses. Her pockets hold chalk, a spare pencil, hand sanitizer, a rubber band she never uses. She clips coupons and buys her own classroom supplies rather than file the reimbursement paperwork, because the paperwork costs more time than the money is worth. The Friday read-aloud happens on a Friday, never moved, never skipped; the week feels unfinished to her otherwise. She barely touches her phone during school hours, and texts her mother back at 3:45 sharp, right after dismissal, every day. First thing every morning, she unlocks the room by seven and waters a spider plant on the windowsill.
