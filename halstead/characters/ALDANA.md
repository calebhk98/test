# Carmen Aldana

Chloe's first-grade teacher: nineteen years into a job she is still good at, running a room of twenty-nine on a fair, exhausted, one-speed system that has no setting for a kid who finishes in ninety seconds and has already learned that the safe thing to do about it is nothing.

## Voice in one paragraph

She talks in verdicts, not sentences. Four words is her ceiling for anything that isn't a direct question, she never explains a decision, and she never raises the stakes of an infraction past the infraction itself — the book is confiscated "for the rest of the day," and it comes back. Her most characteristic line is "'That's what I thought.' Mrs. Aldana doesn't say it mean. She says it tired." (chapters/01_before.md:14) — four words of verdict, and the entire indictment of the character sitting in the one word the narration uses to describe how she says it.

## Signature — theirs alone

A short flat verdict, then an immediate physical action that ends the exchange without further comment:
- "'That's what I thought.' ... Then she taps the worksheet twice with one finger and walks back up to the front of the room." (chapters/01_before.md:14)
- "'Then find chapter four, please.' ... Mrs. Aldana comes down the row and takes the book, gently, with two fingers on the spine." (chapters/01_before.md:115–117)

- YES if the delivery reads as tired rather than angry.

## The kindly-and-inadequate test

Carmen is not failing to notice something obviously extraordinary. What she's looking at is a compliant, quiet six-year-old who has already worked out that the safe answer is the short one — Chloe herself builds this strategy deliberately over the course of the year, and it works precisely because it gives Carmen nothing to notice. A line for Carmen is **kindly-and-inadequate** if the failure is structural: she runs a room of twenty-nine the way a room that size has to be run — wait for everyone, one pace, no exceptions — and the rule that's fair to twenty-eight kids is exactly the rule that starves the twenty-ninth. There is nothing in the curriculum, the staffing, or the school day that gives her a second track to offer, so a well-behaved kid who finishes early simply reads as fine. Nothing in her lines is aimed at Chloe as a person; "That's what I thought" is delivered "tired," not mean, because Carmen isn't diagnosing Chloe, she's naming a pattern she's seen hundreds of times. A line would be **unkind** — and nothing in the text supports it — if it turned the verdict into a judgment of Chloe specifically, or let a confiscated item run past the school day. She never does either.

## Are they acting like themselves?

She keeps a folder on Chloe that predates her own year with the class, but never opens it in front of anyone. She reads papers aloud on Fridays anonymously and lets the class guess. She writes both a real strength and a real concern on the same conference report, honestly, even knowing together they might read as contradictory to a worried parent. What she does not do, because nothing in her day gives her the occasion to, is ever see Chloe finish a worksheet and wonder what would happen if the room had something harder in it.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:** making her cruel or sarcastic (the text specifies "doesn't say it mean"); giving her insight into why Chloe is inattentive that she never shows; writing new dialogue for her outside chapters/01 without flagging it as new invention.

## Subjects and schedule

Teaches first grade, self-contained: reading, spelling, arithmetic, the water cycle, weekly writing, to twenty-nine students. Best at pacing a whole room toward the same idea at once — the green-marker box for carrying, the water-cycle arrows everyone recites back in order. Worst at, structurally rather than personally: has no second track for a child who's finished before the lesson has properly started, because the school has never given her one to offer. Runs a free two-week pre-reading camp at the public library every August for incoming kindergartners, unpaid beyond a small stipend, which she has done for eleven years — it's where her Friday read-aloud ritual actually comes from originally. The year it clicked for her: her fourth year teaching, when a boy who'd been silent since September read his own sentence out loud on a Friday without being asked twice, and she understood the read-aloud ritual was doing real work, not just filling ten minutes. Secretly proud of: in nineteen years she has never once lost her temper in front of a class — she has been tempted plenty, and has always instead just gone quiet and tired instead of loud.

## Home

Grew up two hours away in a smaller town, the daughter of a mechanic and a school cafeteria worker, the first person in her family to finish a four-year degree. Married young, took the name Aldana, divorced amicably in her early thirties, and kept the name because by then several hundred children had already learned to spell it. No children of her own — she has thirty new ones every September and has mostly made peace with that being different. Lives eleven minutes from the school in a rented two-bedroom with a cat named after a state capital she once visited on a teaching conference. Her mother, retired now, lives forty minutes away and calls every Sunday at six; her father died when Carmen was thirty-one, and she still sometimes reaches to call him on the drive home before remembering. Dinner on a school night is whatever's fastest, eaten standing at the kitchen counter while she grades; on Sundays she cooks a proper pot of something — a stew, a chili — that she eats off of all week, the one domestic ritual she actually enjoys.

## Body

Mid-forties, average height, a little soft through the middle in the specific way of someone who stands eight hours a day and sits for none of it. Started needing reading glasses two years ago and wears them on a beaded chain she constantly forgets is around her neck, patting her own chest to find them. Keeps her hair short and practical — long hair and thirty six-year-olds do not mix, she learned this the hard way in year two. Wears cardigans with deep pockets year-round, even in warm months, because the pockets are where the classroom actually lives — chalk, a spare pencil, hand sanitizer, a rubber band she keeps meaning to use and never does. Comfortable, unglamorous flat shoes, replaced twice a year. Moves through the room in short, economical steps, never wasting a trip between desks. Sleeps well, unusually for a teacher — she credits the physical exhaustion of the job itself.

## Personality off the page

She believes the job is being run correctly: one rule, applied the same way to all twenty-nine kids, every day, with no exceptions carved out for anyone — to her, that consistency is fairness. Bored: rarely, there isn't time in her day for it. Wrong: takes correction from a parent or an administrator quietly, turns it over privately rather than arguing in the moment, and usually does change something, slowly. Praised: deflects it onto the class — "they did the work" — and means it. Losing an argument: goes flat and short rather than raising her voice, the same register she uses on a misbehaving six-year-old. Tired: the text's one confirmed emotional register — flat, short, unangry. At 2am: rarely awake for it; when she is, it's report-card season and she's at the kitchen table with a stack of folders. A free Saturday: the library, then her mother's, then home with a book she picked for herself, not for a lesson plan. What she finds funny in other people: earnest overconfidence in someone half her age — she has a soft spot for it because she remembers having it herself.

## Interior life

Wants, unsaid: one year, just one, with a class small enough that she could actually build a second track for the two or three kids who finish everything early — she has never had the staffing or the time, and she has stopped saying so out loud because nothing ever changes when she does. Afraid of: that she is quietly losing a kid to the system's slowness every single year without ever finding out which one, because the ones who go quiet don't make noise on the way down. Thinks about before sleep: tomorrow's read-aloud pick, and whether a particular grade was fair. A free year: she'd spend it reading everything she's never had time for and running her library camp full-time. Believes something the school administration would argue with: that a mandatory single pace for an entire room is a policy failure dressed up as fairness, though she has never said this in a staff meeting because she doesn't see an alternative that's fair to the other twenty-eight. What she's wrong about: that there's no room in her classroom for an early finisher — there is room, she has simply never been given the minutes to build it, and she has stopped believing they're coming.

## The small stuff

Drinks black coffee from a thermos, refilled in the teacher's lounge at 7:10 sharp, every day, without variation. Handwriting: small, fast, correct cursive she was taught in the 1980s and never updated. Her desk is organized around a color-coded folder system nobody else in the building uses but her. Pockets: chalk, a spare pencil, hand sanitizer, a rubber band she never uses. Clips coupons and frequently buys her own classroom supplies rather than filing the reimbursement paperwork, because the paperwork takes longer than the money is worth to her. Superstition: the Friday read-aloud happens on a Friday, never moved, never skipped — the week feels unfinished to her otherwise. Barely touches her phone during school hours; texts her mother back at 3:45 sharp, right after dismissal, every day. First thing every morning: unlocks the room by 7:00 and waters a spider plant on the windowsill before anything else happens.

## Would do

- Take a distraction away calmly and hand it back at the end of the same day, no added consequence.
- Read a quiet or overlooked kid's work aloud anonymously and let the class's own guess be the compliment.
- Put a genuine strength and a genuine concern on the same report card, honestly, even knowing they might read as contradictory.
- Let a struggling reader keep working through a passage without visible impatience.
- Mention a change in a child's demeanor to the parents, plainly, once, and leave the follow-up to them.
- Run a free summer reading program for incoming students on her own time, for over a decade.
- Buy her own classroom supplies rather than fight the reimbursement paperwork.
- Water the same plant on the same windowsill every single morning before the day starts.

## Would never

- Single a child out to the class as the reason a lesson is running slow.
- Discuss what's in a student's file with anyone but that child's own parents.
- Let a confiscated item stay taken past the day it was taken on.
