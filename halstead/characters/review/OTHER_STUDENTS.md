# Other students — risk review for the reading-level pass

Scope: PRIYA.md, ODILE.md, OWEN.md, FEN.md, KAYLEIGH.md, BRYCE.md, checked against
`_CALIBRATION.md`, `_DIFFERENTIATION.md`, `CHARACTER_SHEETS.md`, and all 35 chapters.
All quoted-line and mention counts below were re-counted directly against the manuscript
with `grep -n` (word-bounded where the name collides with ordinary words, e.g. `\bFen\b`
against "fencing"); none were taken on the index's word.

## 1. Verdict

These six can absorb a pass that makes the scenes *around* them longer and more
subordinated — Chloe's narration, other people's dialogue, the density of what's reported
about them — without a fight. What they cannot absorb, in five of the six cases, is a pass
that makes *them* say more or say it in a more complex way, because five of the six sheets
contain an explicit, load-bearing rule against exactly that move: no spoken self-explanation
(Odile), no subordinate reasoning or hedge (Kayleigh), no analysis of the mechanism behind
an observation (Bryce), no self-revelation (Fen), and, most acutely, no dialogue at all
built on a foundation the sheet itself calls invention (Owen). Only Priya is built as an
exception with headroom to spare — she already runs on, already chains clauses, and the
sheet's own warning is against trimming her down, not against expanding her. For Kayleigh
and Bryce the stakes are higher than voice consistency: their flat, unexplained delivery is
the mechanism the book uses to test "matter-of-fact versus insult," so adding subordination
to their lines risks breaking the argument the scene is making, not just their character.
Net: raise the register of the scenes these six sit in; leave what comes out of their own
mouths close to where it is, with volume added only inside the register each sheet already
licenses.

## 3. Per character

### Priya
**Can already carry:** initiation and consequence, not just presence. She gives away a
hard assignment (14_sixty_degrees.md:113), reads a scored exchange faster than the
scoreboard and hands the win away (19_sixteen.md:157-165), dismantles other people's plans
for forty minutes (15_twelve.md:69), and is the only person in the cohort who writes to
Owen for years without telling anyone (PRIYA.md:229-233, an interior-life claim, not yet
staged). She is also the cast's one licensed run-on speaker (_DIFFERENTIATION.md:70,74-78)
and the one character whose sheet actively forbids trimming her toward the group's
economical register: "Trimming her lines to match the cast's dominant economical register...
erases the one trait the cast needs her for." (PRIYA.md:92-93)
**What the sheet forbids:** very little, which is itself notable among these six. The one
real constraint is the figurative-language rule — animals only, and she must not notice
she's done it (PRIYA.md:36, 66) — which rules out the kind of witty or self-aware imagery a
pass reaching for "more voice" might otherwise reach for.
**Single highest-leverage change:** build the two permissions the sheet already grants but
the text doesn't yet use — one unmarked animal comparison, and one on-page instance of
"agrees out loud and carries on doing what she was doing" (both flagged unused in Known
Problems 3-4, PRIYA.md:374-379). That gives a reviser working material inside an
already-approved register instead of material they'd have to invent from nothing.

### Odile
**Can already carry:** physical initiative and a small number of exact, plain lines. She
runs first toward the sound of trouble (14_sixty_degrees.md:49), settles a factual dispute
between two other people in one line (17_fourteen.md:143), and her interior-life section
gives real self-deception, not just habits: she believes "none of this is talent," while the
sheet counters that she is "simply better at this than nearly everyone she will ever train
beside, and no number of hours closes that gap" (ODILE.md:111-113).
**What the sheet forbids:** spoken self-explanation, permanently. "NO if she explains her
own motives or feelings out loud. Never happens on the page." (ODILE.md:46) "Would never...
Explain her own motives or feelings in words after a major result." (ODILE.md:138) Combined
with a total ban on figurative language (ODILE.md:27), this closes off both of the two
moves a complexity pass most often reaches for on a quiet character — give them a
metaphor, or give them a moment where they say what they're feeling.
**Single highest-leverage change:** more of the same three-line register, not a new one.
She has exactly three quoted lines to build a whole voice from; two or three more terse,
number-first or fact-correcting lines (in the register already tested at
17_fourteen.md:19,55) would give a reviser enough material to work a scene around her
without needing to break the no-self-explanation rule to get there.

### Owen
**Can already carry:** almost nothing directly — he has zero quoted lines and is
physically on the page in exactly one chapter. What he does carry is what other characters
do around his absence: Chloe's failed attempt to build a theory of him
(05_behind.md:85), and the gap between Sam's and Kavi's accounts nine months apart
(05_behind.md:69-72 vs. 10_april.md:101-103).
**What the sheet forbids:** effectively all dialogue growth. His own "Would they say this?"
test rules out anything self-analytic ("He's six, and everything about him is concrete and
outcome-shaped, never self-analyzing," OWEN.md:30) or performed for a group (OWEN.md:29).
Giving him more or longer lines to "let a scene breathe" would contradict the one fact that
defines him: he could not produce two finished sentences in fifteen minutes while the rest
of his row filled a page. Eloquence of any kind is the one thing calibration rules out for
him.
**Single highest-leverage change:** the author should resolve, explicitly, Known Problem 3 —
"there's no way to tell 'he was designed to never speak' from 'he simply hasn't been given a
line yet.'" (OWEN.md:120) That single decision, made before the revision pass rather than
during it, removes the biggest temptation in this set: writing Owen a new scene with a
voice the manuscript never gave him.

### Fen
**Can already carry:** action and transaction. The rock collection that outlasts every room
change, and a loan she structures at a stated rate against real collateral
(18_fifteen.md:111) while Priya's parallel loan in the same sentence is unsecured — that
contrast is already textured and already distinguishes her from Priya without a single new
line of dialogue.
**What the sheet forbids:** self-revelation and any dialogue not already in the text. "NO if
it's emotionally expressive or seeks reassurance." "NO if it's about herself unprompted."
(FEN.md:29-30) And plainly: "No verbatim line exists to build on. Any dialogue written for
Fen from this point is new work, not a restoration of an existing voice." (FEN.md:127)
**Single highest-leverage change:** because she persists as a background presence for a
decade of story time (age 8 through 17-18) without ever getting a second scene of quoted
dialogue, the highest-value addition is one more beat in the register her one data point
already supports — practical, mid-task, unsentimental — not an emotional one. A second
transactional or room-history beat costs nothing against the sheet; a confiding one costs
everything.

### Kayleigh
**Can already carry:** exactly what's on the page — five flat, accurate, three-to-seven-word
lines, all verified. Her sheet also flags an untested register that could add texture without
adding forbidden subordination: "YES if it's warm, casual, and unguarded when it's aimed at
a friend rather than at something odd she's just noticed. Her recorded lines are all the
second kind, but nothing about her rules out the first." (KAYLEIGH.md:38)
**What the sheet forbids:** any reasoning attached to her judgments. "NO if it contains a
'because,' a qualifier, or any explanation of why she thinks what she thinks. Asked for a
reason, she declines to give one." (KAYLEIGH.md:40) And the sheet is explicit about the
stakes of breaking that: "Giving her a reason. The instant she explains why something is
weird or unfair, she stops being Kayleigh and starts being an arguer." (KAYLEIGH.md:49)
**Single highest-leverage change:** stage the warm-with-friends register once, aimed at
Maddie rather than at Chloe. That adds a second mode and more total volume without touching
the Chloe-facing lines the book's own "matter-of-fact versus insult" test depends on.

### Bryce
**Can already carry:** the single most load-bearing outside observation in the book, on two
lines. His sheet is honest about the risk of that ratio: "Load-bearing on almost no
material... The character had no other scene, trait, or line to support that weight before
this pass." (BRYCE.md:137)
**What the sheet forbids:** any account of why he noticed what he noticed. "NO if it
explains the mechanism behind the change. He reports the observation; he does not analyze
it." (BRYCE.md:38) "Making him explain the 'weirder' line. He reports it and stops."
(BRYCE.md:46)
**Single highest-leverage change:** one more observation of the same flat before/after shape,
not a deeper one. A second instance of "reports, then disengages" would let a scene lean on
him twice instead of once, without asking two lines to carry more analytical weight than
the character is allowed to have.

## 4. What will fight the next pass, ranked

1. **Kayleigh's and Bryce's bans on explanatory clauses are tied to the book's argument,
   not just their voices.** "NO if it contains a 'because'... Asked for a reason, she
   declines to give one." (KAYLEIGH.md:40) "NO if it explains the mechanism behind the
   change." (BRYCE.md:38) CHARACTER_SHEETS.md is explicit that this is load-bearing:
   "Both sheets carry the test the book depends on: a matter-of-fact complaint versus an
   insult. The second breaks them, and breaks the book's argument." Adding subordination
   to either of their lines to raise the scene's register doesn't just flatten a
   character — it risks converting a flat report into something that reads as reasoned
   cruelty, which the book's chapter-07 scene is built to prove isn't what's happening.
   **Alternative:** raise the register of everything around their two lines — Chloe's
   narration, the frame sentence the book already supplies ("Bryce says it interested, the
   way you'd tell somebody their shoe was untied," 07_the_same_room.md:139) — and leave the
   lines themselves untouched as the fixed, flat points the scene is measured against.

2. **Odile's ban on spoken self-explanation, on a base of three lines total.** "NO if she
   explains her own motives or feelings out loud. Never happens on the page." (ODILE.md:46)
   A pass that wants scenes to breathe will be pulled toward giving her a reaction beat
   after the ten-on-one hold or after the admissions letter; both are explicitly against
   the character ("Would never... Explain her own motives or feelings in words after a
   major result," ODILE.md:138). **Alternative:** grow her presence through what other
   people say about her (the admissions officer's letter already does this,
   21_the_applications.md:58) and through more instances of her own number-first,
   fact-correcting register, not through an emotional register she has never had.

3. **Fen has no verbatim voice to extend at all, and the sheet's Home section is the
   richest, best-written, and least anchored material in this set** — full paragraphs on a
   grandmother, a multigenerational household, and a community history, built from one
   two-line reported passage. The temptation this creates is the opposite of the Kayleigh/
   Bryce risk: not breaking an existing line, but inventing a whole new one wholesale to
   hit a length or complexity target, backed by a sheet detailed enough to make that feel
   safe. **Alternative:** extend her through more of the practical/transactional beats
   already licensed (the loan, the rocks, the room-history list), keeping any interiority in
   Chloe's narration about her rather than in Fen's own mouth.

4. **Owen is the extreme version of the same trap, with the highest cost if it's missed.**
   Zero quoted lines, and a Family/Interior-life section built almost entirely past what the
   text supports (Della, Biscuit, the keychain, the father's job) with the sheet's own
   admission: "everything past that is this sheet's proposal." (OWEN.md:110) Any new scene
   or line for him has to reckon with the fact that he is, by design, the one person in this
   cohort who could not produce the fluent, complex output a "raise the reading level" pass
   is aiming for — that inability is the entire reason he's in the book.
   **Alternative:** resolve Known Problem 3 (OWEN.md:120) before the pass starts, rather
   than during it — decide on purpose whether he ever speaks, and if so, keep any line to a
   named, concrete thing, never an argument or an explanation (OWEN.md:20, 30).

5. **Priya's own run-on register is present but thin — two solid examples across the whole
   book, per her own Known Problems** (PRIYA.md:369-373), which creates a milder but real
   risk: a pass under time pressure either overcorrects her toward the cast's terse
   baseline (explicitly warned against, PRIYA.md:92-93) or overcorrects the other way into
   wit and metaphor the sheet doesn't license ("figurative | allowed, but only about
   animals, and she will not notice she did it," PRIYA.md:36). **Alternative:** use her two
   existing chained-clause passages (04_pluto.md:19; 15_twelve.md:67) as the literal
   template for any new Priya line — same connectives, same lack of a stopping point, no
   added wit.

## 5. What is already working — protect this

- **Priya as the cast's one built exception.** She already runs on, already carries real
  consequence (money lost and named precisely, a scoreboard result handed away, years of
  unmentioned letters), and the sheet already tells a reviser not to sand her down to match
  everyone else. This is the one piece of this group that makes the reading-level pass
  easier, not harder — do not lose it in a general tightening pass.
- **The dialogue counts are genuinely accurate.** Every quoted-line figure this review
  independently re-counted against the manuscript — Priya's 15, Odile's 3, Owen's 0, Fen's
  0, Kayleigh's 5, Bryce's 2 — checked out exactly. That discipline (verify against the
  text, don't inherit a stale count) is worth keeping as the standard for any sheet a
  revision pass touches.
- **The "matter-of-fact versus insult" test in Kayleigh and Bryce.** It is small, plainly
  stated, and it is what lets two characters with almost no page time carry real
  thematic weight. It should be treated as fixed, not as raw material for a complexity pass.
- **Owen's sheet holds the calibration line.** It explicitly rejects the failure mode
  `_CALIBRATION.md` warns against — "Do not write him as a genius who chose to leave"
  (`_CALIBRATION.md:84`) — with its own matching language: "He wanted to stay and could not
  do the work. The leaving was not a preference." (OWEN.md:38) This is the sheet in the set
  most exposed to being rewritten wrong, and it currently isn't. Any editor touching Owen
  should be pointed at this line first.
- **The self-deception written into every interior-life section.** Odile's belief that "none
  of this is talent," Priya's blind spot about other people's relationship to rank, Kayleigh's
  untested assumption about explaining herself, Bryce's naivety about people who don't just
  report what they see, Fen's misreading of her own record-keeping as detachment rather than
  investment — all six sheets give real contradiction, not just preference lists. That's
  usable interiority for a revision pass, so long as it stays in narration and reported
  action rather than being moved into these characters' own mouths.

## 6. One finding not yet flagged elsewhere

Priya's mother "works the front office of a dental practice... scheduling and billing"
(PRIYA.md:168, repeated at PRIYA.md:336) and Kayleigh's mother "works the front desk at a
dentist's office" (KAYLEIGH.md:68, repeated as "a dental-office front desk" at
KAYLEIGH.md:131) are the same invented occupation assigned to two different characters'
mothers. Neither sheet's own audit trail catches this. It sits outside the scope of the
reading-level risk this review was asked to find, but it is a distinctness collision worth
the author's attention before these two families appear on the page together.
