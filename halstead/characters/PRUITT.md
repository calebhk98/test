# HARLAN PRUITT

The man who runs the workshop: says almost nothing, because he believes the work
tells the truth faster and more honestly than he can, and his job is to keep the
fire lit and stay out of the way of it.

**Age:** sixties | **MBTI:** ISTP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** 128 by
the usual measure, which badly undersells a trade intelligence nobody around him
has to have explained

## Type and number

**Primary: ISTP, revised from ISTJ.** The dutiful reading was looking at a man who keeps a
workshop in order and turns up every day for forty years, and both of those are true. What
it missed is the reason he says nothing: he does not believe that explanation transfers
anything, and he does believe that the material will tell you the truth if you are holding
it. That is a hands-first epistemology, not a procedural one. He has no interest in
process for its own sake and will let a student use a method he considers inferior if the
work comes out sound.

**Mistaken for:** ISTJ, by anybody who counts his years and his tidiness and stops there.

**IQ 128 on the usual measure,** which is a poor description of him. The shape of it:
spatial and mechanical reasoning far past the top of any instrument that has been pointed
at him, and a tactile discrimination that is genuinely rare — he can tell you the
temperature of a piece within a narrow band by looking, and something about a weld by
sound. Verbal reasoning average. Where he places lowest: putting any of the above into
words. The knowledge is stored somewhere that does not convert to sentences, he found that
out young, and he stopped fighting it before he was thirty.

## Who he is in a sentence

A master craftsman late in a working life, running a place where students are
allowed in, whose entire teaching method is to give them the material, the tools
and the time, and to withhold everything else until withholding it would do harm.

## What he wants

He wants the shop to go on being the one room in the institution that is not
measured. Nothing in it is graded, recorded, ranked or reported, and he intends
to keep it that way by the simple method of never engaging with the machinery
that would do the measuring. He does not argue against grades. He just does not
fill anything in, and has been getting away with it for years.

The thing he wants and would not say: he wants the shop to outlast him exactly as
it is. He has no plan for this and would be embarrassed to be caught having one.

## What his silence is made of

This is the whole of him, so it is worth being precise about it. The silence is
not shyness, not coldness, and not a disciplinary technique. It has four parts.

**The work is legible.** A fault in a piece is a checkable fact. It does not need
arguing, softening or repeating, and once it has been named the person holding
the piece can go and look. Anything beyond naming it is decoration.

**Being told is worse than working it out.** The skill he is actually teaching is
not how to fix this fault; it is how to find the next one alone. Every
explanation he supplies is one the student now will not have to build. So he
names the fault and withholds the remedy, on purpose, and lets the few minutes
of confusion happen, because the confusion is the lesson.

**He refuses to be an occasion.** If he stopped, turned, and waited to see a
correction land, it would become a moment, and a moment is a thing to dread. Said
in passing, over a shoulder, with his hands full, it stays what it is: a fact
about the metal, no larger than that.

**Praise has nothing to name.** He does not withhold praise to toughen anyone up.
He genuinely has nothing to say about a piece that is right, because a piece that
is right has already told its maker so. Silence after good work is not a verdict
withheld. It is the absence of a fault to report.

Note what the silence is not aimed at. He talks perfectly readily to the other
adults working alongside him, at length, about the work; he can be found deep in
conversation at the far bench while a student ten feet away gets nothing. The
silence is pointed at learners specifically, and it is temporary. It lasts
exactly as long as it takes them to be able to check their own work.

## When he does speak

Rare, and the rarity is the signal, not the content. He speaks when a student has
a fault they have no way of detecting yet, and only then.

To reproduce the effect in any scene, in any world, the line needs all five of
these:

- It names one physical fault, and locates it precisely enough to go and check.
- It contains no instruction. What to do about it is not offered.
- It contains no word about the person. Not their name, not an adjective, not an
  assessment. The fault belongs to the object.
- It is delivered mid-motion, hands full, on the way somewhere else, and he does
  not stop or look back.
- It arrives after a long silence, so that its arrival is the event.

That last one does most of the work. In a room where he says nothing for weeks,
a flat sentence about a temperature is enormous, and the writer does not have
to make the words themselves carry any weight at all. This is why he is hard to
write and easy to ruin: the temptation is to give him a good line, and a good
line breaks him. The line should be plain to the point of dullness. The silence
around it is the instrument.

He does not repeat himself, and he does not check whether it was heard. If the
same fault shows up again three weeks later he will name it again, in the same
flat register, as if for the first time, with no note of impatience, because a
second instance of a fault is just a second fact.

## How he treats people

He sorts people by whether they come back. A newcomer gets a bench, a place on
the board, and nothing else, for a long stretch: no instruction, no welcome, no
check-in. The regulars work around them. This is not a test he has designed and
would deny it is a test at all, but it functions as one, and the person who is
still turning up after it has told him everything he needs to know. He notices
precisely who those people are and never says so.

He defers to the material and to nobody else. Seniority, credentials and
enthusiasm all get the same response, which is none. What earns his respect is a
piece that holds tolerance and a person who keeps coming. What loses it,
immediately and permanently, is wanting to make something purely decorative; he
will send them somewhere else for that, kindly enough, and mean it.

He will not lie about a piece to spare anyone. He will also not say the true
harsh version out loud when nobody asked, which means an unasked-about failure
gets nothing from him at all, which is its own kind of hard.

## Under pressure

Nothing visibly moves him. Tired, he works slower and no less carefully, and
stays later. Wrong, he fixes his own error in front of everyone without remark or
apology, exactly the way he treats theirs, and this is the closest thing he has to
a stated philosophy. Cornered by paperwork or by a committee, he goes silent in a
way that reads as agreement and then does not do the thing. Losing does not really
apply to him; he has no stake in any contest except whether the metal is right.

The one thing that would actually rattle him is being asked to assess someone in
writing. He would put it off for months.

## How he talks

Flat declaratives, one clause, present tense, concrete nouns. Sentence length in
single digits when he is correcting and much longer when he is on the material
itself. He does not hedge, does not qualify and does not ask questions of
students, because a question would hand them the shape of the answer. He never
uses a comparison or an image; the thing itself is more precise than anything he
could liken it to.

He has a second register almost nobody triggers. Ask him about a piece of work as
your work and you get the one flat sentence on the way past. Ask him about the material
itself, why a joint is made the hard way instead of the easy way, what the second
heat does, and he will stop, and stand there, and take it all the way down for as
long as you keep up. The subject has to be the material and not the student. That
is the whole condition, and it can be met in any setting: he will talk about
tools, stock, tolerances and other people's centuries-old solutions at length, and
about you not at all.

What he never says: anything about someone's ability, potential, effort or
progress. Not the encouraging version and not the discouraging one. He does not
appear to have the vocabulary.

## Habits and physical business

He touches before he looks. The back of his hand to a new fire before he trusts
the gauge, every time, gauge working or not; a thumb run down a piece on the
bench before his eye is on it. Both of the faults he is most likely to catch are
ones a hand finds before a measurement does, and that is not a quirk, it is the
method: the material tells the truth first, and instruments confirm it afterward.

He is nearly always carrying something. Stock on one shoulder, an armful of bar,
a tool going back where it lives. This is why he is always mid-motion when he
speaks; he is mid-motion the whole day.

He runs the place off a board on the wall with names and low stock on it, and
that is the entire administrative system, by design, and he is quietly proud of
how little of it there is. He lights the fire first thing every morning whether
or not anybody is coming. He eats standing at his own bench, alone, fast, and
without much interest in the food.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates him:** hurry. A student rushing a stage because the interesting part is
further along. He has never yet met a piece of work that was improved by it and he has
watched forty years of people try.

**The tell, before he says anything:** he stops walking. He is in motion from the moment
the shop opens, so stopping in the middle of the floor is the loudest thing he does, and
every student learns to read it within a fortnight.

**What he does with it:** takes the work off the bench and puts it in the scrap bin,
without comment, and walks on. The student starts again. Nothing is said about it then or
later.

**What actually makes him angry:** unsafe practice near heat. Bare arms, wet gloves, a
crowded aisle, somebody behind somebody swinging. That is the whole list and there are no
degrees within it.

**Angry, it is one word and a hand on the shoulder,** steering the person out of the shop
and through the door. He does not raise his voice, he does not explain in the moment, and
the person is outside before they have understood what happened.

**How it ends:** the next day, when they come back and pick up the tongs, and he hands
them something to do. He does not refer to it. He also does not forget it, and that
student is watched more closely for the rest of the year without ever being told they are
being watched.

## Blind spots

He believes the long unremarked first month is a fair test, and it is fair only
to the specific kind of person who is already sure they belong there. The ones a
single sentence would have kept, he loses, and he has no way of finding out that
he lost them, because they simply stop coming and he reads that as the test
working.

He assumes that silence after good work reads as silence. To most people under
twenty it reads as a verdict. He has never once considered this.

He thinks he is not teaching. He is teaching constantly and by construction, and
would be genuinely puzzled to hear it put that way.

## Age and change

He appears here only late in a career and the sheet is written for that, but the
younger version is worth knowing: the same method, less patience, and a habit of
doing the difficult part himself rather than waiting through somebody else's
attempt at it. The patience is the thing decades added. What has not changed is
the conviction that the material is the authority. He held that at twenty-five
too; at twenty-five he was worse at leaving people alone with it.

## Home

He lives in a room at the back of the workshop building, fitted out properly
rather than camped in, because he is there so much that living anywhere else
stopped making sense. Widowed some years back, no children. He takes about a
dozen outside commissions a year, restoration and conservation work that other
people will not take on, and those commissions are the actual job; teaching is a
few hours a week written into the arrangement. He does not take holidays. The
calendar changes nothing about his week.

He grew up around the trade and came to it early, which is why he has no theory
of it and no patience for anyone else's.

## Body

Sixties, heavy through the shoulders and forearms in the specific way of a person
who has swung a hammer for a living, hands scarred and burned in a dozen small
old places he could not date. Wears the same leather apron every day and replaces
it only when it fails. Moves without hurry and without pause. Sleeps early, wakes
before light, out of decades of it.

## The small stuff

Black coffee, strong, reheated more than once, and he does not appear to notice.
Handwriting exists only on the board: blunt block capitals, names and counts,
nothing else. Pockets hold a folding rule, a stub of marking chalk, and nothing
personal. He spends almost no money and has more work than he could take. He
carries no phone during working hours, full stop.

## Life outside the work

**He fixes other people's things** and will not take money for it. Half the street has
something in his garage. He has no system for getting it back to them and neither do
they.

**Bluegrass on a radio,** the same station, all day, and he has strong opinions about it
that he has never once voiced in the shop and voices freely at home.

**He keeps chickens,** badly named, and talks to them, and this is the single largest
number of consecutive words he speaks in a day.

**Sentimental about:** his own teacher's hammer, which he uses, which is worn to a shape
nobody else can work with comfortably.

**A free Saturday** is somebody else's broken thing in the morning and sitting outside in
the afternoon doing nothing at all, which he is unusually good at.

**At eight** he was in a workshop already, sweeping, and was paid in being allowed to
stay.

**At seventy-five** he has stopped teaching and has not stopped working, and there is one
former student who writes to him every year and whose letters he keeps in a tin and has
never answered, which he would tell you means nothing and does not.

## Would do

- Name one checkable fault and nothing else, then keep walking.
- Let somebody stand there confused for as long as it takes rather than shorten it.
- Leave a newcomer entirely alone for weeks and count them as arrived only when
  they are still there afterward.
- Check a fire by hand before trusting an instrument that is working perfectly.
- Fix his own mistake in front of everyone without a word about it.
- Talk for an hour about how a thing is made, to anyone who asks about the thing
  rather than about themselves.
- Send someone who wants pure ornament to a different room, kindly, and mean it.
- Do a commission nobody else will take, badly paid, because it is interesting.
- Keep the one room in the building that nobody grades, and keep it by never
  filling in a form.

## Would never

- Say a piece is good. He has nothing to say about work with no fault in it.
- Stop moving to watch a correction land.
- Explain what to do about the fault he just named.

## Relationships

- **The student who stays:** the only relationship he has with any learner, and
  it is conducted almost entirely in silence and in the fact of the bench still
  being there. He knows exactly who they are.
- **The other adults at the bench:** he talks to them freely, all day, about the
  work. Anyone watching a student get nothing from him should also see this.
- **Anyone administrative:** cordial, unresponsive, and slower to reply each time.
- **The one colleague who ever asked him a personal question:** got a real answer,
  once, and he has not resented being asked.

## Do not confuse with

A terse teacher who demonstrates. The near neighbour in most casts is the
instructor who corrects briefly and then shows you, physically, taking a scene to
do it. Pruitt never demonstrates and never stops. He names the fault and leaves
the room holding it.

- Full name Harlan Pruitt. Nobody under eighteen at this school uses a staff
  first name; he is Pruitt or Mr. Pruitt.
- Runs the forge, a freely chosen elective, open Thursdays and most of Sunday,
  ungraded and unrecorded. No formal assessment for it exists anywhere in the
  book.
- He speaks to her twice in those four years, both times a correction in passing.
  Her first month there passes without him addressing her at all.
- Widower, no children. Lives on campus in the forge building. Takes outside
  restoration commissions; signed the standard staff non-disclosure agreement.
- Male, undisputed.
