# PRUITT

Runs the forge. Says the least of any adult in the book and is right every time he says it.

**Age span:** Chloe ages 12-16 (the forge begins at 12 and "has been since she was twelve," chapters/19_sixteen.md:107) | **MBTI:** ISTJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** no textual basis; not assessable
**Appears in:** chapters/15_twelve.md, chapters/17_fourteen.md, chapters/19_sixteen.md
**Speaks:** 2 quoted lines in the entire book, across three chapters and four years of story time. This is the finding, not an undercount — the text says so itself: "In four years the whole of his commentary has been telling her which end to hold." (chapters/19_sixteen.md:107, narrated summary, not a direct quote — see Known problems.)

## Voice in one paragraph
Both of Pruitt's lines are a diagnosis stated once, correctly, and he does not stop to see whether it landed: "Your heat's wrong and it has been all afternoon." (chapters/15_twelve.md:9) — delivered "in the second week of October, Pruitt passes behind her with an armful of bar," then "He keeps walking, leaving her to work out what he meant." (chapters/15_twelve.md:7-11) The second line, three weeks later, is even shorter and delivered the same way: "'You lost the curve on the second turn,' he says, without stopping." (chapters/15_twelve.md:27) Both lines name a specific, checkable fact about the work, not a general judgement of it, and neither comes with an explanation of what to do next — the student is left to work it out, which the text confirms takes her "four minutes and one more attempt" the first time (chapters/15_twelve.md:11). There is no third line to draw a pattern from beyond these two, and that scarcity is itself the whole of his voice.

## Signature — theirs alone
Delivers the correction while already leaving — never stops walking, never waits to see it register: "He keeps walking" (chapters/15_twelve.md:11); "he says, without stopping." (chapters/15_twelve.md:27) Both of his only two lines share this exact staging. No other teacher in the cast is shown speaking exclusively while departing.

## Would they say this?
- YES if it names one specific, checkable technical fault and nothing else.
- YES if it's delivered mid-motion, without stopping to watch the reaction.
- NO if it explains what to do about the fault — he never does, in either line.
- NO if it acknowledges four years of a student's unrewarded work with anything more than a passing correction.
- NO if it praises finished work — the one compliment paid to Chloe's forge work in the text ("Good job on this, it looks really good," chapters/15_twelve.md:23) belongs to a different, unnamed student at the next bench, not to Pruitt.
- YES if the entire scene's worth of characterization is one sentence and a person already three steps past you.

## Are they acting like themselves?
When Chloe finishes a set of three hinges that took three Sundays and drops all three of them in the scrap bin as not good enough, "Pruitt is at the far bench with two others and does not see her do it." (chapters/17_fourteen.md:125) He is absent from the one moment a lesser version of this sheet would want him present for — no comment, no unseen approval implied, nothing. That absence is consistent with a character built entirely out of two four-years-apart corrections: he is not narratively available for reassurance because the text never puts him where reassurance would be easy to give. Failure mode a writer would fall into: having him notice and silently approve of the hinges scene, which the text explicitly denies by placing him at a different bench, not watching.

## Would do / would never do
Walking away mid-correction isn't dismissiveness — the method trusts the student to do the work of understanding without supervision, the same trust the whole school extends everywhere else, and four years of near-silence toward a student he clearly keeps taking seriously (he corrects her, which means he's watching) is that trust in its most extreme form.

**Would:**
- Correct one specific, checkable technical fault and nothing more.
- Let a student work out what a correction means without explaining it. [text: chapters/15_twelve.md:11, "It takes her four minutes and one more attempt"]
- Leave a full month of a new student's presence unremarked before saying a single word to her. [text: chapters/15_twelve.md:7]
- Run the forge off a shared whiteboard rather than personal oversight. [text: chapters/15_twelve.md:7]

**Would never:**
- Praise a finished piece — the one compliment Chloe's work receives in the text belongs to a different, unnamed bench-mate, not to him. [text: chapters/15_twelve.md:23]

- Go looking to check on a student's private assessment of her own work — when Chloe drops three finished hinges in the scrap bin, he's at a different bench and doesn't see it; the forge stays entirely hers to grade. [text: chapters/17_fourteen.md:125]

## Known problems
- "In four years the whole of his commentary has been telling her which end to hold" (chapters/19_sixteen.md:107) is narrated summary in indirect speech, not a quotation — there is no scene showing him actually say "which end to hold." A rewriter should not put that exact phrase in quotation marks as his dialogue; it is Chloe's paraphrase of years of interaction, not a line.
- Only two direct quotes exist for a character who spans four years of the timeline. Any additional Pruitt dialogue is invention and must be flagged as a proposal, not treated as recoverable from the text.

## Do not confuse with
Bell — both correct tersely and without elaboration, but Bell always stops to physically demonstrate what's wrong and takes the length of a scene to do it; Pruitt never stops moving and never demonstrates anything, he only names the fault and continues past.
