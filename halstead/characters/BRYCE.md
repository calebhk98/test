# BRYCE DOLAN

A first-grade classmate who takes his time with numbers, works with his hands whenever he gets the chance, and says the single truest thing anyone outside Halstead ever says about Chloe. Not because he's sharp about people generally, but because he looks carefully at whatever's in front of him and reports what he sees.

**Age span:** six to seven (chapters/01_before.md through chapters/07_the_same_room.md) | **MBTI:** ISFP (a writing aid, not a claim; quiet, present-focused, happiest with his hands doing something) | **IQ:** average. "Still counting on his fingers" is an ordinary six-year-old learning arithmetic at an ordinary pace. It only looks slow next to Chloe, who is a textual outlier, and it says nothing about the accuracy of the one big observation he makes.
**Appears in:** chapters/01_before.md, chapters/02_march_4th.md (both non-speaking, narration only), chapters/07_the_same_room.md (his only speaking appearance)
**Speaks:** 2 lines, both in chapters/07_the_same_room.md. Thin, and worth saying plainly: everything about him before that chapter is narration describing his hands, not his voice.

## Voice in one paragraph

His one substantive line is a flat comparative report, present tense against past, with no judgment word attached to either state: "You were weird before and now you're weirder." (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:137). His only other line is a single-word close, "Yeah" (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:141), after which he turns back around and the scene moves on. The text supplies the frame for how to read the first line: "Bryce says it interested, the way you'd tell somebody their shoe was untied. He isn't being mean about it. He's saying what he can see." (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:137). No elaboration, no follow-up, no defense when nobody challenges him. He states the comparison and disengages.

## Signature — theirs alone

One real instance, short of the book's own bar of three to four uses: reporting a change in Chloe as a flat before/after comparison, no evaluative word attached to either half (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:137). If given a second scene, keep the construction to a same-shape before/after report rather than a new kind of line.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is a flat comparison across time with no judgment word attached to either side.
- YES if he disengages the instant he's finished speaking, no follow-up, no waiting to see if it lands.
- YES if the line reports something he's directly observed rather than something he's been told.
- YES if it's an unhurried, careful description of how a specific thing works or looks. His register outside speech is patient and detail-oriented, and a line in that same register fits him.
- YES if it stays under about ten words with no subordinate clause.
- NO if it explains the mechanism behind the change. He reports the observation; he does not analyze it.
- NO if he initiates the exchange. Both his lines are direct reactions to something Chloe has just said or done.

## Are they acting like themselves?

His one non-speaking trait is physical and telling: he does the arithmetic sheet "under the edge of his desk where he thinks Mrs. Aldana can't see him" (chapters/01_before.md:10). He's not indifferent to being watched working slowly; he manages it quietly rather than making it anyone else's problem. That sits in some tension with the confident, unbothered delivery of his one real line months later. "Quietly careful about being watched" and "flatly, unselfconsciously accurate about somebody else" are two modes he's capable of, not one implying the other has gone away.

Failure modes:
1. Making him explain the "weirder" line. He reports it and stops.
2. Making him cruel. The text states plainly that this isn't mockery.
3. Treating two lines as an established pattern. There isn't enough here to know what a third Bryce line sounds like with confidence.

## Subjects and schedule

Ordinary public elementary school, not Halstead. `CURRICULUM_GRID.md` and `_ALLOCATIONS.md` don't apply to him.

- **Worst at: arithmetic.** Explicit and repeated across two separate school years: "still counting on his fingers" in first grade (chapters/01_before.md:10), unchanged the following spring (chapters/02_march_4th.md:117). A real, stated fact, reported the same flat way everything else about him is reported. He's an ordinary kid moving at an ordinary pace.
- **Best at:** building and construction toys, blocks, small models, anything with moving parts. He's the kid other kids ask to fix a jammed toy.
- **Favourite subject:** show-and-tell, because he likes bringing one specific thing from home and describing it carefully, in order, without rushing.
- **Least favourite:** the timed arithmetic sheet (chapters/01_before.md:10).
- **Secretly proud of:** he never forgets how a game or a machine is actually supposed to work, even when he's slow to work out something new.
- **Would want to be when he grows up:** "the guy who fixes things," after his uncle.

**How he feels about a classmate who reads ahead:** the same way he treats anything else true, flatly, without envy, and without it costing him anything. He is, on the page, a child who finds arithmetic hard and the one who says the truest thing about Chloe anyone outside Halstead ever manages. Those two facts sit next to each other without comment from him or the narration. Being slow at one thing doesn't make him wrong about another, and the text never treats his arithmetic as a joke, only as a fact, in the same register as the "weirder" line.

## Home

His uncle runs a small auto-repair garage two streets over, and Bryce is there most Saturdays, handing over wrenches before he's asked because he's watched enough to know which one comes next. His parents both work, his mother in a hospital laundry department, his father driving a delivery route, so weekends with his uncle are the fixed point of his week. His uncle taught him that getting a thing right matters more than getting it fast, and that you diagnose a problem by watching and listening carefully before you touch anything. That's the same instinct behind both his slow, careful arithmetic and the one sharp, accurate thing he says about Chloe.

## Body

Sturdy, a little clumsy, still growing into his hands and feet. Keeps his fingers busy: taps a pencil apart to see how the eraser's held on, turns a bottle cap over and over. Careful, quiet eater, one food at a time on the plate. Falls asleep fast after a day spent outside or in the garage.

## Personality off the page

- Occupies a room by watching before joining in.
- Bored: fidgets with something small in his hands rather than complaining.
- Wrong: shrugs and tries again without embarrassment.
- Praised: a little embarrassed, mumbles thanks, gets back to what he was doing.
- Losing: doesn't mind much, more interested in how the game worked than in the score.
- Tired: quiet, slower than his usual unhurried pace.
- A free Saturday: at his uncle's garage, or outside taking something apart.
- What he finds funny in other people: physical comedy, someone tripping, a genuinely clumsy accident.

## Interior life

Wants, someday, to be fast at something the way he's careful at everything now, and hasn't said this to anyone. A little embarrassed about the finger-counting, but it doesn't stop him from doing it his way regardless. Thinks about how things work, a bike chain, a jammed drawer, before he falls asleep some nights. Believes that if you look at something carefully enough, you'll see what's actually true about it, which is exactly the belief behind his one memorable line. What he's wrong about: he assumes everyone else is also just plainly reporting what they see when they talk. He doesn't yet have a model for people who exaggerate, perform, or say things they don't mean.

## The small stuff

Pockets usually hold a screw, a rubber band, or a small found object. Draws vehicles in the margins of worksheets while he waits for everyone else to finish. Handwriting is careful and slow, like his math. Carries a small toy screwdriver his uncle gave him and likes having it on him.

## Would do

1. Take apart something small at home to see how it works, and mostly put it back together.
2. Hand someone a tool before they ask for it, if he's watching them work.
3. State something true and specific about a person or thing, once, and let it stand.
4. Keep working a problem at his own pace even after everyone else has finished (chapters/02_march_4th.md:117).
5. Notice a small detail, a scuff, a sound, a change, before anyone else in the room does.
6. Play outside or in the garage until it's too dark to see well.
7. Bring something specific and well-chosen for show-and-tell and describe it carefully, in order.
8. Laugh at a friend tripping or a genuinely silly physical accident.
9. Offer a plain, matter-of-fact correction rather than let a wrong fact sit.
10. Sit with the same one or two friends every day without needing the group to be bigger.

## Would never

1. Would never repeat an observation once he's made it, or dress it up as an insult. He says a true thing once and moves on (chapters/07_the_same_room.md:137, 141).
2. Would never rush a task he cares about getting right, even under time pressure. The same unhurried carefulness that's slow at arithmetic is what makes his one big observation accurate.
3. Would never let anyone he doesn't trust watch him still working something out. He hides it from a teacher (chapters/01_before.md:10), which is situational, not a general instinct to hide effort from everyone.

## Known problems

The character had no other scene, trait, or line to support that weight before this pass. A second, different detail would do more work if his first-grade year needs to feel inhabited rather than static.

## Do not confuse with
