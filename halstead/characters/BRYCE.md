# Bryce Dolan

An ordinary boy who takes his time with everything, works with his hands
whenever he gets the chance, and reports what he can see without dressing it up
or thinking twice about it.

**Age span:** six to seven | **MBTI:** ISFP (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** average, an ordinary child learning at an ordinary pace

## The thing to get right first

**Being slow at one thing does not make him wrong about another.** He counts on
his fingers, and he is also the person who says the truest thing anyone outside
the exceptional room ever says about the girl at his table. Those two facts sit
next to each other and neither of them comments on the other. The accuracy
comes from the same place the slowness does: he looks carefully at whatever is
in front of him and does not hurry to a conclusion.

**He is never being cruel.** He reports a change the way you would tell somebody
their shoe was untied, with interest and no judgement in either half of it, and
then he turns back around. Any version of him where the observation is aimed
lands as a different child.

## How they talk

A flat comparison across time, with no evaluative word attached to either side:
this was so before, this is so now. He is reporting a trend rather than passing
a verdict, and the verdict is the reader's to supply.

He does not elaborate, does not follow up, and does not defend the remark when
nobody challenges it. He says it and disengages in the same beat.

He does not initiate. What he says is a reaction to something that just
happened in front of him.

He is unhurried, and a longer line from him is a careful description of how a
specific thing works or looks, in order, without rushing.

He does not explain the mechanism behind a change. He reports what he can see
and does not analyse it.

## When they talk at length

**Machinery, and only when somebody is about to make it worse.** Put him in
front of something mechanical that is behaving wrongly, with a hand reaching
toward it, and he will run the diagnosis out loud in full: what it is doing,
what that means, what to check first, what not to touch until then. His uncle's
rule, diagnose before you touch it, is the whole of his method.

He is not performing knowledge. He is stopping somebody from making a mistake.

It costs him the flat short register he keeps everywhere else, and he does not
notice paying it. The boy who says one true thing and turns back around is the
same boy who will not let a mistake happen in front of him, and the second one
talks.

He does not go long about a person, and never explains an observation he has
already made.

## Signature, theirs alone

The before-and-after report: two states of the same thing named across two
points in time, with no judgement word attached to either. He is the one who
notices the trend rather than the snapshot. Triggered by a change he has been
in a position to watch happen. Once per change, and never repeated once said.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it is a flat comparison across time with no judgement word attached to
  either side.
- YES if he disengages the instant he has finished, with no follow-up and no
  waiting to see if it landed.
- YES if the line reports something he has directly observed rather than
  something he has been told.
- YES if it is an unhurried, careful description of how a specific thing works
  or looks.
- YES if it is short and has no subordinate clause.
- YES if it is a plain correction offered without heat, rather than an
  argument.
- NO if it explains the mechanism behind the change. He reports; he does not
  analyse.
- NO if he initiates the exchange.

## Under pressure

He is quietly careful about being watched while he is still working something
out. He will do the slow part of a task where the person who might judge it
cannot see, and he manages that himself rather than making it anybody's
problem. This sits alongside a completely unselfconscious accuracy about
somebody else, and both are him: the discretion is about his own effort, not
about his opinions.

Wrong, he shrugs and tries again without embarrassment.

Praised, he is a little embarrassed, mumbles something, and gets back to what
he was doing.

Losing, he does not mind much and is more interested in how the game worked
than in the score.

Tired, he is quiet and slower than his usual unhurried pace.

Rushed, he does not speed up. This is the closest thing he has to a refusal.

## What he is good at, and what he is not

An ordinary school, and an ordinary place in it.

**Hardest for him:** arithmetic. He is still counting on his fingers across two
school years, and it only looks slow next to one particular classmate. He is an
ordinary child moving at an ordinary pace, and the text around him never treats
it as a joke.

**Best at:** building things, blocks, small models, anything with moving parts.
He is the child other children bring a jammed toy to.

**Favourite:** show-and-tell, because he likes bringing one specific thing from
home and describing it carefully, in order, without being rushed.

**Least favourite:** anything timed.

**Secretly proud of:** he never forgets how a game or a machine is actually
supposed to work, even when he is slow to work out something new.

**What he wants to be:** the person who fixes things, after his uncle.

## Home

His uncle runs a small auto-repair garage two streets over, and Bryce is there
most Saturdays, handing over tools before he is asked because he has watched
enough to know which one comes next.

Both his parents work, his mother in a hospital laundry department and his
father driving a delivery route, so the weekends with his uncle are the fixed
point of his week.

His uncle taught him that getting a thing right matters more than getting it
fast, and that you diagnose a problem by watching and listening carefully
before you touch anything. That is the instinct behind both his slow careful
arithmetic and the one sharp accurate thing he says about somebody else.

## Body and physical business

Sturdy, a little clumsy, still growing into his hands and feet.

He keeps his fingers busy: taking a pencil apart to see how the eraser is held
on, turning a bottle cap over and over.

A careful, quiet eater, one food at a time on the plate. He falls asleep fast
after a day spent outside or in the garage.

He occupies a room by watching before joining in.

## Personality off the page

Bored, he fidgets with something small rather than complaining. A free Saturday
is the garage, or outside taking something apart. What he finds funny in other
people is physical comedy: somebody tripping, a genuinely clumsy accident.

## Interior life

**Wants**, and has not said to anyone: to be fast at something the way he is
careful at everything.

**A little embarrassed** about the finger counting, which does not stop him
doing it his own way regardless.

**Before sleeping** he thinks about how things work: a bike chain, a jammed
drawer.

**Believes**, and it is exactly the belief behind his one memorable remark: that
if you look at something carefully enough you will see what is actually true
about it.

**Blind spots:** he assumes everyone else is also just plainly reporting what
they see. He has no model yet for people who exaggerate, perform, or say things
they do not mean.

## The small stuff

His pockets usually hold a screw, a rubber band, or a small found object. He
draws vehicles in the margins of worksheets while he waits for everybody else
to finish. His handwriting is careful and slow, like his arithmetic. He carries
a small toy screwdriver his uncle gave him and likes having it on him.

## Would do

- Take apart something small at home to see how it works, and mostly put it
  back together.
- Hand somebody a tool before they ask for it, if he is watching them work.
- State something true and specific about a person or a thing, once, and let it
  stand.
- Keep working a problem at his own pace after everybody else has finished.
- Notice a small detail, a scuff, a sound, a change, before anyone else in the
  room does.
- Play outside or in the garage until it is too dark to see well.
- Bring something specific and well-chosen to show, and describe it carefully,
  in order.
- Laugh at a friend tripping over.
- Offer a plain, matter-of-fact correction rather than let a wrong fact sit.
- Sit with the same one or two friends every day without needing the group to
  be bigger.

## Would never

- Repeat an observation once he has made it, or dress it up as an insult.
- Rush a task he cares about getting right, even under time pressure.

## How he treats people

He defers to adults and to his uncle without thinking about it, and to nobody
else in particular.

He treats everybody the same way, which is the whole of his social technique:
whatever he says lands in the same flat register whether it is a fact about a
machine or a fact about a person.

He changes his behaviour for exactly one kind of person, the one who might
watch him working slowly. In front of them his hands go out of sight.

He is a steady friend to one or two people rather than a member of a group.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Works with his hands whenever he can.
- Notices accurately and says so without being asked.
- Disengages quickly once he has said his piece.
- Collects small found objects in his pockets.
- Draws vehicles in the margins while waiting for others to finish.

## Age and change

He is six and seven on the page. If he is ever written older, the thing that
persists is the method: watch and listen before you touch, and do not hurry.
The finger counting resolves the way it resolves for any ordinary child. The
carefulness does not, and neither does the accuracy that comes with it.

## Do not confuse with

**Kayleigh Burns**, the other half of the same beat. Kayleigh's remarks are
single-point judgements about the present moment with no time dimension in
them. Bryce's are explicitly comparative, tracking somebody across two points
in time. He is reporting a trend; she is reporting a fact.
