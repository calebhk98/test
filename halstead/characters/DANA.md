# Dana Halloran

The older cousin: thirteen years ahead, already living the ordinary adult life the
younger child has not begun to imagine, and warm to that child in the brief, sincere,
completely unsustained way of somebody whose real life is happening somewhere else in the
room.

**Age span:** nineteen when the youngest child in the family is six, so roughly nineteen
to thirty-four across the years she appears | **MBTI:** ISFP (a writing aid, not a claim)
| **IQ:** 111

## The thing to get right first

**She is older, and the gap does the work.** She is not a peer, not a rival and not a
playmate. She is at college when the child is in first grade, she has a job and a partner
by the time the child is in middle school, and she has children of her own before the
child has finished school. Every scene between them is a scene between two people at
completely different points of a life, and the older one has no reason to think the
younger one is interesting yet. Write the age gap and most of her comes out on its own.

**Her kindness is real and it is thirty seconds long.** She asks the question. She puts
the phone down for the length of the answer, genuinely. Then she is gone, back to the
adults, and the going is neither a snub nor a performance. She did the thing you do, she
meant it while she did it, and the conversation was never going to be the centre of her
afternoon.

**She is the ordinary life, and the book needs her to be a good one.** She is not a
cautionary example of the unexamined existence. She is happy, competent, well liked, busy,
and getting on with it, and any version of her that reads as a warning has lost her.

## Type and number

**Primary: ISFP.** No outside reader typed her, so this is the house reading and it is
worth stating why it is not the obvious one. The obvious call for a sociable young woman
at a family party is an extraverted feeling type, and she is not that: she is not
maintaining the room, she is not tracking who has been included, and she does not organise
anybody. What she does is live squarely in the present, hold a small set of things she
cares about very firmly and without argument, and decline, pleasantly, to be curious about
anything outside them. That is the introverted-feeling, sensing-outward pattern, and it
explains the specific shape of her attention: total for thirty seconds, then genuinely
elsewhere.

**Mistaken for:** ESFJ, by anybody who meets her at a wedding.

**IQ 111.** Comfortably above average and entirely unremarkable in her own world, which is
the point of the number. The shape of it: an even profile with a mild lift on the
practical and spatial side — she is good at rooms, routes, timings and the logistics of
getting four people to two places — and no spike anywhere. Where she places lowest:
sustained abstraction with no object attached. She got through a real degree by working
steadily and choosing sensibly, and she has never in her life been the cleverest person at
a table and has never once experienced that as a loss.

## How she talks

Short, warm, and closed at the end. She asks the standard question in the standard words
and she means it, and the sentence that follows the answer is an acknowledgement rather
than a continuation: two or three words that accept what she was told and do not open
anything further. She does not ask a second question. She has no habit of following a
thread, and when a child hands her a long, strange, detailed answer she takes it
cheerfully, at face value, without hearing that anything unusual has happened.

She talks at length about logistics: parking, the drive, who is coming, what time the
thing starts, whether there is enough of something. That is where her fluency lives and it
is real fluency — she can plan a day for eleven people out loud, in order, with
contingencies, and never write any of it down.

She does not hedge and she does not qualify, because nothing she says is a claim about
anything contestable. She has no figurative language at all, and no interest in acquiring
any.

## When she talks at length

Three conditions, and any scene can meet them. First, arrangements: give her a problem
with people, times and vehicles in it and she will run the whole thing out loud, with
alternatives, and enjoy doing it. Second, her own household: her children, the state of
her kitchen, something a builder said. Third, and this is the one worth using, a young
relative in visible trouble — she will sit down properly, for the first time in the whole
book of her, and stay.

What it costs her: nothing, in the first two cases, which is why she does them so freely.
The third costs her an entire evening and she will not mention having given it.

## Signature, hers alone

**The closing acknowledgement.** She receives an answer, accepts it in two or three words
that add nothing and take nothing away, and turns. The construction is: acceptance, no
evaluation, no question, and then a physical turn to the next person. Twice in a book is
plenty; the power of it is that it is identical thirteen years apart, and the child on the
receiving end is the only one who notices that.

## Would she say this?

- YES if it is a standard question asked sincerely and not followed up.
- YES if it accepts what it was told without weighing it.
- YES if it is about who is driving, what time, and whether there is room.
- YES if it is affectionate in a general way and specific about nothing.
- YES if it treats a strange or intense answer as an ordinary one.
- YES if it changes the subject to the person on her other side.
- YES if it is about her own children in unembarrassed detail.
- NO if it interrogates the mechanism of anything.
- NO if it registers that the child in front of her is unusual.
- NO if it is unkind, or aimed, or aware of having landed.

## Frustration and anger

**What frustrates her:** an arrangement changing late. Not difficulty, not inconvenience,
not being asked for something — a plan she had already built in her head being altered by
somebody who did not think to tell her.

**The tell, before she says anything:** she starts confirming details out loud that
nobody has disputed. Times, names, who is bringing what. The confirming is her rebuilding
the plan, and to anybody who knows her it is unmistakable.

**What she does with it:** absorbs it and rebuilds, out loud, and mentions the cost
exactly once, lightly, in a way that is not quite a complaint and not quite a joke.

**What actually makes her angry:** somebody being careless with one of her children. Not
unkind, which she can handle. Careless. A dropped promise, a forgotten pickup, an adult
who did not check.

**Angry, she goes formal and warm at the same time,** which is an unnerving combination.
The temperature of her voice does not change at all and the vocabulary goes several notches
up, and she says exactly what happened in complete sentences, and she does not raise a
single one of them.

**How it ends:** with distance rather than a resolution. She does not have a confrontation
and she does not forgive out loud. The person is simply asked to do slightly less, for a
while, and then for longer, and there is never a conversation about it. Inside her own
household it ends within the hour and completely.

## What she is good at, and what she is not

She was a steady student who worked hard, took a sensible degree at a state university,
and came out with a job she has been promoted inside twice. She is genuinely good at her
work and would describe it as fine. She can drive anywhere without a map, remembers every
route she has ever taken, and can hold a household's whole week in her head.

What she is not good at: sitting with something unresolved. A problem with no available
action makes her restless and then makes her tidy. She is also, straightforwardly, not a
reader any more, having been one until about fourteen, and she has no complicated feeling
about that at all.

## Home

She grew up in the same small city as the rest of that side of the family, four streets
from the grandmother's house, in a family of four. Her father is the sibling who stayed.
Sunday dinners at the grandmother's were compulsory and she has never resented them.

Her own house, later, is a small one bought young and improved in stages, with a chest
freezer in the garage and a wall in the kitchen where the children's heights are marked in
pencil with dates. Dinner is early, loud, and eaten together. She hosts constantly and
finds it easy.

## Body and physical business

Broad-shouldered, quick on her feet, dressed for the weather rather than the occasion. She
does her hair properly for family events and not otherwise. She carries a bag with far
more in it than she needs and can find anything in it without looking.

Her hands are always finishing something small — clearing a plate, straightening a chair,
taking a coat off somebody — and she does this in other people's houses without asking. She
sits down last and gets up first. On a phone she is fast, constant, and entirely
unembarrassed about being on it in company; she puts it face down when somebody speaks to
her, every time, which is a real courtesy she was taught and kept.

## Personality off the page

Bored, she finds something to do with her hands and starts a conversation with whoever is
nearest about something practical. Praised, she says thank you and means it and does not
examine it, which makes her almost unique in this cast. Wrong, she says so easily and
without any cost; being wrong has never been expensive for her and she has no defences
built around it. Losing, she shrugs. Tired, she gets quiet and slightly deaf and keeps
going.

At two in the morning she is asleep, unless a child is ill, in which case she is
completely competent and will not remember much of it afterwards.

A free Saturday is other people's company, food, and being outdoors for part of it.

What she finds funny in other people is self-importance, gently, and she will not say so.

## Interior life

**Wants, and has never said out loud:** to be the one everybody comes to at Christmas once
the grandmother is gone. She would find the ambition embarrassing if it were named and she
is quietly, steadily building toward it.

**Afraid of:** her children not being close to each other. **What she does instead of
admitting it:** engineers occasions relentlessly and calls it nothing.

**Before sleep** she runs the next day in order.

**A free year:** she would not want one and would find the question slightly alarming. Two
weeks somewhere warm with everybody, and then back.

**Believes,** and would be surprised to be argued with: that the life she has is the good
one, and that people who arrange things differently have usually been unlucky rather than
different.

**Blind spots:** she cannot see a child who is unhappy in a way that does not look like
unhappiness. A quiet, polite, articulate child giving her a strange answer registers as a
sweet kid, and she would have been genuinely distressed to be told otherwise, years later,
and would have believed it immediately.

## Life outside the work

**Sport, on television, with real commitment,** and she has followed the same team since
she was eleven because her father did. She goes to matches. She shouts.

**She grows tomatoes** in an unambitious way and gives them away in ice cream tubs to
anybody who visits between July and September.

**She sang in a choir at school** and stopped, and still sings in the car, loudly, and her
children are embarrassed by it, which she enjoys.

**Sentimental about:** the grandmother's kitchen, specifically the table. She will
eventually own it and she has already, quietly, said so to one person.

**At eight** she was an outdoors child who read horse books in enormous quantities for
about three years and then stopped completely and never went back, and she remembers the
titles.

**At nineteen** she is at college, busy, cheerful, and profoundly uninterested in
children.

**At thirty-four** she has two of her own, a house, a job she is good at, and the same
capacity for a sincere thirty-second conversation. She is the family's fixed point. When
somebody dies, she is the one on the phone to everybody, and she is good at it.

## Would do

- Ask a child the standard question and mean it, and not follow it up.
- Put her phone face down for the length of somebody else's sentence.
- Plan a day for eleven people out loud, in order, without writing anything down.
- Take a coat off a visitor and hang it up before they have finished the greeting.
- Drive four hours for a family thing and drive back the same night.
- Give away food in containers she does not expect back.
- Say thank you for a compliment without inspecting it.
- Sit down properly, and stay, for a young relative who is visibly in trouble.
- Host, at short notice, and enjoy it.
- Remember a route once and never need it again.

## Would never

- Ask a second question when the first has been answered.
- Treat an unusual child as a problem, or as a project.

## How she treats people

- **A much younger cousin:** warmly, briefly, and identically at six and at eighteen. She
  wants nothing from that child and offers a real thirty seconds.
- **Her own parents:** dutifully and often, with a running low-grade argument about
  scheduling.
- **The family matriarch:** with genuine deference, and she is watching how it is done.
- **Her own children:** completely, loudly, and with no reserve at all. This is where the
  whole of her is.
- **Somebody being self-important:** politely, while finding it funny, and she will not
  let on.
- **Somebody in trouble:** better than anybody expects. She turns up with food and stays
  the afternoon.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

Likes: a full house, an early dinner, a job finished, a plan confirmed, being outside,
somebody else's baby. Dislikes: late changes, being fussed over, silence at a table,
anything described as an experience. Habits: puts the phone face down, clears plates before
people have finished, confirms times out loud, sings in the car, marks heights on the wall
in pencil.

## Age and change

**Nineteen.** At college, home for the holidays, sociable and fully occupied. Kind to the
small children in the room in short bursts, and then gone.

**Twenty-one to twenty-five.** Working, sharing a place, unhurried about the next thing.
The family gatherings are something she attends rather than runs.

**Late twenties.** Married, first child, and the centre of her gravity moves permanently.
This is when the hosting starts and when the older generation begin, without discussing
it, handing things over to her.

**Early thirties.** Two children, a house, a job, a car she keeps too long. Absolutely
unchanged in the one behaviour that matters here: she asks the standard question, she
listens for the length of the answer, she says the accepting thing, and she turns to talk
about the parking. That constancy is the character.

## Do not confuse with

**The girl who is genuinely mad about horses.** Both read horse books as children. The cue
is tense: for Dana it is finished and remembered fondly, and if it comes up she will name
a title from twenty-five years ago and move on. For the other it is present, permanent and
still being talked about at length this afternoon.

**The mother of the family.** Both are warm, practical women who host. The cue is
curiosity: the mother cannot leave an institution's answer alone and will ask a fourth
question. Dana asks one, accepts the answer, and turns.
