# Sheet template

Every character file in this folder follows this structure. The test a finished sheet has
to pass: **hand it to someone who has not read the book, with one chapter, and they can
find the lines this character would not say and reword them.**

That means self-contained. No "see the synopsis", no "as noted elsewhere". If the sheet
needs a fact, the sheet states the fact.

Length: two to three screens. It is a reference, not an essay. The line-by-line evidence
lives in git history and in the reader's own reading.

---

```
# <NAME>

One sentence: who they are and what they want. No plot summary.

**Age span** | **MBTI** (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ** (reader inference)
**Appears in:** exact file list, so a reader knows where to look
**Speaks:** N lines. Say if that is front-loaded into a few chapters.

## Voice in one paragraph
How they build a sentence, how long, what they do first, what they never do. Concrete
enough to imitate. Name the single most characteristic line and quote it exactly with
its file and line number.

## Dials
| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| sentence shape | their Axis 1 slot, in five words |
| disagrees by | their Axis 2 move |
| wordiness | n/10 and the measured words per line |
| terseness | measured % of lines three words or fewer |
| jokiness | n/10 and what KIND of funny, which matters more than the number |
| hedging | measured %, and whether that is right for them |
| questions | measured %, and whether that is right for them |
| figurative | allowed / not allowed, per the matrix |

## Signature — theirs alone
The one construction nobody else may use. Two real examples from the text if they exist,
or two written to spec and clearly marked as proposals.

## Would they say this?
Six to ten bullets, each starting YES or NO, each a testable property of a line rather
than a personality adjective.
  YES if it names the mechanism rather than the outcome
  NO if it accepts a compliment
Write these so a reader can apply them to a line they have never seen.

## Are they acting like themselves?
Behaviour, not speech. What they do under pressure, what they never do, how they occupy
a room, what they do with their hands. Include the failure modes: the two or three ways
a writer most easily gets them wrong.

## Subjects
Halstead runs eight academic hours plus two afternoon blocks, every year, from age seven
or eight. Everyone takes nearly everything, so what separates people is what they reach
for and what they endure. Give:
  best at | worst at | favourite, and why it is the favourite | least favourite
  the one they are secretly proud of | the one they would drop if allowed
Ground these in the curriculum the book actually describes. Where the text shows a
preference, cite it. Where it does not, mark the choice [inferred] and make it consistent
with everything else on the sheet.

## Personality off the page
Not how they talk — who they are when nothing is happening. Cover:
  how they occupy a room, and where they sit
  what they do with their hands
  how they handle being bored, being wrong, being praised, and losing
  what they are like tired, and what they are like at 2am
  what they do on a free Saturday
  what they find funny in other people

## Would do / would never do
Behaviour, not speech. Two short lists, six or so each, concrete enough to settle an
argument about a scene you have not written. "Would carry someone's bag without being
asked" beats "is kind". The would-never list is the more useful of the two.

## Body and habits
Everything the text gives: build, posture, hands, how they move, what they wear, what
they eat, how they sleep. Mark UNDEFINED where the manuscript says nothing rather than
inventing a face. Physical detail is the thinnest thing in this manuscript, so an honest
UNDEFINED here is a to-do list for the author.

## Relationships
One line each for the people they actually interact with: what this character wants from
that person, and how their behaviour changes in front of them. People behave differently
with different people, and that is most of what makes a character feel known.

## Likes, dislikes, habits
Short lists. Mark [text] where a specific line or action supports it and [inferred]
where it is extrapolation. A sheet that cannot tell the difference is not usable.

## Facts a rewriter needs
Name, surname if any, family, background, what they are good and bad at, what they know
at what age. Everything needed to avoid contradicting the book. Flag anything the
manuscript leaves undefined as UNDEFINED rather than inventing it.

## Known problems
The specific places they are off-voice, out of character, or missing, with file and line.
Each one paired with what to do about it.

## Do not confuse with
The one or two characters they currently blur into, and the exact cue that separates them.
```

## Rules for whoever fills this in

- Quote exactly, with file and line. Never paraphrase into quotation marks.
- Mark every proposal as a proposal. The author needs to know what is theirs already.
- Apply `_DIFFERENTIATION.md`. If the matrix assigns a slot, the sheet uses that slot,
  and says so where it differs from the current draft.
- Nothing in a sheet may describe another character. "Unlike Ruth, he..." fails the
  hand-it-to-an-agent test. The one exception is the final section, which exists for it.
- If a trait is asserted in the reference documents but not supported in the manuscript,
  say so in Known problems rather than repeating it.
