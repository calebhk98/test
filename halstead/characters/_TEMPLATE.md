# Sheet template

Every character file in this folder follows this structure. The test a finished sheet has
to pass: **hand it to someone who has not read the book, with one chapter, and they can
find the lines this character would not say and reword them.**

That means self-contained. No "see the synopsis", no "as noted elsewhere". If the sheet
needs a fact, the sheet states the fact.

Length: as long as it takes to know the person, and no longer. Four to six screens is
normal now. What it must not be is a line-by-line inventory of their dialogue — that was
the old failure and it is not the same thing as detail.

---

```
# <NAME>

One sentence: who they are and what they want. No plot summary.

**Age span** | **MBTI** (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ** (reader inference)
**Appears in:** exact file list, so a reader knows where to look
**Speaks:** N lines. Say if that is front-loaded into a few chapters.

## Voice in one paragraph
How they build a sentence, how long, what they do first, what they never do. Concrete
enough to imitate. Name the single most characteristic line and quote it exactly with
its file and line number.

## Dials
| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| sentence shape | their Axis 1 slot, in five words |
| disagrees by | their Axis 2 move |
| wordiness | n/10 and the measured words per line |
| terseness | measured % of lines three words or fewer |
| jokiness | n/10 and what KIND of funny, which matters more than the number |
| hedging | measured %, and whether that is right for them |
| questions | measured %, and whether that is right for them |
| figurative | allowed / not allowed, per the matrix |

## Signature — theirs alone
The one construction nobody else may use. Two real examples from the text if they exist,
or two written to spec and clearly marked as proposals.

## Would they say this?
Eight or more bullets, each a testable property of a line rather than a personality
adjective. Mostly YES; two or three NOs at most.
  YES if it names the mechanism rather than the outcome
  YES if it answers the question actually asked and then stops
  NO if it accepts a compliment
Write these so a reader can apply them to a line they have never seen.

## Are they acting like themselves?
Behaviour, not speech. What they do under pressure, how they occupy a room, what they
reach for first. Include the two or three ways a writer most easily gets them wrong.

## Subjects and schedule
`CURRICULUM_GRID.md` is CHLOE's timetable, not the school's. The six permanent tracks are
shared; the language each year, the two rotating academic slots and both afternoon blocks
are individual, and two students in the same year are doing different subjects in a
different order. `_ALLOCATIONS.md` records what the sheets currently say; those choices are
yours to keep or change. Give:
  their language list and what they did with it | best at | worst at | favourite and why
  the subject they took that nobody else did | the year something clicked | what they teach
  the twelves, from thirteen | the one they are secretly proud of

## Home
Where they are from, the house, who is in it, what their parents do, what dinner is like,
what they are like when they go back. Invent it, and make it specific.

## Body
Build, face, hands, how they move, how they take up space, what they wear when nobody is
assigning it, how they sleep, what they eat and how fast. Concrete and unglamorous.
Invent what the manuscript does not give, which is nearly all of it.

## Personality off the page
Who they are when nothing is happening: how they occupy a room, what they do with their
hands, how they handle being bored, being wrong, being praised, and losing; what they are
like tired and at 2am; a free Saturday; what they find funny in other people.

## Interior life
What they want that they have not said out loud. What they are afraid of. What they think
about before sleeping. What they would do with a free year. What they believe that the
others would argue with. What they are wrong about.

## The small stuff
Food, music, how they take coffee or tea, their handwriting, their room, what is in their
pockets, what they do with money, a superstition or ritual, how they are on a phone, what
they do first thing in the morning. This section is where a character stops being a
function.

## Would do
Eight or more, concrete, positive. Actions a reader could stage. "Would carry the heavy
end without being asked" beats "is helpful."

## Would never
Two or three only, and only where the negative carries something the positive cannot.
This section is deliberately much shorter than the one above it.

## Relationships
One line each for everyone they actually deal with: what this character wants from that
person, and how their behaviour changes in front of them.

## Likes, dislikes, habits
Short lists, concrete. Things a reader could put in a scene.

## Facts a rewriter needs
Name, surname, family, background, what they are good and bad at, what they know at what
age. Everything needed to avoid contradicting the book. Where the manuscript is silent,
decide — do not leave a hole. If a surname does not exist, give them one.

## Known problems
The specific places they are off-voice, out of character, or missing, with file and line.
Each one paired with what to do about it.

## Do not confuse with
The one or two characters they currently blur into, and the exact cue that separates them.
```

## Rules for whoever fills this in

- **Read `_CALIBRATION.md` first.** It sets how good these people are, how the school
  behaves, and what a weakness means here. Several sheets currently get it wrong.
- **Invent.** The book is Chloe's point of view, so almost nothing exists about anyone
  else's home, body, family or private life. That is a gap to fill. UNDEFINED is not an
  acceptable answer anywhere on a sheet — if the manuscript is silent, decide and write it.
- **Write the character, not the plot.** Never build a detail as setup for a later event.
  A twelve-year-old picking a language is picking a language.
- **Turn secondhand into firsthand.** Most of what exists about anyone but Chloe arrives
  reported or overheard. Take that material and write it as the character's own action.
- **No tags.** Do not mark lines [text], [inferred] or [invented]. The sheets are read by
  people writing scenes and the tags get in the way. Write the character as a person.
  Where the manuscript shows something, quoting it is still the strongest way to say it.
- **Positive framing, roughly five to one.** Say what they do, reach for, like, and would
  choose. Keep the negatives for the few places where nothing else carries the point.
- **Differentiate, but never against the character.** Where the text or an established
  trait points somewhere, follow it even if another character lands nearby. Invent the
  difference in the open ground instead - and there is a great deal of open ground.
- Quote exactly, with file and line, when quoting. Never paraphrase into quotation marks.
- Apply `_DIFFERENTIATION.md`. If the matrix assigns a slot, the sheet uses that slot,
  and says so where it differs from the current draft.
- Nothing in a sheet may describe another character. "Unlike Ruth, he..." fails the
  hand-it-to-an-agent test. The one exception is the final section, which exists for it.
- If a trait is asserted in the reference documents but not supported in the manuscript,
  say so in Known problems rather than repeating it.
