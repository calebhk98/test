# Sheet template

Every character file in this folder follows this structure.

**Read `_SHEET_RULES.md` before you fill this in.** It is the specification, and the two
things it asks for most are the two this template used to work against: **no quotations
from the manuscript, anywhere, for any reason**, and **no line or chapter references as
the basis of a trait**. A sheet describes a person, not this draft.

The test a finished sheet has to pass, in the author's words: **you should be able to pick
the character up off the sheet and drop them into a science-fiction lunar colony, a horror
novel with ghosts, or a Korean drama, and still write them correctly.** Anything on the
sheet that would be meaningless on the lunar colony is describing the manuscript, and it
does not belong.

The second test is the older one and still holds: **hand the sheet to someone who has not
read the book, with one chapter, and they can find the lines this character would not say
and reword them.** That means self-contained. No see the synopsis, no as noted elsewhere.
If the sheet needs a fact, the sheet states the fact.

Length: as long as it takes to know the person, and no longer. What it must not be is a
line-by-line inventory of their dialogue.

```
# <NAME>

One sentence: who they are, what they are for in a life, and what they want. No plot
summary.

**Age span** | **MBTI** (a writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ** (reader inference)

## The thing to get right first
Only if the character has one: the single misreading that keeps happening, stated as the
correction, with a line saying that this section wins over anything below it that
contradicts it. Most sheets will not need this. The ones that do need it at the top.

## How they talk
Properties, never examples. Sentence length and rhythm. What they do first in a sentence.
Whether they answer the question they were asked. Whether they hedge. Whether they ask
questions and what shape those take. What they do when they are wrong. What they never say
and why. Whether they are allowed figurative language, and if so what kind and how they
deliver it.

Concrete enough to imitate without quoting. Instead of naming their most characteristic
line, name the move that produced it, so a writer can produce the next one.

### Dials
| | |
| :-- | :-- |
| sentence shape | their Axis 1 slot, in five words |
| disagrees by | their Axis 2 move |
| length | short or long, and with whom. Never a number |
| at length | the conditions that open this person up, written so a scene that has not been written yet can meet them, and what it costs them |
| jokiness | what KIND of funny, which matters more than how much |
| hedging | whether they hedge before a claim, and what it means when they do |
| questions | whether they ask, what shape, and at what ages |
| figurative | allowed / not allowed, per the matrix, and what kind |
| emotional range | how wide, and who is allowed to see it |

**No measured statistics.** Words per line, percentage terse, percentage hedged. Those
describe a draft that keeps moving, and writers reasonably read them as targets. One of
them was even an artefact of a script that could not see untagged dialogue, and it went
onto a sheet as a law of the character. If a number was pointing at something real, write
the tendency it was measuring in words a writer can apply to a scene that does not exist
yet.

## When they talk at length

**This section is an invitation, not a permit.** It exists to get more speech onto the
page, and it is written for scenes that do not exist yet. Every character needs at least
one condition here, and it has to be a condition, in the present tense, that a future
scene can meet: the subject they cannot leave alone, the person they explain themselves to
and nobody else, the situation that makes them argue instead of answer.

What it must not be is a sighting. *She goes long exactly once, in chapter eight* is a note
about the draft, not a condition, and a sheet written that way can only ever license the
instance that already exists.

*Never* is not an available answer. A character with no such condition cannot carry a scene
that needs one, and every later attempt to write them one will read as out of character. If
nothing in the draft shows this person opening up, that is a gap in the draft, and the
condition still has to be named so the gap can be filled.

Two things keep this honest. Say what it costs them, because someone who talks freely when
cornered is a different person from someone who talks freely only when safe. And keep the
condition specific: it should be possible to read a scene and say plainly whether the
condition is met, without arguing about it.

A named condition being met is authorisation on its own. Nobody needs to come back and ask.

## Signature - theirs alone
The one construction nobody else may use, described as a construction. Say what shape it
has, what triggers it, and how often is too often. Do not reproduce an instance of it: a
writer who has the shape can build a new one, and a writer who has only the instance can
only repeat it.

## Would they say this?
Eight or more bullets, each a testable property of a line rather than a personality
adjective. Mostly YES; two or three NOs at most.
  YES if it names the mechanism rather than the outcome
  YES if it answers the question actually asked and then stops
  NO if it accepts a compliment
Write these so a reader can apply them to a line they have never seen.

## Under pressure
The most portable section on the sheet and usually the thinnest. What happens to this
person when they are tired, wrong, cornered, losing, frightened, or caught out. What their
body does. What their speech does. What arrives afterward and when. Include the two or
three ways a writer most easily gets them wrong.

## What they are good at, and what they are not
Their language list and what they did with it, best at, hardest for them, favourite and
why, the subject they took that nobody else did, the year something clicked, what they
teach the year below from thirteen, the one they are secretly proud of.

Write a weakness the way `_CALIBRATION.md` requires: someone excellent, outperformed by
ninety other people who are also excellent, and with no way of knowing that is what
happened. State plainly what they can actually do in the subject alongside where they
place.

`CURRICULUM_GRID.md` is CHLOE's timetable, not the school's. The six permanent tracks are
shared; the language each year, the two rotating academic slots and both afternoon blocks
are individual, and two students in the same year are doing different subjects in a
different order. `_ALLOCATIONS.md` records what the sheets currently say; those choices are
yours to keep or change.

## Home
Where they are from, the house, who is in it, what their parents do, what dinner is like,
what they are like when they go back. Invent it, and make it specific.

## Body and physical business
Build, face, hands, how they move, how they take up space, what they wear when nobody is
assigning it, how they sleep, what they eat and how fast, what their hands do while they
think. Concrete and unglamorous. Invent what the manuscript does not give, which is nearly
all of it.

## Personality off the page
Who they are when nothing is happening: how they occupy a room, how they handle being
bored, being wrong, being praised, and losing; what they are like tired and at 2am; a free
Saturday; what they find funny in other people.

## Interior life
What they want that they have not said out loud. What they are afraid of, and **what they
do instead of admitting it**, which is the part a writer can stage. What they think about
before sleeping. What they would do with a free year. What they believe that the others
would argue with. **Their blind spots**: what they consistently get wrong about themselves
and about other people.

## The small stuff
Food, music, how they take coffee or tea, their handwriting, their room, what is in their
pockets, what they do with money, a ritual, how they are on a phone, what they do first
thing in the morning. This section is where a character stops being a function.

## Would do
Eight or more, concrete, positive. Actions a reader could stage, written as dispositions
rather than as recaps. *Would carry the heavy end without being asked* beats *is helpful*,
and both beat *carried the heavy end in chapter nine*.

## Would never
Two or three only, and only where the negative carries something the positive cannot. This
section is deliberately much shorter than the one above it.

## How they treat people
Whom they defer to, whom they push, what earns their respect and what loses it, and how
their behaviour changes in front of each person they actually deal with. One line each.
Say what this character wants from that person.

## Likes, dislikes, habits
Short lists, concrete. Things a reader could put in a scene.

## Age and change
How each of the above differs at the ages they appear. Several of these people are on the
page from six to twenty-one, and a trait that is a bruise at seven may be gone at twenty.
Say which traits are permanent, which are injuries that heal, and which arrive late.

## Do not write them as
Only if the character has a documented misreading. The failure mode, what produces it in
the writing, and the fix.

## Do not confuse with
The one or two characters they currently blur into, and the exact cue that separates them.
This is the one section allowed to mention another character.

---

## Book-specific: navigation and continuity only

*Facts a writer must not contradict. Nothing here characterises them.* Which chapters they
appear in, surname, birth month, family relationships, the order of a list that must stay
fixed, anything invented here that another sheet now depends on.

**The sheet must still be complete and correct if this section is deleted.** That is the
check: delete it, read what is left, and see whether you could write the character into a
haunted house.
```

## Rules for whoever fills this in

- **Read `_SHEET_RULES.md` first, then `_CALIBRATION.md`.** The first sets what a sheet is
  allowed to contain. The second sets how good these people are, how the school behaves,
  and what a weakness means here.
- **No quotations from the manuscript. None.** Not as evidence, not as illustration, not as
  a signature line, not in a table, not in a parenthesis. The draft moves and the sheet
  cannot follow it: at the last count 165 of 298 quotations across these sheets no longer
  matched the book, because the dialogue and reading-grade passes rewrote the lines they
  were quoting. A sheet that cannot be quoted cannot go stale. If a line seems too good to
  lose, that is a sign it belongs in the manuscript, where it already is.
- **No file or line references as the basis of a trait**, and no counts of how many times
  something happens in the draft. Say what the person does and when a person like them does
  it.
- **A single scene is not a rule.** The commonest defect in these sheets is a trait
  over-fitted to one moment: a signature move that happened once, a subject a character will
  not discuss because they once did not discuss it. Ask whether it is true of the person or
  only of that page. When in doubt, describe the disposition and let the writer find the
  moment.
- **Invent.** The book is Chloe's point of view, so almost nothing exists about anyone
  else's home, body, family or private life. That is a gap to fill. UNDEFINED is not an
  acceptable answer anywhere on a sheet: if the manuscript is silent, decide and write it.
- **Write the character, not the plot.** Never build a detail as setup for a later event. A
  twelve-year-old picking a language is picking a language.
- **Turn secondhand into firsthand.** Most of what exists about anyone but Chloe arrives
  reported or overheard. Take that material and write it as the character's own action.
- **No tags.** Do not mark lines [text], [inferred] or [invented]. The sheets are read by
  people writing scenes and the tags get in the way. Once the quotations are gone the tags
  have nothing to distinguish anyway.
- **Positive framing, roughly five to one.** Say what they do, reach for, like, and would
  choose. Keep the negatives for the few places where nothing else carries the point.
- **Differentiate, but never against the character.** Where the text or an established trait
  points somewhere, follow it even if another character lands nearby. Invent the difference
  in the open ground instead, and there is a great deal of open ground.
- Apply `_DIFFERENTIATION.md`. If the matrix assigns a slot, the sheet uses that slot.
- Nothing in a sheet may describe another character. *Unlike Ruth, he...* fails the
  hand-it-to-an-agent test. The one exception is the final characterising section, which
  exists for it.
- If a trait is asserted in the reference documents but not supported in the manuscript,
  either write it as the disposition it was reaching for or leave it out. Do not build a
  list of draft defects inside a character sheet; that belongs in a pass document under
  `passes/`.
