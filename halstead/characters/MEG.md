## Voice in one paragraph

She builds a sentence as an escalating three-clause question with the follow-up already loaded before the other person has answered the first part — "Is it a choice thing? You can tell me if it is. Nobody's in trouble here, I'm not going to be mad, I'd just rather know." (chapters/01_before.md). When she isn't asking, she's repeating the last thing said back at the speaker, flat, before she reacts to it. She opens institutional lines with "So" more than any other word. She never hedges — not even when she's lying. Her single most characteristic line is "'I'm doing my best guess,' she says. 'Okay?'" (chapters/02_march_4th.md:13) — the one place her two modes, competent interrogator and private guesser, sit in the same sentence, closed with a tag question that asks a six-year-old for permission.

## Signature — theirs alone

She repeats the other speaker's exact phrase back, flat, before she answers or reacts to it.

- "'You're shooting rifles.'" ... Chloe: "'Yeah, since September.'" Meg: "'Since September.'" (chapters/17_fourteen.md) — then moves straight into the real question, "Chloe. Why is this the first I'm hearing about it?"
- Dave: "'She said something on the phone in October.'" Meg: "'She said something.'" (chapters/17_fourteen.md) — repeating his own minimizing phrase back at him, unimpressed, before "And you didn't think to tell me?"

Both instances are in the same chapter (chapters/17_fourteen.md), which is a real gap — the tic needs at least one more instance elsewhere in the book to read as hers rather than a one-scene device. **Do not use the "Usually pretty good" exchange in chapters/03_the_letter.md:63–64** as a third example — that's Dave echoing Meg and Meg confirming in alternating dialogue, which is a different, working mechanism and belongs to neither of them as a signature.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it's a question that already contains its own follow-up loaded inside it before an answer arrives ("Is it a choice thing? You can tell me if it is.").
- NO if it's a flat declarative on a subject she's actually anxious about, with no question anywhere in the line — when she's scared, she asks.
- YES if it repeats the other speaker's last phrase back, unchanged, before she says anything new.
- NO if it uses a built or invented image — her deflections run on dead domestic idiom only ("on the same page"), never a fresh metaphor.
- YES if it pairs a direct emotional question with false brightness or a joke ("So you're all good... Everything's great.").
- NO if it accepts an institution's first reassurance without one more question — she always asks again.
- YES if it enumerates a danger clause by clause (ratios, hours, "what happens if it's worse than a fever").
- NO if it hedges with "maybe," "I think," "probably," or "kind of" — she doesn't, even mid-lie.
- YES if it's three words or fewer and shuts a topic down rather than opening one ("Go to bed." "Nine.") — her terseness is control, not warmth.
- NO if she starts a joke that isn't covering for something — her humor exists only as deflection, never as play for its own sake.

## Are they acting like themselves?

Under pressure she escalates toward institutions, not toward Chloe directly: she calls, she interrogates, she checks the emergency number twice from the parking lot to confirm it rings (chapters/03_the_letter.md). She touches before she talks in an emotional scene — a hand flat on Chloe's back, holding her on the bedroom floor, fixing a collar that doesn't need it (chapters/08_the_asking.md; chapters/09_february.md; CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md Ch18). She occupies rooms at counters, sinks, and doorframes, moving, hands busy with an object — checking boxes on a clipboard, moving a water glass two inches — rather than sitting still to talk something through. She never stays visibly upset in front of Chloe; the one place she's shown falling apart is alone, in a parked car with the engine off, seen only through a kitchen window (chapters/09_february.md) — off her own daughter's direct sightline.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:**
1. Writing her as openly weepy or expressive in front of Chloe. The text keeps her composure intact until she's alone; breaking that on the page changes her.
2. Having her accept an institution's first answer. She always has one more question — if a line lets a school or a doctor's reassurance stand unchallenged, it's not hers.
3. Writing a joke from her with nothing underneath it to deflect from. Her jokes are load-bearing, not decorative.

## Known problems

- **She loses her own story, and it's measurable.** 10–20 speaking turns a chapter through chapters/01–09 (the collapse and the "yes" are hers), then 4 turns in chapters/10 — all secondhand, by phone — 0 in chapters/13, and only 3 lines at the graduation that her own chapters/09 scene explicitly promises a payoff for. Neither parent appears at all in the final eight chapters of the book. **Fix:** give her one unshared scene with Chloe alone at or after graduation — the ending she was promised in chapters/09.
- **"She got polite" is not in the manuscript.** BETA_NOTES.md cites it four times, SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md twice (including as a timeline anchor at age 5), both presenting it as a direct quote — "She stopped talking. At home. She used to talk the whole time... She got polite." I searched every chapter file and MANUSCRIPT_FULL.md for "got polite," "she stopped talking," and "talk the whole time" and found no match. **Fix:** either write the scene the reference documents assume exists, or correct the reference documents.
- **Question rate is inverted.** She asks in 4% of her lines against a target of 20%+, while Dave — whose competence is documents, not people — asks in 15%. Since interrogation is specifically her established skill, every revision pass on her dialogue should default to a question before a statement.
- **Terseness is currently doing avoidance's job instead of control's.** 54% of her lines are three words or fewer, the highest in the book — higher than Sam, higher than Kavi. Some of that is right (her shutdown mode, "Go to bed," "Nine"), but at this volume it reads as underwritten rather than clipped-on-purpose. Target 40%; rebuild the cut lines as the escalating three-clause questions in her Dials row, not as restored filler.

## Do not confuse with

**Dave.** A blind test of 10 lines each, stripped of attribution, currently reassigns 17 of 20 correctly to the wrong parent — the worst score of any pair in the book. The one reliable cue in dialogue alone: she asks questions and repeats the other speaker's phrase back before answering; he closes a beat with "Huh." and asks what something costs. Everything else — the exact-repetition tic, the multi-clause enumeration, the plain declarative register — currently reads as one voice in two bodies, which is the defect this sheet exists to fix.
