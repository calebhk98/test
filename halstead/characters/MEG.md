## 2. Voice profile

**Sentence shape.** Short declaratives stacked without much subordination when she's anxious ("It's free. Four weeks, food, a bed, a bus, and it's free. Things are not free."), but she's capable of long, exactly-controlled multi-clause sentences when she's doing due diligence — the roof line runs three independent clauses joined with "and," each one landing on its own beat. She rarely uses a sentence over 25 words except when enumerating conditions (the phone interrogation, the gas-oven speech).

**How she opens a line.** Frequently with a flat restatement of what was just said ("Careful how?" pattern — repeating the previous speaker's key word back as a question), or with "No" / "Okay" as a beat before the real sentence starts. She opens more lines with "So" than any other tracked word — "So you're all good," "So how do they have her name," "So what is that" — a verbal tic that reads as her trying to move a conversation toward a conclusion she can act on.

**Repetition.** This is her most identifiable tic, and it's also shared with Dad (see head-to-head, below): she repeats a phrase exactly, twice, under pressure. "Usually pretty good." "Usually pretty good." "We're saying yes, Chloe. We're saying yes." "She said something." (echoing him). It reads as a stalling device — buying a second to control her voice — more than as emphasis.

**Questions vs. statements.** Question-heavy in institutional settings (the district call, the Halstead call, the doctor's office), statement-heavy at home. The ratio flips depending on whether she's extracting information from an authority or delivering a verdict to her family.

**Jokes.** Two on the page, both flagged by the synopsis as evasions: "She probably just wants everybody on the same page. Literally." (ch1, re: the confiscated book) and the lie in the car, "So you're all good... Everything's great." (ch2), which isn't a joke exactly but performs the same function — false lightness deployed to avoid a real conversation.

**Figurative language.** None. Not one simile or built image in any line attributed to her. This matches the style guide's rule that figurative language belongs only in a POV character's mouth (Chloe), and it means Meg's plainness is by design, not a gap.

**Most characteristic line:** "I'm doing my best guess. Okay?" (chapters/02_march_4th.md). It's the one line where her two dominant modes — institutional competence (she's filling out a clinical intake form) and private uncertainty (she doesn't actually know how her daughter feels) — collide in one sentence, and the "Okay?" tag turns a private confession into a request for permission from a six-year-old. Nothing else she says compresses that much.

## 2a. Register vs. the students — do the adults sound older?

*(This section is identical in FATHER.md.)*

Yes, early — genuinely, measurably — and the gap closes or reverses later, which is itself the finding.

**Ages 6–7 (ch1–9): real separation.** Child dialogue in this band is short and repetitive by design: Kayleigh Burns's "You talk weird" (three words, no clause), Bryce's "That was different." / "How was it different, though?" / "It just was," (ch1) — flat assertion, no elaboration, no willingness to defend the claim. Against that, Meg's institutional register is unmistakably adult: "I'm asking whether anybody has ever made an exception to it, for any reason, in any year" (ch7) has a subordinate clause and a hedge ("for any reason, in any year") no child in the book produces at six or seven. This is real and it's the clearest evidence in the manuscript that the author can write more than one register.

**But Chloe herself complicates it immediately.** At six, arguing library policy: "The rule is about at a time, though. So six is fine, because it's still four at a time, it's just four and then four again, and two of them are ones you already gave me." (ch1) That's a longer, more tightly reasoned sentence than most of what Meg says in the same chapter — a six-year-old out-arguing the adult register around her. So the "adults sound older" finding is true on average, not true as a hard rule, and it's true despite Chloe rather than because the book withholds complexity from children categorically.

**Ages 8+ (ch10 onward): the gap closes, then reverses.** Once Halstead training starts, the cohort's diction gets denser and more technical while the parents' does not grow with them. Compare Dad's longest sustained arguments — "So how does a four-year-old school get that guy. What does it pay him. With what." (ch3); "This is four pages of the same trick, isn't it." (ch12) — against Chloe at eleven: "It's bread with a filling in it, so it's a sandwich, and I don't think there's a way out of that... The only thing you've given me is a hinge, and a hinge is a fact about bread, not a fact about food." (ch11) or Ruth at nine, unprompted: "Because it's already in everything... That's not learning a language. That's collecting on one you did four years ago." (ch14). By the tariffs chapter (ch13), Chloe is constructing multi-step economic arguments ("If you're wrong about one of those you can't get it back. The factory goes, then the people who knew how to run it get other jobs, and then in fifteen years they're sixty and there's nobody left who's ever done it.") that are longer and more structurally ambitious than anything her father says in the same scene — he's asking short, genuine follow-up questions, and she's the one doing the sustained reasoning.

**The honest summary:** the adults sound older than the students only in the first third of the book, when the students are genuinely young. Once Halstead's curriculum takes over the children's syntax, the parents' register stays essentially flat — plain, short, undecorated, the same "Cool" / "Huh" / short-declarative register at sixteen that it was at six — while the children's complexity keeps climbing. That flatness may be the right choice (ordinary adults staying ordinary against extraordinary children is arguably the point), but as a test of "can this author write more than one adult register," the honest answer is: one clear adult register early, largely overtaken by the children's own trained register later, with no second or evolving adult register to compare it against.

## 3. The swap test

I stripped attribution from ten of Meg's lines and ten of Dad's lines and tried to reassign them blind. Full results and the count are in the **Head-to-head** section below, shared with FATHER.md. Short version: of Meg's ten, roughly eight or nine survive being reassigned to Dad without a reader noticing from the words alone. The two that don't survive are the graduation lines (a physical gesture — fixing the collar — paired with hyperbolic emotional excess, "I'm so proud of you I could be sick," which nothing on Dad's page matches) and, more weakly, the gas-oven itemization, which only barely reads as hers because Dad does the identical rhetorical move (clause-by-clause enumeration of a problem) aimed at a different target.

Beyond the ten in the formal test, a much longer list of her lines would survive a swap with almost anybody in the cast who is playing "worried adult": "Do you want to talk about it?" "Okay, honey." "That's a very big question and it's not a car question." None of these carry a fingerprint. If you handed them to a reader cold, next to an unlabeled line from Dr. Ammons or Ms. Vance, I don't think the reader could sort them reliably either.

## 5. Personality, likes, dislikes, habits

- **Excellent at institutional due diligence** [textual] — the 25-minute Halstead call (ch3), the district call (ch3), the school-safety interrogation on arrival (ch4: "She does not leave until she has seen the room, the food, the nurse, and the front desk"), calling the emergency number twice to confirm it rings (ch3).
- **Bad at sitting with a hard thing** [textual] — the book joke (ch1), the car lie (ch2), landing on "you'll make friends this year" instead of hearing what Chloe actually said (ch8).
- **Physically affectionate under stress, verbally withheld** [textual] — hand flat on Chloe's back (ch8), holding her on the floor (ch9), fixing a collar that doesn't need fixing (v2 Ch18). She touches before she talks, consistently.
- **Repeats herself exactly when under pressure** [textual] — see Voice profile, above. A verbal tic, not an emotion being named.
- **Keeps one exit at all times** [textual] — refusing the house (ch9) is the clearest instance, but the same instinct shows earlier: she never fully trusts an institution's own account of itself (checks the emergency number personally rather than taking Halstead's word for it, ch3).
- **Protective of routine/normalcy as a coping mechanism** [inferred] — the fridge magnet, the sticky note, the report card ritual (ch2) suggest she manages anxiety by converting it into small fixed objects and rituals, though the text never states this as her interior reasoning (correctly, per the style guide — no interiority for non-POV characters).

**The count: 17 of 20 survive an honest swap.** Only three lines resist reassignment, and one of those three (#12) resists only because it contains the word "Meg" — take the name out and it goes back in the "survives" column, which means the real number of lines separable by voice alone is closer to **18/20 interchangeable, 2/20 distinct**.

**What actually separates them, since diction mostly doesn't:**

1. **Target, not tone.** Both parents run the identical rhetorical move — an escalating, multi-clause interrogation of a problem — but Meg always aims it at an *institution or a danger* (the district, the school, the gas oven, the rifle range) and Dad always aims it at a *document or a number* (the faculty roster, the vendor proposal, tariffs, the website's production budget). Swap the target and the sentences are near-identical in shape.

2. **The exact-repetition tic is shared, not distinguishing.** "Usually pretty good. Usually pretty good." (Meg) and "I thought you knew. I thought you knew and it was fine." (Dad) are the same device. If the goal is separable voices, this needs to belong to only one of them.

3. **Physical vs. verbal register.** Meg touches — a hand flat on the back, holding Chloe on the floor, fixing a collar. Dad photographs, drives, builds (the back steps), and once suppresses a laugh into his glass rather than voicing it. Neither parent's dialogue alone signals this; it's almost entirely stage direction, which means it's invisible in a transcript-only swap test and only visible in full scene context.

4. **He says her name; she says his.** "Meg" (Dad, twice) and "Dave" (Meg, twice — ch6: "Four hours away, Dave"; ch9: "She said take my birthday, Dave"). This is the single most reliable tag separating them in the charged scenes, and it's a name-drop, not a voice marker — which means if a reader ever loses track of who's talking, the text's actual fallback is proper nouns, not diction.

5. **One small, real, and unremarked tic: Dad says "Huh."** as a low-commitment closing beat at least twice (ch1, ch15/ch14 depending on chapter — "'Huh,' her father says, and holds his hand out for the ham"). I found no instance of Meg saying "Huh." It's minor, but it's genuine, and it's the kind of thing that should be built on rather than left as a coincidence.

**Verdict:** the two parents are reliably distinguished by *what they investigate* (danger vs. money) and by *stage direction* (touch vs. photograph), not by *how they talk*. On the page, stripped of tags, they are close to one voice wearing two roles.
