# Nadia

Runs on proportion: fixes exactly what's broken and no more, and wants to build something an actual person uses instead of something built for other companies to buy.

**Age span:** ~15–21 (est.) | **MBTI:** ESTJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** est. 160+ (reader inference)

**Speaks:** 41 lines total, badly front-loaded — only 5 lines in the first 20 chapters, the rest split across two later files, a third of it one- or two-word chat filler ("yeah," "hang on," "since when").

## Voice in one paragraph
Short independent clauses with no connectives — she doesn't join ideas with "and," "but," or "so," she sets one fact next to the next one. Near-imperative even outside actual commands: "Go back and fight the waitress." She opens on the fact, never on a lead-in ("so," "well," "look"). She essentially never asks — questions measure 0% of her lines, and her one rhetorical exception answers itself in the same breath ("why would a machine have a port"). She disagrees by flat refusal or correction, proportionate to the offense, and then moves on without relitigating. Zero figurative language anywhere. Most characteristic line: "if youre going to come at me, come at me. dont insult me with a cleaner" (CHAPTERS_23_30_v2.md, "Nadia," line 47) — paired imperatives, no adjective, no explanation of why a cleaner counts as an insult. The reader supplies that.

## Home
A small industrial city a few hours from anywhere Halstead's other families have heard of. Her parents emigrated from Gujarat before she was born and run a hardware-and-repair shop two blocks off the main road — tools, small appliances, whatever a neighborhood wants fixed instead of replaced. She's kept the books since she was eleven, in a paper ledger her father still trusts more than the software their accountant keeps suggesting. Gujarati is spoken at the counter and at the table — the one language of the eleven Halstead ever assigned her that isn't it, which she finds more funny than sad. [invented]

A younger brother, Rohan, thirteen, works Saturdays and would rather be anywhere else; she doesn't resent him for it, she just does the parts he won't. Dinner is late, after the shop closes, and half of it is her parents disagreeing out loud about a supplier or a customer asking for a discount for cash. She has opinions and states them, and by fourteen her father sometimes took them over his own. [invented]

She has watched adults be wrong about money her whole life — not dishonest, wrong: her mother discounting her own hours to match a competitor's price, her father calling a slow month bad luck when the ledger says it's the same three customers switching suppliers every March. That's where "they all put labor under costs" comes from — a decade of watching her family's actual work get treated as the free part of the business, not an idea she picked up from reading about the economy. [invented, ties CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md's labor speech to a personal source]

When she goes back she doesn't announce herself. She checks the till against yesterday's tape before she says hello, notices what's been moved, and has usually fixed one small thing by the time anyone asks how school is. [inferred]

## Body
Compact, fast, always already moving — she's rarely photographed still because she's rarely still. Pushes her sleeves up before saying anything difficult, a habit that reads to anyone who's seen it enough times as a warning the way a throat-clear is for other people. Dark hair kept short enough that she doesn't have to think about it before block one. Hands are the most-used part of her — short nails, a burn scar on one knuckle from the shop she's never explained to anyone at school, a grip that surprises people the first time she shakes a hand rather than the last. Eats fast and standing up when nobody's watching, sits properly when someone is because it's faster than arguing about it. Sleeps in short, complete units rather than long ones and wakes without a transition — the four minutes sitting up in the diner booth is the rule, not the exception. Wears what's practical over what matches; the one thing she's picky about is boots, because the shop taught her what happens to feet in bad ones. [invented]
