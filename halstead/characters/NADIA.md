## Voice in one paragraph
Short independent clauses with no connectives: she doesn't join ideas with "and," "but," or "so," she sets one fact next to the next one. Near-imperative even outside actual commands, as in "Go back and fight the waitress." She opens on the fact, never on a lead-in ("so," "well," "look"). She essentially never asks; questions measure 0% of her lines, and her one rhetorical exception answers itself in the same breath ("why would a machine have a port"). She disagrees by flat refusal or correction, proportionate to the offense, then moves on without relitigating. Zero figurative language anywhere. Her most characteristic line is "if youre going to come at me, come at me. dont insult me with a cleaner" (chapters/28_nadia.md through chapters/35_nine_minutes.md, "Nadia," line 47): paired imperatives, no adjective, no explanation of why a cleaner counts as an insult. The reader supplies that.

## Subjects and schedule
Her language list, one a year from seven: Spanish, French, Arabic, Mandarin, Russian, Turkish, Farsi, Urdu, Bengali, Malay, Portuguese. Nobody else in her cohort has this list or this order.

Her ten-page reports skew practical: a customs form, a labor contract, a trade publication with real numbers running through it. She reads plenty of literary work in every language on her list. What she wants back for an hour of effort is something checkable.

**Best at, and known for:** Farsi. She started it the year negotiation went live with real counterparts across languages, needed it to hold her own in the room, and kept reaching for it after the requirement ended. It's also the one language on her list nobody else in her cohort has.

**Worst at, though still fluent enough to argue a fine point in it inside an hour with no formal study behind her:** anything with nobody left alive to use it on. She helped Kavi with a Latin passage once and worked out the line he was stuck on before he did. She still doesn't count it as something she's good at: there's no one on the other end of it, nothing to test the result against, and she doesn't trust a result she can't test.

**Favourite, and why:** the negotiation term itself, not a language. Live, adversarial, and scored where everyone can see the result: the shape of everything she does outside a classroom.

**Least favourite:** the written component of anything. She'll take a language, a negotiation, a repair, over a report about one every time.

**The year something clicked:** the negotiation term. Farsi and the exercise landed together, and she worked out that a language is another way of finding out what somebody wants, not a separate skill from the one she already had.

**What she teaches the twelves, from thirteen:** negotiation, one hour a week. She grades them on whether they walked out with what they came in for, not on technique.

**Secretly proud of:** she keeps a Farsi phrasebook long after the requirement that made her start it, and calls it "useful" if anyone asks.

**Block two, over the years:** poker, auto mechanics, gardening, glassblowing. Auto mechanics is the one that stuck: she can hear a fault in an engine before the hood is up, the way some people can hear which string on a guitar is flat. Nobody else in her cohort has this. Poker gave her the stone face early. Glassblowing she dropped fast, since most of the work is waiting on the material to be ready and she'd rather make it ready herself. Gardening lasted one growing season and stopped the day it stopped producing anything that needed fixing.

## Geography
A small industrial city in the Ohio River valley, upriver from Pittsburgh. Nobody else in her cohort comes from this stretch of the country.

## Home
Her parents emigrated from Gujarat before she was born and run a hardware-and-repair shop two blocks off the main road: tools, small appliances, whatever a neighborhood wants fixed instead of replaced. She's kept the books since she was eleven, in a paper ledger her father still trusts more than the software their accountant keeps suggesting. Gujarati is spoken at the counter and at the table, the one language of the eleven Halstead assigned her that isn't it. She finds this funny.

A younger brother, Rohan, thirteen, works Saturdays and would rather be anywhere else. She quietly does the parts he won't. Dinner is late, after the shop closes, and half of it is her parents disagreeing out loud about a supplier or a customer asking for a discount for cash. She has opinions and states them, and by fourteen her father sometimes took them over his own.

She has watched adults be wrong about money her whole life: her mother discounting her own hours to match a competitor's price, her father calling a slow month bad luck when the ledger says it's the same three customers switching suppliers every March. She formed strong, specific opinions about what running a business costs at a kitchen table with a ledger open on it, long before anyone at Halstead asked her opinion about anything.

When she goes back, she checks the till against yesterday's tape before she says hello, and by the time anyone asks how school is, she's usually already fixed one small thing that had drifted out of place.

## Body
Compact, fast, always already moving: rarely photographed still because she's rarely still. She pushes her sleeves up before saying anything difficult, a habit that reads to anyone who's seen it enough times as a warning, the way a throat-clear is for other people. Dark hair kept short enough that she doesn't have to think about it before block one. Hands are the most-used part of her: short nails, a burn scar on one knuckle from the shop she's never explained to anyone at school, a grip that surprises people the first time she shakes a hand. Eats fast and standing up when nobody's watching, sits properly when someone is because it's faster than arguing about it. She sleeps in short, complete units and wakes without a transition; the four minutes sitting up in the diner booth is her ordinary pattern. She wears what's practical over what matches. The one thing she's picky about is boots, because the shop taught her what happens to feet in bad ones.

## Personality off the page
- In a room she's running (an interview, the third-floor drill, her own company), she's physically forward and central, closest to the problem, closest to the door. People orient to her without being told to.
- In a room she isn't running, she plants herself in one spot and works from there. At the dinner table she holds a corner of a napkin down with one finger through an entire argument instead of getting up or leaning in (chapters/16_thirteen.md).
- Bored, she finds another task rather than saying so.
- Wrong, she corrects course immediately once she's spotted the mistake and doesn't relitigate the decision that got her there. She keeps making her own hiring test easier without noticing she's the one who moved the bar; the moment she does notice, she says so flatly and moves straight to fixing it.
- Praised, she turns it back onto the mechanism rather than accepting it about herself: a compliment about her company becomes a comment about the process.
- Targeted, or on the losing end of something, she treats it as information to act on. When someone plants a USB stick on a cleaning contract to get into her systems, she feels insulted, not threatened: "im FINE. im insulted." She goes straight to fixing it.
- Tired, or at two in the morning, she sleeps in short complete units and comes back online without a transition: asleep sitting up in a diner booth for four minutes, then awake, finishing the sentence she left off on. She treats sleep as a task to complete.
- A free Saturday goes to something with a visible end state: a repair, a fix, physical work.
- What she finds funny in other people is confidence that's outrun competence, played straight without comment: Sam wanting a "real" opponent while a fifty-year-old waitress on her feet nine hours a day would take him apart (chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md).

## Interior life
Wants to be trusted with something bigger before anyone thinks to offer it to her, rather than earning her way into it by increments.

Afraid of being right about a person too late, having already extended trust the way she extended it to a set of glowing references, and having it cost more than a USB stick next time.

Before sleeping, thinks about whatever she didn't finish that day: not people, tasks. The repair she didn't get to. The candidate who wouldn't give her a straight second answer.

A free year, she'd spend taking apart something too big for one person to justify taking apart alone: an engine, a company, a system, to see the whole shape of it once.

Believes a felt sense of unfairness is data, not a feeling. If a system produces a result that seems wrong, she trusts that read over an official explanation, every time.

She's wrong about this: she assumes most people can be read as fast as a maintenance supervisor with a badge copied off him in forty seconds. Most people take longer. Interviewing four hundred candidates for her own company is where that assumption fails her: she keeps a fast read on people who needed a slow one, waters her own bar down looking for a shortcut, and doesn't notice the bar moved until the pass rate is under a third.

## The small stuff
Black coffee, no doctoring, drunk too fast to taste. Handwriting small, fast, leaning right, with numbers noticeably cleaner than letters: the ledger hand. Room kept sparse by choice rather than neglect; a toolbox gets more care than the furniture. Pockets usually hold something that isn't hers yet: a part, a tool, an ID she'll hand back before the day's out. She sends a fixed amount home from every real paycheck before spending anything else on herself, and has never mentioned it in the chat. No superstition she'd admit to; the closest thing is checking a lock twice and calling it thoroughness. On a phone or in the chat she replies fast and short and drops out of a thread the moment it stops being useful. First thing in the morning, she's awake and moving before she's decided to be, the same as coming out of sleep anywhere else.
