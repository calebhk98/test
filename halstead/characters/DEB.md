# Deb

Chloe's colleague at the translation company: the textual proof that Chloe's isolation is circumstance, not temperament — she likes this woman, and works to be understood by her, and never once gets a quoted line of her own.

**Age span:** adult, ongoing coworker relationship starting when Chloe is nineteen (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch20 "Nineteen") | **MBTI:** ESFJ (writing aid, not a claim) | **IQ:** not inferable — nothing in the text shows her reasoning, only her presence and her resistance to a certain kind of explanation
**Appears in:** CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch20 "Nineteen," only — two passages, both narrated summary rather than scene. **A second, unrelated character also named Deb appears in chapters/05_behind.md** — see Known problems; do not treat these as the same person without flagging it to the author first.
**Speaks:** 0 quoted lines. Everything about her — what she talks about, how she reacts to Chloe's explanations — is reported in Chloe's narrated summary, never rendered as dialogue in scene. This is itself the finding a rewriter needs: there is no voice on the page to imitate, only a behavior pattern to write toward.

## Voice in one paragraph

There is no voice to describe. Deb has zero quoted dialogue anywhere in the manuscript I was given. What exists instead is character established entirely through Chloe's report of the relationship: "Deb who sits opposite her talks about her son constantly and Chloe learns his entire life story and is genuinely invested in it by October" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch20 "Nineteen"), and later, "She'll explain something at work, plainly, and Deb will not have it, so Chloe will find another way in" (same chapter). A rewriter who wants Deb to speak on the page is not restoring anything — they are writing new material from a two-sentence behavioral sketch: a woman who talks readily about her family, and who pushes back, more than once, on a plain first explanation of something Chloe knows.

Not applicable in the standard sense — there are no lines to test against. ## The kindly-and-inadequate test

Deb's behavior isn't framed by the text as a failure at all — unlike the school-era adults, nothing about her is shown costing Chloe anything. If a rewriter needs the vocabulary anyway: a **kindly-and-inadequate** version of "Deb won't have it" would be ordinary human friction — she genuinely doesn't follow the plain version and needs the concrete one, the way plenty of people do, with no judgment attached; Chloe herself files the extra work as "ordinary courtesy," not as a burden. An **unkind** version — unsupported anywhere in the text — would have Deb dismiss Chloe's plain explanations as showing off, or resent needing the second pass. Nothing in the text supports that reading; the relationship is presented as warm on both sides.

## Are they acting like themselves?

There is no scene of her acting — everything is summarized across a period of months, not staged moment to moment. What the summary establishes: she talks about her son "constantly," to the point that Chloe knows his "entire life story" by October and is "genuinely invested" in it, which is offered as evidence Chloe likes people fine when the setting lets her (compare SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md:69, which lists "Deb from work and Deb's son's entire life story" among Chloe's *likes* — a claim the primary text directly supports). Separately, she resists Chloe's plain, direct explanations at work and needs them reframed — a pattern Chloe notices in herself only in aggregate, never in Deb: "It does not occur to her that she is doing it constantly" (CHAPTERS_16_22_v2.md, Ch20 "Nineteen") is about Chloe's own blindness to the pattern, not about anything Deb does wrong.

**Failure modes for a rewriter:**
1. Writing dialogue for her without flagging it as invention. Every existing sentence about her is summary; there is no scene to extend.
2. Treating "Deb will not have it" as friction or conflict. The text frames it as ordinary — Chloe reclassifies it as "courtesy," not an obstacle — and there's no evidence Deb experiences it as anything either.
3. Conflating her with the unrelated swim-instructor Deb in chapters/05_behind.md (see Known problems) — the two share nothing but a name.

## Known problems

- **Name collision with an unrelated minor character.** chapters/05_behind.md:55 introduces "a woman called Deb who has them blow bubbles with their faces in the water for two days" — the swimming instructor at the Halstead summer camp, when Chloe is seven. This is a different person in every respect: different setting (a children's summer program, not a workplace), different decade of Chloe's life, no shared trait, and no textual link connecting them. SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md's own "Deb" entry (line 391) describes only the colleague and does not mention the swim instructor at all, which supports reading these as two separate characters who happen to share a name — very likely the coin-flip naming collision the author flagged as a known risk across the manuscript. **Flag to the author directly: confirm whether these are meant to be the same person (in which case the sixteen-year gap and complete absence of any callback needs addressing) or, far more likely, rename one of them to remove the collision.**
- **Zero quoted dialogue for a character load-bearing enough to appear in the synopsis's character list and in Chloe's own "likes."** The claims the reference documents make about her — "the evidence that the isolation isn't misanthropy" and "the person Chloe has to explain everything to twice" — are both supported by the primary text (see Voice paragraph above), but entirely through summary, never through a rendered scene. If the author wants Deb to do more work in the book, she currently has no scene to build from, only a description of one.
- **The "twice" framing in the task brief is slightly imprecise.** The text doesn't show Chloe explaining a single thing to Deb exactly twice — it describes a repeating pattern ("she keeps having to say the same thing twice" as a general habit, not a one-time count) that generalizes beyond Deb to how Chloe operates at work overall. Worth the distinction: it's a recurring mode, not a single two-step exchange.
