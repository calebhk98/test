# DR. SANDOVAL

Lead researcher on an internal paper Chloe contributes to. Makes a request instead of an instruction, and names the exact reason she has to phrase it that way.

## Voice in one paragraph
Sandoval names the power imbalance in her own request before making it, rather than letting it stay implicit: "I need to ask you for something, and I would like you to understand while I am asking that it is a request and not an instruction." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:133) She then explains, in a single long turn built from short clauses joined without softening, exactly why she cannot make it an instruction: "You have not signed anything, and I am not going to put a federal NDA in front of a fifteen-year-old, because I do not think anybody should be asking you to sign one." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:137) She is precise rather than warm — "Plus, it's not legally binding" is delivered as a correction of the record, not a reassurance — and she states the actual cost of the choice she's asking for plainly instead of minimizing it: "The consequences land on the four of us." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:137) Most characteristic line: "You can't be held to anything. I can. So asking is what I have." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:145) — because it is the only line from any teacher in this cast that explicitly states the mechanism of its own persuasion out loud, rather than just using the mechanism.

## Signature — theirs alone
States the structural reason for her own restraint as the content of the request itself, rather than leaving the power imbalance unspoken: "You can't be held to anything. I can. So asking is what I have." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:145) No other teacher in this cast narrates the mechanics of their own authority out loud this way.

## Would they say this?
- YES if it names a request as a request, explicitly, before making it.
- YES if it states plainly who bears the consequence of a choice, without minimizing it.
- YES if it corrects an imprecise legal or factual assumption in the middle of an otherwise emotional ask. ("Plus, it's not legally binding.")
- NO if it pressures, guilts, or implies obligation rather than stating the actual stakes and leaving the decision open.
- NO if it treats a fifteen-year-old's understanding as automatically less complete than an adult's — she explicitly declines to simplify the situation for Chloe's age.
- YES if the whole persuasive weight of the line comes from naming the mechanism rather than hiding it.

## Are they acting like themselves?
Given the choice between a legal instrument (an NDA) and a personal one (a request), she picks the personal one and says why, on the record, to a minor, rather than taking the easier legal route: "I am not going to put a federal NDA in front of a fifteen-year-old, because I do not think anybody should be asking you to sign one." (chapters/18_fifteen.md:137) That is a real cost to her — the text is explicit that the consequences of Chloe talking land on Sandoval and three others, not on Chloe — and she states that cost herself rather than letting Chloe discover it later. Failure mode a writer would fall into: having her frame the request as being for Chloe's own good, or soften it with reassurance that "it'll be fine" — the actual line does neither; it states a real, asymmetric risk and leaves the decision entirely with the student.
