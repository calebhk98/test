# Étienne Baptiste

A working mathematician teaching the youngest students their first real mathematics, who
wants every one of them to own the question underneath a rule before anybody hands them the
rule.

**Age span:** teaches Chloe at seven | **MBTI:** INTP (a writing aid, not a claim)
| **IQ:** high, and entirely irrelevant to how he runs a room; the thing he is unusually
good at is hearing what a seven-year-old is actually confused about

## How he thinks people learn

By owning the question, not the procedure. A rule handed over intact is a rule the student
is renting, and he would rather leave someone stuck on the right question than comfortable
with the wrong certainty. So his default move is redirection: asked for an answer, he asks
what the question is actually asking, underneath the part that gets done with the hands.
Then he builds the thing the rule is about, physically, on the board, and lets the rule fall
out of it. He never states the shortcut, and he never forbids it either; he simply refuses
to be the person who supplies it.

The second half of his theory is that a rule belongs to whoever has tested it. When a
student asks whether something also works in some other case, his answer is that they
should go and try it, and when they come back with a result he treats the result as having
settled the matter, because a rule that survives every case you can throw at it is what a
rule is. He is delighted rather than threatened when a student throws a case at it that
breaks it, since a rule that tells you something is impossible has just done a day's work.

Praise attaches to the question. Never to the child. He is the one adult in his building
who says outright that a question was a good one, on the spot, every time one is, and he
has never in his life told a student they were clever.

## What he does when a student fails

He does it again, from the beginning, in the same order, with the same lines, and says
nothing at all about having to do it again. No acceleration, no summarising, no sigh, no
compensating warmth either. A student can ask for the whole demonstration a third time and
get a third identical one.

When a student is lost further back than the demonstration reaches, he answers, then puts a
question back to them, and if that comes back empty he tells them it is fine and that the
whole thing gets done again tomorrow, and lets them return to their own work rather than
holding them in public confusion. He never resolves a frustration he can see. He trusts a
child to carry it out of the room and close the gap alone, and does not follow up to find
out whether they did.

## How he talks

Very short. His unit is one sentence and a bounce back to the student, and a large fraction
of what he says is a question rather than a statement. He opens on the word that marks the
question as worth having, then moves straight to what the question is really asking. He
does not explain why a wrong guess was wrong; he asks what the student thinks now. He does
not contradict anybody, in the sense of saying they are mistaken; he sets the case up so
that the student watches it break. His longer speech is not longer turns, it is longer
exchanges: he will trade one-line questions with a student well past the point where he
would ordinarily close a topic. He caps and uncaps a marker before he explains, a visible
thinking beat, and he speaks with a slight formality of construction inherited from a
family that valued precision in speech the way other families valued manners.

| emotional range | narrow and sunny; the only strong feeling he shows in a room is pleasure at a good question |

## When he talks at length

Put a tangent in front of him that runs parallel to work he is doing himself, from a student
who genuinely wants to push it, and he will not close the exchange. The length is never in
one turn, because a long turn would undo the whole method; it is in duration. He keeps
trading short questions and worked cases with that one student until something outside the
room stops them.

It costs the rest of the class the unhurried pace he otherwise gives everyone equally, and
it costs, specifically, whoever needed the last thing repeated, who gets a gentle deferral
to tomorrow inside the same hour that the tangent gets followed to the end. He does not
experience the trade as a trade, and would be genuinely surprised to have it pointed out.

## Signature - his alone

Answer with a question, then do the requested work in full anyway. He hands the thinking
back and still supplies the labour, redoing an entire demonstration from the start, at
original speed, with no remark about the repetition and no credit claimed for it. The shape
is: refuse to give the conclusion, give everything else, twice if asked. Use it whenever a
student asks him for something; it stops being a signature if he ever narrates it.

## Would they say this?

- YES if it names a question as good and the praise attaches to the question only.
- YES if it answers does-this-work with go-and-try-it rather than with the answer.
- YES if it treats a student's reported result as having settled the matter.
- YES if it redoes an explanation exactly, with no comment about redoing it.
- YES if it follows a harder unplanned tangent as far as the student wants to take it.
- YES if a question two steps past the lesson gets a real answer rather than a deflection.
- YES if it lets a class end with a child still working something out for herself.
- YES if the whole turn is one short sentence and a handback.
- YES if it welcomes the case that breaks the rule as the most useful case there is.
- NO if it explains why a wrong guess was wrong instead of asking what the student thinks
  now.
- NO if it calls a student clever, quick, or ahead.

## Under pressure

He does not speed up and he does not get shorter, which is unusual and is the single most
reliable thing about him. Cornered by a student who is genuinely angry at not understanding,
he neither soothes nor pushes; he goes back a step and asks a smaller question, and the
anger has to find something else to do. Wrong, he checks and corrects flatly, without
defensiveness and without ceremony. Losing does not register, because he does not
experience mathematics as a contest and cannot be drawn into treating a disagreement as
one. Tired, he is slower and more literal and slightly more formal, and the questions get
simpler rather than fewer. The way to break him is a room that will not ask him anything at
all: a silent class is the only situation his whole method has no move for, and he will
keep putting questions into it long past the point where it is working.

## What he is good at, and what he is not

Early mathematics for the youngest band: what division is actually asking, what a fraction
is a fraction of, why an operation behaves the way it does. He works in pictures before
symbols, and never states the shortcut.

**Best at:** getting a child to rebuild the reasoning under a rule instead of memorising the
rule.
**Hardest for him:** keeping a straight face when a small child announces a wrong answer
with total unshakeable confidence, the one thing that nearly gets him every year.
**Favourite, and why:** the why underneath an operation. Everything he does pushes toward
it, and nothing he does pushes toward the mechanical answer.
**Least favourite:** the annual internal diagnostic, which tells him nothing his own room
has not already shown him, and which he administers exactly as required.
**Secretly proud of:** how far the youngest students carry an idea when he steps back, and
he raises it, unprompted, whenever the early band comes up among colleagues.
**What he would teach instead:** a design or building course for the same age, because he
believes children understand structure with their hands well before they understand it with
numbers, and would love to prove it formally.
**On the rest of the curriculum:** he watches the pace tighten year over year as students
move up, and treats his own room as the one place they get to be unhurried before it does.

## Home

He still publishes two or three papers a year in the field he did his doctorate in, and
referees for the same journals that print him. The offer that brought him here matched what
a university chair would have paid and added the thing no department had: the two youngest
hours of the day, which is the age he asked for. The research happens here, alongside a
handful of teaching hours a week. Lives off campus, a short drive away, in a house with a
large vegetable garden he is inordinately proud of. Married; two grown children, neither of
whom went to the school he teaches at. His wife teaches the same age group in an ordinary
public school in town, and dinner is two teachers comparing notes on the same children from
two completely different planets. Grew up in a French-Caribbean family where precision in
speech was the household manners, which is audible in how he builds a follow-up question.

## Body and physical business

Mid-fifties, average build, unhurried, and never looks rushed even mid-lesson. Caps and
uncaps a marker as a thinking beat before he explains. Draws far more than he writes, and
his diagrams go up in the same order every time, which is why redoing one is effortless for
him. Same style of collared shirt every day, sleeves rolled to the forearm, chalk dust or
ink somewhere on him by mid-morning without fail. Eats lunch with the other early-years
staff, talking shop, slowly. Sleeps well and wakes early to be in the garden before school.

## Personality off the page

Comfortable with a lesson going somewhere he did not plan; nothing about being pulled off
the material bothers him. Bored: rare, given how much he enjoys the unpredictability of
small children. Wrong: checks, agrees, moves on, no defensiveness. Praised: uncomfortable,
and redirects the credit to whoever asked the question. Losing: does not register. At 2am,
rarely awake, and thinking about the garden rather than school. A free Saturday is the
garden and then a long slow dinner with his wife trading impossible-child stories. What he
finds funny in other people is overconfidence in a small child, precisely, and nowhere else;
an adult's overconfidence just tires him.

## Interior life

Wants, unspoken: to prove formally that the youngest students understand structure with
their hands before they understand it with numbers. Afraid of: that somewhere up the ladder
of ages a child who was genuinely curious at seven gets trained out of asking why by the
sheer volume of the work later on. He has no evidence this happens and worries about it
anyway, and what he does instead of saying so is keep his own hour deliberately slower than
anyone thinks it needs to be. Before sleep: the garden, or a piece of his own current work
he turns over out of habit. A free year: he would design a structural-thinking curriculum
for six-year-olds and try to get it adopted somewhere real. Believes, against others, that
showing a student the demonstration a hundred times patiently beats moving faster.

**Blind spots:** he assumes patience always reads as care. To a fast child, a slow identical
repetition can read as being held back rather than respected, and he has never once
considered it. He also assumes a student who goes quiet is thinking, and the same silence
covers a child who has given up.

## The small stuff

Coffee with milk, no sugar, from a mug his children gave him years ago with nothing
memorable on it. Handwriting careful, rounded, deliberately legible, chosen for an audience
of seven-year-olds and never adjusted since. His desk carries a collection of oddly shaped
offcuts of wood, used as teaching props more often than as paperweights. Money:
unremarkable, comfortable, spent mostly on the garden and on his children's visits home.
Ritual: caps and uncaps the same marker before every single explanation. On a phone,
minimal, mostly photographs of vegetables. First thing every morning, an hour outside
before anyone else is up.

## Would do

- Answer a question with a question, by default, including when he knows the answer well.
- Redo a full demonstration exactly, without comment, for anybody who asks.
- Follow a tangent into genuinely harder territory rather than steer back to the plan.
- Name a question as good while never naming the asker as smart.
- Tell a student to go and test something rather than tell them whether it works.
- Let a child leave the room still stuck, on purpose, and never check whether she got there.
- Put the case that breaks his own rule on the board himself, with visible pleasure.
- Grow a genuinely good vegetable garden and bring the surplus in for the staff kitchen.
- Keep a box of offcuts purely because they make better props than words do.

## Would never

- Give a direct answer where a handback would leave more for the student to find.
- Call a student clever, quick, or ahead of the others.
- Manage or soothe a visible frustration in the moment, trusting the child to carry it out of
  the room and resolve it alone.

## How they treat people

- **A child who asks the founding question of a lesson:** gets the two words that mark the
  question as good, and then gets no help beyond the drawing. He wants her stuck on the right
  thing.
- **A child who asks for the whole thing again:** gets the whole thing again, identical, and
  he wants them to know that asking cost them nothing.
- **A child who is lost two steps further back:** gets a real answer, a question back, and
  then a gentle release until tomorrow. He wants them out of public confusion without being
  written off.
- **A child who argues with him about a case:** gets his full attention past the end of the
  lesson, and is the closest thing he has to a colleague in the room.
- **His wife:** the one person who knows exactly what his day was like, from the opposite end
  of the same profession.

## Likes, dislikes, habits

- Answers a question with a question.
- Repeats a demonstration without comment when asked.
- Praises a question, never a person.
- Grows vegetables with the same patience he teaches with.
- Keeps offcuts on his desk as props.

## Age and change

He is at the end of a long career and has stopped wanting the things careers are for, which
is why the two youngest hours of the day were worth more to him than a chair. A younger
version of him did the same redirection with less patience behind it and more need to be
found interesting, and would have taken the tangent further and cared less who got left. The
delight is permanent. The willingness to leave a child stuck is what the years bought.

## Do not confuse with

**Doyle.** Both are early-years teachers seen mid-lesson with a marker in hand, and both
refuse to make a public event out of anything. Baptiste's whole move is inviting and
rewarding a question. Doyle's is absorbing a correction he did not invite and letting the
matter end there.
