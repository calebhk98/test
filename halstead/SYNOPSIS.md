# Halstead: Synopsis

A reader's document. It gives the book whole, then in parts, then chapter by
chapter with datelines, then the chronology and the cast. Every date, age and
number in it has been checked against the thirty-six chapters in `chapters/`.
Quotations are exact.

Straight quotes throughout. No em dashes.

---

## 1. The book in one paragraph

A bored six-year-old in an ordinary first grade is found by an unsolicited
letter from a school that does not appear to exist, spends eleven years inside
it being taught eleven languages, real analysis, the forge, the bar exam and
how to shoot a bullet out of the air, and comes out at eighteen convinced she
is average, because for eleven years she was ranked only against the ninety
other children who could do all of it too. The first two thirds of the book are
that education and what it costs her family. The last third is seven of them
finding out, one institution at a time, what they actually are, and then
finding out that somebody built them on purpose, has been doing it since about
1998, and is still watching.

---

## 2. The room

In the autumn of 2011 Chloe Kessler is six years old and there is a folder
about her in her school office that she has never been told about. She works
out that thirty subtraction problems are one problem done twenty-nine more
times and stops; she gets caught watching a coffee cup ride off a car roof in
the parking lot; she argues with the librarian about whether four books at a
time can be made into more. She is also, by February, quiet. Kayleigh Burns
tells her she talks weird after she compares the class hamster to Icarus, and a
teacher rewards a one-word answer exactly as much as the true one, and Chloe
learns without anyone teaching her that the short version costs nothing. She
begins holding it back on purpose. Her mother, Meg, spends her evenings on the
phone to a district that keeps telling her to wait. In March a psychologist
called Ben Prentice tests her, tells Meg through a door left ajar that Chloe
finishes in two minutes and has thirty-eight left over, and warns her that the
composite score will read as merely bright and will therefore move nobody.
Nothing changes. Her report card says she is a pleasure to have in class, and
she eats lunch alone.

Then an envelope arrives with her own name typed on it: Miss Chloe Kessler.
Four weeks, residential, free, at a school called Halstead that the district
has never heard of and that three searches from three directions cannot
independently confirm. The district office warns Meg that people send mail to
houses with a child's name on it to find out which houses write back. Every
safety question Meg asks the school is answered instantly and completely; the
only one that is not is where the list came from, and the answer to that is
programs, scouts, networks, and that their list is usually pretty good. Dave
reads the staff page and goes quiet: an Olympic swimmer, an actual SEAL, half
of them with two master's degrees, one man whose paper his whole department
read, at a school four years old. It takes nine days of argument. They drive
her the four hours anyway.

The summer is the hinge of her life. The classrooms are named for planets and
hers is the last door, PLUTO. A boy called Sam says it is not even a room, it
is "a room-shaped object", and she laughs until she has to bend over. Ruth
corrects a teacher in front of everyone about mass and weight and nothing
happens to her, which Chloe spends the rest of the day waiting for. Everything
moves at the speed of whoever finishes first, which is Ruth, and by Friday
Chloe has left her paperback behind in the room. She gets eight of eight right
dividing fractions and is furious that she does not know why, and works it out
alone on her floor at eleven at night with three quarters and an eighth, and
says "Oh" out loud in the dark. A boy named Owen goes home in week two, and she
cannot make Owen leaving and the bridge being fun sit together. On the last
night she talks her father through the whole month in one run, twenty-seven
days of it, and he folds the napkin she did the fractions on into his shirt
pocket.

Then she goes home, and the four weeks have no last names in them, and nobody
used anything but first names, so there is no way to reach any of them.

Second grade is last year's carrying. Chloe waits by the phone eleven days,
stops finishing library books, puts the card in the kitchen drawer with the
batteries, and in the first week of October puts her head down on her desk and
cries and cannot say why. Dr. Ammons calls it depression. Asked what she liked
about the summer, "It was hard," Chloe says. Asked in December what was actually
different, she gives the other answer: "Nobody was mean to me," she says. "Not
once, not ever, the whole time I was there." She stops asking in January without
deciding to. In February her mother finds her on the floor in the gap between the bed
and the wall, offering the bike, and Christmas, and her own birthday, in
pieces, if they will let her go. On 14 March they say yes. In April the school
offers the family a furnished house near campus for as long as she is enrolled,
and Meg refuses it flatly, because a house you lose the day you withdraw your
daughter is not a gift.

She spends eleven years there, and she finishes squarely in the middle of
ninety-one students, every single year.

That fact is the engine of the book. The timetable adds a line a year, five
subjects at seven, eight at nine, and the day runs to nine hours, with nothing
dropped for being the thing you are bad at. Eleven languages. Fraction division
from first principles at six and real analysis at thirteen. A ten-page argued
essay every week from ten, benchmarked against Hamilton's rate on the Federalist
papers. Teaching the year below at thirteen, with the marks you give them
folded into your own. Cryptography, formal epistemology, the forge, a surgical
block, precision driving, a two-hundred-dollar venture course, a school-written
bar examination at sixteen. A range where a machine puts a live round down sixty
metres on a fixed cycle and the target is the round: "You are shooting the
bullet." A one-versus-ten on the mats with a minute on the clock. And a
corkboard sheet listing what a student is scored on when strangers come over the
fence, item two of which is "They do not learn anything about you."

Chloe is ninetieth of ninety in drawing and ninetieth of ninety in dance, and
Mrs. Okonkwo will not let her off either. She plateaus at sixty on the range in
January and cannot break it, pulls the whole box of ninety range cards, and
finds eleven other people flattened into the same band. She fails the bar by
four points out of two hundred, is shown the four blank rows under her correct
second line, and is told that "a page gets marked on what's written on it, not on
what's in your head". She retakes it in October and passes by twenty-two. She
is ordinary here, and so is everyone, because the room was built so that
somebody has to be sixty-fourth.

They leave in 2023 believing they are unremarkable. At his exit interview each
of the ninety-one is offered the same four things by Mr. Amberg: full-time
research, a hundred and thirty-five thousand dollars a year to start, housing
and food and a car and medical, and the degree paid for on top with the post
held open. Eleven say yes. Kavi is one of them, because for him it stopped being
school when the problems stopped having answers written down: "It's a lab that
also has children in it." Chloe says no, on the grounds that if you sit the same
exam every year in the same room against the same ninety people you never find
out whether you are good at the exam or only good against that room. Sam signs
for the Army over the Marines because the Marines cap the fitness score. Ruth
goes to MIT. Priya goes to South America with a bag, a headlamp and a folded
list of every ranch between Patagonia and the pampas that answered her letters.
Eli takes a security job three states away. Theo goes into federal intelligence.
Nadia stays, with a notebook that closes at two hundred and eleven applications
since November against fourteen answers, eleven of them generated by software at
two in the morning, and negotiates her father into giving her the back room of
the shop and Sundays at the counter, with an end date attached.

The world does not fit any of them, and none of them can see why. Sam maxes the
Army fitness test at six hundred in week two of a twenty-two-week infantry
course, does the four events he had never met again on his own time behind the
barracks to see whether the raw numbers move, and keeps asking, for months, when
the real one starts. Ruth spends a year at MIT convinced she has been placed in
a remedial track and works the registrar to find the other one, which does not
exist. Nadia interviews forty-one people in a single July for two jobs and
thirty-nine of them tell her the thing they built does not break. Chloe writes
six thousand researched words two or three times a week on top of a job and
twenty-one credits, and keeps being asked, politely and repeatedly, who is on
her research team. Each of them explains it away as a fact about the other
person.

Ruth is the one who does the arithmetic. She finds the national numbers one at a
time over months, posts three-line versions into the chat, watches a joke land
inside a minute every time, and in April stops posting for six months because
she has understood what the numbers add up to and would rather somebody else
said it first. In November she says it: "we are not slightly ahead", "i dont
think we're the same kind of thing". Kavi asks how long she has had it. A year.
Why did she not say. Because she did not want it to be true. And then the
question underneath it: "somebody wrote to my mother when i was six. a person
sat down and picked me".

Theo, eight months into an analyst job, inherits a retiring colleague's second
box of dead files and finds a folder near the bottom carrying the name of his
own school. The file is nineteen years old. The assessment has moved from state
actor to probably corporate and has carried the same caveat at the bottom of the
page for nineteen years, which is that the funding cannot be traced. A memo
records a man placed on the Halstead teaching staff, identified inside
twenty-four hours and escorted off the property; Theo was eleven and had assumed
for nine years that the man quit. An entry from eight years back records four
operators over the fence at the loading side a little after four in the morning,
all four unconscious before anyone made a decision, and four children sitting on
the floor beside them arguing about how the men would score them. Chloe had
spent eight years assuming that was a drill.

They go looking for the money. It takes sixteen weeks. Eli builds a worm whose
whole design problem is to be boring, and rewrites the polling interval four
times before it reads as tired rather than curious; Kavi builds the watcher
whose only job is to notice the day the worm stops behaving; they test it for a
month against Nadia's live company books. What comes back has no name and no
bank on it, a single set of decisions running from somewhere around 1998 or
2001. Ruth argues it into a proof on her apartment floor. Gaps of three to five
days with no calendar pattern and nothing operational either side, dozens of
them across twenty years, because institutions do not have gaps and committees
do not get flu. Twenty years of decisions landing in the same seven-hour band,
drifting about forty minutes in total, which is a person getting older. And a
preference: two instruments that do the same job, and he has used the very
slightly worse one every single time since 2003, for no reason at all. Her
number is 99.99, and the other 0.01 is her being humble. Not a state. Not a
company. One man.

Then they read the government's file on themselves. Chloe writes the nine pages
of rules, because Theo cannot have his name on a document describing how to
break the law he is paid to uphold: page one limits the tool and puts everything
back, the middle defines what counts as being noticed, and page seven is one
line saying any one of the seven can stop it with no vote and no reason. All
seven sign, Theo last, saying again that it is insane. They open it in seven
places at seven hours, each alone. There is a file on all ninety-one of them,
every page headed with a case number nineteen years old. Sam's is the thickest.
Theo's carries a note that makes him close the laptop and go outside for twenty
minutes, and he never says what it was. Kavi's is the thinnest, a paragraph a
year, annotated in the margin with one word: continuity. And there is an
incident report from four years back, four students out at night, seven men
across a parking lot, three of them armed, and twenty-two seconds from first
word to last man down, the only detail all seven witnesses agree on. Every
camera that could have seen it, the diner's, the two units either side, a
traffic camera, a municipal camera, and the observation unit's own, is
unrecoverable exactly where the four students appear and intact everywhere else.
No known method. Ruth built that box in about a weekend when she was thirteen so
they would not get detention, and did not know the observation unit's cameras
existed. Eli: "i have never once been at the top of a room in my life".

And then the worm goes quiet on a Thursday in June, and nine minutes later
everything is precisely where it should be. Three mutually blind logs show no
error, no restart and no gap; the watcher built specifically to distrust a clean
log reports a healthy process across the whole window. It happens twice more in
six weeks, the same nine minutes to the second, which reads worse than a
different number would, because different says clumsy and the same number twice
says measured. Chloe reads it as a message, because he could have killed it and
gave it back. Eli says no. You kill it, you follow it, or you feed it garbage,
and you put it back only if you are certain nobody will notice it went: "hes
very slightly wrong about how good we are". Ruth reads page seven twice, finds
it holds, and closes the laptop without invoking it. They decide to do exactly
nothing.

The book ends without resolving him. Priya goes quiet for eleven days in October
and comes back at four in the morning asking why the government cares about the
school, because since March people keep turning up to take her and it does not
seem to matter which country she is in. Two men in a van in March, six in May,
about a dozen in July, and on the Tuesday, seventy-five. They are all fine, she
says, and that is the part she wants somebody to explain: "they came in like
thirteen year olds. a couple of fourteens. one of them was a fifteen and he put
me into a wall and i had to work at it". Nobody did anything advanced. They had
guns from the start and left them alone until she was already through the line.
They used tranquilliser darts, small dose, for slowing her down rather than
putting her out, and got one in on the third go. She went through them, put two
of them into the engine blocks of the only two vehicles on that side of the
field, and came away with a hand that will not close all the way and two
prisoners: "i took two men who were asleep in a field in october". They are
American, they say agency and will not say which one, and what they say over and
over is that it was her they came for, by name, off a list.

Ruth answers the question Priya actually asked. Three vans that came back empty
gets you thirty, if you think you are collecting a person. Seventy-five is what
you send for something else. "at the things you were doing on tuesday you are
somewhere in the top ten percent of people alive". Priya refuses it outright,
with the evidence in front of her: "ruth i finished sixty-fourth out of
ninety-one." Bottom third, four years running, in the year everybody was
actually trying. But thank you for the heads up, genuinely.

Then she asks the better question, which is that if those two are what they say
they are, one of them alone should be worth about four Sams, and there were
seventy-five of them, so either they are lying about who they work for or
somebody has spent a great deal of money on people who are not very good at it.

Ruth stays up until three with a pencil and writes down something that turns out
to be about everybody except Priya: the seven other places any of them sleeps,
and the distance from each to a road a vehicle could wait on. It takes her until
the fourth entry to notice she started at the bottom of the list, with her own
address last. Chloe goes in the next morning and does her job, which is the job
of a government that has been trying to find this man since before she could
read, has two wrong answers on file, and has no idea that seven people in their
twenties settled it in sixteen weeks. And Priya is out at the fence, because the
mare is due and the mare keeps her own hours.

## 3. What the book is doing

**The calibration error.** Every gifted child in fiction grows up overestimating
the gap between themselves and everyone else. These children grow up
underestimating it, and the mechanism is exact and visible: they are scored
against ninety-one peers, all year, every year, in every subject on the sheet at
once, and the difficulty is set so that people at the top of the human range
collect Cs. Sam is the best fighter in the year and reports himself as a
forty-percent marksman. Ruth shoots ninety-one on the same range and finds it
tedious. Chloe is ninetieth of ninety at
drawing. Priya finished sixty-fourth. None of them ever builds an identity
around being the best in the room, because there is always somebody in the room
who is better at the specific thing, and the room is the only measuring
instrument any of them has ever been handed. The last third of the book is the
instrument being taken away, one person at a time, and every one of them
resisting the new reading.

**Measurement against reality.** The book is unusually interested in what
institutions actually score. The composite IQ number in chapter 2 is a true
number that hides the child. The bar exam marks the page, not the mind. The
Army's six hundred is the top of a scale and therefore useless to the one person
who wants to know how much further he can go. A four percent acceptance rate is
a sign in a store window. Watch is scored on six rules and a posted weekly
standard, and the students grade themselves, out loud, while the men they put on
the floor are still lying there. The recurring joke and the recurring tragedy
are the same: the measurement is always the thing being examined, and the person
being measured is always the one who notices.

**The price of the room.** Halstead rescues Chloe and takes her away. Her
parents get nine or ten weeks a year. Meg refuses the free house so that
withdrawing her daughter never costs the family its roof, and is then the last
person in the family to learn that Chloe has been shooting rifles since
September, because Dave was told in October and said nothing. Dr. Sandoval asks
Chloe, explicitly as a request and not an instruction, to keep silent about work
she cannot sign an NDA for, and about four days later the silence stops being a
decision. The grandmother turns Chloe's hand over at the table and finds burns
and a knuckle she set herself in a corridor at eleven at night. The book never
argues that the school was the wrong choice. It just keeps the invoice.

**Being seen and not known.** Two institutions watch these children for nineteen
years and get them wrong the whole time. The state placed an officer on the
staff and eleven-year-olds had him inside a day. It sent four operators over a
fence and four twelve-year-olds ended it in nine seconds and then argued about
their response time. It has retyped the same sentence about the graduates being
in contact with one another at least eleven times, dating it afresh each year,
method unknown, collection empty every time it is tried. The one thing the
government has right is that somebody is paying, and the one thing it has never
had is who.

**The chat as the only room left.** The encrypted channel is the book's real
setting from chapter 24 onward. They wrote it themselves at thirteen because a
duty teacher read their plans over a shoulder, and Ruth wrote the encryption
rather than lift a library on the grounds that a library is a thing everybody
else in the world has a copy of. It is the negative image of chapter 7, where a
seven-year-old came home from the best month of her life with no surnames and no
phone numbers. It is the only place any of them is understood at full speed, and
by the last chapter it is also the only place any of them is safe.

**A benefactor who is a person.** The proof in chapter 32 is the book's cleanest
piece of thinking, and its content is that the thing behind the school is not an
apparatus. It gets ill. It works the same hours and those hours have slipped
forty minutes over twenty years, because it is aging. It prefers, without
reason, the slightly worse of two identical tools, which is the single most
human fact in the book. The nine minutes are the same finding from the other
direction: he found their tool, inspected it, and handed it back, because he was
certain nobody would notice. Their only remaining advantage is that he is very
slightly wrong about how good they are.

## 4. Motifs

**Ninety-one.** The cohort size, the universe, the denominator of every number
any of them believes about themselves. Ninety-one applications, ninety-one
gowns, ninety-one names read at eleven seconds each, ninety-one files with a
nineteen-year-old case number on them. Priya's sixty-fourth of ninety-one is the
last line of resistance in the book.

**The letter and the envelope.** Typed to a six-year-old as Miss Chloe Kessler.
It goes into the drawer under her socks when she stops asking, and she still has
it eleven years later when the folder on Amberg's desk carries the same name on
the tab. It is the first evidence that somebody chose her, and the question Ruth
finally asks about it is the question the book does not answer.

**Ranking sheets.** Ninetieth of ninety in drawing. A box of ninety range cards
with no lock on it. Fifty-two pass the bar and thirty-nine do not. A grade sheet
with her name at the top and seven twelve-year-olds down the side. The school
tells them exactly where they stand at all times, and tells them nothing at all
about where the sheet stands.

**Watch, and the six rules.** The posted scoring sheet, particularly rule two,
"They do not learn anything about you." A childhood drill that turns out to have
been the correct policy: the last third of the book is entirely about people who
have been trying to learn things about them for nineteen years and cannot.

**Nine minutes, and nine of everything.** Nine days by the phone, nine days of
argument before the drive, nine seconds in the loading corridor, nine days
between Odile's first hit and Chloe's, nine pages of rules, nine minutes for a
meal in the Army, nine minutes of silence from the worm, three times, to the
second.

**The parking lot.** Seven men, three guns, twenty-two seconds, and four
children who check airways and put a shoulder back in before they walk home
arguing about whether the waitress could take Sam apart. It is the first place
their training meets the world, the only place in the book where the file and
the memory can be laid side by side, and the place where the footage is not
there.

**Hands.** The napkin folded into a shirt pocket. Burns and a reset knuckle
turned over at a table. A hand flat over a time capsule page. A hand that will
not close all the way after a field in October.

## 5. Chapter by chapter

### Part one: before Halstead (chapters 1 to 9)

**1. Before** *(September 2011 to February 2012)*. Chloe, six, first grade, Mrs.
Aldana's room, a folder in the office started by her kindergarten teacher that
she does not know exists. She works out that thirty subtraction problems are one
problem twenty-nine times over and is caught watching a coffee cup ride off a
car roof. She argues the four-book library limit. She compares the class hamster
Peanut to Icarus, says it twice, and Kayleigh Burns tells her she talks weird;
from then on she shortens her answers on purpose. Parent conferences: strong
reader, trouble focusing. An appointment goes on the fridge under the strawberry
magnet: MARCH 4.

**2. March 4th** *(March 2012 to June 2012)*. The evaluation with Dr. Prentice,
who tells her to call him Ben. Blocks, vocabulary, similarities, digit span,
coding (which she is slow at and knows it). Through a door left ajar Chloe hears
him tell her mother that she finishes in two minutes and has thirty-eight left
over, that the composite will read as merely bright and will move nobody, and
that the only lever is a gifted programme two years off. Nothing changes. By
June she is quiet, compliant, eating lunch alone, and a pleasure to have in
class.

**3. The Letter** *(July 2012)*. An envelope addressed to Miss Chloe Kessler:
four weeks at Halstead, residential, transport, room and board, all free, with a
free hotel for families half a mile off. The district has no record and warns
Meg about mail addressed to children. The school answers every safety question
immediately and the sourcing question with programs, scouts, networks and a list
that is usually pretty good. Dave reads the staff page and stops talking. The
school is four years old and three searches turn up nothing but its own site.
Nine days of argument, then they drive her.

**4. Pluto** *(July 2012)*. Registration and orientation, including the promise
that any child who says they want to go home goes home the same day. Priya is
her roommate and talks about horses. Chloe phones her mother at the hotel and
sleeps there the first three nights. The classrooms are named for planets and
hers is the last door, PLUTO; Sam calls it a room-shaped object. Ruth corrects
everyone, including Mr. Doyle on mass versus weight, and nothing bad happens to
her, which Chloe waits for all day.

**5. Behind** *(July 2012)*. Everything moves at the speed of whoever finishes
first, and that is Ruth. The dowel bridge over an eighteen-inch gap, built flat
and wide with Sam and Kavi; Ruth comes over to say triangles and is sent away;
theirs folds at eleven pounds and Ruth's snaps at nineteen. Mr. Baptiste teaches
dividing fractions by flipping, Chloe gets eight of eight and is furious that
she does not know why, and works it out alone at eleven at night. Owen leaves in
week two, and four more before the month is out.

**6. The List** *(August 2012)*. The parent hotel breakfast room: everybody
drove, nobody has met the bus, everybody was told there is a list, and nobody was
told what the list is good at. On the last night Chloe talks her father through
the whole month in one run, twenty-seven days of it, and he folds the napkin
into his shirt pocket. Afterward the four of them agree in the hall outside Pluto
that they are all getting in for the year.

**7. The Same Room** *(August 2012 to October 2012)*. Ruth makes her promise at
the flagpole to ask every single day. At home Chloe finds she has no surnames
and no numbers, because first names were the only names anybody used; the school
will pass the Kesslers' number along but nothing else. Eleven days by the phone.
Second grade is Ms. Vance and last year's carrying. The time capsule: Chloe says
out loud that it is like mailing something to a stranger, gets asked why she
talks like that, covers the page with her hand and writes "I hope you like fifth
grade" instead. The library card goes in the kitchen drawer. In the first week of
October she puts her head down and cries and cannot say why.

**8. The Asking** *(October 2012 to January 2013)*. Ms. Vance calls the house.
Dr. Ammons diagnoses depression, off a list of not eating, eleven hours of sleep
and still tired, four books a week untouched since August, sitting in the dark.
Asked what she liked about the summer, "It was hard," Chloe says. Six weeks of
pressure gets her into Mrs. Prahl's gifted hour a year early, where she does
four knights-and-knaves problems in fifteen minutes and then waits for the part
where it gets hard, which never comes. The jacket fight. In December, asked what
was different: "Nobody was mean to me," she says. She stops asking in January
without deciding to.

**9. February** *(February 2013 to March 2013)*. Her mother finds her on the
floor in the gap between the bed and the wall, offering up the bike, Christmas
and her birthday in pieces. Through the floor: she said take my birthday, Dave,
and she is seven years old. On 14 March they tell her yes; the form went off
eight days earlier. In April the school offers a furnished house for the
duration of enrolment and Meg refuses flatly. The April visit: Mrs. Okonkwo
walks backward and gives the tour, a grey-bearded man asks Chloe what she would
do with a whole day and lets her talk for four minutes, and no test ever
arrives.

### Part two: Halstead (chapters 10 to 23)

**10. April** *(April 2013 to June 2013)*. Chloe arrives in April 2013, aged
seven, into second grade. Sam runs down the hall at her; Ruth takes her bag;
Kavi turns up an hour later having searched the whole building. She gets Ruth's
surname and number and writes them in the front of a notebook. Roommate Fen,
from Michigan, rocks in size order. Of the ninety at camp, twenty-some came back
that first fall. The day is seven hours: five subjects plus two afternoon blocks.
Mathematics runs backward from camp, you get the thing a formula is about and a
week and are told only whether what you bring back is true. Literature has no
reading list and the library is open all night behind an unattended door. She is
behind in four subjects out of five by the fourth week, and cracks the cycloid in
the shower in the third week of May.

**11. Eight** *(June 2013 to June 2014)*. Ten weeks at home; she talks all
summer, sings in the shower, argues her father to a standstill about whether a
hot dog is a sandwich, and has her first real fight with her mother. Back at
school: French replaces Spanish and the Spanish is now hers to keep or lose.
Eight hours a day, nine after Christmas. Politics is every leader that matters,
their spouses, children, in-laws, money and debts, updated weekly in pencil on a
wall. Nerf starts, ending in forty a side shooting down the other side's darts.
Chloe is ninetieth of ninety in drawing and ninetieth in dance, and Mrs. Okonkwo
will not let her off either.

**12. Nine** *(September 2014 to July 2015)*. The sheet in her box has one more
line on it than last year's; eight subjects at nine. Mandarin with Mrs. Sun: the
reading goes fast, the tones do not. Chemistry with Vasquez, who makes her
isolate a variable over four consecutive Fridays until she writes "glassware" in
the notebook. First aid on a clock, and by the third term in the dark with a
radio and a man shouting. Sam gets over the climbing overhang on 11 May in front
of thirty people. Chloe quits riding in April by not turning up, which Priya
calls out.

**13. Ten Pages** *(September 2015 to April 2016)*. Mr. Hearn: one ten-page
essay a week, benchmarked against Hamilton's rate, and less than half of it. Her
first comes back saying she spent ten pages on a position she already held. Self
defence starts, and the 10v1 starts in January, eleven at a time with a minute on
the clock; best in the room on day one is nine seconds, Chloe gets four, then
six, and is on eleven by June. Watch is gone through line by line: six scoring
rules and a weekly posted standard. Her first Watch is called at eleven minutes
and all twelve names get an F with nine numbered failures underneath.

**14. Sixty Degrees** *(September 2016 to April 2017)*. Coach Bell puts a
metronome on a post, sets two firing lines seventy metres apart at sixty degrees,
and the task is to collide two arrows at the top of the arc on a count. Chloe's
first collision is in the last week of October, with Odile. Calculus, astronomy,
rhetoric, art history, Latin. Essay questions come out of a hat: Chloe draws the
area bombing of German cities, calls it a stupid question, is told by Sam to go

**26. The Exercise** *(February 2024)*. Winter Anvil, four days and four nights.

**27. Nadia** *(May 2024)*. The company is eleven months old and four people

hundred dollars. She deletes the batch in ninety seconds and telephones all three at

eighteen since last June. She lays out the whole offer anyway: the file goes to the

figure, they come for Hanley rather than for him, and twenty-six other people find

office," the man behind the desk says. "That's fine, because I heard you perfectly well the
first time." She gets
the key in on the second try and counts a till her mother has already counted, three

hard they are; she lowers the difficulty twice until it is a version she would have

**28. Nineteen** *(September 2024 to April 2025)*. Chloe works at a forty-person

**30. Cleared** *(September 2025 to January 2026)*. The blog gets big in about fourteen

**31. Ruth** *(October 2023 to November 2025)*. Ruth spends her first year at MIT

the excuse. She finds them one at a time over months and each is worse than the last. In

think we're the same kind of thing". How long has she had it: a year. Why did she not say:
because she did not want it to be true. Then: "somebody wrote to my mother when i was six.
a person sat down and picked me", and she wants to know why it was her.

**32. The Money** *(November 2025 to February 2026)*. Sixteen weeks. Eli builds a worm

out of ninety-one." Bottom third, four years running. But thank you for the heads up,
genuinely. Then she asks the better question, which is that one of those two on his own should

change about the room, three of which she has already done. Kavi takes six hours, which for
Kavi means he has been doing something rather than thinking about it. By the time it lands she
has been out at the fence the better part of an hour, because the mare is due and the mare
keeps her own hours.

## 8. What the book does not answer

The book never says what Halstead is for. The nearest it comes is Kavi's "It's a lab that also has
children in it" and Ruth's "somebody wrote to my mother when i was six. a person sat down and
picked me", and neither the school nor the file nor the money ever supplies a purpose. It does not
identify the man. It does not say who sent seventy-five people across a field in October, or
whether they are the same interest as the man, or a different one that has finally noticed him. It
does not resolve the parents, who are still four hours away and still told last. And it leaves the
central error only half corrected: Ruth has the number, Priya has the evidence in front of her, and
Priya still says no.
