# Halstead: synopsis from the text

Written from the 35 chapters in `chapters/` alone, in order, without reference
to the existing synopsis. The comparison section at the end was written
afterward. Straight quotes throughout; no em dashes.

Chapter datelines are quoted exactly as the chapter carries them.

---

## 1. What the book is about

Chloe Kessler is a bored, bright six-year-old in an ordinary American first
grade whose parents cannot get the school to do anything about her, and who
learns instead to be quiet so the room will leave her alone. In July 2012 an
envelope arrives addressed to her by name, unsolicited, offering a free
four-week residential summer session at a school called Halstead that nobody
can find any independent trace of. She goes, is happy for the first time in a
year, comes home, and falls into a depression her mother eventually takes to a
psychologist. After nine months of asking, her parents let her enrol, and she
spends eleven school years there, from April 2013 to June 2023, four hours from
home. The book is the education itself, at length and in order: languages,
mathematics, essays, the forge, the range, self defence, teaching the year
below, a school-run bar examination, and a standing defensive exercise called
Watch in which children repel intruders. It is also the price of that
education, which is that Chloe's parents get her nine weeks a year and hear
about the rifles six months late. In the last third the four friends she made
in July 2012, plus three more, learn as adults that they are not what they
thought they were: that the school was never a school in the ordinary sense,
that a government unit has held a file on it and on all ninety-one of them for
nineteen years and has twice been wrong about who runs it, that the four men
they put on a corridor floor at twelve were real, and that the money behind the
school runs back to a single living person. They prove that in sixteen weeks,
read the government's own file, and then discover somebody has been switching
their tool off for nine minutes at a time and putting it back.

## 2. Chapter by chapter

### Part one: before Halstead

**Ch 1, "Before"** *(September 2011 – February 2012)*. Chloe, six, first grade,
Mrs. Aldana's room. A folder about her sits in the school office, started by her
kindergarten teacher; she does not know it exists. Her mother is on the phone
to the school most evenings getting nowhere. Chloe works out that thirty
subtraction problems are one problem twenty-nine times over and stops; she is
caught watching a coffee cup ride off a car roof in the parking lot. She reads
four library books at a time and argues with the librarian about the limit. She
compares the class hamster Peanut to Icarus, says it twice, and Kayleigh Burns
tells her she talks weird; from then on she shortens her answers on purpose.
Parent conferences: strong reader, trouble focusing. A December checkup
produces the pediatrician's advice to have her evaluated for attention. In
February an appointment is booked and goes on the fridge under the strawberry
magnet: MARCH 4.

**Ch 2, "March 4th"** *(March 2012 – June 2012)*. The evaluation with Dr.
Prentice, the psychologist, who tells her to call him Ben. Blocks, vocabulary,
similarities, digit span, coding (which she is slow at and knows it), picture
completion, mazes. Through a door left ajar Chloe hears him tell her mother
that he is not seeing what the school sees, that Chloe finishes in two minutes
and has thirty-eight left over, that the composite score will read as merely
bright and will therefore move nobody, and that the only lever is the school
itself, whose gifted programme starts in third grade, two years off. Nothing
changes afterward. By June Chloe is quiet, compliant, and eats lunch alone. Her
report card says she is a pleasure to have in class.

**Ch 3, "The Letter"** *(July 2012)*. An envelope addressed to Miss Chloe
Kessler: four weeks at Halstead, residential, transport, room and board, all
free, families welcome at a free hotel half a mile off. Her mother calls the
district, which has no record of any such school and warns her that people send
mail to houses with a child's name on it to find out which houses write back.
Her mother calls the school and gets every question answered immediately: staff
ratios, night cover, the nurse, illness at 2 a.m., withdrawal at any time. Where
did the name come from: "programs, scouts," "networks," and "their list is
usually pretty good." Over a hundred children, from all over; the Kesslers are
one of the closer families at four hours. Her father reads the staff page and
goes quiet: an Olympic swimmer, an actual SEAL, half of them with two master's
degrees, and one man whose paper his whole department read. The website is
better than his employer's. The school is four years old. Three searches from
three directions turn up nothing but the school's own site. It takes nine days
of argument; her mother's position is that she wants to know who is paying
before anybody gets in a car, her father's is that she should go anyway. They
drive her.

**Ch 4, "Pluto"** *(July 2012)*. Registration, orientation (meals at seven,
twelve and six; green laundry bags; a nurse in the office at every hour; lights
out at nine for the under-eights; and the promise that any child who says they
want to go home goes home the same day, no argument). Chloe's roommate is
Priya, who talks about horses. Chloe phones her mother at the hotel on the
first night and sleeps there; she does it again on the second and third.
Classrooms are named for planets; Chloe's is the last door, PLUTO. Sam says it
is "a room-shaped object" and Chloe laughs. Ruth corrects everyone, including
about Pluto being a dwarf planet. Kavi corrects Sam about volcanoes at length
and Sam never recovers. Ruth corrects Mr. Doyle on mass versus weight and
nothing bad happens to her, which Chloe spends the rest of the day waiting for.

**Ch 5, "Behind"** *(July 2012)*. The pace: everything moves at the speed of
whoever finishes first, and that is Ruth. Chloe stops carrying a paperback. The
dowel bridge over an eighteen-inch gap, built with Sam and Kavi, flat and wide
and neat; Ruth comes over to say triangles and is sent away. Predictions
written in pounds (Chloe: fourteen). On the Tuesday theirs folds at eleven,
Ruth's snaps at nineteen. Mr. Baptiste teaches dividing fractions by flipping;
Chloe gets eight of eight right and is furious that she does not know why, works
it out alone that night on her floor with three quarters divided by an eighth,
and says "Oh" out loud in the dark at eleven. Swimming with Deb at the shallow
end. Owen leaves in week two because he was not having fun; four more leave
before the month is out, and Chloe cannot make the bridge being fun and Owen
leaving sit together. At dinner she calls the moon a liar and holds her own
across an argument with Sam, Ruth and Kavi, then checks the sentence afterward
out of habit and finds it clean.

**Ch 6, "The List"** *(August 2012)*. The parent hotel breakfast room: eight or
nine adults comparing notes. Everybody drove; nobody has met the bus. Everybody
was told there is a list, and that the list is a good list, and nobody got
told what it is good at. On the last night the families eat on campus and Chloe
talks her father through the month in one long run: Gary the armless CPR
manikin who clicks if you push hard enough; the bridge and the corner; the pool;
dividing fractions worked twice on a napkin; Spanish at lunch on purpose; sign
language across the library; a twenty-sentence puzzle in an unknown language
with no dictionary; wood shop; cooking, including a dish with onions she cut;
paintings, one of which cost more than a house; typing code instead of dragging
blocks, plus a tour of a freezing machine room with water pipes under the floor;
chess; karate; nerf. Her father asks how many days, gets twenty-seven, and folds
the napkin into his shirt pocket. Afterward the four of them sit in the hall
outside Pluto and agree they are all getting in for the year; Ruth says there is
a form and a day in the fall where people come and look at you. In the car home
Chloe fakes sleep and hears her father tell her mother they taught her CPR, and
that she said getting it right and knowing why it is right are two different
things.

**Ch 7, "The Same Room"** *(August 2012 – October 2012)*. Ruth makes her promise
at the flagpole to ask every single day. At home Chloe discovers she has no
surnames and no phone numbers for any of them, because first names were the only
names anybody used; her mother phones the school, which will not give out other
families' details but agrees to pass the Kesslers' number along. Chloe waits by
the phone for eleven days. Second grade is Ms. Vance; the first math sheet is
last year's carrying. The class time capsule, to be opened in fifth grade; Chloe
says out loud that it is like mailing something to a stranger, Kayleigh asks why
she talks like that, Bryce says she is weirder than before, and Chloe covers the
page with her hand and writes "I hope you like fifth grade" instead. She stops
finishing library books and the card goes in the kitchen drawer with the
batteries. By late September she is asking to go to Halstead several times a
day. Her mother works the school in the last week of September: third grade is
the floor for the gifted programme, testing only in the spring, for a programme
starting the following fall. In the first week of October Chloe puts her head
down in class and cries and cannot say why.

**Ch 8, "The Asking"** *(October 2012 – January 2013)*. Ms. Vance calls the
house. Dr. Ammons diagnoses depression; the mother's list is not eating,
eleven hours of sleep and still tired, four books a week untouched since
August, sitting in the dark. Asked what she liked about the summer, Chloe says
"It was hard." Six weeks of pressure gets Chloe an exception into the school's
gifted hour a year early. Mrs. Prahl's THINKING CORNER: nine children, a cart,
knights and knaves, twelve problems. Chloe does four in fifteen minutes and
then waits for the part where it gets hard, which never comes; she finishes the
take-home packet in forty minutes and later lies about still working on one. The
jacket fight, in which she tells her mother she hates her. In December her
mother asks what was actually different at Halstead and Chloe says "Nobody was
mean to me. Not once, not ever, the whole time I was there." A bike for
Christmas. She stops asking in January, without deciding to. The envelope stays
in the drawer under her socks.

**Ch 9, "February"** *(February 2013 – March 2013)*. In February her mother
finds her on the floor in the gap between the bed and the wall, and Chloe
offers to give up the bike, Christmas, and her birthday, in pieces, if they will
let her go. Overheard through the floor: "She said take my birthday, Dave, and
she's seven years old." On 14 March they tell her yes. The form went off eight
days earlier; there is a day in April where Halstead looks at her; the school
will take a student in any month. In April the school offers the family a
furnished house ten minutes from campus, paid for the whole time she is
enrolled, ending if she leaves; her mother refuses flatly, because taking it
would mean that pulling Chloe out is the day the family has nowhere to live.
The April visit: Mrs. Okonkwo walks backward and gives the tour; the rooms down
that hall have always been planets; the school will not confirm who is enrolled.
Chloe spends the afternoon in a second-grade room of twelve; a grey-bearded man
asks her what she would do with a whole day and lets her talk for four minutes.
No test ever arrives. She talks for the entire four-hour drive home; her mother
stays in the car afterward with the engine off.

### Part two: Halstead

**Ch 10, "April"** *(April 2013 – June 2013)*. Chloe arrives in April 2013, aged
seven, into second grade. Sam runs down the hall at her; Ruth takes her bag;
Kavi turns up an hour later having searched the whole building. She gets Ruth's
surname (Aymar) and phone number and writes them in the front of a notebook.
Roommate Fen, from Michigan, with a rock collection ordered by size. Of the
ninety who were at camp, twenty-some came back that first fall. The day is seven
hours: five subjects (Spanish, mathematics, writing, literature, history) plus
two afternoon blocks, one compulsory (swimming, taught by a man who went to the
Olympics) and one chosen (cooking, with Ruth). Mathematics runs backward from
camp: you are given the thing a formula is about and a week, and told only
whether what you bring back is true. Literature has no reading list at all; the
library is the whole back of the ground floor over two levels, open all night
behind an unattended door, no card and no limit. Chloe takes eleven books the
first time. She is behind in four subjects out of five by the fourth week. In
the third week of May she cracks the cycloid problem in the shower and Ruth
makes her do it again slower. She spends three weeks establishing what gets a
person sent home and is told: nothing except a family deciding they want their
child home, two or three times in an ordinary year. Kavi tells her Owen wanted
to stay. Sunday phone calls with her mother from the first-floor alcove, with
people waiting behind her.

**Ch 11, "Eight"** *(June 2013 – June 2014)*. Ten weeks at home. She talks all
summer, sings in the shower, argues her father to a standstill over whether a
hot dog is a sandwich (the hinge argument), and has her first fight with her
mother in her life, about a nine o'clock curfew. Back at school: a new roommate,
Marguerite ("Margie"), from Ohio. French replaces Spanish in the same slot;
Chloe is told the Spanish is hers to keep or lose from here, and starts reading
Spanish novels before dinner off the shelves upstairs. Eight hours a day, nine
after Christmas: mathematics, writing, literature, political history, French,
logic. Politics is the leaders of every country that matters, their spouses,
children, in-laws, money and debts, updated weekly in pencil on a wall. Nerf
starts at eight: paper targets, then a swinging target, then a thrown disc, then
forty a side across a net shooting down the other side's darts. The afternoon
block is fixed all week: Nerf Monday, wood shop Tuesday, team sports Wednesday,
drawing Thursday, dance Friday. Chloe comes ninetieth of ninety in drawing and
ninetieth in dance; Mrs. Okonkwo refuses to let her off either. Elective: choir,
which turns out to be matching rather than singing, and she quits in the sixth
week. Sam takes climbing because of the overhang. In June her notebook shows she
has been reading ahead since about Christmas.

**Ch 12, "Nine"** *(September 2014 – July 2015)*. Eight subjects at nine.
Chemistry, statistics and biology are new. Dance and drawing are off the sheet
and Chloe announces it standing in the dining hall. Kavi got none of his three
requested subjects and lays out three objections at breakfast. Mandarin with
Mrs. Sun: the reading goes fast, the tones do not, and the fix is recording
herself, sitting next to someone who has it, and running tapes in the library.
Statistics she takes to dinner. Chemistry is Vasquez, who sets things on fire
and makes her isolate a variable over four consecutive Fridays until she writes
"glassware" in the notebook. First aid is on a clock, and by the third term it
runs in the dark with a radio and a man shouting at her. Textiles: three shirts
before one fits, the fix being to trace a shirt that already fits rather than to
draft from measurements. Two people in her year she dislikes, Bex and Iyad, both
of whom she works with anyway. Sam gets over the climbing overhang on 11 May in
front of thirty people. Block two is riding, because Priya rides; Chloe quits in
April by not turning up, which Priya calls out. Ten weeks home: eleven hours a
night for nine days, a coast trip, cribbage on a walnut board, and reading a
vendor proposal over her father's shoulder and finding the paragraph that is a
description of a job dressed as a promise.

**Ch 13, "Ten Pages"** *(September 2015 – April 2016)*. Mr. Hearn: one ten-page
essay a week, benchmarked against Hamilton's rate on the Federalist papers. Her
first comes back saying she spent ten pages on a position she already held.
She reads all fifty-one of Hamilton's by mid-December and starts on Madison. In
November Hearn tells her the third page is doing the work of the first six.
Self defence starts in September, an hour a day; the 10v1 starts in January,
eleven at a time with a minute on the clock. Kowalczyk's reasoning: five people
can reach you at once, ten is five on you and five resting, and a hundred on one
is a 10v1 that lasts longer. Best in the room on day one is nine seconds; Chloe
gets four, then six; by June she is on eleven, Sam holds fourteen from February.
Paintball joins in October and Chloe works out with Ruth that you cannot react
to the ball, you watch the barrel and the feet and move before he shoots. Watch
is gone through line by line in the second week of September: the six scoring
rules, plus the note that response time is scored against a weekly standard.
Her first Watch: four adults over the north fence, inside a building in under
two minutes, called at eleven minutes, all twelve names get an F and nine
numbered failures. Russian starts; Ruth is on Arabic. In the second week of
February she writes one in two hours that finally works, and by March Hearn has
moved her onto Burke.

**Ch 14, "Sixty Degrees"** *(September 2016 – April 2017)*. Archery: three weeks
of butts, then Coach Bell puts a metronome on a post and sets two firing lines
seventy metres apart at sixty degrees, and the task is to collide two arrows at
the top of the arc on a count. Chloe's first collision is in the last week of
October, with Odile. Latin, which Ruth calls cheating and Chloe calls the year
paying for itself twice. Calculus arrives in the mathematics slot; astronomy
runs the autumn; rhetoric is an hour a week of finding where the speaker is
being paid; art history in the spring, where nobody will tell her what the
auction price is actually measuring. Wood shop: a chair that has to take an
adult, and eleven attempts at a Japanese corner joint before one holds. In
November essay questions come out of a hat, twenty-eight of them, seven days,
seven thousand words; Chloe draws the area bombing of German cities, calls it a
stupid question, is told by Sam to go and build the other side properly, does
so at fourteen thousand words over twenty-eight pages, and gets a B with an
eleven-line note about hedging in three places. **The Watch on 11 April is
billed as the whole school**: Sinclair announces over the intercom that every
member of staff is now hostile until 1600. Ruth runs the east stairwell, Nadia
takes the third floor in four minutes, Sam is out inside ninety seconds. Hearn
is the last teacher standing, in the library, taken at 3:42. The students get a
B and eleven listed failures, of which number nine is that nobody ever
established how many adults were in the building.

**Ch 15, "Twelve"** *(September 2017 – January 2018)*. The forge, run by Pruitt,
open Thursdays and most of Sunday; Chloe has a bench by October and starts
missing dinner. Bell pockets the metronome and makes one archer call the count
aloud for the other. Anatomy on herself; physics; psychology. She outgrows her
cuffs and finds she is taller than Ruth. **The bread test:** Sam insists the
Wednesday loaf is better, Ruth says it is the same bread and that two hundred of
them have talked each other into it, and the four of them run a blind test in the
kitchen at half past eleven at night with the big oven, are caught by Mrs.
Adeyemi, and score five right out of eight. Punishment is a four-hour Saturday
stock count of tinned tomatoes, which Ruth speeds up by counting cases.
**The 4 a.m. Watch:** the first one nobody posted in advance. Four men over the
fence at the loading side. Kavi is onto their comms in thirty seconds (AES-256
on a default six-digit pairing code, four seconds of compute). Chloe volunteers
to walk round the corner as bait because she is twelve, small, and in pyjamas.
Nine seconds: Kavi takes two, Ruth one, Sam misses his stick and goes again. They
tie the four men wrists and ankles, Ruth monitors respirations on her arm in pen
for lack of paper, and they argue about their own grade in front of the men.
Kavi inventories the equipment and finds bolt cutters that are wrong for the
fence the men came over. Chloe works at the wrongness and gets nowhere. Sinclair
arrives, asks for numbers, and sends them to bed. Grades: Chloe C, Sam C, Ruth
B minus, Kavi B; response 7:50 against a standard of 6. Thanksgiving: a letter
home about the kitchen; Chloe's defence is that she has been signed off on that
oven since she was nine, and her mother tells her she can come home any time she
says the word. By Christmas Chloe holds twenty-four seconds in the 10v1, ranked
thirtieth of ninety.

**Ch 16, "Thirteen"** *(September 2018 – July 2019)*. **Teaching starts at
thirteen**: you take the year below, and because Chloe's cohort is the oldest in
the building it is the only cohort doing it, which makes the twelves the only
year taught by a student and the thirteens the only year with no teacher of
their own. Seven twelve-year-olds on her list. Six are straightforward. Marek
hands in nothing all year on the principle that a receipt is a different object
from work, answers every question she asks him orally, and is working through a
1981 paper with a real mistake in it. Chloe has to fail him, and the composite
grade drags hers down. He is still there in January; whatever a fail costs, it is
not expulsion. Her own mathematics turns to real analysis, and her first proof
comes back with a line through it and "This step is where you assumed it."
Eleven laboratories split the year; hers is materials, where a postdoc called
Sanders hands her six months of instrument logs against a paper that reports
1,100 of 4,000 runs, and nineteen days later she finds the recalibration on a
Tuesday in March that split the data. Cryptography under Amberg, who insists on
thirty broken locks before you build one. Ruth builds a shoebox device that finds
networked video recorders and writes noise over parts of the recordings. Kavi and
Ruth build the group's encrypted chat after a duty teacher reads their plans over
a shoulder; Chloe breaks it in nine days, not through the encryption but through
the network's own connection log, and the fix is traffic that looks like the
hundred and forty machines already on the network. They are on the eight o'clock
bus into town the first Saturday in December. Formal epistemology. Fencing and
game theory. **Law starts with Mr. Amberg**, who says that at sixteen they will
take the bar. Dinner is ten or twelve at the long table by the window; Nadia
reads Chloe's hand over a napkin cipher.

**Ch 17, "Fourteen"** *(September 2019 – April 2020)*. Marksmanship replaces
archery, taught by Voss: three weeks of dry drill, naming nine weapons off the
silhouette, stripping four by feel under a cloth; live fire from October; a
swinging target in November; a clay trap every nine seconds in February, where
Ruth times forty of them and proves the printed card wrong. Self defence is now
three rounds an hour, all eleven taking the middle three times; Chloe holds
fifty seconds in February against a forty-five average. She teaches seven
thirteens, and in November all seven get the same question wrong in the same
way because she taught them the ordering reversed; she tells them so. Japanese,
abstract algebra, probability, contracts, logistics, comparative religion.
Engineering and design: she writes a two-page clamp specification and a boy
called Dov builds exactly what she wrote, which closes on the wrong axis, because
the spec says how hard to hold and never says what is held. The no-libraries
project: she writes a text renderer, nine thousand lines across fourteen files,
laying out live inside sixteen milliseconds; Ruth does live synthesis and finds
forty milliseconds of silence she has been cutting off the front of the note.
Her name goes into the acknowledgments of a materials write-up in March. Sam is
six foot two. **Winter break, second night:** her mother learns from a story
about Odile's burn that Chloe has been shooting rifles since September, and that
her father was told in October and said nothing. The row does not happen; the
plates get stacked instead.

**Ch 18, "Fifteen"** *(September 2020 – July 2021)*. The turret lane: a machine
fires a live round down sixty metres on a fixed cycle and the students shoot
across its path from behind glass, at the round. Voss: a gun you can only point
at a man is a weapon; a gun you can put a bullet onto a bullet with is a tool.
Five weeks of zeroes across the whole year. Odile scores first on 11 November,
Chloe nine days later. She reaches sixty by late January and stops there. She
pulls every range card in the year, a box of ninety handed over with no lock on
it, and finds eleven people flattened into the same narrow band. Voss tells her
it is real and hands the pages back. Ruth sits behind her for two hours on a
Saturday with a clipboard and finds nothing. Self defence goes backward: forty-one
seconds against forty-five at fourteen, because the year has caught up;
Kowalczyk tells her it will happen again at eighteen when the boys finish
growing. Odile holds a minute and nine seconds in March. Hark's money course:
two hundred dollars each, keep what is left in June. Chloe lends at interest,
finishes up seventeen dollars against Rustem's nine hundred and forty, and is
told at the board that she bought a piece of somebody else's ceiling. Surgical
block, accountancy, taxation, Hindi, topology. Her name lands sixth of nine on an
internal paper. **Dr. Sandoval asks her, explicitly as a request and not an
instruction, to keep silent about work she is not eligible to sign an NDA for**,
because the consequences would land on the four adults who did sign. Chloe takes
a week over it and then stops saying what she does in the afternoons. A teacher
confiscates Priya's phone for three days with two and a half years of the chat on
it, so Kavi rebuilds the chat as a window rather than a box, with a password plus
a number off a card kept away from the phone; Ruth cuts the cards. Her father
teaches her to drive in a church lot in July. Her grandmother turns her hand over
at the table and finds burns and a knuckle Chloe set herself in a corridor at
eleven at night.

**Ch 19, "Sixteen"** *(April 2022 – June 2023)*. **The bar**, two days, six hours
each, in the long examination room, proctored by the eleven teachers who wrote and
mark it. Chloe writes four pages where Sam writes eleven. Fifty-two in the year
pass and thirty-nine do not, Chloe among them, failing by four points out of two
hundred. Amberg shows her the mark scheme: four marks sit underneath her correct
second line, for the rule, where the rule comes from, why a shed is inside it, and
what happens if the shed burns down, and all four rows are blank. "A page gets
marked on what's written on it, not on what's in your head." Drama, where she is
told she is editing. She writes thirty-one practice answers between May and
September, marked by Kavi and Nadia. Precision driving with Delacroix, who kills
the ignition at sixty and sabotages the brake pedal, and tells her she hits what
she looks at. She retakes the bar in October and **passes by twenty-two**. The
forge stays Thursdays; Pruitt tells her to take the tongs she made. Summer at
home on the back steps, where her father asks about Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Priya and
Nadia rather than about her classes.

**Ch 20, "The Parking Lot"** *(June 2022)*. The first students to sneak out in
ten years. Eleven days of preparation split four ways: Nadia duplicates a
maintenance supervisor's badge in forty seconds of small talk; Chloe loops the
corridor cameras; Ruth brings the box she built at thirteen; Sam clocks the night
patrol's second pass. Kavi drops out at the last second. Over the fence at 11:20,
forty minutes down an unlit road to an all-night diner. Two hours of waffles, the
"hither" argument, Ruth's news that her brother is in and starts in September nine
years behind her, and a shopping cart in a hardware store lot. Four blocks later
seven men come out between two parked cars, three with guns. Sam asks to have this
one; the other three go over a chain link fence and crouch on a loading dock.
**Twenty-two seconds.** Sam takes all seven. They check every man, put a shoulder
back in, and leave. Nadia rules out the police, because a report with four names
on it means home and not coming back. Sam is not satisfied: "That was nothing."
Chloe explains why it was always going to be nothing, and says that people who
fight like that over a phone at two in the morning have no usable skill at all.
Nadia tells Sam to go back and fight the waitress, and the argument runs all the
way home. Ruth posts four lines and a photograph to the chat and there are sixty
replies by breakfast.

**Ch 21, "The Applications"** *(October 2022 – March 2023)*. Chloe has been ranked
against the same ninety-one people since she was seven and sits squarely in the
middle, so she expects the state school. Fourteen applications. Essays written in
Ruth's room on a Sunday; six hundred words is harder than four thousand. The
transcript is legal, with an accreditation number, and nine admissions offices
write back asking for the real one. Then somebody reads the essays and assumes
ghostwriting, and the template theory dies when two of the ninety-one argue
opposite sides of the same question well. An admissions officer at Penn reads a
sentence about a ten-person self-defence assessment three times and picks up the
phone; within two hours every file from the school is flagged, and by January there
are open files at more than thirty institutions. Three applicants list research;
the school will not send copies, and instead offers a chair in a windowless room
with an escort, no phone, no bag, and everything written in the room read before it
leaves. Four institutions accept. The Caltech representative reads for seven hours
and asks to speak to the third author, who is sixteen and in class until four, and
is told no. Eleven schools send visitors; two requests are always refused, the
fighting hour and the range, with a different word each time for what is in the
sports building ("Sport," then "Athletics," then "Recreation"). Decisions come over
nine days in March. Ruth gets MIT; Kavi gets MIT, Caltech and Chicago; Sam gets
eleven of twelve; Odile gets in everywhere and says nothing; **Chloe gets twelve of
fourteen** and cannot make the arithmetic work against a four percent acceptance
rate, and Ruth tells her the number on the site is doing a job that has nothing to
do with the store.

**Ch 22, "The Offer"** *(April 2023)*. Amberg runs exit interviews himself, fifteen
minutes each, ten or eleven a day for nine days. Chloe's is the Wednesday of the
second week. The offer, in four parts: full-time research starting the day she
would otherwise walk into a dorm; **a hundred and thirty-five thousand dollars a
year to start**; housing, food, a car and medical all covered; and the degree paid
for on her own schedule with the post held open. Six-day weeks the first year, five
after. She says no, and the reason is that if you sit the same exam every year in the
same room against the same ninety people you never find out whether you are good at
the exam or only good against that room. **Ninety-one names go up over the nine days
and eleven of them say yes**; ten of the eleven had already been on a floor with a
badge on the door. Kavi is one of them, and tells Chloe it stopped being school for
him when the problems stopped having answers written down: "It's a lab that also has
children in it." Chloe phones home; both parents say plainly that it is a mistake,
and her mother makes her say out loud that Georgetown is not free.

**Ch 23, "The First One"** *(June 2023)*. **The first graduation Halstead has ever
held.** Ninety-one gowns in three sizes, binder clips for the rest, stairs built by
the graduating class over two Saturdays in May on the corner joint alone. Chairs
matched exactly to confirmed family headcounts, with an untouched contingency stack
at the tree line. The rest of the school, eight hundred children, sits on the grass.
The head of school's voice catches on "accomplishment" and the applause track lands
half a beat late. Eleven seconds a name, dead steady. Ruth's mother borrows Meg's
spare jacket, as she has for eleven years. Dave works out the stage bracing with a
stranger who turns out to be Kavi's father, a physics professor. Chloe's grandmother
holds court from a folding chair. Then the dispersal: **Sam** signs for the Army in
July outside Pittsburgh, choosing it over the Marines because the Marines cap the PT
score; **Ruth** goes to MIT; **Nadia** stays, works her parents' shop counter, starts
a tally of people who need a form filled in rather than a repair, and by September is
hiring; **Eli** takes a security job three states away; **Theo** goes into the federal
government as an analyst; **Kavi** stays at Halstead; **Priya** goes to South America
with a bag and a list of stables. **Chloe** finds that the Foreign Service exam needs
twenty to sit and twenty-one to be sworn in, works the six stages forward from
eighteen, and takes Georgetown so the two years of waiting become the first two years
of the six.

### Part three: after

**Ch 24, "The Chat"** *(September 2023)*. The chat is five years old, written by them
at thirteen, and is the only one any of them has ever used. First September with all
of them somewhere else. Eli finds five vulnerabilities in his employer's systems in an
afternoon after his boss jokes about a million-dollar bounty that does not exist, and

or ten intermediate steps he cannot see himself skipping. Chloe buys a laptop off a

Chloe cannot find a common sinus medication anywhere; her doctor has never heard of it;
Kavi asks and is told twice that it is "internal," including the formulation, and Chloe
offers to synthesise it in about a week and then lets it drop. Nobody in the chat
remarks on that.

**Ch 25, "Ten Targets"** *(November 2023)*. Sam likes the Army immediately. He maxes the
fitness assessment in week two and beats his own score off the books in week three. Ruck
marches, land navigation, letters to his grandmother in block capitals every Sunday. In
month four they put him on a range with ten targets and he hits ten. A captain says it is
the best score he has seen this cycle and asks his accuracy; Sam says about forty, meaning
the intercept drill, explains that a stationary target measures whether the rifle works,
and says there are people he went to school with at ninety. The captain asks where he went
to school. That night the captain writes it up as **a concern about the soldier rather
than a recommendation for him**, with the phrase "appears to have received specialised
instruction from an unidentified source," and recommends the question be answered before
Sam is put anywhere interesting. It is read three floors up by the following Thursday.
None of it reaches Sam.

**Ch 26, "The Exercise"** *(February 2024)*. A four-day field exercise. Three contacts: two
men at a fence line on night one (under a minute), four men in a culvert on night two
(eleven seconds, and a thumb bent back), and on night four a single man who has lain in
position since nine the previous evening and takes two seconds. Sam's own critique is not
the culvert fight but the fifteen seconds before it, where he had thirty feet of open
ground to read and went in anyway. At the debrief he asks whether that was the assessment
or still setup, and offers that he would want it harder or with more of them. The major
takes the note, has him repeat it to the room, and refuses the opinion: difficulty is set
above him, and a soldier who wants the next one made harder has decided the exercise is
about him. "It goes upward anyway."

**Ch 27, "Nadia"** *(May 2024)*. Nadia's company is forty people and eleven months old,
making a diagnostic reader for independent mechanics, out of a converted fastener
warehouse. She catches the night cleaner hunting for a USB port on boards that have none,
fires him on the spot without explaining how she knew, and then spends a week of early
mornings tracing the cleaning firm's reference to a consulting LLC eight months old in a
suite above a tyre shop four miles away. On the seventh day she drives out alone, walks in
without knocking, tells four men what happened and that she is not calling anyone, and
says: "If you're going to come at me, come at me for real. A cleaner's an insult, not an
attack." One of them apologises. Separately, she interviews four hundred people that year,
makes the take-home easier twice until it is a version she would have handed a twelve-year-old
at Halstead and the pass rate is still under a third, and finds that most candidates run out
of road on the second question, the one about the edge of what they built. She hires excellent
people who stop exactly where the job description stops. "i cant find anyone who can finish a
thought."

**Ch 28, "Nineteen"** *(September 2024 – April 2025)*. Chloe works at a forty-person document
translation company an hour from her apartment, alongside fifteen credits in the autumn and
twenty-one in the spring plus an evening seminar she gets by asking the registrar which written
rule forbids it. Deb sits across from her, runs the birthday calendar, and talks about her
eleven-year-old son Tyler. Chloe catches a decimal in a forklift manual that would have had a
warehouse loading four times a machine's rating. She starts noticing that she says everything
twice: the accurate version, then the version with a comparison in it, and the second one is the
one that lands, so she gets fast at building the second version before anyone asks. She starts a
blog in September, six thousand words with a five-hundred-word summary on top, two or three
pieces a week written between eleven and two. A man emails in February to ask, sincerely, how
many people are on her research team, and does not reply when she says it is her. Meanwhile
Ruth begins posting bare statistics into the chat: 25% of Americans speak a second language
conversationally, 21% of adults read below a sixth-grade level, about a third can do 3/4 minus
1/2 on paper. Each time the chat makes a joke inside a minute and moves. **In April Ruth stops
posting.** Six months.

**Ch 29, "The File"** *(October 2025)*. Theo, eight months into his analyst job, inherits a
retiring colleague's second box of dead files. A folder near the bottom carries the name of his
own school. **There is a file on Halstead and it goes back nineteen years.** Early assessments
say state actor, two candidates named; six years on that is revised down because nobody inside
either has turned anything up, and corporate becomes the likelier answer; the current assessment
says probably corporate, with the same caveat at the bottom of the page for nineteen years, which
is that the funding cannot be traced. Then a memo: a man was placed on the Halstead teaching
staff, identified inside twenty-four hours and escorted off, the school's stated reason recorded
word for word, that they do not permit unidentified adults around children. Theo was eleven; he
remembers a new teacher for a day and a half and had assumed for nine years that the man quit.
Then the entry from eight years back: **four operators over the fence at the loading side a little
after four in the morning**, inside the first building for under two minutes, one of them seeing
a child alone in a corridor in pyjamas asking who they were; all four unconscious before anyone
made a decision; they came round tied at wrists and ankles with four children sitting on the floor
beside them arguing about how the men would score them and whether a response time of nearly eight
minutes counted as a fail. One of them was asked directly what he would have given them. An adult
arrived, sent the children to bed, told the men that an attack on a school in the United States
would not be accepted, and let them leave. No reply on file. Theo sits with it for two days, then
posts: "hypothetically / if you found something out about the school. from work. that you couldnt
actually say / how badly would you want to know." Ruth answers "bad." It is the first thing she has
posted in six months.

**Ch 30, "Cleared"** *(September 2025 – January 2026)*. Chloe's blog gets big in about fourteen
months. A third of the people who ask who her team is stop replying when she answers; another third
ask again in different words. She sits the Foreign Service exam three weeks after she turns twenty
and reads it as having sat an exam. The background investigation takes ten weeks and a man named
Whitaker comes twice. She gives him eleven years, residential; eleven languages listed in the order
she learned them (Spanish, French, Mandarin, Russian, Latin, Arabic, German, Japanese, Hindi, Korean,
Swahili); every class; the school's internal law examination, which she volunteers has no standing
anywhere; two published papers he has already pulled; and a third she will not describe, because
the lead researcher asked and everyone else had signed. Asked whether she would tell him if he said
it was necessary, she says she would want to talk to the researcher first. On the second visit he
asks who funds the school and she says she has no idea: "If you're seven, and somebody's already
built the swings, you climb on and use them." The clearance comes through on an ordinary Tuesday.
She starts in January.

**Ch 31, "Ruth"** *(October 2023 – November 2025)*. Ruth spends her first year at MIT believing she
has been put in a remedial track, because the first-year sequence covers in a semester what she had
at eleven. She works the registrar, advising and the department with a folder of problem sets in date
order and is told there is no other track. In November a joke she makes in a study group has to be
explained and dies. In February she asks a study group when the course will get past fifth-grade
concepts, and cannot work out why the table goes quiet. She writes down that MIT tracks students by
ability and keeps it quiet, and holds that for about ten months. What breaks it is a professor she
likes, in office hours, who cannot follow her past the third step, asks her to start again, and then
tells her, as an observation, that it took her four tries to explain what most of his graduate students
would get in two. She sits on a wall until the light changes twice, then goes to the library and starts
looking for the number instead of the excuse. She finds them one at a time over months, and each is
worse than the last. She posts three-line versions and deletes the fuller drafts. In April she stops
posting, because she has understood what the numbers add up to and would rather somebody else said it
first. In November she says it: Nadia cannot find anyone who can finish a thought, Sam asked when the
real assessment was, Theo is told he skips ten steps, Eli found five holes in an afternoon, Chloe
writes what a newsroom writes and people ask who her team is. "we are not slightly ahead / i dont think
we're the same kind of thing." Kavi asks how long she has had it; a year; why did she not say; "because
i didnt want it to be true." Then: "so who did this / somebody wrote to my mother when i was six. a
person sat down and picked me / i want to know why it was me."

**Ch 32, "The Money"** *(November 2025 – February 2026)*. **Sixteen weeks.** Eli builds a worm whose
whole design problem is to be boring, sitting where a badly maintained reconciliation job would sit and
moving as slowly; he rewrites the polling interval four times before it reads as tired instead of
curious. Kavi builds the watcher, whose only job is to notice the day the worm stops behaving like a
reconciliation job, and tests it against three years of his own coursework logs. They test the whole
thing for a month against Nadia's live company books; her one correction in four weeks is a timestamp
in the wrong time zone. It runs two months and finds a shape with no name and no bank on it, a single
set of decisions running back to somewhere around 1998 or 2001. **Ruth argues it into a proof**, on her
apartment floor: gaps of three to five days with no pattern, no calendar and nothing operational either
side, dozens of them across twenty years, because institutions do not have gaps and committees do not
get flu; twenty years of decisions landing in the same seven-hour band, drifting about forty minutes
total, which Kavi names as aging; and a preference, two instruments doing the same job with the slightly
worse one used every time since 2003. One man. Her number is 99.99, and the other 0.01 is her being
humble. Then Theo tells them the rest: nineteen years of file, wrong twice, a man placed on staff when
they were eleven, and four operators over the fence eight years ago. Chloe says "theo that was a drill."
Theo: "it wasnt a drill. i read the file." Ruth: they have nineteen years of being wrong in detail, and
they know things about our own school that we don't. Eli: "so lets go and read it."

**Ch 33, "The Other One"** *(May 2026 – June 2026)*. Eli proposes the second worm in a twelve-page
document sent to all six others at two in the morning, so that seven people read the same pages rather
than six different memories of them. The target is the unit that has kept the file. **Theo says no for
nine days**, then agrees on the reasoning rather than the arithmetic. Ruth prices it: section 1030, five
years first offence, ten with a financial angle, and a different statute entirely if anything they pull
touches something classified. Eli: "then i go away for fifteen years, so im not going to be wrong." Nadia
insists the two people touching the code carry it, not seven. Kavi says the risk was never the door but
a manual review of the exact files in the exact week, so they control the week. Theo will not write the
rules, because his name cannot be on a document describing how to break the law he is paid to uphold, so
**Chloe writes the nine pages**: page one limits the tool to the target list and puts everything back;
the middle section defines "noticed" as a specific confirmable event, deliberately excluding a blip that
settles; **page seven is the stop clause**, one line, any one of the seven can end it with no vote and no
reason, and the rest have one hour to comply. Ruth argues for requiring a reason and loses to Nadia. The
tool tests for five weeks. All seven sign in the shape of the person doing it, Theo last, saying again
that it is insane.

**Ch 34, "The Files"** *(June 2026)*. They open it in seven places at seven hours, each alone.
**There is a file on all ninety-one of them**, every page headed with a case number nineteen years old.
Addresses, employers, families, where everybody banks. Sam's is the thickest, three years of Army file
on top of everything else, with an officer's assessment flagging his honesty as the interesting part.
Theo's carries a note that makes him close the laptop and go outside for twenty minutes, and he never
says what it was. Nadia's stitches her filings to a competitor's background check from eighteen months
ago. Eli's records the five vulnerabilities as worth continued attention. Kavi's is the thinnest, a
paragraph a year, margin-annotated with one word: *continuity*. Chloe's includes a note, three lines
above the current entry, that she has recently accepted employment requiring a background investigation.
A standing line says the graduates are believed to be in contact with one another; it has been retyped
at least eleven times, dated afresh each year, method unknown, collection against the channel empty every
time it is tried, recommendation to keep attempting it. **And an incident report from four years back:**
four students out at night, two hours in a Waffle House, a shopping trolley in the car park, seven men
across the lot, three armed. None of the seven agrees on the order. All seven agree on twenty-two seconds,
first word to last man down, and all seven come back blank on what the four looked like. Footage from the
Waffle House, the two units either side, a traffic camera and a municipal camera is unrecoverable exactly
where the four students appear and intact everywhere else. No known method. And the unit assigned to
observation of the school lost their own recordings of the same night. Ruth: "i built that box in about a
weekend when i was thirteen so we wouldnt get detention / it says here it doesnt match any known method /
they had a team on us and my box got their cameras too and i didnt know their cameras existed." Kavi makes
her read it back. "i was thirteen and it was a weekend and i wasnt trying / how good are we." Eli: "i have
never once been at the top of a room in my life."

**Ch 35, "Nine Minutes"** *(June 2026 – July 2026)*. The worm goes quiet on a Thursday in June. The
eleven-forty check-in misses; **nine minutes later everything is precisely where it should be, same state,
same position**. Eli pulls three mutually blind logs and there is no error, no restart and no gap anywhere
in them; the process reports itself running the whole time. Kavi's watcher, built specifically to distrust a
clean log, reports a healthy process across the whole window. Eli reads page four twice, decides on the only
reading he can defend that this is not "noticed", and keeps the decision his own for now. "somebody stopped
it and started it and cleaned up after themselves well enough that the cleanup isnt there either." Ruth asks
whether it could be the government; Eli says if it were the government they would be in custody, because you
do not find someone else's tool and hand it back. **It happens twice more in six weeks**, the second on the
second Tuesday of July, nine minutes again to the second, which reads worse than a different number would,
because different says clumsy and the same number twice says measured. Chloe reads it as a message: he could
have killed it and gave it back. Eli says no: you kill it, you follow it, or you feed it garbage, and you put
it back only if you think nobody will notice it went. "hes not signalling. he made a mistake / hes very
slightly wrong about how good we are." Ruth reads the stop clause on page seven twice, finds it holds, and
closes the laptop without invoking it. They decide to do exactly nothing: no patch, no move, no looking at him
looking at them. Theo: "and if hes made one mistake / how many has he made that we didnt catch." Chloe reads it
at one in the morning with work in six hours, and goes in the next day and does her job, which is the job of a
government that has been trying to find this man since before she could read, has two wrong answers on file,
and has no idea that seven people in their twenties settled it in sixteen weeks.

## 3. Through-lines

### 3.1 Chloe's schooling and what it costs her

The engine of the first ten chapters is that Chloe finishes early and has nowhere to put the time. Dr. Prentice
puts it at thirty-eight minutes a day (ch 2). The costs arrive in a fixed order and never fully reverse:

- **Self-editing.** October 2011: Kayleigh Burns says "You talk weird" (ch 1). By November Chloe takes a full
  answer about the water cycle down to one word. She still runs the check on her own sentences in July 2012
  (ch 5, where the sentence comes back clean) and again in ch 7 with the time capsule, where she puts her hand
  flat over the page.
- **Disappearing on purpose.** April 2012, ch 2: "finish first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the
  room leaves you alone." She knows only that being quiet is what makes her disappear.
- **Depression.** August to December 2012. Not eating, eleven hours' sleep and still tired, books untouched,
  sitting in the dark; diagnosed by Dr. Ammons in October (ch 8). It is triggered by the return from Halstead,
  not by anything at Halstead. The floor of it is ch 9: offering up the bike, Christmas and her own birthday.
- **The distance.** Four hours each way, nine or ten weeks a year at home. Her mother refuses the free house
  (ch 9) so that withdrawing Chloe never costs the family its roof.
- **What her parents do not get told.** Ch 17 is the load-bearing scene: her mother learns in December 2019
  that Chloe has been shooting rifles since September, and that her father knew from October. The narration
  names the real sentence and then has the room stack plates instead. Ch 18 adds the Sandoval request, after
  which Chloe stops saying what she does in the afternoons, and it takes about four days to stop being a
  decision. Ch 15's Thanksgiving letter about the gas oven and ch 18's grandmother turning over her burned hand
  are the same beat at other pressures.
- **Being ranked in one room only.** The ninetieth-of-ninety sheets in ch 11, the range-card box in ch 18, the
  bar failure in ch 19, and finally the reason she gives Amberg for refusing the job in ch 22.

### 3.2 Her family

Meg and Dave Kessler. Meg is the one who calls, who asks the ratio at night, who writes "same day" underlined
twice on the back of a map (ch 4), who says no for nine months and then yes, and who refuses the house. Dave is
the one who does the train-station voice for the packing list, who reads the staff page and stops talking, who
says yes in July 2012 and then stands in doorways with a dishcloth until Meg makes him say it out loud (ch 9),
and who teaches her to drive anyway when the school already has it scheduled (ch 18). The recurring shape is
that Meg carries the decisions and resents carrying them alone, and Dave agrees early and says so late. Also
present: a grandmother (Christmas, the spare room floor, the folding chair at graduation, the hand across the
tablecloth), a grandfather, an aunt and uncle, and cousin Dana, nineteen in 2011, whose two identical
conversations with Chloe in ch 1 and ch 8 mark a year passing with nothing changed.

### 3.3 The friend group, and how it becomes the chat

Formed in Pluto in July 2012: Chloe, Sam, Ruth, Kavi. Priya is Chloe's camp roommate and joins the group at
Halstead. Odile, Nadia, Eli and Theo accrete at the long dinner table by the window from about ch 13 to ch 16.
The group of seven that carries the last third is Chloe, Ruth, Sam, Kavi, Nadia, Eli, Theo.

- **Ch 7** is the negative image of the chat: Chloe has no surnames and no numbers, and eleven days by the phone
  produce nothing.
- **Ch 10**: she writes Ruth's name, spelling and number in the front of a notebook and leaves four blank lines
  for Sam's.
- **Ch 16** (age thirteen): a duty teacher reads their weekend plans over a shoulder, so Kavi stands up a service
  and Ruth writes the encryption herself rather than lift a library, on the grounds that a library is a thing
  everybody else in the world has a copy of. Chloe breaks it in nine days through the network's connection log,
  not the cipher, and the fix is traffic camouflage.
- **Ch 18** (age fifteen): Priya's phone spends three days in a lectern drawer, so Kavi rebuilds the client as a
  window rather than a box, with a password plus a card kept away from the phone; Ruth cuts the cards.
- **Ch 20**: the chat carries the parking lot photograph and sixty replies.
- **Ch 24**: five years old in September 2023, the only one any of them has ever used, and untested by anyone with
  real reason to try it.
- **Ch 34**: the government has been trying to collect against it for eleven documented attempts and files the
  method as unknown.

### 3.4 Halstead itself, and what the school turns out to be

What the manuscript establishes, in order:

- Founded about four years before July 2012 (ch 3). Unsolicited letters to children by name, sourced from a list
  compiled by "programs, scouts, networks" that is "usually pretty good."
- Free: tuition, board, transport, a parent hotel, and (ch 9) an offer of a furnished house for the duration of
  enrolment.
- Staff who make no sense for a four-year-old school: an Olympic swimmer, a former SEAL, and a man whose paper
  Dave's whole department printed out (ch 3, confirmed in ch 10).
- No test at admission. A day where people look at you and a man asks what you would do with a whole day (ch 9).
- No mechanism for expulsion. Children leave only because their families take them (ch 10, ch 16 with Marek).
- Rooms named for planets; nine brass plates on the ground-floor hall (ch 4, ch 9, ch 23).
- A library open all night behind an unattended door, no card and no limit (ch 10).
- A curriculum that escalates yearly and never repeats: CPR at six, fractions from first principles, forty-a-side
  Nerf at eight, real analysis and teaching the year below at thirteen, cryptography, law and a school-written bar
  examination at sixteen, live-fire bullet-on-bullet intercept at fifteen, precision driving at sixteen, a surgical
  block, and a two-hundred-dollar venture-capital course.
- **Defensive Watch**, six scoring rules plus a weekly posted standard, run announced and unannounced, at night and
  at four in the morning, against people who come over fences.
- A research floor with badged doors, real postdocs, real papers, and federal NDAs (ch 16, ch 17, ch 18, ch 21).
- Eleven of ninety-one graduates are offered staff jobs at eighteen on $135k plus everything (ch 22), and ten of the
  eleven had already been on the badged floor.
- The exterior view, ch 21: admissions offices assume the transcript is a formatting error and the essays are
  ghostwritten; the school will not release papers but will provide a chair, an escort and a pencil.
- The interior view from outside, ch 25 and ch 27 and ch 31: Sam's intercept score reads as specialised instruction
  from an unidentified source; Nadia cannot hire anyone who can finish a thought; Ruth finds the national numbers and
  concludes "we are not slightly ahead."
- The government view, ch 29 and ch 34: nineteen years of file, assessment moving from state actor to corporate, one
  man placed on staff and removed in a day, four operators put on the floor by four twelve-year-olds, and the funding
  untraceable throughout.
- The answer, ch 32: not a state and not a company. One living man, since about 1998 to 2001, identified by his
  absences, his working hours drifting forty minutes over two decades, and a consistent preference for the slightly
  worse of two instruments since 2003.

Note that the book never says what the school is *for*. The nearest the text comes is Kavi's "It's a lab that also has
children in it" (ch 22) and Ruth's "somebody wrote to my mother when i was six. a person sat down and picked me. i want
to know why it was me" (ch 31), which is the question the last four chapters do not answer.
