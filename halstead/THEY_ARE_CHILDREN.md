# Chloe reads as non-human, and the rules did it

Outside readers were given chapter 1. The ones who did not think she was human
said she was an alien anthropologist, an artificial intelligence, or a creature
passing as human. The ones who thought she was human said she was autistic. Not
one of them read a gifted six-year-old.

Their words:

> She reads like an alien anthropologist, an artificial intelligence, or a highly
> evolved entity trying to pass as human.

> Chloe experiences the world as a series of systems, patterns, and rules to be
> understood.

> Watch how she watches people. She watches the recess game for two days just to
> learn the rules. She is studying human behavior like a scientist observing a
> different species.

The one reader who found her human named the mechanism exactly: the narration is
locked so tightly to her that she never labels a feeling, and the feeling has to
be reconstructed from an absence. That reader could do it. Most could not, and a
book that requires it is not working.

## Why it happened

The book is in tight third person on Chloe. `STYLE_RULES.md` says the narrator is
extremely flat and the people are not, and that is right for everyone except her.
Every other character is seen from outside, so their feeling arrives as something
they do. Chloe is seen from inside a narrator forbidden to feel.

The prose rules then close every remaining door, each one sensible on its own:

| rule | what it removes |
| :-- | :-- |
| 23 | naming the feeling |
| 24 | "she notices", "she knows", every perception verb |
| 26 | reported thought |
| 6 | explaining an image after rendering it |
| 1, 2 | verdicts, negative framing |

Named feeling gone, perception gone, reported thought gone, commentary gone.
What is left is exterior behaviour and analysis. Run that through a
six-year-old's viewpoint for thirty pages and you have built an observer, not a
child.

## The two worst sentences in chapter 1

Both are narrator statements about her interior, and both report an absence:

> From inside Chloe's head, nothing is wrong.

> Nothing about it is big enough to cry about.

These are the sentences that make her inhuman, and they already break rule 3,
which bans normalising by reporting a reaction that did not happen. The second
one is worse than empty: a six-year-old told she talks weird, in front of the
class, by a girl she wanted to play with, is hurt. The book rules the hurt out by
measurement and then has her debug the incident over lunch.

## The five mechanisms, and what to do instead

**1. Analysis replaces the feeling instead of following it.**
Chapter 1: "Nothing about it is big enough to cry about. At lunch she eats her
sandwich and works on which part of it was the wrong part." A clever child who is
hurt and then picks the moment apart is recognisable and human. A child who picks
it apart *instead of* being hurt is not. Put the hurt on the page first, in the
body or the behaviour, and let the analysis be how she copes with it. The order is
the whole difference.

**2. She observes where a child would participate.**
"She watches it for two days before she asks if she can play." Two days of
observation before joining a ball game is field work. A six-year-old who wants in
hovers, gets closer, asks on the first day and gets it wrong. Wanting to be
included should look like wanting, not like sampling.

**3. She has no body.**
Almost nothing in chapter 1 happens below the neck: no fidgeting, no running, no
being tired or hungry or too hot, no wanting to be held. The one moment of real
childhood in the chapter is the dinner scene, where she talks with her fork down,
forgets to eat, holds up a finger so nobody starts a different conversation, and
has to be told three times. That scene works. It is the register the rest of the
book is missing, and it is already in the book, so this is not a new voice to
invent.

**4. The exact-number tic runs the narration, not the character.**
Two days, four minutes, twenty-seven blanks, three separate occasions, four days
afterward, eleven minutes. Rule 32 already says this: a counting habit
characterises when it is tied to what it reveals, and reads as authorial autopilot
when every scene gets it. Precision is Chloe under pressure. It should not be the
default measuring unit of her ordinary afternoon.

**5. Nothing is ever wanted, only pursued.**
She reads constantly and asks to go to Halstead a hundred times, and the text
never renders the wanting itself, only the behaviour that follows from it. Desire
in a child is physical and impatient and badly hidden.

## High intelligence, low wisdom

They are children, at a school they believe is a normal school, and they believe
they are normal children. They should sound like children: social, talkative,
emotional, silly, unfair to each other, wrong about things adults are right about.
What marks them out is what they notice and how many steps they hold at once,
never a cold register. Rule 31 already says this and the manuscript does not do it.

Their morals are a child's morals, slightly off baseline, not an operative's.
The test case is chapter 20. Four sixteen-year-olds put six men on the ground
outside a diner, and the scene has them calmly weighing whether an incident report
is worth the follow-up questions, and assessing a concussion. What sixteen-year-
olds would actually have: adrenaline, shaking, and a fear of being in trouble for
sneaking out, alongside not wanting the men hurt and being relieved the concussed
one wakes up. They avoid the police because they broke a school rule, not because
they have run the reporting calculus. Both endings can be the same. The reasoning
has to be a teenager's.

## What does not change

- The narrator still reports. It does not editorialise, rank, or explain a feeling
  it has just shown. Everything here is achieved through what a character does,
  wants, says, and refuses, not through the narrator naming a mood.
- No physiological clichés. Rule 23 stands: the specific unexpected physical
  detail, not "her heart is going".
- The plot, the events and the chronology are untouched by this work.
- The other cast members keep their signatures. This is a register correction, not
  a personality rewrite.

# The corrections to this document

Everything above was applied across twenty chapters and produced three faults.
They are recorded here because the same instructions will otherwise produce them
again.

## 1. Negative space became the default sentence

Told to show a feeling without naming it, agents reached for the same shape every
time: the thing the character did not do. He doesn't look up. Nobody says
anything. She reads it twice and doesn't add to the thread. He doesn't put his
hand up again. She isn't hungry.

It is a real device and the book overuses it. The measurement, printed on every
run under `monitored, not graded`:

| | negative-beat rate |
| :-- | --: |
| corpus median, 23 books | 7.8% |
| corpus highest of 23 | 17.5% |
| this book before the pass | 15.5% |
| this book after the pass | 17.8% |

Nearly one sentence in six, above every book in the comparison set. Chapter 24
runs at 42%, chapter 25 at 33%, chapter 33 at 33%, chapter 10 at 26%.

**The budget: at most one per chapter, and the book should be aiming nearer one
every few chapters.** Above that it stops reading as restraint and starts reading
as a verbal tic, which is what rule 32 says about every other tic in the book.

What to use instead, since the point of the original instruction still stands: a
thing the character *does*. Not "he doesn't put his hand up again" but what his
hands do instead. Not "nobody says anything" but what the next person says. A
positive action carries the same withholding and does not announce itself as a
withholding.

## 2. Explaining the thing that was just shown

The second fault is rule 6 arriving in a new costume. Having staged something,
the prose then tells the reader what it meant: "Nobody explains to her afterward
that she got the length wrong the first time. Nobody has to." "The chat is the
one place all of them are still in the same room."

The reader can read. Stage the thing and stop. If the meaning is not reachable
from the staging, the staging is wrong and the sentence of explanation will not
rescue it.

## 3. Fear was applied at every age, and it should decay

This is the correction with the most reach, and the original document got it
wrong by treating "they are children" as one setting for the whole book.

They are children, and they are also children who fight daily, stand the watch,
and have done both for years. **Fear and adrenaline burn out of them as the years
at the school accumulate.** A sixteen-year-old eight years in, mugged in a parking
lot, is not frightened of a mugging, because she has been in harder rooms every
week since she was nine. She is competent, the competence is earned, and it is not
a defect in the writing.

The twelve-year-olds in chapter 15 need the same reading, and for an extra reason
that the chapter states on its own page. They do not believe there are armed men
in the building. The alarm is the watch alarm, response time is scored against a
standard posted weekly, the first question anybody asks is "Is it a drill?", and
Ruth's own line is "They're actors." They are twelve, they are four years in, and
they think they are being marked. Annoyance at being woken, competitiveness about
the score, and competence are the register. Fear is not, and the fear added to
that chapter in this pass has been taken back out.

What survives there is the one thing that should: the details do not fit a drill,
Chloe keeps finding them, and she cannot get the list to come out one way rather
than the other. That is not fear. It is a twelve-year-old with a good eye holding
two readings of the same night and no way to choose between them.

So the fear-and-adrenaline additions made to chapter 20 in this pass are wrong and
are being reduced. What stays from that work is the *reasoning*: they do not call
anyone because they broke a school rule and will be sent home, which is a
teenager's reason and not an operative's.

What replaces fear at the older ages is not nothing, and this is the part worth
writing well. It is the strangeness of not being frightened. Sometimes they notice
it about themselves and cannot place it. More often they do not notice, and the
adults and outsiders around them do, and the reader sees it over their shoulders.
That gap is the interesting thing about these characters at sixteen, and it is not
available at eight.

## What did not change

They are still 160-plus. Nothing here asks for a less capable child. The dinner
scene in chapter 1 is a genius six-year-old and a six-year-old at once, and that
remains the target. Making them emotional was never meant to make them ordinary.
