## 1\. Continuity — load-bearing

These change what the story means, not just how it reads.

### 1.1 Second grade happens twice, incompatibly

- **Ch2 (ending):** "In second grade her report card says: Chloe is a pleasure to have in class." Mother puts it on the fridge under the strawberry magnet.  
- **Ch7–9:** second grade is the collapse. Stops eating, eleven hours of sleep and still exhausted, stops reading entirely, cries at her desk in September, screams that she hates her mother in November, depression diagnosis, floor breakdown in February.

No teacher writes "a pleasure to have in class" that year and no mother keeps it.

Most likely cause: ch1–2 is the only unversioned document. That ending was probably written before Halstead existed, when the book's thesis was "nothing changes, forever." Worth telling the author the image is good enough to keep — it just belongs to first grade now.

### 1.2 The father does not exist in ch1–2

He is absent from the phone calls, absent from the March 4th evaluation, absent from the car ride home. Mother fills out the rating scales alone. From ch3 onward he is an engaged co-parent in an intact marriage who pushes hardest for Halstead.

Not a hard contradiction (nothing says he isn't there), but once ch3 establishes him, his absence from the single most important appointment of Chloe's childhood is conspicuous. The household texture shifts too: ch1–2 reads out-of-depth and non-academic, ch3 has a laptop at the kitchen table and a father whose department circulates papers.

### 1.3 September vs Christmas

- **Ch2:** mother, in the hallway — "in like September, she just... she got polite."  
- **Ch3:** father — "She's been flat since Christmas."

Same phenomenon, three months apart. Defensible as two parents noticing at different distances, which would be good characterisation. Currently unsignposted, so it reads as a slip.

### 1.4 Chapter numbering collision

- Ch16 is both **"Watch"** and **"The Applications."**  
- Ch17 is both **"The Parking Lot"** and **"The Offer."**

Version suffixes suggest a renumber in progress, so probably known.

### 1.5 "Somebody in the year above"

Ch17 (Parking Lot), final line. Ch18 establishes Chloe's cohort as the first graduating class — nothing exists above them. One of the two has to go.

### 1.6 Ninety vs ninety-one

"A year of ninety," "ninety applicants from one school," "ninety for ninety" — then "ninety-one names in alphabetical order" at graduation and "a file on all ninety-one of them."

### 1.7 Ruth's box: age thirteen or "the year before"?

- **Ch17:** "a box she built for a networking class the year before."  
- **Ch29:** "i built that box in about a weekend when i was thirteen so we wouldnt get detention."

These reconcile only if the Waffle House night happens at fourteen. But Ruth says in the same chapter that Sam "could have done that at thirteen," which implies he is past thirteen. Pin the date of that outing and both lines fall into place or one has to change.

### 1.8 Nineteen-year file vs a school that should be \~16 years old

The website in ch3 says the school is four years old. Chloe is roughly six or seven then and roughly twenty-one at the end, so Halstead is \~16–18. The federal file goes back nineteen years.

Either a slip or a plant — somebody watching whatever preceded Halstead. If it's a plant it needs one line somewhere to signal that it's deliberate, or every reader will file it as an error.

### 1.9 The mugging report doesn't match the mugging

Ch29: "Two of them independently report being told, during or just after, that this had been sloppy and needed more practice."

Nothing like that happens in ch17. Sam says he got nothing out of it; Ruth's critique happens on the walk home.

There is a better version available: the roof commentary ("he's slow on the transitions," "look at his feet") was audible from ground level, and seven men on the floor reasonably assumed it was aimed at them. One clause in ch17 buys it.

### 1.10 Languages: the count has drifted upward

- Ch11: "A language every year, which everybody does." Mandarin at nine, Russian at ten.  
- Ch16 (Watch): "Then she goes to Russian" — at twelve, two years after starting it.  
- Ch25: **eleven languages**, listed to a federal investigator, three of them spoken on request.

The Russian line at twelve supports a *continuation* model (a new slot each year, previous ones carried), which is the version that survives scrutiny. Eleven sequential languages by eighteen does not, against any real acquisition-hour benchmark. This is the single least defensible number in the manuscript and ch25 puts it under oath. One clause in ch11 clarifying continuation would fix the whole thread.

### 1.11 The gifted program is a planted gun that half-fires

Ch2 ends on "Then that's two years," which is the hinge the entire nothing-changes thesis hangs on. Ch3 mentions in passing that Ellie Peterson is in the pull-out group and moves on. Ch8 finally pays it off (the cart, "let's not rush"), but the ch3 mention sits there unresolved in between and reads like a dropped thread.

### 1.12 Dr. Prentice never comes back

Prentice tests her, says the school is the lever, learns the program is two years out, and drops out of the book. When she collapses at seven, the family sees a new clinician (Dr. Ammons) instead of the man who already knows her and already said nobody was going to help.

He is also the most obvious candidate for who put her on the list. Introducing a second clinician costs a scene that two documents of setup already paid for.

### 1.13 No treatment between October and February

A seven-year-old gets a depression diagnosis in October. Then nothing until the February collapse. No therapy, no follow-up, no school notification, no call to Ammons after the November explosion.

Medication is correctly absent (fluoxetine is approved from eight, and she's seven), but weekly psychotherapy is first-line and its absence is a hole.

The stronger fix is to put therapy **on the page and have it fail** — a competent therapist treating this as a mood disorder, working sleep hygiene and cognitive reframing, and getting nowhere, because the problem isn't in her cognition. Same outcome, more credible, and it strengthens the case for Halstead instead of dodging it.

### 1.14 Characters who appear at main-cast weight with no prior mention

- **Nadia** opens ch17 with the badge clone, gets the best line, and becomes one of the core seven. Zero prior appearances.  
- **Kavi is absent from ch17** with no explanation, having been core since ch4.  
- **Priya** is introduced at camp, mentioned once in ch10 ("still does the horse thing"), then gone. She was Chloe's roommate for twenty-six days.  
- **Fen** is introduced as her Halstead roommate, gets one line, never returns.  
- **Odile, Eli, Theo** all arrive late and become load-bearing.

Normal at this stage. The fix is seeding, not restructuring. The risk is a reader having to retroactively install importance into a name they skimmed, which reads as a cheat even when the writing is good.

### 1.15 Smaller items

- **Nerf:** recreational at camp (age 6–7), then "Nerf is eight" as a formal course.  
- **Ch6 arithmetic:** "twenty-six days" plus "there's two more" equals 28, but she leaves the next morning. Also the father says "in twenty-six days" on day 28 — reads as him echoing her, but sits next to a real wobble.  
- **Ch14 order:** Sandoval (age 15\) is presented before the rifle dinner (age 14), and at fourteen she already has things she "can't say anything about anyway." Either there were earlier restrictions or the Sandoval request isn't the first one.  
- **Mother's education:** ch14 gives her a bachelor's in accounting. Not contradicted by ch1–2, but ch2 has her not knowing when gifted programming starts and not pushing back on the psychologist, which reads differently now.

## 2\. Chronology — where the book is deliberately out of order

Not errors. But the reader is being asked to reassemble a lot, and right now three of these have no signal at all.

| Ch1 | January → October → November | October → November → January |
| Ch10–15 | Thematic threads, each running years | Interleaved ages 7–16 |
| Ch14 | Age 12 → subject list → Sandoval (15) → dinner (14) | 12 → 14 → 15 |

**Ch1 is the one worth fixing**, because the ordering creates an apparent regression. October (Icarus, shut down) and November (learns the flat version works) establish that she's learned to hide. Two defensible fixes: move the reading scene earlier, or let the January scene show her deciding to stay quiet and the words coming out anyway. The second is more interesting than either current version.

**Ch16 (Watch) is the other one.** Jumping back four years immediately after the bar exam needs a marker or readers will read it as a numbering error — especially given 1.4.

## 3\. Future POV

The author's most consistent tic. It disappeared entirely in ch3–6 (v2) and came back, which supports the theory that these are notes-to-self surviving revision rather than a style commitment.

**Instances:**

| Location | Line | Type |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Ch2 | "she will remember this hallway for a long time" | Emphasis stamp |
| Ch2 | "That's the part Chloe would tell you about, if you asked her later" | Implies an adult frame that doesn't exist |
| Ch8 | "thinks about pushing it for a long time afterward" | Regret |
| Ch9 | "gets it out and looks at it again when she's about fourteen" | **Plot promise** |
| Ch12 | "a noise she will hear in her head at forty" | Emphasis stamp |
| Ch12 | "Sam... talks about it constantly for a decade" | Mild |
| Ch14 | "she is not certain, later, that she was wrong" | Retrospective narrator |
| Ch14 | "finds it strange later that most people don't" | Retrospective narrator |

**Two tiers, and they need different fixes.**

The emphasis stamps ("she will remember this hallway") spoil nothing — they *assert* that a scene is significant, which is a confidence tell. The hallway was already working. The stamp is the author standing next to the painting saying this part is good.

The ch9 line is different in kind. "When she's about fourteen" tells you Meg will be proven right and roughly when. That's a plot commitment and it can't be unread. If anything on this list has to come out, it's that one.

## 4\. Register — where the prose calls attention to itself

The brief is plain, down-to-earth, no literary performance, important scenes shouldn't announce themselves. Most of the manuscript already meets that. The best writing in it is invisible — "she just puts it somewhere and keeps it," "he says it tired," "I'm doing my best guess," "nobody was mean to me. ever. the whole time."

What follows is the material that reads as *written* rather than as *happening*.

### 4.1 Classical and literary allusion in children's mouths

- **Icarus (ch1).** A six-year-old comparing the class hamster to Icarus is the single most conspicuous literary reach in the book. It is also load-bearing for the plot — it seeds the Pluto panic five years later. Worth flagging anyway, because it's the moment most likely to read as an assignment.  
- **"Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else" (ch1).** Genuinely beautiful and genuinely a professional writer's metaphor placed in a six-year-old.  
- **"The moon is a liar" (ch12).** Same. Partly rescued by the table arguing with it instead of admiring it.  
- **Pluto as the room name (ch4).** The demoted planet, at the end of the hall, past the water fountain, housing the child who thinks she's defective. The symbolism is doing a lot of work in the open.

### 4.2 Constructed images

- "looks at her the way you'd look at a stain" (ch1)  
- "Lighter and heavier at the same time" (ch2)  
- "reaching into your pocket for your keys and finding out you already put them somewhere" (ch5)  
- "It's a lab that also has children in it" (ch17)  
- The strawberry magnet with the sticky note swapped for the report card (ch2)  
- The nine brass plates read on the way out and not stopped at (ch18)

These are good. They are also the sentences a reader will notice as sentences.

### 4.3 Thematic statements delivered as dialogue

- The gallery exchange (ch15): "It's not an answer. It's a story about why the number is the number." / "That's what a price is." / "No. That can't be right."  
- "Because the kids are why" (ch10)  
- "There's nothing wrong with her." / "There's nothing wrong with her." (ch2)  
- Hearn's Hamilton arithmetic (ch11)

The gallery scene in particular is the book's thesis wearing dialogue. It works, and it is unmistakably The Important Scene.

### 4.4 Scenes that announce their own significance

The brief says the reader should be able to miss things. These currently can't be missed:

- The folder, in the first sentence of the book, returned to at the end of ch6's list  
- "She stopped talking. At home. ...She got polite."  
- The hallway door "isn't all the way closed"  
- Dr. Ammons **stopping writing** at "the happiest I have ever seen her"  
- "It was hard," and Dr. Ammons **writing it down**  
- The envelope in the sock drawer, taken out and put back  
- Mother refusing the house  
- Mother in the parked car with the engine off  
- Sinclair standing "a second longer than he needs to"  
- Chloe noticing the bolt cutters are wrong and letting it go  
- Ruth sitting on a wall for a long time

The italicised verbs are the mechanism. A character noticing a detail is invisible; a character *stopping* to notice it is a spotlight. Several of these could survive with the spotlight removed and would land harder for it.

### 4.5 The one register note that runs the other way

The chat chapters (19, 23, 26–30) are the most naturally written material in the manuscript. No lowercase-affectation problems, the joke timing is right, and the emotional beats land in four words. Whatever the author is doing there, more of it.

## 5\. Plausibility strain

Not errors. Places a technically literate reader will stop.

### 5.1 The rifle intercept

The progression is nerf (8) → paintball (11) → arrows (11) → rifles (14). The first three are argued honestly:

- Nerf is explicitly easy and the text says why — a dart is fat and slow.  
- Paintball at \~90 m/s and 17 mm is a real intermediate step.  
- Arrows get two pages of correct geometry: fire at 60° so vertical position changes second-order near apogee, keep both archers coplanar so timing error slides the meeting point along the line instead of sideways.

**Rifles get four words**: "behind glass, fixed mounts, known ballistics. Same progression."

The argument doesn't transfer. A rifle round covers its own width in roughly ten microseconds; human timing against a metronome is good to maybe fifteen milliseconds. That's three orders of magnitude, and counting doesn't close it. Chloe at 60% is outside human range and Ruth at 90% is further outside again.

The asymmetry (two pages on arrows, four words on rifles) is conspicuous in a writer who is otherwise careful, which suggests deliberate deferral. **If the explanation lands later, the text still needs one line here signalling that a question is being held open**, or a reader who does the arithmetic experiences a missing floor rather than a closed door.

### 5.2 Nothing discharges in the parking lot

Seven men, three firearms, one already up and being waved at a target closing to three meters, and no round is fired all night. Sympathetic muscle contraction under startle is well documented in exactly this situation. **One shot into a wall costs the author nothing and buys back most of the plausibility.**

### 5.3 Sam takes no damage

Seven men, fifty-one seconds, and he walks away clean — and he is the group's self-described weakest fighter. One injury, mentioned once, would do enormous work.

### 5.4 The clearance

Whitaker's file contains: eleven years at a boarding school with untraceable funding, three research projects the applicant declines to describe on the authority of a private party with no legal instrument behind it, and a stated willingness to consult that party before complying with a government requirement.

That is a denial on any ordinary reading. It cleared in seven months.

Add that Theo was hired into federal analysis and Sam's Army file is "the thickest and the newest," and you have three Halstead graduates inside institutions holding a nineteen-year file on Halstead. Either the system missed the connection entirely, or they are being admitted deliberately and watched — which would make Theo being handed the retirement box either a colossal blunder or a test.

**The author should pick.** Both are good. Neither is currently signalled.

### 5.5 The group chat

Ch19 resolves this ("they wrote the encryption themselves at thirteen"), and ch29 confirms it beautifully ("collection against the channel has returned no content of any kind, on any occasion"). **This one landed.** Noting it only because it was an open hole for three documents before it closed, and the fix was one sentence.

### 5.6 Threads currently sitting untouched

- **The medication.** Nobody has heard of it, it isn't findable, Kavi is told "internal," and the chat moves on in ten minutes. Never raised again — including after the group becomes willing to commit federal crimes for answers. Conspicuous.  
- **Halstead's reaction to the two worms.** The school surveilled these children for eleven years, sells hardware only to current students and staff, and still employs Kavi. Two graduates run financial reconnaissance against the school's own funder and the institution is silent. Eli rules out the government; nobody rules out the school.  
- **Who funds it.** The parents stop investigating entirely after Meg refuses the house. Reasonable in-world, but it means nobody asks for four documents.  
- **Owen and the four who left.** Never mentioned again after ch5.  
- **Ruth's box getting the surveillance team's own cameras.** She describes a tool that writes noise into recorders. The report describes selective deletion keyed to specific people in frame, on equipment she didn't know existed. Either she built more than she says (in character) or the tool exceeded its spec (more interesting). Currently unaddressed.

## 6\. Structure

### 6.1 Chapter density is wildly uneven

- **Ch8** carries six full movements (the asking, the school fight, the gifted program, Ammons, the jacket explosion, the December bed scene, the stopping). Any two of those is a chapter. The December bed scene — "Nobody was mean to me" — is the most important adult failure in the book and gets rushed past.  
- **Ch14** opens with a great observation and then delivers two long paragraphs of subject names. This is where a reader's eyes slide.  
- **Ch28** is the moral hinge of the novel and is six lines. Nine days of argument, a felony decision, and Chloe writing nine pages of operational rules while holding a federal security clearance — all compressed to a page.

### 6.2 The curriculum list is doing too much work

Ch14's inventory is the evidence for what Halstead is, and the author appears not to trust the reader to infer the institution from six subjects instead of thirty. Once the reveal lands, most of it can go.

### 6.3 The ending is Act Two

Ch30 is a decision to hold position, and it's explicitly a pause: "it keeps running and it keeps dying and we keep not noticing."

Unresolved at the close: why her, what the school is for, what the medication was, who he is, whether the seven get caught, and what Chloe does about the clearance. That's a middle, not an ambiguity.

Ruth's real question arrives in **ch26** — *a person sat down and picked me, and I want to know why it was me* — which is thirty chapters of setup finally producing a protagonist-level want. Four chapters later the book stops.

The last line is correct for this book. It just isn't the last line of this book.

### 6.4 Mom loses her own story

She is the emotional centre of ch1–9 and has the best line in the manuscript ("I'm doing my best guess"). Then Dad drives Chloe home from camp, Dad gets the twenty-minute recitation, Dad gets both hands flat on the table, and Meg gets the news secondhand through a car speaker. Her one line there is strong and it's all she gets.

There's a defensible reading where the skeptic being held at a distance is the point. It needs one more beat to read as a choice.

### 6.5 POV expands late and without ceremony

Ch21 is Sam's. Ch22 is Theo's. Ch23 is Nadia's. Ch26 is Ruth's. The book has been Chloe-close for twenty chapters. The expansion works — Theo's file chapter is the best structural payoff in the manuscript — but it arrives with no preparation.

## 8\. Author profile

Everything here is inference from text. Confidence flagged.

### 8.1 Working method — high confidence

The author is spending their drafting time on **voice** and deferring **architecture**. The sentences are getting real attention ("reaching into your pocket for your keys and finding out you already put them somewhere" is not a first-pass sentence) while the timeline, the father, the roommate, and the chapter numbers stay broken across multiple versions.

**Practical consequence for feedback:** they can already see the continuity errors. A list of those is low-value. The high-value feedback is the questions the errors are hiding — how old is she, who is the father, what is the camp measuring, and what is the medication.

### 8.2 Skill level — high confidence, and unevenly distributed

**Sentence level: strong. Well above the stated brief.** The restraint is the tell. "It isn't that big. It's the smallest thing that ever happened to her. She just puts it somewhere and keeps it." A weaker writer names the emotion. This one refuses, repeatedly, for thirty chapters.

Dialogue is the other strength, and it improves markedly across the documents. The ch16 corridor scene is the first time four characters talk over each other, and it's the most alive the book gets. The chat chapters are better still.

**Novel architecture: weak or inexperienced.** The problems are consistent and structural rather than random.

**Best guess at background:** a short-form or serial writer moving to long form. The chapter-as-vignette structure, the willingness to jump years between sections, the late POV expansion, and the very high drafting speed all point that way. Possibly fanfiction or web-serial experience — the "protagonist has no idea how good they are" engine is a load-bearing web-fiction trope, executed here with more discipline than the genre usually gets.

### 8.3 Intelligence and research — high confidence

Clearly high, and specifically the kind that researches before writing. The technical material is accurate at a level fiction usually doesn't reach:

- **Circadian phase advance used as forensic evidence** (ch27). A decision band drifting forty minutes earlier over twenty years is a person aging. This is the single best piece of reasoning in the manuscript and it is not common knowledge.  
- **Apogee geometry for the arrow intercept** — correct, including "sideways is the one that kills you."  
- **Pediatric depression presentation** — irritability as primary, somatic complaints, hypersomnia with fatigue, anhedonia, "I don't know" as the honest answer. Textbook and rarely written correctly.  
- **Opioid-class immobilisation** — respiratory monitoring every two minutes, antagonist out and unused, six-second IV onset, a missed stick requiring a second attempt.  
- **Trauma triage in ch17** — airway, breathing, cervical spine, pupils for intracranial pressure, ribs for flail segment. In order.  
- **AES-256 vs. a default pairing code** — "the box always says AES-256" is the correct insight about where real systems fail.  
- **Moore method mathematics**, applied to a seven-year-old.  
- **Crowd dynamics** — five attackers can reach you at once, six if they're good.  
- **Foreign Service age rules** (exam at 20, appointment at 21\) — correct.  
- **Federalist arithmetic** — Hamilton's fifty-one papers in six to eight months while practising law is accurate, and the omission that this rate was exceptional even among the founders looks deliberate.

**Notable tell:** the one place the research runs out (the rifle intercept) is compressed to four words while the defensible version gets two pages. Knowing when your argument is weak and shortening it is a smart writer's instinct.

### 8.4 Personal experience — moderate-to-high confidence

The author was almost certainly a gifted child, identified or otherwise.

The evidence is ch1's central error: Chloe concludes she is **broken**, not smart. "Everybody else can get through being bored like a normal person and she can't, and that's the thing that's wrong with her." That is the actual private theory these children build, and it is not the version fiction usually reaches for. Fiction defaults to the prodigy who knows they're special.

Supporting: the thirty-problem worksheet solved at problem two; getting in trouble for reading, in reading; the flat version working every single time; asking when it gets harder and being told not to rush.

### 8.5 Age — moderate confidence

**Best guess: early thirties to mid forties.**

Reasoning: the parents are written with adult empathy rather than as obstacles. Meg's exhaustion, "I'm doing my best guess," the father who "asks every time," the mother standing in the kitchen secretly delighted by a curfew fight. That's someone old enough to see their own parents clearly, and plausibly someone who has been one.

Cultural reference points (Pluto's demotion, the Federalist, AES, default credentials as a security failure mode) are compatible with a wide band and don't narrow it much.

### 8.6 Gender — low confidence

Genuinely uncertain, and I'd say so rather than guess confidently.

**Toward female:** the interiority of a specific girl's childhood is very precisely observed. The mother-daughter material is the strongest writing in the manuscript. "She got polite." The collar that doesn't need fixing. The grandmother noticing weight. Chloe weaponising being a small girl in pyjamas is written from inside that experience rather than about it.

**Toward male:** the trajectory of authorial interest. The emotional precision peaks in documents 1–3 and the technical enthusiasm climbs steadily after. Weapons, tactics, procurement, network tooling, and combat mechanics get lavish specificity. The bus-timetable explanation of crowd dynamics, the equipment inventory in the corridor, the disassembly protocol.

**Net:** a slight male lean on the trajectory-of-interest argument, held loosely. The counter-evidence is strong and the safest answer for the form is "unclear, with the technical material reading male and the childhood material reading female."

### 8.7 Comparable authors and influences — moderate confidence

**Plot lineage:** *Ender's Game* is the obvious ancestor and the resemblance is structural rather than superficial — children selected by an opaque authority, trained under a curriculum whose real purpose is withheld, and the reveal that an exercise the children experienced as routine was the real thing. Ch22 (Theo reading the file on a night he remembers as a boring Tuesday) is that move executed cleanly.

Adjacent: the substantial "hidden elite school" genre, and the rationalist-adjacent web fiction tradition where the engine is competence combined with systematic underestimation of the self.

**Prose lineage: much less obvious, and this is the interesting part.** The style is literary minimalism — flat declaratives, present tense, refusal to name emotion, vignette structure, white space doing the emotional work. That's the Carver/Hempel/Robison line, filtered through contemporary literary fiction.

The combination is unusual and I can't name a direct match. Most books with this plot are written in genre prose. Most books with this prose don't have this plot. That's the manuscript's actual distinguishing feature and worth saying in the feedback.

### 8.8 Political and ideological leanings — moderate confidence

**Clear and consistent: educational reformism.** The book is unambiguously arguing that American public schooling fails exceptionally able children. The evidence is arranged as argument: the thirty-problem worksheet, the confiscated book, "let's not rush," a mother fighting six weeks for a cart, and "a pleasure to have in class" deployed as an *indictment* rather than a compliment. Acceleration is presented as correct and underused, which happens to match the research consensus and is still a position.

**Clear and consistent: institutional skepticism.** Every institution in the book signals rather than informs. Four percent is read as marketing. Performance reviews are a retention technique. "The box always says AES-256." Halstead's own grading scale has no ceiling. This is a worldview, not a plot device.

**Present but milder:** Nadia's "they all put labor under costs... it's a resource, it's the only one that gets better on its own" is a light anti-financialisation note. Her declining to involve police over the USB stick, and the students never once calling police or an ambulance across two violent incidents, reads as either deliberate characterisation or a low-grade anti-institutional streak.

**Notably absent:** any partisan marker, any religious position, any culture-war content, any position on gender. Comparative religion appears in a curriculum list and nowhere else.

**Notably even-handed:** the area bombing chapter. An eleven-year-old is required to argue for a moral atrocity, builds the strongest case anyone has made, and is marked down for hedging. The book never says whether the argument is correct. The government is portrayed as well-intentioned, procedurally constrained, and badly outclassed — not malevolent. Neither of those is a lazy position.

### 8.9 Summary judgement

A capable, well-read, high-output writer with real technical range and probable personal experience of the subject, working faster than their structural planning can support. The prose is publishable now. The architecture is a first draft.

## 9\. Verdict

**Pass, with confidence increasing across the documents rather than declining.**

The manuscript's distinguishing feature is a single narrative instrument used at escalating scale: the same event shown twice with different denominators. A six-year-old decides she's broken. A twelve-year-old experiences an armed federal incursion as an annoying school night. A nineteen-year-old files a complaint about being in the remedial track at MIT. A federal analyst reads a boring Tuesday as a classified operation and puts his hand over his mouth. Three teenagers on a loading dock roof critique footwork.

That instrument is used consistently for thirty chapters and it never once gets explained to the reader. That's the book.

**Principal risks:**

1. The ending is Act Two, and the real question (why her) arrives four chapters before the manuscript stops.  
2. Two registers have to hold for the full length — literary realism and institutional thriller — and they currently separate cleanly only because the mystery stays in the adults' register while Chloe's chapters stay about fractions and friends.  
3. Whatever is under the list has to be worth ch5 and ch26. Those are better than most of what gets published in this space.
