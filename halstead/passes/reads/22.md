# Chapter 22: The Offer

## 1. Does it read like a person wrote it

Yes, mostly, but there are clear fingerprints from a mechanical revision pass, and the chapter was expanded at least 10x from its core.

**Repeated formula: showing emotion through physical action rather than naming it.** This appears at least 8-10 times: hands flat on knees, standing, bag on shoulder, hands on window sill, cable turned in fingers, picking at carpet, pacing that stops and starts, careful mark on a list, face staying flat. Each instance is well-observed, but after three or four times it becomes visible as a *technique*, not a choice. By the time the runner marks a name "a little more carefully than the others," it reads like a system-generated demonstration of "show don't tell" rather than natural prose.

**Scenes that exist only to add length.** The entire "Ninety-one names go up over the nine days" section (lines 46-97) catalogs student reactions: the girl grinning and saying the number twice; the boys debating which car; the boy with the spreadsheet folding it quickly; the boy with shaking hands; the girl with her bag still on. Each observation is fine in isolation, but the section's function is purely cumulative—it shows that students process the offer in different ways. This could work in 500 words; instead it takes up half the chapter. The constructed feeling peaks with the whiteboard scene where someone chalks the sum and "a second hand adds a question mark to the end of it before the day is out"—that's demonstration, not narrative.

**Details precise without being meaningful.** "The overhead light in here has buzzed since October, low on anyone's list to fix" serves no function except to fill space with specificity. The same is true of "edges lined up flush with the bench's own edge" when Kavi sets down the cable—exact but empty.

**Sentences that sound written rather than spoken.** "Call the rest of it another twenty, a number he left out and she adds anyway" requires a re-read. "A hundred and fifty five thousand dollars a year, at eighteen, and she would still be here" delivers the calculation with a punch that feels engineered.

The core of this chapter—the Amberg interview, the phone call home—feels genuinely written. Everything else feels added.

## 2. Is it easy to read

Mostly yes. I slowed at:

- "Call the rest of it another twenty" (took a second to parse the instruction)
- The parade of waiting students; they blur together and require tracking who is who
- "School teaches you things on somebody else's schedule...It's a lab that also has children in it" (Kavi's explanation is conceptually dense and the metaphor doesn't quite land)

The opening paragraph and the phone call scene flow cleanly. The waiting room section is the biggest bottleneck—not because sentences are badly built, but because the repetition of character observations (each doing a slightly different thing with his or her bag, posture, or expression) slows momentum.

## 3. Could a ninth grader read this comfortably

They could decode it, but not without effort. The novel assumes context (what Halstead is, what research means, what kind of school this is) that isn't always supplied in this chapter. The language is sophisticated: subordinated clauses, complex noun phrases, paragraphs that jump forward and back in time.

A strong ninth grader would keep going. A typical one would skim the waiting room section or feel it dragging.

The phone call would hold them—it's emotionally clear and direct.

## 4. Is it logical

Yes. The timeline is slightly tangled (we jump from Chloe's interview on "Wednesday of the second week" to "by the third one" and then "by the sixth day"), but it holds up: Chloe's interview is #3 overall, so references to "the third interview" make sense. The arc is clear: Amberg runs interviews, Chloe refuses, the school processes the offers, Chloe tells her parents. No contradictions.

One small issue: we never learn which day Chloe's call home happens. Is it the same night? A few days later? The chapter doesn't specify, though "that evening" for Kavi's scene suggests the call is same-night.

## 5. Is it any good

The core is excellent. The Amberg interview—especially the image of him reading her file in silence for the remaining four minutes, watching the clock, respecting the full fifteen-minute appointment—is precise, powerful, and says everything about his character and the weight of the offer. Her refusal is simple and credible: "It's my school. Nobody stays at their school."

The phone call is affecting. The mother pacing, the father asking about the catch, the sense of financial strain underneath their approval—this is real emotional work.

Kavi's explanation of why he said yes is sharp and philosophical and gives the offer genuine weight.

But the chapter reads like it was expanded with a instruction to "show student reactions" and "add more detail," and those instructions left their fingerprints. The waiting room parade—while individually crafted—feels like it exists to demonstrate the school's collective processing of the offer rather than to advance the story. The same is true of the entire first section about how Amberg runs the interviews (which is vivid but could be a paragraph). By the time you finish the waiting room section, you've spent 600+ words on color rather than consequence.

If the chapter had stayed at 500-700 words, keeping only the interview, a brief note about other students saying yes, Kavi's acceptance, and the phone call, it would be tighter and would hit harder. As it stands, it's well-written in pieces but feels assembled rather than composed. I would keep reading the book, but I would skim parts of this chapter.
