# Chapter 14 Comprehension Review

## What I understood

The chapter covers September 2016 through April 2017 of Chloe's school year, during which she is age 11 (inferred from position; no explicit self-statement in the chapter).

**Archery.** Coach Bell introduces a metronome-based shooting exercise. Two lines of archers shoot from positions 70 metres apart; their flight paths cross overhead at the top of the arrow arc where they briefly hang. Success requires precise release timing. Chloe struggles with timing—her release runs early when anxious and late when cold. She practices the count silently (four fingers against her leg) with Ruth comparing their counts in the hallway. In late October, Chloe and Odile's arrows collide mid-air, breaking and falling separately. This is celebrated as an achievement. By March, most of the cohort has achieved at least one collision; Odile does it repeatedly.

**Other classes.** Latin (Ruth argues it's "cheating" because Chloe already knows Spanish and French; Chloe counters it's efficient, not cheating). Calculus in mathematics. Astronomy on orbital mechanics and universe-age measurement (two methods produce different results, which bothers her). Rhetoric on identifying where speakers are being paid. Art history examining why a painting's value increased ninefold between 1959 and 1998 despite being identical. Wood shop on progressively larger projects, including a Japanese corner joint that takes eleven attempts to master.

**Essay assignment.** In November, Chloe draws "Was the area bombing of German cities the right decision?" She initially dismisses it as stupid—obvious answer, obvious evil. Sam challenges her to argue devil's advocate. She reads intensely over two days, becomes sick on Tuesday evening, continues reading, and spends Wednesday researching a 1961 counterargument, identifying a logical gap in it, filling it, and finding three objections that she then answers. The essay grows to 14,000 words (28 pages). She receives a B with feedback: she hedges and keeps apologizing for her arguments rather than stating them confidently. The note identifies specific hedging on pages 9 and 22 and in the conclusion.

**Christmas home.** Chloe tells her mother about the essay. She realizes that explaining it requires first explaining the hat-drawing process, the seven-day period, and the feedback about hedging—more context than the actual conclusion. Her father asks if she still does archery and directs her to the grandmother. Chloe draws a sixty-degree angle on an envelope while explaining the archery. Her grandmother initially misunderstands, thinking Chloe hit the target once in all of October, then understands it was the first time achieved in October, with many successful repeats since.

**The Watch.** On April 11, the school announces a game called "The Watch." An intercom announcement by Sinclair says "every member of staff currently in the building is now hostile" until 4 PM or staff are eliminated. Two hundred students pour out of classrooms. There are only nine doors and forty adults, but students know building shortcuts. Ruth takes command of the east stairwell, organizing defensive positions. Nadia takes the third floor. Sam jumps the bannister, lands badly, and gets marked out. Chloe spends the whole Watch on the third floor landing "shooting paintballs out of the air" for most of an hour while "balls come up the stairwell in twos and threes." When paintballs meet, they create a crack sound. Staff are eliminated; Mr. Hearn holds the library alone for forty minutes before finally being overtaken at 3:58 PM. The next day's board says staff lost at 3:42, students get a B overall, and there are eleven named failures. Failure #9 states that nobody established how many adults were in the building during the two hours. Chloe stands in front of this failure, disturbed, because she spent two hours on a landing without once checking staff count. Sam at dinner is describing the bannister jump for the fourth time.

## Where I got lost

**Opening field description:**
> "There is a fence at the end of it, with a road past that and open field the whole way to it."

Possible readings: Does "it" at the end refer to (a) the fence, (b) the road, (c) something unstated, or (d) the entire described landscape? "The whole way to it" implies movement toward a destination, but the destination is unclear. A reader needs to infer the field's full extent.

**Archery geometry and lanes:**
> "Both lines shoot the same way, north, from two positions seventy metres apart, and the lanes lean in toward each other by a few degrees so that the two flight paths cross a long way out over the middle of the field, where the arrows meet at the top of the arc."

Possible readings: If both lines shoot north from two positions, how do their lanes lean toward each other? Are the shooting positions not aligned on the same north-south axis? Are they offset east-west so their trajectories angle together? The spatial geometry is difficult to visualize without a diagram. What does "both lines shoot the same way, north" mean if the lanes are angled toward each other—are they shooting at slight angles to each other rather than in parallel?

**Watch game mechanics and rules:**
> "The intercom comes on at two in the afternoon... Every member of staff currently in the building is now hostile... this continues until sixteen hundred or until there are no staff left."

Possible readings: What are the actual rules? How do you "eliminate" a staff member? The text never states this explicitly. From context, one infers:
- It is a game, a simulation, not actual violence
- Students are attacking staff (implied by staff being "hostile")
- Students and staff move through the building
- "Marked out" seems to be elimination (Sam "gets marked out" and sits on a bench)
- Paintballs are involved
- But exactly how marking works, whether staff also shoot paintballs, or whether it's purely physical contact—none of this is stated

> "Chloe spends the whole Watch on the third floor landing shooting paintballs out of the air. There are six of them up there. The balls come up the stairwell in twos and threes for most of an hour, and when two of them meet there is a flat wet crack and paint everywhere at once."

Possible readings: Does "shooting paintballs out of the air" mean (a) Chloe fires paintballs (as a weapon/defense) while positioned on the landing, or (b) Chloe shoots at paintballs coming through the air toward her (defensive interception, like the arrow apogee game)? "The balls come up the stairwell" suggests paintballs are moving upward. Do they come from staff below? Are they being shot UP at students, or do they rise another way? When "two of them meet there is a flat wet crack," are these (c) two paintballs colliding mid-air, (d) a paintball hitting something, or (e) something else? Who shoots them?

## Ninth grader

A ninth-grade reader could follow most of this chapter but would get stuck in specific places:

**Archery section:** The geometry of two crossing lanes and the arrow apogee concept would require careful re-reading. The metronome and release-timing problem are understandable, but the spatial setup is confusing.

**Watch game:** This would stop them cold. The rules are never stated. A reader would have to infer from context that it's a competitive game involving paintballs and elimination, but "marked out," "Kit," the specific roles, and the objective are all unexplained. Most ninth-graders would read past this without fully understanding what's happening.

**Essay section:** The concept of steelmanning an argument (presenting the opponent's case well before refuting it) is sophisticated. A ninth-grader might not grasp why presenting the bombing case fully and well is the right move pedagogically. The "hedging" feedback assumes understanding of rhetorical strategy.

**"Shooting paintballs out of the air":** The ambiguity between firing paintballs and intercepting them would confuse them. They'd lose clarity on what Chloe is doing on the landing.

A ninth-grader would successfully follow: the archery training progression, the Christmas family scene, the sense of the essay assignment and feedback, and the general shape of the Watch as some kind of competitive exercise. The comprehension failures would be localized in specific phrases rather than fatal to overall understanding. They'd keep reading.

## Against the synopsis

The synopsis emphasizes that this chapter marks a turning point: "The same year, she stops being able to talk to her parents, because the explanations would need three prior explanations."

**What I thought happened:** Chloe did archery training, took various classes including writing an essay about bombing, went home for Christmas and had difficulty explaining the essay to her mother, then participated in a schoolwide game called the Watch.

**What the synopsis emphasizes:** The chapter is about the beginning of compartmentalization—Chloe's life becoming incompatible with her parents' ability to understand it. The essay (requiring three prior explanations), the Christmas scene (inability to convey), the constant intellectual engagement, and the Watch game (complete absorption in her school world) all reinforce this theme of separation.

**The sentence that sent me the wrong way:** I didn't identify the unifying thread. I described the chapter's plot correctly but treated the components as separate subjects rather than as manifestations of a single theme: the growing distance between Chloe's escalating life and her parents' capacity to follow. The chronology note "She passes her parents and doesn't notice" (age 12, ch14) flags this, but the chapter itself doesn't explicitly name this as the connective issue. A reader assembles it from scattered moments: the Christmas scene, the father seeing her "furniture moving" at night, the Watch consuming her entire afternoon, the essay requiring impossible backstory. But these are presented as separate events, not as evidence of one thread. The chapter title "Sixty Degrees"—the angle at which arrow paths cross—is a metaphor for this divergence, but it's not explained, and most readers won't make the connection.
