# Chapter 26 comprehension review: Ten Targets

## What I understood

In November 2023, Sam enters the Army and immediately likes it. He appreciates the schedule, food, and people. He's especially polite to a sergeant named Ruiz who shouts at him for four weeks. Sam is competitive with metrics: in week two he maxes the fitness assessment, then does even better in week three. The text suggests Sam has a personality trait where he doesn't stop at a goal—once he hits a number, he treats it as just the next number to beat. By week nine, his file contains many numbers that have started being printed out.

In month four, Sam is put on a shooting range with ten targets. He hits all ten. A captain he hasn't met before congratulates him, saying it's the best score of the cycle. But when asked his accuracy, Sam says "about forty"—not the perfect ten out of ten the sheet shows. The captain is confused. Sam explains that hitting stationary targets "isn't accuracy"—accuracy is hitting another round in flight while both shooters fire on the same count from opposite lanes. Sam claims he's at about 40% true accuracy, while others from his school (Halstead, a small boarding school) are at 90%. He finishes by saying "So I'm forty percent of a rifle."

That evening, what Sam said is written up as a report. By Friday, it's been forwarded twice. The four-paragraph report contains language about Sam appearing "to have received specialised instruction from an unidentified source." By the following Thursday, a woman three levels above the captain is reading it. Sam doesn't know any of this is happening. Sam is having a great month.

## Where I got lost

1. **"About forty"** — When the captain shows Sam the sheet saying "ten out of ten," Sam still insists his accuracy is "about forty." I had to re-read to understand that Sam is talking about a completely different metric of accuracy than hitting stationary targets. The text doesn't explicitly state this distinction until Sam explains it, so the first exchange reads as potentially confusing: "How accurate are you?" / "About forty [when you just hit ten out of ten]?" could initially suggest Sam is either delusional about his performance, or the sheet is wrong, before the explanation clarifies he means something else entirely.

2. **The specialized shooting technique** — The phrase "put a round into another round" in flight is described as something people do "on the count, opposite lanes, you both fire on the same beat." This is a specific competitive shooting format, but the text assumes reader familiarity with this skill. I couldn't confidently paraphrase this exchange on first read without understanding whether this is a real, measurable technique or metaphorical language.

3. **What the report actually asserts and why it matters** — The text says the report "turns Sam's own words into something else" but never quotes or paraphrases the report's actual claims. The phrase "appears to have received specialised instruction from an unidentified source" is ambiguous: is this flagging a security concern? Recommending him for a special program? Simply noting his background? The escalation (forwarded twice, reaching a woman three levels up) suggests it's significant, but whether significantly good or significantly suspicious is unclear.

4. **"A woman three levels above the captain"** — This person is not named or identified. She's introduced only as someone significant enough to be reading the report by Thursday, but her role and why her reading it matters are unexplained.

5. **The time compression and the frame of "by the following Thursday"** — The report is "written up that evening" and "forwarded twice by Friday." If this is Wednesday, Friday is two days later; if Thursday, one day. Then "by the following Thursday" (a week later) it reaches the senior woman. The timeline is mathematically coherent but the exact day of the initial conversation is never anchored, so the rate of escalation feels unclear.

## Ninth grader

A ninth grader could follow the basic plot: Sam joins the Army, he's good at it, he shoots perfectly, he explains to a captain that he's actually not that accurate at the *real* skill, and then someone reports this up the chain. However, they would get stuck on:

- What "shooting another round in flight" actually means and why it matters (too specialized, no context)
- Why Sam's explanation is concerning enough to report (what is the report claiming?)
- What will happen as a result (the chapter ends with setup but no resolution or consequence shown)

They could understand Sam is being flagged for something significant, but not clearly what or why.

## Against the synopsis

The synopsis confirms that Sam "asks a captain when the real assessment is," which is exactly what Chapter 26 shows. Sam doesn't think the fitness test or the shooting test are the real assessments—he thinks there's something more. His explanation to the captain about what real accuracy is aligns with the synopsis's portrayal of Sam wanting to find the real assessment.

However, the synopsis does not address:
- That this conversation results in a formal report being written
- What that report claims or what "appears to have received specialised instruction from an unidentified source" means in terms of consequences
- Whether this report helps or harms Sam's position
- Who the woman reading the report is or what her role is

The chapter and the synopsis are not contradictory, but the chapter introduces a consequence (the formal report escalating up the chain) that the synopsis never mentions or explains. This is not a case of the blind reading assembling the wrong event, but rather the chapter leaving a significant event's purpose and consequence unexplained.

The text does create potential for a reader to misinterpret whether the report is recommending Sam for advancement or flagging him as a security risk. The phrase "turns Sam's own words into something else" leans toward the latter, but neither the chapter nor the synopsis clarifies which it is.
