# Chapter 16 Comprehension Review

## What I understood

Chloe teaches mathematics to seven year-twelve students during her own fifth year. Six of them are straightforward—some want answers handed over, one already knows the material, others work steadily or have personal crises. Marek attends every session but refuses to hand in any work, claiming that if he already knows the answer it's just a "receipt" not work, and that asking him to submit sheets is nonsense. When she offers him advance material or asks him to hand in solutions, he refuses, citing that she already watched him prove he can do it. By March he tells her nothing would make him hand something in. His blank December sheet brings down her grading curve.

Her own mathematics study pivots from producing answers to proving things must be true from first principles. Proofs are returned with corrections: "This step is where you assumed it." She resents this through November but stops resenting it by February.

German grammar improves through Latin and French roots. In materials lab, she spends nineteen days uncovering why six months of instrument logs disagree with the paper written from them: the instrument was recalibrated on a Tuesday in March, everything before that is in the paper, everything after only in the logs. Someone stopped reporting without saying.

In cryptography she breaks systems others built. Eli discovers in November's set that a live parking system sends the same message every Tuesday. Ruth brings her a device in a shoebox—it hunts for network recorders, tries default passwords first, then runs cracking/sniffing/brute force simultaneously to write noise into recordings so they look like camera glitches rather than missing files.

Formal epistemology opens with disassembling "I know I'm sitting in this room" until the room doubts it knows anything. Game theory teaches her the math of right play; she learns it in a fortnight but loses repeatedly to Kavi, who plays the person not the board. Block two is the forge—Thursdays, both Saturday sessions, most of Sunday.

Reading news, she finds pairs of English and Russian reports on the same events that contradict each other: a bridge that fell in December, reported in January (English: design fault in bearings) and February (Russian: overloading by contractor). Same commission, same report, different conclusions. She finds a third contradictory pair and begins looking for a fourth.

Law starts with Mr. Amberg explaining the state code—at sixteen they'll take the bar exam, the test whether a citizen can actually know the law.

At dinner, ten to twelve of them gather nightly at a long table. Eli puts a cipher on a napkin. Kavi solves it in a minute. Ruth suggests the key is probably a date in March. Priya objects to holding it at all. Odile questions why a parking company bought such a system. Nadia insists it's not a date but a plate with a letter in the middle, and that Chloe already knows what it is. Chloe keeps her hand over the corner and never says. By 9:30 p.m. they have six conflicting opinions.

Nadia works the November cipher set with Chloe and says flat things on principle. Sam sits between them most nights, and neither has said a word to him about it.

That summer her father replaces the back steps. The first riser comes out four millimeters proud. She points this out; he says it will do and screws it down. Nobody will measure the steps. They finish the Tuesday at the end of July. Her mother stands on them, bounces once, and says they're a great improvement.

## Where I got lost

**1. Timeline clarity:**
"A Russian piece in February covers a bridge that came down in December, and she read an English piece on the same bridge in January."

*Two possible readings:*
- The bridge came down in December (past), she read an English piece in January, a Russian piece in February (all chronological).
- OR—less clear—the English piece was written before December but she read it in January, talking retroactively about something the bridge was destined to do.

The phrasing suggests the first reading, but the structure is disorienting. The chronology of Formal Epistemology, the newsreading project, Law starting, and the cipher at dinner is also unclear. Do these all happen in the same term?

**2. Marek and the "December sheet":**
"The December sheet has six names doing well on it and one still blank. The grade at the bottom covers seven students, and it is lower than her grade in anything else that term."

*Two possible readings:*
- A master grade sheet that sums all students, with six passing and Marek (one) still blank, and the aggregate grade she receives is lowered by his failure.
- OR—less likely—a single submission sheet from December where one student (Marek) turned in nothing.

The pronoun reference is ambiguous. It seems to be about Marek from context, but "one still blank" could mean one column or one name.

**3. The cipher identification:**
"It's not a date. It's a plate. It's got a letter in the middle of it. You know what it is."

*Three possible readings:*
- Nadia is saying the cipher key is a car license plate (which would have letters), and Chloe has figured this out but is refusing to say.
- OR the key is literally the word "plate."
- OR Chloe knows some other specific thing (a date? a plate number?) and is deliberately not sharing it with the group for unstated reasons.

We never learn what it is, so Chloe's silence remains unexplained. Ruth's suggestion that it's "a date, probably in March" directly contradicts Nadia's assertion that "It's not a date."

**4. Block two and the forge:**
"Block two is hers, so she puts fencing and game theory on the sheet. Fencing she is bad at... Game theory she has inside a fortnight, then loses to Kavi... What she actually does with block two is the school forge. Thursdays, both Saturday sessions, most of Sunday..."

*Two possible readings:*
- She signs up for fencing and game theory, but then abandons them in favor of the forge (what she "actually" does).
- OR she does all three and the forge is mentioned as the one that takes up most of her time.

The word "actually" suggests replacement, but it's not explicit.

**5. The shoebox device's purpose:**
"It doesn't pick between them, it runs the lot and takes whichever one gets there. It isn't clever. It's eight ugly things in a box and one of them is always working."

*Possible confusion:*
The device runs eight simultaneous attack methods and uses whichever works first. This is clear technically but the passage doesn't explicitly state which method succeeds for the test case Ruth is describing, or how many cameras/recorders they've actually found this way.

**6. Nadia and Sam:**
"They work the November cipher set together and it goes well, though Sam sits between them most nights, and neither of them has ever said a word to him about it."

*Two possible readings:*
- They've never discussed with Sam their working relationship or their cipher-solving together (why would they need to?).
- OR there's an implied dynamic between Chloe and Nadia that Sam is aware of or implicated in, and they deliberately don't discuss it with him.

The latter reading feels intended but is never clarified.

**7. The father and the steps (minor):**
"That riser's four millimetres proud." "It's four millimetres proud on a back step." "Chloe." He is already reaching for the next board, attention on the next cut. "Nobody is going to measure the steps."

*Possible confusion:*
Is this a lesson in acceptable imperfection, or is the father being dismissive of her standards? The point of the conversation isn't thematically connected to anything else in the chapter, so its significance is unclear.

## Against the synopsis

**Overall alignment:** My blind reading matched the synopsis on all major plot points—what Chloe teaches, the Marek conflict, her own mathematics shifts, the shoebox device, cryptography, game theory, formal epistemology, the forge, news contradictions, law starting, and the dinner scene.

**One significant discrepancy on the shoebox device timing:**

My blind reading: "In April Ruth brings her a device in a shoebox."
The synopsis/chronology: "Ruth builds the box in a weekend when she was thirteen so they wouldn't get detention." The device was built in April when Chloe was 13, which matches my reading.

However, the chronology reveals a structural problem: Chapter 20 ("The Parking Lot") describes the same device and says Ruth built it "the year before" that chapter's present. If the box was built in April when Chloe was 13, then Chapter 20 should be set when Chloe is 14. The current numbering places Chapter 20 after Chapter 19 ("Sixteen"), which would make it Chloe's age 16–17, contradicting the box's build date. **This is not an error in Chapter 16, but an error in the book's structure that Chapter 16 reveals.**

**The Marek grading question** — A potential comprehension issue, but the synopsis doesn't fully resolve it:

My blind reading of the December sheet: "Marek's blank submission brings down her grading curve."

The chapter states: "The December sheet has six names doing well on it and one still blank. The grade at the bottom covers seven students, and it is lower than her grade in anything else that term."

The synopsis confirms Marek "hands nothing in" and that "From thirteen you teach the year below and their results go on your record." But it doesn't specify whether Marek is graded as zero/failing despite knowing everything, or whether he has some incomplete/ungraded status. The chapter shows that she's upset ("reads it twice in the corridor, folds it into quarters, and puts it in her pocket instead of the bin") but never states the grading mechanism. A reader could reasonably think Marek is failing, when he might actually be refusing the *submission* while demonstrating the *knowledge*. The text conflates the two without resolving which is actually being recorded in her grade. This is not an error in the chapter, but a real ambiguity.

**News reading section unchanged:** The synopsis does not explain where the bridge-reading project comes from. My confusion on this point is not resolved—the text reads like an independent research interest, not a classroom assignment, and no chapter frames it as such.

## Ninth grader

A ninth grader could follow the narrative: Chloe teaches math, one student refuses to submit work, she takes various classes, has friendships, her father builds steps. But they would get lost in:

- The entire Marek episode (the philosophical point that "work" requires struggle even if you know the answer is subtle)
- Formal epistemology and its implications
- The cryptography attacks and why the parking system cipher matters
- The shoebox device and its implications for privacy
- The game theory section (why does it matter that she plays the board but Kavi plays the person?)
- The news contradictions project—it's never framed as an assignment or explained why she's looking for these pairs
- Why Chloe knows what the cipher key is but refuses to say, and why Nadia pressures her about it
- The final anecdote about the steps: what is the point?

They could probably make it through if reading passively, but would have no idea what the chapter is *for*.
