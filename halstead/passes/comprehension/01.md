# Comprehension Review: Chapter 01

## What I understood

This chapter covers September 2011 – February 2012 and follows Chloe, a six-year-old girl who is exceptionally intelligent and bored in school.

The chapter opens with her mom on the phone in the kitchen discussing something about Chloe with someone at school (appears to be Mrs. Aldana). Her mom seems frustrated that nobody at the school is taking her concerns seriously.

At school, Chloe quickly sees the pattern in 30 identical subtraction problems and finishes the first three correctly. Instead of completing the rest, she watches a man in the parking lot load boxes into his car and leave his coffee cup on the roof. She gets distracted and doesn't finish the worksheet. When Mrs. Aldana notices, Chloe says "I don't know," but then completes problems 4-30 quickly and correctly when there's remaining time.

Chloe is an exceptional reader. At the library, she argues with the librarian about the four-book checkout limit, proposing a loophole. At home, she reads constantly and stays up late reading.

In September, at dinner, she tells a long, detailed story about a book featuring a girl who runs away to live in a train station with her brother. She analyzes the relationship between them deeply, explaining how the dynamics change as the girl becomes more independent. Her parents listen but seem less engaged than she is passionate about the subject.

At recess, she joins a game with four girls using a tennis ball. The rules seem to involve catching behavior, but Kayleigh Burns enforces them inconsistently (regarding whether catching on one bounce means you're out). This confuses Chloe about the rules.

In October, watching the class hamster/gerbil Peanut climb cage bars and fall, Chloe makes a literary connection, saying "He's Icarus. He flew too close to the sun and now he's in the wood chips." Kayleigh Burns hears this and responds, "You talk weird." This comment makes Chloe extremely self-conscious.

After this incident, Chloe begins self-editing her answers. When asked what clouds are made of in November, she almost says "Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else" but stops herself and just says "Water." She continues this pattern—giving brief, simple answers instead of her more elaborate thinking. She writes a good paper about a dog missing a leg that Mrs. Aldana reads aloud on Friday, which makes her uncomfortable (Maddie looks down at her desk afterward). In December, she writes a shorter paper about a boat, and Maddie's paper gets read aloud instead.

At parent-teacher conferences in December, Mrs. Aldana tells her parents that Chloe is smart and "one of the strongest readers she's had," but has trouble focusing. Her mom interprets this as possibly a choice thing, but she doesn't think it actually is a choice. Her dad asks if she likes school; she says "It's good."

At a doctor's checkup in December, her mom mentions Chloe's school difficulties. The doctor asks about attention. Chloe answers carefully that she gets in trouble "about once a week" for "not paying attention," and that she finishes work "if it's something I haven't already done." The doctor says Chloe is healthy and there's nothing medically concerning, but since "the school's putting it in writing," he recommends getting her evaluated for attention by a psychologist rather than "sitting on it another year." He provides two names.

At Christmas at her grandmother's house, Chloe gets books as gifts, reads during the meal, and has a brief conversation with her cousin Dana (19) about reading. Her dad comments on the way home that "She was quiet tonight."

In January, during an aloud reading of a book about a boy and a sled dog, Chloe has already read the entire book. When they're on chapter 4, she skips ahead to chapter 9. Mrs. Aldana notices (calls her name in a flat voice), tells her to find chapter 4, and when Chloe explains she already read it all, Mrs. Aldana gently takes the book and puts it on her desk for the rest of the day. At dinner, Chloe tells her parents. Her mom makes a pun about getting "everybody on the same page." Her dad does his laugh noise. Her mom's expression then drops and stays there, and she gets up to do the dishes early.

The narrative reflects that school is boring, and Chloe is worse at the ordinary sitting-still part than the other kids. By February, she's learned to fake compliance—holding her pencil over the page and moving it, stopping her feet from bouncing, still touching her book in her desk but not taking it out.

The chapter ends with her mom receiving a phone call in February scheduling an appointment for March 4 with someone (a name Chloe can't say). Her mom writes it on a yellow sticky note on the fridge and checks it every morning.

## Where I got lost

1. **"In the school office there is a folder about Chloe, who is six."** - I don't understand what's in this folder or its purpose. Is it for tracking behavior? Development? Special needs assessment? The opening is atmospheric but I'm unclear on what this folder represents.

2. **The opening phone call** - The mom says "because from where I'm sitting it's a piece of paper that says the same thing you just said." I can't determine what specific intervention or step is being discussed. Is someone recommending evaluation? A label? A placement? The mom's objection is clear but what she's objecting to remains vague to me.

3. **"You're allowed to read once you finish the sheet, which she has decided not to do."** - This is ambiguous. Has Chloe decided not to finish the sheet (so she can't read)? Or has she decided not to follow the rule about reading after finishing? The implication seems to be she's sitting idle, but the grammatical reference of "decided not to do" is unclear to me.

4. **The recess game rules** - I gather that catching on one bounce means you're out, and Kayleigh's inconsistent enforcement (saying Maddie was out for catching on one bounce, but later saying her own one-bounce catch "was different") confuses Chloe. But I'm not entirely sure of the full rules or why Kayleigh's inconsistency is so jarring to Chloe beyond the unfairness.

5. **"A chair squeaks somewhere behind her, while Peanut keeps washing his face, so they must not have heard her."** - This sentence is confusing. She says "He's Icarus" quietly. A chair squeaks and Peanut keeps washing, so "they must not have heard her." But then Kayleigh turns around. Is the chair squeak a sign of someone reacting? Or is it just an incidental sound? The logic of "squeaking chair + Peanut washing = nobody heard me" doesn't quite track for me.

6. **"Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else"** - I'm unsure if this is Chloe's own metaphor or from a book. Either way, is this poetic/wrong for a classroom answer, or just more elaborate? The text implies she's self-censoring it, but I don't know if she's hiding it because it's wrong or because it's weird/too fancy.

7. **Her mom's interpretation of "choice thing"** - The mom says "what she actually said was that you can do the work when you want to, and to me that sounds like she's saying it's a choice thing, and I don't think it's a choice thing." I'm inferring she means an attention deficit disorder rather than willfulness, but the phrase "choice thing" itself is unclear.

8. **Maddie's reaction** - When Mrs. Aldana reads Chloe's paper aloud in November, "Maddie looks down at her own desk." I'm inferring Maddie feels inadequate because her paper wasn't chosen. But this is inference; it could mean other things.

9. **The sticky note name** - "a name that she can't say" - I'm inferring this is a doctor/psychologist name Chloe can't pronounce or recognize. But "can't say" could mean she can't read it or can't pronounce it.

10. **"She had to be told three times to eat, because she couldn't do both"** - In the Christmas section, the mom tells someone on the phone that Chloe had to be told three times to eat because she couldn't do both—eating and talking/listening. I understand the meaning, but it's slightly unclear if the mom means Chloe was being rude/refusing, or if Chloe literally cannot divide her attention between talking and eating. The tone (her mom thinks it's funny) suggests the latter, but the phrasing is a bit unclear.

## Ninth grader

Yes, largely. A ninth-grader could follow the plot and emotional arc:
- Chloe is smart but bored in school
- She gets distracted easily
- After being told she "talks weird," she becomes self-conscious and stops speaking up
- She's now being referred for a psychological evaluation

Where they might struggle:
- The opening phone call's purpose might be lost without context
- They might not understand why the water cycle answer matters
- They might not catch the significance of the Icarus moment or why it's shameful
- The nuance of self-editing to fit in might be subtle for some

But the basic story is followable: girl is smart, girl is bored, girl gets told she's weird, girl shuts down, girl gets referred to a psychologist.
