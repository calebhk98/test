# Comprehension Review: Chapter 13

## What I understood

The chapter covers September 2015 to April 2016 and follows Chloe through multiple activities while she improves her essay writing.

**Essays and the Federalist Papers**: Mr. Hearn assigns 10-page essays each week on the Federalist Papers. He uses Alexander Hamilton as motivation—Hamilton wrote 51 essays in 6-8 months while working full-time, roughly one essay every four days at 8-16 pages each. Hearn expects students to write one 10-page essay per week, well-researched and argued. Chloe starts badly: her first essay (on whether a small state can be trusted with a veto) gets feedback that she's just written ten pages of a position she already held, with no genuine engagement with the opposing side. She reads Hamilton's 51 papers between October and mid-December. Her early essays follow the same pattern—one good page (page 3) doing real work while the other nine pages remain unfocused or repetitive. By late February, something shifts. She writes an essay on whether an unenforced rule counts as a rule in about two hours, hands it in without revision, and gets feedback praising it. Later essays take 2-4 hours and all ten pages carry weight. She moves on to reading Burke.

**Self-defense training**: An hour every day starting in September, running through the end of the year. First unit is basic movements: standing, falling, getting up with someone's hand on your collarbone, repetition of single techniques slow and then faster. November shifts to "twos." In January, 10v1 drills begin: one student in the middle, ten attacking, one minute on the clock. Kowalczyk explains that five people can reach you at once, up to six or seven if you're standing badly, so ten is "five on you and five waiting." If you can hold a minute, you have five capable fighters left—the core of a real fight. No one in the room manages more than nine seconds initially. Chloe gets four seconds on her first attempt, six on her third. By March, most students are in the six-to-ten-second range on a good day. Sam hits fourteen seconds in February, a record that stands all year. Chloe reaches eleven seconds by June.

**Swimming**: Gets harder from the first week. Some sessions are in clothes, some in cold water, third term includes towing a person who resists while managing their head.

**Paintball**: Starts in October. For two weeks everyone is poor at it because the ball travels four times faster than a dart. Students realize they're reacting instead of anticipating. Chloe discovers the answer: watch the barrel and feet to know where the shooter is aiming before the ball is fired. She hits her first target on Monday. By end of that hour her group has eleven hits while everyone else has four. By Thursday they have nineteen. Kavi figures it out independently in November. By Christmas most of the year has passed the performance baseline from dart practice.

**Russian, Music, Algebra**: Russian starts in September; two months in Chloe can argue in it. Ruth takes Arabic; they trade insults at dinner. Music is split between ear training and notation. In the third week of music class, a teacher plays eight bars and Chloe reproduces or captures the whole thing while the girl next to her only gets four notes. She stays in the class all year and re-enrolls in June. Algebra appears as a new mathematics topic mid-September.

**Defensive Watch drills**: Introduced in September with a scoring sheet posted on the corkboard. The scoring prioritizes: preventing entry to buildings, preventing intelligence gathering about the school's numbers/capability/positions, taking comms and equipment, establishing attackers' objectives, and using minimum personnel. Response time is scored against a standard posted weekly. Most drills are announced; some are surprise drills. In Chloe's first drill (announced Thursday, happens Thursday), four adults breach the north fence and enter Building Two in under two minutes. One student goes down, two more at the stairwell, another answers a question about numbers. The drill runs eleven minutes. The debrief sheet shows all twelve students with an F and lists nine failure items. Chloe stands at the north stairwell for the entire six minutes it's marked as "uncovered" but doesn't understand what covering it would have meant. Ruth interprets item six as a personal failure; Chloe notes that four of them were standing there without knowing what they were supposed to be doing.

**Thanksgiving conversation**: Chloe's father asks what she's been writing about. She says tariffs. He asks which side. She says for (protection). She argues that some industries are needed in twenty years and you can't get them back once lost. He challenges with steel, which has been claiming protection for decades. She says steel, textiles, and others all use the same argument whether right or not—that's the problem. She had framed a solution: look at whether you could rebuild it in a war and how long it would take. She admits it isn't right, that it was Tuesday and she had to submit something. Her father asks what happens if rebuilding takes five years but the war is two. She says having the answer doesn't help then. He starts a response about it being "the b-" but her mother arrives with dessert.

**By April**: Chloe no longer dreads the weekly essays. Sam asks if Hearn has gotten easier. She says no—same length, same deadline, same feedback style. But she's no longer miserable about it.

## Where I got lost

**1. "He underlines the four and leaves it there."** (line 9)

The sentence is "Fifty-one in roughly thirty weeks comes out at an essay every four days, two to four thousand words a go. Call it eight to sixteen pages. He underlines the four and leaves it there."

Possible readings: Does he underline the number 4 from "every four days"? Or does he underline something related to "eight to sixteen pages"? The math shown is 51 essays in 30 weeks = roughly 1.7 per week = 1 per ~4 days, at 2-4k words = ~8-16 pages. I think he underlines the 4, but the phrasing "the four" is ambiguous—is it the numeral 4, or a range, or a page count?

**2. "Those came back marked on the Mandarin."** (line 13)

Does this mean: the essays were marked/graded in Mandarin (comments in Mandarin)? Or that the teacher marked her Mandarin proficiency? The phrase "marked on the Mandarin" is unclear. Does it mean the feedback was written in Mandarin, or that her understanding of Mandarin was graded?

**3. The detailed Federalist numbering** (line 23)

"Hamilton has number 1, then 6 through 9, then 11 through 13, then 15 through 17, then 21 through 36, then 59 through 61, then 65 through 85."

The significance isn't explained. Is she reading exactly these? Is this just showing the distribution? The text does say "all fifty-one of Hamilton's done by the middle of December," so presumably she reads all of them, but the list feels like raw data inserted without context.

**4. Self-defense drills description** (lines 36-37)

"Standing, falling, getting up off the floor with somebody's hand on your collarbone, and forty minutes at a stretch of one person in front of you doing one thing slowly and then the same thing slightly faster."

Does "one person in front of you doing one thing slowly and then the same thing slightly faster" mean: one opponent/partner does one technique slow, then fast? Or is there a sequence of people? The structure suggests a single opponent, but the phrasing is ambiguous.

**5. "Every essay she writes for the next month has a good third page in it, but the other nine pages are still there."** (line 31)

After Hearn tells her that page 3 is doing the work of pages 1-6 and she should look at what those pages are for, she makes page 3 good. But then "the other nine pages are still there"—does this mean pages 1-6 still aren't doing their work, or that pages 7-10 are still empty/unfocused? The phrasing suggests the latter, but it's unclear which pages are the problem.

**6. "Gets the whole of it"** (line 126)

"In the third week he plays eight bars and she gets the whole of it while the girl next to her gets four notes."

What does "gets" mean here? Can she reproduce it, understand it, play it back? The contrast is with "gets four notes," suggesting one person captured all 8 bars and another only 4 notes. But the skill being demonstrated (ear training?) isn't explicit.

**7. Watch drill failure items and interpretation** (lines 108-122)

The sheet shows "nine numbered things underneath" the F grades. The text lists: "Number one is response time and number three is that four adults entered a building. Number six says the north stairwell was uncovered for six minutes."

Then Ruth says "Six is me" and Chloe says "Six is four of us." Then they clarify that Chloe, Ruth, and Wes were all standing at the north stairwell. So is "Six" a personal failure assigned to Ruth, or a group failure? Does the sheet assign it to one person, or are they interpreting it? The dialogue makes it sound like Ruth initially thought it was a personal failure ("Six is me") but Chloe corrects her.

**8. "Algebra starts in mathematics in the second week, on the sheet, in the slot where the mathematics already was."** (line 126)

Is Algebra replacing the previous mathematics block, or being added to it? "In the slot where the mathematics already was" suggests one time slot, so Algebra is now what happens then, but it's unclear if this is a replacement or supplementation.

## Ninth grader

A ninth grader could follow most of this. The main plot is clear: a student struggling with essays, learning to write better, and also training in various physical disciplines. However:

- The Federalist Papers discussion assumes historical literacy. Many ninth graders wouldn't know what the Federalist Papers are or their significance.
- The Watch section is confusing. The scoring system, the failure sheet, and the concept of "covering" the stairwell are presented without clear explanation. Even after reading, it's unclear what students were supposed to do differently.
- The tariffs conversation requires economics background: understanding import protection, industry dependence, rebuilding timelines.
- Multiple time jumps ("By March," "In November") without clear marking of elapsed time could lose a reader.

A ninth grader would probably stay with Chloe through the essays and physical training, but would likely struggle or re-read the Watch drill section.
