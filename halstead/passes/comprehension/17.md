# Comprehension Review: Chapter 17 (Fourteen)

## What I understood

The chapter spans September 2019 through April 2020, following Chloe at age fourteen.

**Marksmanship and shooting:** Archery is replaced by marksmanship in September. Chloe progresses through dry drill (weapon identification, assembly/disassembly blindfolded), then live fire at 50 meters prone, advancing to kneeling, standing, 100 meters, moving targets. In February, a clay-throwing machine arrives with a printed card saying the interval is nine seconds. Aymar measures it and finds it's actually 9.2 seconds. Voss gets a new card made. Kavi notes afterward that unlike the two-person arrow system they used before, bullets from a machine don't allow for shared timing errors if you're late, and predicts another escalation is coming.

**Self-defense:** Chloe holds fifty seconds defending against ten people in February on her third round, nearly a minute. She's then moved to pair work instead of continuing the three-round rotation.

**Teaching:** Seven thirteen-year-olds are assigned to her teaching list. She teaches them differential equations. In November, all seven get the same question wrong on the same test. She realizes she taught them the ordering backward in her notes from the third week, and all seven learned it that way. She tells them the test won't count but they'll get a harder version in two weeks with the correct method.

**Academics:** Japanese progresses faster than expected, 40 minutes nightly by November, ahead of where Mandarin had her. She also takes abstract algebra, contracts, logistics, comparative religion, and engineering/design. In design, she writes a two-page clamp specification. When Dov builds it, it closes on the wrong axis. Reviewing her specification, she realizes she never wrote what the clamp should hold. In the spring "no-libraries" project, she writes a text renderer (~9,000 lines across 14 files) that reads font files, adjusts for 8-point screen display, kerning between letter pairs, and does live page layout in under 16 milliseconds.

**Ruth's sound project:** Ruth generates sound live based on user input. Chloe helps her at 1am when Ruth discovers she's been cutting 40 milliseconds of silence off the front of recordings for days.

**Materials lab:** Chloe spends five weeks troubleshooting inconsistent fracture tests. She discovers samples cut on Friday with one saw are three degrees off-square; Tuesday samples from another saw are true. The log lists date and operator but not which saw was used. Her name goes in acknowledgments on the paper in March.

**Sam's growth and relationship:** Sam is 6'2" by September (grew four inches over summer). He asks out a girl from the year below in October, saying "Do you want to do something?" without knowing what they'd do. The relationship lasts until March. Priya says the girl ended it, Sam says they agreed, and Odile (who was there) says both of them have the wrong day.

**The forge:** Chloe finishes a set of three hinges in November (three Sundays' work). She checks them against light, sees the seam is off, and throws all three in the scrap bin.

**The dinner scene (winter break, second night):** Chloe tells her mother about Odile's scar from the rifle ejection port. Her mother realizes for the first time that Chloe is shooting rifles and is shocked it's the first she's hearing about it. Chloe explains it's supervised and safe, listing other things that started in September: teaching, the clamp, the text renderer, the lab work, Japanese, contracts, logistics, abstract algebra—six other things she didn't mention. Her father says he knew from an October phone call but thought her mother knew. Her mother is upset he didn't tell her. After tension, they watch a game and eat pie. On Sunday her mother asks detailed safety questions. Chloe calls home twice weekly through early February, Sundays only during March exams, then twice weekly again by April.

## Where I got lost

1. **"I got it by nine" (line 15).** Context: Kavi is predicting how long the clay-shooting phase will last. He says "I got it by nine" after saying "six on the nerf and six on the paint, so six. Then they hang it off something, then they make it move, then we get the good one." On first reading this reads like "he got it by nine points" (passed by nine), but contextually should mean "I predicted it to be nine [seconds]." The phrasing is genuinely ambiguous—it could also mean Kavi got a score of nine, or passed something by a margin of nine. A clearer phrasing like "I've got nine" or "So nine" would clarify.

2. **"There's nothing on here about what it's holding" (line 87).** The clamp specification lists "pressure," "travel," "tolerance," and "which way it opens," but Chloe realizes nothing specifies "what it's holding." Is this the dimensions of the workpiece? Its shape? Its material? What an engineer means by "what it's holding" is unclear from the text, and the gap in the specification isn't explained—only that a gap exists.

3. **"Eight point stays sharp on a screen" (line 91).** Is "eight point" "eight-point font"? The context suggests it, but it's not explicit, and "point" as a typography term might not be familiar to all readers.

4. **Ruth's sound project—technical setup unclear (lines 93–109).** The text says sound "generated live off whatever the user is doing" but doesn't clarify: Is Ruth recording sound, synthesizing it, or playing back existing audio? When she and Chloe talk about "the attack" and "the tail," are these audio engineering terms or musical ones? What does "cutting that off" mean technically—manual trimming of a digital file? What is the "forty milliseconds of nothing"—silence, a technical artifact, or something else? The resolution is that Ruth has been "cutting that off" since Tuesday, but what the actual issue was remains vague.

5. **"Both of them have got the day wrong" (line 141).** Odile was in the room when Sam and the girl's relationship ended, so she should know who initiated it. Instead, the text says "both of them have got the day wrong." Does this mean they got the *date* wrong (when they broke up), not who broke up with whom? Or are the two possible "narratives" of the breakup both factually off on some detail? The sentence doesn't resolve which reading is correct.

## Ninth grader assessment

A ninth-grader could follow most of this chapter. They would understand:
- Chloe learning to shoot and progressing through different distances
- The teaching plot (she taught seven kids something wrong, has to fix it)
- Sam growing taller and asking out a girl
- The emotional arc of the dinner scene—a parent discovering something hidden and being upset

They would likely struggle with or skip:
- **Technical projects:** The text renderer description (fonts, kerning, 16 milliseconds, live page layout) is specialized software engineering that requires context most ninth-graders don't have.
- **Materials lab section:** Fracture testing, saw variance, and the log inconsistency are technical and abstract.
- **Ruth's sound project:** Audio engineering / synthesis terminology and the precise nature of the technical problem.
- **Engineering spec section:** Terms like "tolerance," "travel," and "what it's holding" assume engineering background.
- **Language and academic scaffolding:** The comparison to Mandarin progress, the differential equations teaching, abstract algebra—these read as "she's busy with hard classes" but the specifics don't register.

Overall, a ninth-grader would get the chapter's narrative and emotional throughline but would lose roughly 30–40% of the technical and academic detail. The dinner scene would be the most intelligible and impactful part.
