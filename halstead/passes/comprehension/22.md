# Comprehension Review: Chapter 22 - The Offer

## What I understood

Mr. Amberg conducts exit interviews in April 2023, running fifteen-minute meetings with about ninety-one students over nine days. Chloe has her interview on Wednesday of the second week, third appointment after lunch.

Amberg presents her with a job offer: full-time research position starting when she would otherwise move into a college dorm. The package includes:
- Base salary of three times the market rate (~$135,000 from a $45,000 entry level)
- Housing, food, a car, and medical coverage all paid
- Option to pursue a degree on the institution's dime while working, at her own pace, with the position held open indefinitely
- Work schedule: six-day weeks the first year, five days after that, same as staff

Chloe immediately declines, explaining: "It's my school. Nobody stays at their school. You go and do the next thing." Amberg doesn't argue, just quietly reviews her file for the remaining four minutes and dismisses her at exactly fifteen minutes.

The narrative then describes the nine-day period when students go through these interviews. By day three, the word spreads about what's being offered. Students waiting in the corridor calculate the salary and debate it. Different reactions emerge: one boy rejects it because his mother wouldn't accept him skipping college for money; a girl accepts with emotion; a boy who accepted says he's had "the desk" since age fifteen and this just makes it official; another boy comes out visibly upset and shaking.

Chloe learns that Kavi is one of eleven students who said yes. She finds him in the network room later that evening. He explains his decision: he wants to do what's happening "in one building" and he's already standing in it. He contrasts the offer against school—school teaches on someone else's schedule and grades against pre-written answers, but in the lab upstairs, he brought a false positive rate that nobody had checked yet, so they couldn't tell him if it was wrong. "It's a lab that also has children in it," he says flatly, and she leaves him to strip cable.

That night, Chloe calls home. Her mother answers mid-loop in the kitchen. Chloe explains the offer in the same order Amberg gave it. Her mother gets quiet, pacing. She calls Chloe's father (Dave) to the phone. He asks, "What's the catch." Chloe explains her reasoning about leaving school to do the next thing. Both parents accept her decision. Her father asks who else said yes. She tells him Kavi said yes; Ruth is still going to MIT; Sam is still doing the Army thing. Her mother—before they hang up—repeats her standing offer to pay for a state school an hour away if needed, saying they'll manage it as they manage everything else.

## Where I got lost

**Ambiguous emotional resolution:** "A boy from Chloe's own year, later in the second week, comes out and stays on his feet. He stands at the window with both hands flat on the sill until they stop shaking, then leaves before the question of whether he's all right gets an answer."

The text does not state whether he accepted or declined. His visible emotional distress (shaking hands) could indicate either a major life decision, rejection, or difficult acceptance. The phrase "The runner marks his name off the list a little more carefully than the others" suggests something notable happened, but the direction is unclear. A reader might interpret this as him accepting and being overwhelmed, or rejecting and being distraught.

**Unfamiliar character references:** "Ruth and Sam, he means, the only two names in that building already fixed in his memory."

Chloe mentions Ruth (going to MIT) and Sam (doing the Army thing) as if her father already knows them, and he does. These characters are not introduced in this chapter. A reader encountering only this chapter has no context for who they are, their relationship to Chloe, or why her father would remember them specifically. The phrase "the only two names" suggests they're significant to the story arc, but that significance is external to this chapter.

**The "twenty thousand" estimate:** "Forty five, three times over, is a hundred and thirty five. Housing on top of that. Food on top of that. A car, medical, all of it covered. Call the rest of it another twenty, a number he left out and she adds anyway."

Chloe adds "another twenty" thousand to the $135k base. The text does not explain how she arrives at this number or what benefit or cost category it represents. Is it housing alone? All non-salary benefits combined? A rough estimate? The math is opaque.

**Unclear timeline:** The chapter states interviews run "nine days starting the first Monday of April" and Chloe's slot is "Wednesday of the second week." This phrasing suggests there is a first week (presumably Mon-Wed, three days) and then a gap, then a second week begins. But the sentence "starting the first Monday of April" implies a continuous nine-day block. The exact calendar arrangement is ambiguous.

**Syntactic comparison:** "The folder open on the desk in front of her now has the same thing typed on the tab. [Earlier:] Eleven years ago the only thing that mattered about this place was that a letter had her name typed on the front of it."

This is a backward-looking comparison, linking Chloe's acceptance letter to the school (eleven years earlier, when she was seven) to the research offer folder. The connection is thematic (her name in both places) but requires readers to hold two disparate moments in mind and notice the parallel. Some readers might miss the echo.

**Lab terminology:** "It's a lab that also has children in it."

Kavi uses "children" to refer to students in the school. The phrasing is unusual and could confuse readers unfamiliar with the institutional structure. Does he mean literal children? Students below him? The lab's location "upstairs" and its coexistence with what sounds like a school is not explicitly connected earlier in the chapter.

## Ninth grader

A ninth-grade reader could follow the main thread: Chloe is offered a very lucrative research job at her school, turns it down because she believes it's important to leave school and do the next thing, and her parents accept her choice.

They would stop being able to follow or understand:

1. **Context for the characters**: Ruth and Sam appear without introduction. What is their relationship to Chloe? Why does her father remember them?

2. **Institutional structure**: What is the "lab upstairs"? What is the "materials wing"? What is a "badge on the door"? These details suggest a specialized research facility embedded in or adjacent to a school, but the structure is never explained.

3. **The comparison**: When Chloe links the folder's name to a letter from "eleven years ago," the reader doesn't know what letter or why it mattered that it had her name on it.

4. **Kavi's explanation**: The contrast between school (pre-written answers) and the lab (no authority knows the answer yet) is sophisticated and might not land with full force on a ninth-grader.

5. **"The next thing" philosophy**: Chloe's reasoning—that you can't stay at your school—is presented as obvious to her and her parents. A ninth-grader might understand the words but not feel the weight of the conviction.

A ninth-grader could grasp maybe 60–70% of the chapter clearly; the rest would be context-dependent.
