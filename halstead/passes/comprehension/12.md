# Comprehension Review: Chapter 12 (Nine)

## What I understood

The chapter covers September 2014 through July 2015—a full school year for Chloe when she's 9 years old.

Chloe receives her course schedule and notices she now has one more subject than last year (9 subjects instead of 8). Her new subjects are Chemistry, Statistics, and Biology. She also notices that Dance and Drawing have been removed from the curriculum entirely, which Chloe is happy about (she didn't like them, though Sam says he did).

**Mandarin:** Chloe learns with Mrs. Sun. She progresses quickly at reading—by November she's reading full pages, by February she's reading novels, by March she's writing 10-page reports in Mandarin. However, the tones are difficult. She can't distinguish the pitch differences in her own speech initially. Mrs. Sun explains that tone is the word, not emphasis on top of it. Chloe tries multiple strategies (recording herself, sitting with Ruth who has good tone). By June she's improved: people familiar with accents can follow her, but strangers lose her by the second sentence. She still cannot hear the difference between certain speakers' tones, even speakers who grew up 800 miles apart.

**Statistics:** Chloe enjoys breaking down numbers. She and Kavi debate a study all through the second and third weeks—he says it shows something, she says an 11-person study is too small to draw conclusions. The debate is strong enough that Sam covers his ears.

**Chemistry:** Vasquez intentionally sets things on fire. Chloe burns a hole through her sleeve. Later she makes something that comes out grey instead of white. Vasquez tells her to dry all the glass thoroughly and come back Friday. She does, and it comes out white.

**First aid:** Classes on Mondays. Initially normal. In term two, the lights are dimmed. In term three, lights are dimmed, a radio plays behind her, and a man 4 feet away keeps asking the same question loudly, simulating a car accident scenario with a frantic husband.

**Textiles:** Tuesdays. Chloe makes a shirt with the shoulder seam an inch too far inboard, so it drags. She takes it apart, redraws the armhole, rebuilds it. The second shirt fits.

**Biology:** The class involves a fish tank. Kavi becomes convinced a small striped fish is being bullied and watches the tank obsessively in October, making diagrams. Ruth argues Kavi is reading bias into it—he picked a favorite early and is interpreting everything through that lens. By February they learn in Statistics about this bias and Ruth starts using the term on him. In March the striped fish dies (four years old). Kavi is upset, but the teacher says the fish was old and he'd expected it since Christmas.

**Peer relationships:** Chloe dislikes Bex (who talks over people) and Iyad (who agrees loudly without listening). However, she builds a strong 19-pound bridge with Bex, and she works well with Iyad in chemistry and would work with him again.

**Sam's year:** Sam spends the whole year trying to get over an overhang on the gym wall (a 2-foot lip). He falls in October, twice in November, once in December from high enough to land badly. In January a younger girl gets over it on a Wednesday using proper left-foot placement. Sam asks her how she did it. On May 11 he gets over it with about 30 people watching (he announced he was going to do it). He uses the left foot first and gets a hand over on the second try. The crowd is loud enough that a teacher comes out. Ruth screams, Kavi has his arms up, and Chloe tears her book corner (without noticing).

**Riding:** In Block Two, Chloe takes riding because Priya does. The stables are 11 minutes past the north field. Chloe learns horse names and behavior quickly (e.g., Cricket can't see straight in front of her). By February she can canter, by March she can jump a low fence. She's fine at riding but it doesn't engage her—Priya is better at it (been riding since age 6) and Chloe enjoys watching Priya more than riding herself. In April, Priya's riding day changes to Tuesday and Chloe stays on Thursday. After two more solo Thursdays (22-minute walk), she quits. Four days later at dinner Priya asks why she quit. Chloe says it's boring—she can do it all, it's just 11 minutes there, an hour of boredom, 11 minutes back. Priya says "It's fun for me, though" and Chloe replies "That's the bit I kept waiting for"—implying she was waiting for it to become fun for her too but it didn't.

**Summer (10 weeks home):** Chloe sleeps 11 hours a night for the first 9 days without anyone stopping her. Her mother jokes about it days 4 and 5, then stops. On day 6 the mother brings water and leaves. Chloe watches films with her father (feels no particular opinion about them, finds that restful, so they watch two). In the second week she bikes 9 miles to the reservoir and back, arriving filthy and starving at 2:15 PM.

In July, her mother takes 4 days off and they go to the coast (3 hours away, motel with pool). Chloe swims for most of two days. On the second evening her mother teaches her cribbage on a walnut board her grandfather made around 1961. Chloe loses the first three games (keeps counting the same card twice), wins the 4th, wins the 5th. They play until the pool lights turn off at 11 PM.

**Father's proposal:** In the third week (of July), Chloe's father has a vendor proposal on the table—6 weeks of work, $40,000. Chloe reads over his shoulder and stops on the second paragraph, saying she doesn't understand it even after 3 readings. Her father explains it means the vendor will do the integration work and finish it. Chloe asks how he'd know if they hadn't. He starts to answer, stops, rereads the paragraph, and says "You wouldn't." Chloe says the problem is she can't work out what breaking it would look like. Her father realizes there is no version where they fail—the language is so constructed that you can't describe failure. He takes a pen and underlines the whole paragraph, asking if the whole 4-page proposal is the same trick. Chloe says she only got that far. That night he goes through the rest and finds 9 more paragraphs with the same problem. On Thursday he asks the vendor which sentences in section three they'd put dates and numbers against. Two come back with dates, but the rest come back reworded (same content, different words). He tells Chloe about this on Friday in the car while she eats a sandwich.

## Where I got lost

1. **"nine-year-olds get eight"** — The opening is a bit ambiguous. The woman says "Nine-year-olds get eight. You were on seven." I initially read this as: Chloe is currently nine and gets eight subjects (and last year when she was eight she got seven?). But re-reading, I think it means: now that Chloe is nine, she gets nine subjects, and last year when she was eight she got eight. Actually, that doesn't match the quote. The quote says "nine-year-olds get eight," so if Chloe is nine, she should get 8. But the first line says the sheet "has one more line on it than last year's did." If she got 7 last year and now gets 8, that's "one more." So the quote is consistent: "You were on seven" (last year) and now "nine-year-olds get eight" (this year, at age 9). That seems right. But I had to reread this exchange multiple times.

2. **Chloe's age progression** — The chapter is titled "Nine" and starts with her getting her new course sheet. The text says "nine-year-olds get eight" which suggests she's entering her ninth year of age. Is she currently 9 or turning 9? The exchange is ambiguous about whether she's already 9 or about to turn 9. (Though "You were on seven" suggests she was 8 last year, so she is now 9.)

3. **The riding section — what actually happened** — Chloe takes riding because Priya does. By April, Priya switches to Tuesdays and Chloe "rides on the Thursday twice more, walking the twenty-two minutes on her own both times, but she is back in her room by five." Does "back in her room by five" mean she quit after two rides? It seems to. Then at dinner "four days later" Priya asks "You quit riding." So it appears Chloe rode two more Thursdays alone, then quit. But the text never explicitly says "she quit" in the narrative—it requires inference that "back in her room by five" means she's done. The word "quit" comes from Priya's mouth, and Chloe confirms it with "Yeah."

4. **Chloe's motivation for quitting riding** — She says "It's boring. It isn't hard, I can do all of it, it's just eleven minutes there and an hour of being bored and eleven minutes back." This could read as: (a) it's boring because it's too easy, or (b) it's boring because she was waiting for the emotional/social connection to kick in (as in, waiting for it to become fun) and it didn't. Priya says "It's fun for me, though" and Chloe says "I know. That's the bit I kept waiting for." The second reading seems right—she was waiting for it to be fun but it stayed boring.

5. **The fish bias discussion** — "By February they have a name for that from statistics, and Ruth starts using it on him at meals." What is "that"? It's the bias of picking a favorite and interpreting everything through it. I think this is confirmation bias or observer bias, but the text never names the concept. I had to infer from context.

6. **The vendor proposal ending** — "On the Thursday, he asks the vendor which of the sentences in section three they would put a date and a number against. Two of them come back with dates, but the rest come back as the same sentences with different words in them." This is saying: the father asks the vendor to put dates/numbers on vague sentences to make them concrete/falsifiable. The vendor comes back with two sentences that have dates, but the rest are reworded to stay vague. This shows the vendor is intentionally evasive. But the text doesn't explicitly say "the vendor is being deceptive" or "this is a red flag"—I had to infer the significance.

## Ninth grader

Could a ninth-grader follow this chapter? 

**Yes, mostly.** The narrative is chronological and clear. The school subjects are familiar. The interpersonal dynamics (friendships, rivalry, boredom, disappointment) are relatable. 

**But there are a few rough spots:**
- The Mandarin tones explanation (paragraph starting "The sound is the other half") is moderately technical and might require re-reading. A 9th grader would probably understand the gist but might get lost in "pitch is the word" vs "pitch sits on top of the word."
- The first aid scenario (lights off, radio, man shouting questions) is described in a way that's a bit abstract. A 9th grader might not immediately picture what's happening.
- The vendor proposal section at the end is somewhat abstract. Understanding that the language is deliberately evasive requires reading for subtext rather than explicit statement.

A 9th grader would likely read smoothly until the Mandarin tones section and the vendor proposal section, where they'd slow down or re-read. But they'd get the overall arc and wouldn't "stop being able to follow."
