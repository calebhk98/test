## The assigned question: "Problem sets go in early" vs "Problem sets get turned in early"

**Current text, chapter 31, line 49:** *"Class happens. Problem sets get
turned in early, hand-delivered to the box outside the office. Dinner is in
the hall nearest her building, most nights..."*

**Verdict: "get turned in" is right. Keep the current text. Do not revert.**

Both versions are legal under the real rule. Neither is a person-dropped-out
sentence: the missing doer is Ruth, she is not a specific person the reader
needs restored here, and the sentence is about the problem sets, not about
who walks them to the box (rule 2, agent obvious; rule 3, receiver is the
topic). So this was never a voice violation in either direction, and the
choice between the two comes down to which one the book actually writes in
this shape, not which one is more grammatically defensible, because both are.

Two pieces of evidence, both checked against the live files, point the same
way:

1. **The book's own idiom for this exact shape is "get + participle," not
   "go in," and it sits right next door.** Chapter 32, line 15: *"a
   compressor motor gets fixed on a Tuesday, a lawnmower blade gets sharpened
   for cash, the register closes within a dollar of itself most nights."*
   That is the identical construction: a short list of routine facts, an
   inanimate or mundane item kept as the subject of a get-passive, no doer
   named because naming Nadia at the workbench three times in one sentence
   would tell the reader nothing they don't already know. "Problem sets get
   turned in early" is the same list-of-routine-facts sentence in the
   previous chapter, doing the same job with the same construction. Reverting
   it to "go in" would make chapter 31 the odd one out against a pattern the
   author is clearly using on purpose one chapter later.

2. **"Go/goes in(to)" is real house idiom elsewhere in the book, but it is
   never used the way this sentence would need it.** Checked across the
   manuscript (`chapters/01_before.md`, `07_the_same_room.md`,
   `21_the_applications.md`, `23_the_first_one.md`, `27_nadia.md`,
   `30_cleared.md`, `36_seventy_five.md`): every instance names a destination
   in the same clause, "goes into her desk," "goes in this box," "goes into
   your file," "goes in the book." "Problem sets go in early" has no
   destination in the clause; the box only shows up two beats later, in the
   trailing appositive. That is a different, more bureaucratic sense of "go
   in" ("the paperwork goes in Friday"), one the book does not otherwise use,
   whereas "turn in" is the plain, universal word for handing in schoolwork,
   the one every ninth grader already owns. It is not more literary than "go
   in"; if anything it is plainer.

Both facts point the same direction, so this is not a coin flip: keep "get
turned in."

## Confirmed correct: passives kept for topic continuity, not touched

The task for this span asked whether the reviewer can tell a real
rule-1-of-three passive from a fault. These all keep the paragraph's own
receiver as sentence topic, with an agent (almost always Ruth, once a
grader) that is obvious and beside the point. None of them lose a specific
person the reader needs. None are changed.

- Ch 31, line 21: *"a class already pitched at the right level for the
  room"*: the room's difficulty is the topic; whoever set the pitch is
  irrelevant.
- Ch 31, line 53: *"The proof gets fixed, the thanks given"*: the proof and
  the thanks are what this exchange is about; Ruth and Devansh are both
  already on the page two sentences earlier, so naming them again here would
  be the flat, wrong kind of "fix."
- Ch 31, line 57: *"every problem set returned without comment... filed as
  evidence for the theory rather than against it"*: the tally is the topic
  of the whole sentence; the graders returning the sets are unnamed
  throughout the chapter and stay that way correctly.
- Ch 31, line 95: *"Adult reading levels get checked against grade-level
  texts... a fractions benchmark from a testing organization gets checked
  against a worksheet"*: same construction as the problem-sets sentence
  above, four paragraphs later in the same chapter: get-passive, object kept
  as topic, Ruth-as-agent obvious from the surrounding sentences.
- Ch 31, line 103: *"Every message gets read, but barely any get
  answered."*: the messages are the topic of the paragraph's whole point
  (what she does and doesn't do with the chat); no reader needs "by Ruth"
  bolted onto either clause.
- Ch 32, line 11: *"The polling interval is rewritten again and again..."*
  and *"the version he keeps is committed at 3:14 a.m."*: the code
  parameter is the topic of a paragraph that is otherwise entirely about
  Eli's process; Eli is the only person it could be, and saying so twice more
  would be noise.

## Confirmed deliberate, not a voice fault: Eli's two-finger tap

Ch 32, line 11: *"The two-finger rhythm he's had for years goes against the
desk edge, faster whenever a line of the design won't sit right..."* reads on
first pass like the fault the brief asked me to hunt for, an abstraction
pulled from a person's own action and handed an active verb of motion, with
the real actor (Eli) sitting right there unused.

It is not a one-off. The same tic, described the same way, with the tap
itself carrying the verb instead of Eli, recurs in `chapters/35_nine_minutes.md`:
*"the two-finger tap against the desk edge running ahead of it the way it
always does"* and *"the tap keeping its own rhythm against the desk edge
whether or not anybody's there to see him doing it."* `chapters/34_the_files.md`
gives the plain version with Eli as subject, *"taps two fingers against the
wood while he reads,"* so the book clearly has both forms available and
chooses the personified one specifically for the moments where the tic is
running underneath his attention rather than in it. That is a real, repeated,
cross-chapter motif, not a dropped agent. Left alone.

## Call-outs

### chapters/31_ruth.md, line 89

**Current:** "Once she's up off the wall she walks past the dorm and leaves
her phone in her pocket. The walk takes her past the dining hall and into the
library, to a terminal, and to the number instead of the excuse."

**Why the current voice is wrong:** This is the third failure mode in the
brief, active voice with nobody in it. "The walk" is not a real actor; it is
Ruth's own walking, renamed as a noun and handed the verb "takes," so that
the sentence reads as though the walk is choosing where she ends up rather
than Ruth choosing to go find the number instead of the excuse, which is the
entire point of the paragraph. The real actor, Ruth, is on the page in the
sentence immediately before this one.

**Why it was not auto changed:** The obvious active fix starts with "She,"
and `sentence opens She/He + verb` is already over its target (203 per
100,000 against 120, per the last pass's own numbers). Voice2's own
instruction is to swap in the character's name rather than force a "She"
opener, but "Ruth" was already the subject two sentences earlier ("Ruth
walks out and sits on a wall") and appears again mid-paragraph ("Once she's
up off the wall she walks past the dorm"), so a third "Ruth"/"she" walking
verb in one short paragraph risks flat repetition of "walks," which may be
exactly why the sentence was varied into "the walk takes her" in the first
place. I was not sure the fix is a net improvement rather than a different
problem, so this is a call-out, not a change.

**Active:** "She walks past the dining hall and into the library, to a
terminal, and to the number instead of the excuse."
**Active:** "Ruth walks past the dining hall and into the library, to a
terminal, and to the number instead of the excuse."
**Passive:** No good passive exists here; the sentence is about where Ruth
goes and why, and a passive would have to either drop her (which is the
existing problem) or promote "the number" to subject before she has found it,
which does not make sense yet. Writing this direction is unwritable without
changing what the sentence is doing.

### chapters/32_the_money.md, line 101

**Current:** "Minutes pass before Theo posts again. Those minutes go where
anything with real weight goes with him: laptop closed, out onto the back
steps of his apartment building, phone still in his pocket."

**Why the current voice is wrong, maybe:** Same shape as the chapter 31
call-out above: "those minutes" is not a real actor, it is standing in for
Theo's own habitual response to something heavy, and "go" is a verb of motion
only Theo can actually perform. The list that follows the colon is entirely
things Theo does.

**Why it was not auto changed:** Less sure of this one than the chapter 31
case. The sentence reads less like a dropped actor and more like a
transitional device announcing a known habit ("this is where heavy things go,
for him") before the participial list takes over and does the actual
narrating with Theo implied throughout ("laptop closed," "phone still in his
pocket"). It is also possible the personified "minutes" is doing real work,
marking that this is elapsed time being described, not a single tracked
action. I could not settle whether this is the fault or a legitimate
scene-transition idiom, so it is a call-out rather than a change.

**Active:** "Theo goes where anything with real weight sends him: laptop
closed, out onto the back steps of his apartment building, phone still in
his pocket."
**Active:** "He goes through what he always goes through when something
lands with real weight: laptop closed, out onto the back steps of his
apartment building, phone still in his pocket."
**Passive:** "Minutes pass before Theo posts again. What happens next is
what always happens when something lands on him with real weight: laptop
closed, out onto the back steps of his apartment building, phone still in
his pocket."
**Passive:** No stronger passive exists; the paragraph is about what Theo
does, so keeping him as topic in an active clause is the more natural
direction, which is itself evidence this reads as a fault rather than a
correct passive substitute.

## Dialogue, protected: "anybody could have done Halstead"

Chapter 31, lines 139-151, the chat where Ruth argues nobody was special:
*"a person sat down with a list of six year olds and drew a line through most
of them and i want to know what the line was"* (line 143) and *"put any kid
off my street in that building at seven and you get this. thats the part

change what a character says. Not touched, and no voice fault to report here
regardless: Ruth is the active subject throughout ("i want to know," "you
get this"), so even setting the dialogue protection aside there is nothing
to fix.
