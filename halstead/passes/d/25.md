## Stage two: sitting in the chairs

**The registrar (L20-26).** I am a college registrar being asked to justify a credit-hour cap on the record. What I've been given is a first-year student's pointed question ("is a guideline the same as a rule, for the purpose of this form?") after I already admitted, out loud, that there's nothing written down. What I'd actually do: a real institution's overload cap almost always exists for a documented reason even when no *specific rule* names the exact ceiling (advising sign-off, GPA floor, financial-aid banding) — a registrar caving on a bare technicality and signing a first-year up for 21 credits plus a separate evening seminar, with no advisor consulted anywhere on the page, reads thin next to how obstructive the same office was a paragraph earlier. *Unsure* — this may simply be the chapter's established pattern of Chloe finding the seam in a bureaucracy (matches the MIT/four-percent seam-finding elsewhere in the book), which would make it intentional rather than a hole.

**The loading-dock man (L14).** I am someone who asks "how was your weekend" as a two-line social ritual. What I'm given, the first time, is an unsolicited eleven-second literal account. What I'd do: this is exactly what the text has him do — wait her out, more patient than confused, say "Nice," disengage. Consistent with a stranger's low-stakes courtesy; no finding.

**Deb, assigning the birthday calendar (L8).** I am the person who has run this ritual for years. What I've been given is a new hire's start-date paperwork. What I'd do: the text has her add Chloe unasked, going off the form date rather than anything Chloe told her — ordinary office efficiency, and it sets up (without contradicting) the deeper pattern the chapter builds around Deb needing the concrete/procedural version of things. No finding here on its own.

**Deb, on the tray (L29).** I am a colleague who has watched a new, much younger hire over-volunteer for scut work three times running. What I've been given is an empty tray carried back a third time. ## Stage three: findings, cleared against the checklist

Quoted and line-checked against `chapters/25_nineteen.md` as read in this session. Every candidate below was checked against "what does this person actually know" and "is calm/competence itself the finding" before being kept; several were dropped for exactly those reasons (e.g. the man who never replies to the research-team email, and the cable-company caller's unresolved forty minutes, both read as ordinary adult behavior rather than a missing beat, and are not listed).

**Objects / arithmetic**

1. L32: "she catches a decimal moved one place to the left in a forklift safety manual, load capacity rendered as a **tenth** of what the source document says... A wrong number in that particular document is a warehouse somewhere loading **four times** what a machine is rated for." A decimal moved left renders the *translated* capacity lower than the true one (understated, not overstated). A warehouse following the translated document would under-load a machine relative to what it can actually take, not overload it 4x. The stated danger requires the error to run the other direction.

2. L98 vs. established curriculum: "Six thousand words is about a day's work, and **she was doing ten of these a week at school** on top of everything else, so this is less than she's used to." Ten pieces at six-thousand-plus words each is roughly 60,000 words/week. Every other source on her school writing load gives a flat, unchanging pace of **one** ten-page essay a week from age ten straight through age seventeen (`characters/CHLOE.md` "Best at: mathematics" section cites `chapters/13_ten_pages.md`; `chapters/13_ten_pages.md:11`, "One ten-page essay a week"; `CURRICULUM_GRID.md:52`, "Ten pages a week at ten and ten pages a week at seventeen. ... Nobody ever says the standard went up, because it didn't"). Ten pages is roughly 2,500–3,000 words. The chapter's own claim is off from established canon by close to a factor of twenty.

3. L86: "**The first piece takes most of a Saturday.**" ... "She opens both countries' newspapers in adjacent tabs before noon and **reads them against each other for three days before she writes a line.**" The paragraph's own topic sentence claims the whole first piece cost about a day; the very next sentences describe three full days of reading before a single word gets written, with the writing itself happening afterward. The stated total and the described process disagree by a factor of three or more.

4. L69: "She keeps calling it that even after **a manager asks what a Tyler rate is and has to be told it's shorthand, not a joke**, the client the only one who's ever confused by it." The clause naming the manager's confusion sits directly beside the claim that the client is "the only one who's ever confused by it" — the manager needing it explained is itself a second instance of confusion, contradicting "only one," inside the same sentence.

**Joining words**

5. L26: "The evening seminar meets **twice a week** from seven to nine, which leaves her eating dinner standing up at the kitchen counter... **most nights**, because sitting down for it would cost her the twenty minutes she needs to get there." The stated cause (leaving for a twice-weekly seminar) accounts for at most two of seven nights; the effect is stated as happening "most nights" — five or more.

No roommate, partner, or visitor is introduced anywhere else in the chapter. *Unsure* — may simply be an awkward way of restating "her own apartment" rather than a claim about occasional company, but the plain sense of the words introduces a person who never appears.

**Order**

7. L29 ("for the first two weeks she takes that to mean she is basically the intern...") narrates events from the very start of the job, but arrives after L14 has already reached "the third week" of the same job and after L17–26 has already carried the reader through a full fall-and-spring academic year. The chapter loops back to week one with no signal (no "back at the start," no date marker) after already being months ahead.

**Specificity / arithmetic**

8. L17: "Fifteen credits in the autumn... cost her about six hours a week once the reading is counted in." Fifteen credit-hours ordinarily means roughly fifteen hours of scheduled class time alone, before any reading; six hours total (class plus reading) is a fraction of that, and unlike reading speed, scheduled class attendance isn't a function of how fast someone reads. *Unsure* — plausible if the courses are asynchronous/online, which the text never states either way.

9. L29: "Twice before her second week is out, she volunteers to do the filing for the whole floor. Deb... watches her carry an empty tray back to the shared bin **a third time**." Two stated volunteering instances produce a "third time" carrying the tray, with no third instance described. *Unsure* — plausible if each volunteering episode involved multiple trips, but that's not stated.

**Reaction**

L95–101: a man precise enough to cite two pieces by title and "genuinely" try to work out the arithmetic of her output is told, flatly, that one teenager alone is out-producing what he assumed took a team — and the text gives him no reaction of any kind beyond silence. Quoted on either side of the gap: "so this is less than she's used to." / "He doesn't reply." Weighed against the brief's caution on missing reactions: this isn't a Halstead-trained calm, it's an outside adult, and "doesn't reply" is also just ordinary email behavior — kept here at low confidence rather than dropped, since the surprise being confirmed is specific and large. *Unsure.*

## Notes on scope
