## Stage two: sitting in the chairs

**Mrs. Okonkwo (tour guide), asked "is there a boy here called Sam."** I am staff giving a rain-soaked family a tour, one of several running that day; my whole job in this exchange is to protect other families' privacy while keeping the visit moving. What I've been asked is whether a specific enrolled child exists. What I would actually do: decline to confirm or deny anything about any specific student, the same non-answer every time — that's the only version of this that doesn't leak information. What the text has her do instead: refuse once ("I can't tell you who's enrolled, sweetheart. Those are other families," line 117), then confirm his existence and her own knowledge of him one line later ("I know you do," line 121). That's a leak inside her own stated policy, and it happens in the space of two lines.

**Dave, in the doorway with a dishcloth (line 27).** I am a father who has just come partway upstairs, dishcloth still in hand from a kitchen task I abandoned, and gotten as far as the doorway of the room where my wife is holding our daughter on the floor. What I've been shown is a beat I clearly haven't stayed for — Meg looks up at me, and I go back down. What I would actually know, later, is only what I saw in that doorway moment, not the birthday litany that came earlier in the same scene, which I wasn't present for by the text's own account. What the text has him say instead, twice, that same night and the next morning in effect: "I heard her. I was standing right there with a dishcloth in my hand" (line 33) and "I know exactly what she offered you, I was about four feet away from her" (line 37) — both claiming direct proximity to an exchange his own doorway appearance, placed after Chloe "has stopped" (line 27), doesn't support.

**Meg, hearing "she said take my birthday... a second time" from her own memory (line 31).** I am the mother who was on the floor for the whole scene and am now recapping it to my husband. What I actually witnessed, per the dramatized version two paragraphs earlier, is my daughter offering the birthday once, inside a longer unbroken litany (line 15). What I would do, accurately reporting it, is say she offered it once, as part of everything else she offered. What the text has me do instead is tell Dave she said it "a second time" — a repetition the reader was never shown.

**The bearded, unnamed evaluator (line 125).** I am a Halstead staff member (or affiliate) conducting an unstructured character interview with a prospective seven-year-old, the kind of open-ended question ("what would you do with a day nobody organized for you") that's clearly diagnostic. What I've been asked to do, implicitly, is form an impression that feeds into whether this child is admitted. What I would actually do, if I were staff at an institution that names every other adult on sight — Sinclair, Hearn, Kowalczyk, Doyle, Pruitt, Amberg, Sandoval, even the tour guide two paragraphs earlier — is have a name attached to me somewhere in the scene, especially given how load-bearing this exact interview apparently is. What the text does instead is leave me as "a man whose beard is going grey," a physical tag that happens to match Dr. Prentice's one identifying feature from two chapters back, with no indication whether that's the same man or a coincidence the text never resolves.

**Chloe, on the rug, overhearing "roof" and "nowhere" (line 111).** I am a seven-year-old half-listening to the television while my parents argue about a house, a topic I don't have the financial vocabulary to fully track. What I've caught is enough fragments — house, roof, nowhere — to know this is about whether we have somewhere to live. What I would actually do with that fear, given everything else this same chapter has just shown me capable of (a birthday-and-bike bargaining spree, please repeated nine times, a scream that startles three people) is something louder or more anxious than getting up once to confirm the car is still there and then tuning back into the television. The gap between the size of the fear implied by the words she's caught and the size of the response (one glance, then disengagement) is worth naming even though the paragraph reads smoothly.
