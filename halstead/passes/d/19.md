## Stage two: sitting in the chairs

**The rhetoric-drill teacher (L59–67).** I am an instructor running a rapid two-person improvisation exercise. I've just heard a student stop twice in thirty seconds and explain herself: "I'm choosing." That's not a discipline problem, it's a claim about method — she's saying she skips the connective tissue on purpose. What I'd actually do: ask what she means, or at least clock it before moving on. What the text has me do: already looking past her at the room, then "Sit down. Next two." No engagement at all with an answer that is, by this chapter's own standard (Amberg's "showing your work"), exactly backwards — she's claiming credit for cutting corners in the one room where cutting corners is what's being tested. The gap between what she said and zero acknowledgment of it is a finding.

**Pruitt (L105–113).** I am the forge instructor. My established relationship with this student is four years of near-total silence, broken only for exact technical corrections aimed at her metal, never at her. I've just watched her leave early, again, for the fifth week running. What I'd actually do, per everything else on record about me: nothing, or one flat technical line if there's a fault on the bench to name. What the text has me do: ask why she's early, get told, note aloud that I've noticed the pattern for two weeks, then hand over tongs with a small speech attached. That's three lines of conversation about her, not the metal — a register I'm not established to have, in the same scene that claims my whole four-year commentary has been one instruction about grip.

**Delacroix (L85–93).** I am a stunt/competition driver-instructor narrating constantly while a teenager handles a car near an obstacle course. I've got a cup balanced on my knee and my elbow on the door. What I'd actually do with both hands needed for a teaching gesture (roof strap, knee, then pointing) is put the cup somewhere first. What the text has me do: never mention the cup again once my hands get busy.

**Chloe's mother (L121–131).** I am watching my daughter do nothing for two weeks from the kitchen window. I know this pattern — I've watched her disappear before. What I'd actually do: go out there deliberately, at a moment I chose. What the text has me do: it says only that I "come out," with the actual transition from window to porch elided; we cut straight to dialogue. Minor, but it's a position gap the brief flags for.

**Chloe's father (L133–163).** I am a man whose established habit is following his daughter's technical explanations closely and asking sharp follow-up questions (per the Thanksgiving tariff scene elsewhere). Handed "she's on ninety-one and it bores her rigid, but she does it because I asked," the interesting question is why Ruth submits to boredom for Chloe specifically. What I'd actually do: ask about that. What the text has me do: laugh, then pivot straight to whether Ruth is miserable — a tangent, not a follow-up to what was actually said.
