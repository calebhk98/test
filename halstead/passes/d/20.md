## Stage two: sit in every chair

**The maintenance supervisor** (L7, L201) — I am a Halstead maintenance staffer with an access badge on my person. I've been given forty seconds of small talk about the weather from a student in the dining hall. What I'd actually do: nothing — I have no reason to suspect anything, which is the premise the plan relies on. But the text asks more of me than that: at L201 my badge is put "back in [my] jacket before he's noticed it was gone," meaning at some point between L7 and L201 — several hours, across an entire off-campus round trip — I was without my own badge and never once needed it, reached for it, or noticed its absence. That's a bigger gap than the scene admits to.

**The waitress** (L17, L169, L195) — I am a fifty-year-old career waitress nine hours into a shift, alone at the till at 2am. I've just watched four teenagers I served waffles to scale a fence into the dark holding a phone I have not yet used to call anyone. What I'd actually do: at minimum, this is the most dramatic thing that's happened on my shift, and the text gives me a reaction — "what is on her face is the thing none of the four of them are wearing" — but never lets it land anywhere. Nobody in the group's later "does this get reported" conversation (L141–L151) mentions me at all, even though I am, on the page, a named eyewitness standing at a phone. That's a real gap for a group otherwise shown running down every angle of exposure.

**The front-man robber** (L87, L109–L119) — I am one of seven, holding a gun, opening a robbery with "Phones. Watches. Come on." I've been shown four teenagers who don't run. What I'd actually do: escalate or retreat once the situation stops matching an ordinary mugging — the text has me do neither; I simply get taken apart in place, which is consistent with Chloe's read of the group as a whole (L89) but means I never register as a person making choices, only as the first domino.

**The sixth man** (L107, L115) — I am one of the group, hanging back where the streetlight doesn't reach. I've watched my own crew get dismantled in under a minute. What I'd actually do: run, or actually go for "a car at the corner" as Nadia is specifically worried I might (L107). The text has my hand go "to his jacket once, halfway," then come back down "when the fight on the ground stays too slow to be worth joining" (L115) — which reads as a strange calculation for someone watching five armed friends lose to one teenager in real time; "too slow to be worth joining" implies I'm still weighing whether this is worth my effort rather than whether it's survivable.

**Ruth's younger brother** (L31–L39, mentioned only) — I am twelve, just admitted to Halstead, already taught by my sister how the corridor cameras loop. What I've been given: information about bypassing my future school's security before I've set foot in it. What Ruth would actually do, per her own character (corrects an unjustified claim in anyone's mouth, including her own): the text has her defend this exactly once ("That's not the same as telling a stranger," L39) and then drop it the moment Nadia changes the subject to Rohan — there's no follow-up on whether Ruth is actually troubled by having handed a minor a security workaround, which is the more interesting question the scene raises and abandons.

**Chloe** (viewpoint character) — Wherever she is told, shown, or asked something in this chapter (Amberg's mailbox story at L19, Sam's "why are they so bad at it" at L183–L185, the waitress's face at L169), she registers it and either answers flatly or files it away without further comment. The one moment this costs the reader something is L169: she "sees it going over and files it with the other things about the outside that come out crooked, and is over the fence and gone before she has got anywhere with it" — a private observation the narration flags as significant and then never returns to, in this chapter or (as far as this pass checked) anywhere flagged in the reference material.
