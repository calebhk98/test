## Stage three: cleared against the list

### Findings

1. **Line 23 vs. line 164 — she is shown reading Hamilton after the chapter says she'd already finished him.** Line 23: "She has all fifty-one of Hamilton's done by the middle of December, and then she starts on Madison's." The same paragraph gives the exact number ranges that are Hamilton's, including "65 through 85." Federalist 70 and 78 both fall inside that range — they're Hamilton's, by the chapter's own accounting. Yet line 164, set "over Christmas" (after "the middle of December"), has her reading "Federalist 70 and 78... on the floor of the spare room" with no indication it's a reread. The chapter tells the reader she moved on to Madison, then shows her back in Hamilton with no explanation. Madison is never shown or mentioned again after line 23.

2. **Line 108 — a named rule violation with no reaction anywhere in the drill or its aftermath.** Nine lines after the scoring sheet's rule two is printed in full ("They do not learn anything about you. Not your numbers, not your capability, not your positions," line 96), a named student answers an intruder's question about headcount directly (line 108). The nine-item results sheet (line 110) that follows only surfaces response time, entry, and stairwell coverage (line 112) — Wes's disclosure, arguably the cleanest violation of the doctrine the whole chapter has just spent a page establishing, gets no line item, no dialogue reaction from anyone, and no consequence. See Stage two above.

3. **Line 152 — the father's sentence is cut off and never completed or returned to.** "'Yeah.' He points the fork at her. 'That's the b—'" is interrupted by the pie arriving (line 154) and never finished, in speech or otherwise; the "eyebrow" a minute later is not a completion. Whatever he'd worked out — arguably the actual payoff of the whole Thanksgiving exchange — never reaches the page.

4. **Lines 116–122 — four lines of dialogue about who failed the stairwell, with no speaker attributed to three of them.** "Six is me." / "Six is four of us." / "I was standing in it." / "So was I, and so was Wes..." Only the last line carries an action tag ("Ruth is still looking at the sheet"), and even that only pins down the speaker of that one line by inference. Line 112 has already told the reader specifically that *Chloe* was the one standing in the stairwell the whole time — which means "I was standing in it" ought to be identifiable, in a scene whose entire content is establishing who is confessing to what, and it isn't.

5. **Line 25 — "the second essay is due six days after the first" sits awkwardly next to the stated weekly cadence.** Line 11 establishes "one ten-page essay a week." A week is seven days; the text specifies six. Possibly the first essay was assigned off-cycle and the six days is the gap before the class's real weekly rhythm starts, but the chapter doesn't say so — it's presented as a flat fact immediately after "one... a week," with no bridge between the two numbers. Unsure whether this is intentional.

6. **Lines 37–39 — the November transition to "twos" is narrated before the October note that precedes it.** "In November it goes to twos. Kowalczyk tells her in October that she is planting her back foot..." states the later event first, then backtracks a month. Minor, but it's a small step backward in a chapter that is otherwise scrupulously chronological month by month.

7. **Line 61 — "two of them she wasted" has nothing to attach to.** Of Chloe's eleven seconds against ten opponents, "two of them she wasted" — on what? The sentence never says. This is the specificity gap the brief calls out directly: a real ten-year-old holding a clock would know exactly what those two seconds were spent on; the line reaches for the category ("wasted") instead of the particular thing.

8. **Line 178 — "a thing she got wrong on page six" is never named.** Four lines of feedback are described (two about an error, two about further reading), and the error itself — on a page-six of a ten-page essay about an unenforced rule — is never specified. Compare this to the sharper, exact detail the chapter gives elsewhere (Hearn's four-line economy, the specific numbered Federalist Papers) — here, where a concrete error would cost nothing to name, it's left as "a thing."

9. **Line 108 — "a bag between them" is introduced and never used again.** Four adults come over the fence with a bag; nothing is said about what's in it, whether it's found, or whether "you take their equipment" (rule 4, line 98) even applies to it. It's the only physical object the intrusion introduces, and it does nothing.

10. **Line 91 — "he goes through it line by line anyway" leaves "anyway" without a clean opposing side.** The sentence right before it says Chloe "stopped reading it somewhere around eight" — but the "anyway" is doing work against an unstated premise (that a student who already knows the sheet makes the full read-through pointless), which the sentence never actually claims. It reads naturally, but the contrast being brushed past ("obviously she doesn't need this, and yet—") is implied rather than stated.

11. **Voice: lines 63 and 126, one-sentence subject summaries that tell rather than show.** "Swimming gets harder and the teacher says so in the first week" and "Algebra starts in mathematics in the second week, on the sheet, in the slot where the mathematics already was" are both compressed narrator statements with no scene, no dialogue, and no reaction attached — closer to a syllabus note than a moment. Not wrong on their own, but both sit inside a chapter otherwise built from specific, dated, witnessed beats, which makes their flatness noticeable.

12. **Redundancy — the "she decides to write a good one" beat (line 27) restates ground line 17 already covered.** Line 17 already establishes, via Hearn's note, exactly what "good" would require (representing the other side fairly). Line 27's "she decides she is going to write one that is good" doesn't add a new problem; it restates the assignment she was already given ten lines earlier, before line 29 finally shows her actually trying different approaches.

13. **Line 71, cut off with no completion given or needed — flagged for consistency, not as an error.** "'...and by the time we—'" Chloe's interruption (line 73) is clearly the point of the cut, so this one resolves itself; included to show it was checked, since line 152's identical construction does not resolve.

14. **Unsure — line 124's "Ruth is on Arabic" may collide with her established language sequence.** Per the characters/RUTH.md language list (Spanish, Portuguese, German, Arabic...), Arabic is her fourth language; the parallel entry for Chloe (Spanish, French, Mandarin, Russian) has Russian, her fourth, starting in this same chapter — which lines up. But the same sheet's own citation for Ruth doing Arabic is drawn from chapter 14 ("Sixty Degrees"), the *following* year. If the one-language-per-year rule holds as strictly as it does everywhere else in the material, Arabic shouldn't span two consecutive year-chapters. This depends on inference layered across two reader-built character sheets rather than a direct manuscript statement, so flagged low-confidence.

15. **Unsure — SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md states a "first fighting score" of 31 seconds for this age/chapter span (its row for age 10, chapters 11 and 13); the chapter's own numbers for the same drill are 4, 6, then 9 seconds on debut day (line 51), rising to 11 by June (line 61).** These are not close. Per chronology/BOOK.md's own stated method, the manuscript should be treated as authoritative over the synopsis where they disagree, so this likely means the synopsis document is wrong rather than the chapter — noted here only because the brief specifically directs a check against that file, not raised as a fault in chapter 13 itself.

16. **Unsure — the historical/arithmetic framing in lines 7–11 is self-consistent but rounds aggressively.** "Fifty-one in roughly thirty weeks" gives one essay every ~4.1 days (checks out as "four days"), but "less than half his rate" (line 11) only holds if you take the top of the stated 8–16 page range; against the bottom of the range (8 pages roughly every 4 days ≈ 14 pages/week), ten pages a week isn't "less than half." Hearn is speaking loosely and the imprecision may be the point of his character (a flat claim delivered with more confidence than the underlying math quite supports), so this is flagged rather than asserted as an error.
