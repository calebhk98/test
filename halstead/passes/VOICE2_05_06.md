# Voice, second pass: chapters/05_behind.md, chapters/06_the_list.md

Re-checked against the real rule (house rule 8: topic continuity, agent
unknown/irrelevant/obvious, or receiver-is-the-point), not the old flat
person-in-subject ban. Looked both directions and for fake agency. Five
sentences changed, all in chapter 5; chapter 6 was read the same way and
nothing in it failed the rule. One sentence called out, not changed.

Of the five changes, one is a new fake-agency fix. The other four undo
insertions of "Mr. Doyle" or "Mr. Baptiste" that the previous pass made under
the old flat rule, in the four places where naming the teacher actually made
the classroom busier and cost the sentence something else on the way in.

## Changed

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 7

**Before:** "The next thing arrives the moment the last is done, all morning
and all afternoon, so that Tuesday passes without any of the empty stretches
she had budgeted for, the kind she used to spend with a paperback wedged into
her desk, its corner against her palm."

**After:** "Chloe picks up the next thing the moment the last is done, all
morning and all afternoon, so that Tuesday passes without any of the empty
stretches she had budgeted for, the kind she used to spend with a paperback
wedged into her desk, its corner against her palm."

**Why:** This was called out and left alone last pass as an uncertain case.
It is not actually a passive question at all: "the next thing arrives" is the
book's other recurring fault, an inanimate subject taking an active verb of
motion, with Chloe standing right there in the surrounding sentences as the
real, obvious doer. The paragraph's own premise ("everything here moves at
the speed of whoever finishes first") is that the children pace themselves,
so having her pick the next thing up herself is truer to the scene than
having it arrive at her. Used "Chloe," not "she," because the sentence before
ends on Ruth, and "she" right after "Ruth is ahead again" would point at the
wrong girl.

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 27

**Before:** "Mr. Doyle puts Chloe with Sam and Kavi, then sends Ruth to the
next table, but she comes across anyway before the glue has finished
heating."

**After:** "Chloe is put with Sam and Kavi, but Ruth, assigned to the next
table, comes across anyway before the glue has finished heating."

**Why:** Reverted. Doyle is named one sentence earlier for delivering the
glue-gun safety rule, so naming him again immediately after, for the separate
job of assigning table groups, doubles him up in three sentences for two
different jobs. Assigning lab groups is exactly the "school pairs students
for a drill" case the house rule gives as an obvious, irrelevant agent: no
reader needs telling which adult did the assigning. This is also the same
shape as the paired-off boy in chapter 13 ("the boy she is paired with"),
named in the house rule as a correct person-subject passive. Putting Chloe
back in the subject slot also keeps the paragraph on the children, which is
what it is about, and clears Doyle's name for the two places he actually
matters in this scene: delivering the rule, and ending the session.

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 51

**Before:** "Sam wants a hundred, but Mr. Doyle tells him the number has to
be one he believes, so he comes down a long way;"

**After:** "Sam wants a hundred, is told the number has to be one he
believes, but comes down a long way;"

**Why:** Reverted. This clause sits inside a list of three: "Chloe writes a
low number; Sam [...]; Ruth writes hers with one hand curled around the edge
of the paper." The other two items keep the child as the subject of a single
clause. The original passive does the same for Sam across three verbs
(wants, is told, comes down); naming Doyle mid-list breaks that parallel and
turns the middle item into a different shape from the other two. The agent is
also a stock classroom correction (a teacher asking a kid for a believable
number), obvious and not worth a name here, unlike Doyle telling the whole
room what to do with a half-built bridge two sentences earlier, which is a
real instruction worth keeping active.

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 79

**Before:** "Kavi says he already knew it, out of a book, and Mr. Baptiste
tells him that this is fine but he should listen anyway;"

**After:** "Kavi says he already knew it, out of a book, and is told that
this is fine but he should listen anyway;"

**Why:** Reverted, same reasoning as line 51. This is the middle item of a
three-part list: "Sam gets it, [...]; Kavi [...]; Ruth, well down the packet,
is on to the next question." Sam and Ruth each keep their own name as
subject of their own clause; putting Baptiste in Kavi's slot breaks the
pattern. The correction itself is routine and the agent is obvious from the
lesson already underway, so nothing is lost by leaving him unnamed here, and
he is still named as the one running the whole exchange two lines earlier and
two sentences later.

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 125

**Before:** "Swimming is the last thing every afternoon, and because Chloe
arrived unable to swim, an instructor called Deb puts her in the group at the
shallow end of the pool, where they blow bubbles with their faces in the
water for two days before anybody moves on."

**After:** "Swimming is the last thing every afternoon, and because Chloe
arrived unable to swim, she is put in the group at the shallow end of the
pool with an instructor called Deb, who has them blow bubbles with their
faces in the water for two days before anybody moves on."

**Why:** Reverted. This is not a Doyle/Baptiste case, but the same fault:
Deb is introduced in this clause for the first and only time in the excerpt,
and the paragraph is about Chloe's swimming, not Deb. Making Deb the sentence
subject drags in a one-off character to do a placement (topic continuity, the
rule's first test, argues for keeping Chloe there), while the specific,
meaningful thing Deb does, running the actual lesson, stays active ("who has
them blow bubbles") because that is a real instructional act, not
bookkeeping. This is the same split as the Doyle changes: placing a child
somewhere is the obvious-agent case; directing what happens once they're
there is worth an active, named subject.

## Called out, not changed

### chapters/05_behind.md, line 113

**Current:** "A second try gets her no further, so Chloe draws it a third
time with her own numbers, because his numbers have been sitting in her head
since the lesson and taking her nowhere: three quarters divided by an
eighth."

**Why the current voice is wrong:** "A second try" is an abstract event noun
standing in the subject slot with an active verb ("gets"), in a sentence
where Chloe is the one actually trying and is the real subject of every verb
around it (she draws, she shades, she tries the problem). This is the "active
with nobody in it" fault, milder than "the next thing arrives" because "gets
no further" is a settled idiom, but the same shape.

**Why it was not auto changed:** Kept as a call-out for the same reason the
last pass gave: this construction (an attempt or event as the grammatical
subject of an idiom like "gets nowhere") is common, settled English, and I am
not certain it is the fault the house rule is naming rather than ordinary
phrasing. A house rule does not block this one; it is a judgment call the
author should make, not a case a rule protects.

**Active:** "Chloe tries a second time, gets no further, and draws it a
third time with her own numbers, because his numbers have been sitting in
her head since the lesson and taking her nowhere: three quarters divided by
an eighth."

**Passive:** "No further ground is gained on a second try, so Chloe draws it
a third time with her own numbers, because his numbers have been sitting in
her head since the lesson and taking her nowhere: three quarters divided by
an eighth." This direction is close to unwritable here: there is no second
party for a "by" phrase to name, since the whole point is that Chloe is
alone with the problem, and the only passive I could build reads like a
progress report, more literary than the page around it.
