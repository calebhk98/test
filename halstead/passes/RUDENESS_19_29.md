# Rudeness pass: chapters 19-29

Scope: the eleven chapters in `.../rude/b1/` (19_sixteen.md through 29_the_file.md).
Read against `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`, `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, and `THEY_ARE_CHILDREN.md`.
Every quotation below was copied from the chapter file and checked against it.

**Counts:** 6 deliberate items (covering roughly a dozen individual moments),
4 unintended, 3 uncertain, 5 unanswered questions.

---

## DELIBERATE, KEEP

The book already knows about these. Listed so the author can see they were seen,
not as defects.

**1. Bex talks over Chloe and keeps other people's work.** Three moments, same
character, same pattern:
- 19_sixteen.md, line 43: after telling Chloe the exact fraction of her miss,
  "Bex has both of those right, and she has them out to the corridor rather
  than to Chloe... Then she is past her and on to the next of them, laughing
  at something before she has finished telling it." Chloe's protest at line 45
  ("I can do the fraction myself, Bex.") lands on her back and "doesn't slow
  her down at all."
- 21_the_applications.md, lines 143-165: Bex answers the Caltech rep's
  question about the negotiation-board geometry as her own work ("'That was
  me,' Bex says"), though the drawing was Chloe's; confronted at breakfast she
  says "I did tell him that, and it was true by the time I said it" and turns
  to tell the next boy the same story. Ruth calls it out ("That's the second
  time this morning") and Bex "doesn't slow down."
- 21_the_applications.md, line 237: "'Chloe's are half up already, but the
  rest of hers are tomorrow,' Bex says, over her shoulder and out to the room,
  before Chloe has got to the bottom of the list." Chloe's "I was going to say
  that" (line 239) is "not loud enough for Bex to hear."

Bex is a named antagonist. This is the character working as designed.

**2. Iyad manages other people's private business out loud.** 22_the_offer.md,
line 11: he has "the next day's running order in his head before the sheet is
taped up" and goes around asking people how their exit interview went and
"whether they have told anybody at home yet." Line 155: he does the same to
Chloe at dinner the day of her own interview, in front of the table. Iyad is
the second named antagonist; this is a consistent, deliberate trait, not a
lapse.

**3. Chloe's grandmother doesn't wait for the answer.** 23_the_first_one.md,
lines 49-57: she asks Sam, Kavi, and Chloe the identical question in turn
("And you, young man?" / "And what are you doing next?"), and moves down the
row before two of the three answers are finished — with Chloe specifically,
"her grandmother has gone down the row to whoever is coming after her some way
before the answer is finished. 'Well, that's something,' she says." This
reads as a single-scene comic device (a "receiving line" built entirely of one
chair) rather than a trait bleeding in unmarked; it lands the same way three
times running and the narration frames it as a bit, not an oversight.

**4. Institutional stonewalling, several instances, same shape.** All of these
are officials or authority figures declining to give outsiders (or, in one
case, a soldier) more than the minimum:
- 21_the_applications.md, lines 107-113: the Caltech representative asks to
  speak to the third author of a paper; told "She's sixteen... and she's in
  class until four," he asks "Would four work?" and gets "No."
- 21_the_applications.md, lines 123-129: a Duke representative asks three
  times, on three separate days, what happens in the sports building, and
  gets three one-word evasions — "Sport," then "Athletics," then "Recreation."
- 25_forty_targets.md, line 41: Drill Sergeant Ruiz, asked when "the real one"
  starts, answers at length and then, "Get out of my face."
- 25/26, throughout: the captain and the major both give Sam short, final
  answers and move on rather than entertain a second question.

These read as the point — institutional secrecy and command-authority
distance, not a manners slip.

**5. Kavi's non-answer in the chat is a running bit, and the text says so.**
20_the_parking_lot.md, line 175: "Kavi sends a laughing face, his entire
contribution... but he lets it sit every time it comes up, and keeps letting
it sit long enough for people to start bringing it up purely to watch him do
it again." The narration names the pattern as a thing other characters notice
and provoke on purpose.

## UNINTENDED, FLAG

These are the ones a reader would register as rude, done by a character the
book is not treating as rude, with nothing in the scene marking it.

**1. Deb interrupts Chloe mid-explanation, twice, and it is never marked.**
28_nineteen.md, lines 37-43. Deb is written throughout the chapter as kind and
sympathetic (the birthday calendar, the son, "not unkindly" elsewhere). Two
back-to-back moments:
- Line 39: Chloe gives a full, careful answer about certification pricing.
  "Deb's eyes go somewhere past her shoulder halfway through. 'Okay, but what
  does that actually mean for what I tell him?'"
- Line 43: same pattern with the discount explanation. "Deb's eyebrows go up.
  ... Deb stops her halfway through. 'Okay, but in English, what does that
  actually mean for what I charge him?'"

Both are Deb literally not attending to, and then cutting off, a colleague who
is answering a question Deb herself asked. Nothing in either scene treats this
as rude; it reads as a plain fact about how Deb works. This is the closest
analogue in this range to the "not looking up from her book" instance already
caught elsewhere in the book. Reasoning: a warm, non-antagonist character,
doing it twice, unremarked.

**2. Chloe gives the group nothing when Ruth goes quiet.** 28_nineteen.md,
line 181: "Sam brings it up in June... Kavi says she's probably just buried...
Chloe reads both messages twice, then closes her phone and goes back to the
file already open on her screen." Sam and Kavi both raised a real worry about
a mutual friend in the group thread; Chloe reads it, visibly hesitates
("twice"), and gives the group no reply at all — she instead messages Ruth
privately later. Whatever the private message accomplishes, Sam and Kavi's
contribution to the group gets silence from her. Given the author's specific
interest in rudeness Chloe herself commits without the text noticing, this
fits: nothing marks the non-response to her own friends as a thing.

**3. Chloe answers Ruth without looking up from the paper she failed.**
19_sixteen.md, lines 121-123: "'What's that from,' Ruth asks, from the other
bed. 'The one I failed.' Chloe doesn't look up from it." Weaker than #1 and
#2 — Chloe does answer immediately, and Ruth's question is casual rather than
a conversation that matters to her — but it is the same construction the
author has already flagged once elsewhere in the book, applied again to the
same two characters, and worth having on record for that reason alone.

**4. An unnamed classmate answers Chloe while already walking away.**
22_the_offer.md, line 19: Chloe says "That was fast," and he answers "I
already had the desk, since I was fifteen, and he just made it official," but
he says it "resettling the strap on his shoulder, already moving, then past
her by the last word, a hand up in a fast, half-formed wave." He is a
one-line extra, not one of Chloe's circle, which is why this is listed last
and lightly — but it is a clean, unmarked instance of walking off
mid-answer.

## UNCERTAIN

**1. Nadia talks about Chloe to the table instead of to Chloe.**
20_the_parking_lot.md, line 51: "'You take the thing you're worst at and you
stay in it,' Nadia says, to the table rather than to Chloe. 'Every year. It's
the only pattern you've got.'" Chloe's hurt reaction is on the page ("her fork
is still in her hand, and she sets it down harder than the plate needs"), so
the scene is not blind to the exchange landing badly — but nothing marks the
choice to address her in the third person, specifically, as its own small
rudeness on top of the content. What would settle it: whether the author
means this as ordinary end-of-year friend-group friction (in which case it's
working as intended) or as an unnoticed manners slip riding along with
intended content friction.

**2. Dana barely engages with Chloe at graduation.** 23_the_first_one.md,
lines 59-65: on her phone until Chloe reaches her in the receiving line, she
asks "How's school?", gets "Finished. About an hour ago... Georgetown in
August," says "That's good," and turns to talk to someone else about parking.
Chloe's own reaction (she "doesn't give her the rest of it this time" and
"doesn't wait for the next chair in the row") shows the narration is aware
something landed short here. Whether this is a second beat of the same
graduation-small-talk device as the grandmother (deliberate, comic, a set
piece about family gatherings) or a separate unnoticed slip is not clear from
the text alone. What would settle it: whether Dana recurs anywhere else in
the book with a consistent characterization that this scene is servicing.

**3. Kavi deflects a direct question about his pay.** 24_the_chat.md, lines
451-453: "eli: how much" / "kavi: enough." This could be ordinary discretion
about salary (a normal, expected non-answer) or a small brush-off of a
friend's direct question. Given the group's habitual bluntness with each
other elsewhere in the same chapter, a flat non-answer stands out slightly,
but not enough to call it clearly one or the other.

## Unanswered questions

Every question in dialogue in this range that gets no answer of any kind on
the page, with a note on whether it reads as accidental or as a device.

1. **19_sixteen.md, line 191.** Chloe to her father: "Did you and mom ever
   want me to just stay here. Go to the state school, be twenty minutes
   away." The chapter ends two lines later ("Her father takes a while with
   that one too" / the porch lamp going on) with no answer given. Reads as
   deliberate — a chapter-ending suspension, not an oversight.

2. **24_the_chat.md, line 127.** Priya: "what timezone are we even doing this
   in. ive been offline more than on for a month." Nobody answers; eli's next
   line ("so thats nobody") addresses the scheduling problem generally but
   not her specific question. Likely just chat-thread drift rather than
   intended.

3. **24_the_chat.md, line 171.** Ruth, following up on the cheese-horse story:
   "im asking about compensation generally." The thread moves straight to
   Kavi asking the horse's name; Ruth's line gets no reply. Minor, likely
   unintended drift.

4. **25_forty_targets.md, lines 215-221.** Sam to Okoro: "whether he reckons
   the screening is finished with yet." Okoro's response is to laugh rather
   than answer, and Sam "puts it to somebody else" on the next page. The
   narration explicitly frames the laugh as reasonable ("Sam permits him
   this"), so this reads as intended character texture rather than an
   oversight.

5. **29_the_file.md, lines 59-63.** In the recovered incident report, an
   operator is "asked, directly, what he would have given them" — a score, by
   the children who caught him. The very next line, after the scene closes
   out, is the narration's own: "There is no reply on file." This is the
   clearest deliberate instance in the set — the text names the absence of an
   answer as a fact about the record, not as a slip.

Of the five, #1 and #5 are clearly intentional devices. #2, #3, and #4 read as
incidental thread drift rather than anything load-bearing; none of the three
involves a character ignoring another in a way that matters to the person
asking.

## The five to fix first

1. Deb interrupting/not attending to Chloe, twice, in 28_nineteen.md (lines
   39 and 43) — the strongest match to the pattern already caught once
   elsewhere in the book, and it happens to the same character trait twice in
   four pages.
2. Chloe giving the group thread nothing when Sam and Kavi raise a real worry
   about Ruth (28_nineteen.md, line 181) — the one instance here that is
   Chloe's own unnoticed rudeness toward her friends, which the author has
   specifically asked to see.
3. Chloe not looking up from the failed exam paper while answering Ruth
   (19_sixteen.md, line 123) — small, but it repeats a construction the
   author has already ruled a defect once; worth checking whether it is
   spreading.
4. Nadia addressing Chloe in the third person at the diner (20_the_parking_
   lot.md, line 51) — worth a decision either way, since it currently sits
   exactly on the line between intended friction and an unmarked slip.
5. Dana's disengagement at graduation (23_the_first_one.md, lines 59-65) —
   worth a decision on whether she is meant to pair with the grandmother as a
   deliberate two-beat joke about family small talk, or whether she needs
   either more characterization to justify it or a line acknowledging it the
   way the grandmother's does.
