# Manners pass: chapters 1-18

Scope: the eighteen chapters in the review folder, read against `HOUSE_RULES.md`,
`DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, and `THEY_ARE_CHILDREN.md`. Nothing here has been changed in
any chapter. Every quotation below was copied from the chapter file and
checked against it a second time before this document was written.

**Counts.** Deliberate (seen, not a defect): 5 clusters, covering roughly 15
individual beats across the antagonist pair and the Halstead-asking scenes.
Unintended (the actual findings): 6. Uncertain: 3. Unanswered questions in
dialogue: 9, of which 2 look like real oversights and the rest look like the
book doing something on purpose.

**The five I'd fix first**, in the order I'd take them:

1. Ch. 12, line 121 — Ruth's "goes back to her plate" the one time Chloe
   actually tells someone about the Bex bridge theft. This is the exact shape
   of the instance you already caught elsewhere.
2. Ch. 3, lines 153-155 — Dad "stays fixed on the screen the whole time"
   while Chloe sits down next to him with her own book, having gotten only
   "it's fine" for an answer.
3. Ch. 3, line 141 — both parents let a direct question from Chloe go by
   without either of them looking over, in the middle of the letter's central
   mystery.
4. Ch. 17, line 265 — Meg's "What in God's name is clays?" gets no answer
   anywhere on the page; the scene moves on without it.
5. Ch. 18, line 145 — Odile asks the Aurel question, gets a flat no, and asks
   it again anyway two months later. Small, but it's a friend not taking
   Chloe's word.

## Deliberate — keep

Listed briefly, as asked, so you can see they were seen.

**Bex Alcantar takes credit as a running trait**, not a single lapse: the
bridge in ch. 12 ("she says we in her first sentence... and gives everything
after it in I," line 115), the paintball insight in ch. 13 (she carries
Kavi's and Chloe's work to Bell "at the volume she says everything at," lines
91-96), the astronomy ratio in ch. 14 (Bex "gives it to the boy on her other
side complete, beginning to end, Sam's name on the front of it," line 81),
and the Saturday-plan takeover in ch. 16 (line 111-116). It reads as
character, not accident, and it's consistent book-wide.

**Iyad Mansour's running trait is broadcasting what he's not supposed to**:
the Mandarin-accent jab in ch. 12 (line 123), piling onto Chloe over the
missed stairwell count in ch. 13 (line 148), the napkin-cipher story in ch.
16 (lines 169-195), spreading the "taught them backward" story in ch. 17
(line 67), and the Aurel rumor he won't drop in ch. 18 (lines 143-153).
Same note: consistent, load-bearing, clearly on purpose.

**Kayleigh Burns and Bryce Dolan** ("You talk weird," ch. 1 line 93; "You
were weird before, but now you're weirder than that even," ch. 7 line 137)
are recurring school antagonists and the sting is clearly intended.

**The Halstead-asking non-answers** in ch. 7 (lines 169-183, ending "Go to
bed") and ch. 8 (lines 87-115, ending twice in a dodge or a "Goodnight,
Chloe") match `DO_NOT_FLAG.md`'s own note that the repetition across
chapters 3, 7 and 8 is deliberate, built to show what the asking does to the
parents. I list the individual unanswered lines below only because the brief
asked for every one; none of them is a new finding.

**Owen's crying and departure** stay unresolved on purpose (ch. 5 lines
181-187; ch. 10 lines 137-139), per the unreliable-narrator ruling already on
record.

## Unintended — flag

### 1. Both parents let a direct question go by entirely
**Ch. 3 (`03_the_letter.md`), line 141.**
> "Is the network the one with cartoons," Chloe says, from the top of the
> stairs, a minute behind the part of the conversation she is answering, and
> neither of them looks over.

Who does it: Meg and Dave, together. Who receives it: Chloe. This sits in the
middle of the chapter's central mystery (the anonymous letter), where the
parents are legitimately absorbed — but the text gives Chloe a real question
and then has nobody register that she spoke at all, with no camera detail
(a look, a pause) marking that it happened. Nothing else in the chapter
treats either parent as neglectful.

### 2. Dad stays fixed on the screen while Chloe sits down next to him
**Ch. 3 (`03_the_letter.md`), lines 153-155.**
> ...he stops doing the voice, reading a whole paragraph start to finish,
> then going back up to click another, and when Chloe asks him what, he
> tells her it's fine, then goes down the list all the way to the bottom of
> it, before going back up to start again from the first, slower this time.
>
> Chloe waits a while for him to keep talking, then goes up for her book and
> comes back and reads at the table next to him, and he stays fixed on the
> screen the whole time.

Who does it: Dad. Who receives it: Chloe. "It's fine" answers nothing, and
Chloe's response — going and getting her own book rather than pressing — is
the exact behavior described in the brief (someone left to read next to a
person who won't engage). Dad is warm everywhere else in this book; nothing
marks this scene as a lapse rather than normal attentiveness.

### 3. Ruth's flat dismissal the one time Chloe actually raises the bridge theft
**Ch. 12 (`12_nine.md`), line 121.**
> At dinner she tells Ruth the whole fortnight over, out loud this time. Ruth
> says she should have said so to the teacher on the day and not to Ruth on
> a Monday, and goes back to her plate.

Who does it: Ruth. Who receives it: Chloe. This is the culmination of the
whole Bex Alcantar subplot (lines 113-119: Bex takes credit for the bridge,
Chloe rehearses telling the teacher and never does, and finally brings it to
Ruth instead). Ruth's answer is correction rather than sympathy, delivered
while returning to her food, and the chapter's next sentence is Chloe
deciding to stop saying "we" about anything of hers "for the rest of the
year" — the cost lands, but nothing marks Ruth's response itself as the
close-friend failure it is. This is the same shape as the instance you
already flagged elsewhere (a friend not looking up during a conversation
that mattered to the other person).

### 4. Dad's "Cool" during the month-long recap
**Ch. 6 (`06_the_list.md`), lines 99-101.**
> Her dad looks past her at the room and the other tables long enough that
> she turns around to see what he has found, and what he has found is people
> eating.
>
> "Cool," he says.

Who does it: Dad. Who receives it: Chloe. This lands in the middle of the
biggest talking jag in the book — everything from camp, non-stop, the scene
that ch. 8 later reveals was the happiest month of her life. His eyes leave
her mid-recap for long enough that she turns to look at what took his
attention, and what he finds is nothing. He re-engages a few lines later
(the fractions on the napkin, keeping it in his shirt pocket), so this may be
ordinary parental attention ebbing and flowing across a very long monologue
rather than the book missing something — see below.

### 5. The epistemology professor's brush-off
**Ch. 16 (`16_thirteen.md`), lines 121-123.**
> ...Chloe stands in the corridor afterwards with her bag still open, then
> catches her at the lecture hall door. "Say I've got the true belief and the
> justification, and the whole chain checks out end to end. What's still
> missing that isn't just luck wearing a good coat?"
>
> The professor looks at her over the top of a folder. "Office hours," she
> says, and goes.

Who does it: the epistemology professor. Who receives it: Chloe. Chloe made
a point of catching this teacher specifically to ask a real question, and
gets a two-word deflection with no invitation in it, then is left standing
at the door. The school's other teachers who withhold answers on purpose
(Baptiste, Hearn, the mathematics teacher on real analysis) do it as an
established pedagogical style and Chloe eventually gets there; this professor
appears once and never again, so there's no way to tell whether "office
hours" is meant as the same kind of Socratic withholding or a plain brush-off.

### 6. Odile re-asks a question she already got a clear answer to
**Ch. 18 (`18_fifteen.md`), line 145.**
> At breakfast Odile asks whether it is true; Chloe says no. Odile says all
> right, then asks it again, differently, in May. It is still going in the
> summer.

Who does it: Odile. Who receives it: Chloe. This is one of the author's own
named examples of rudeness — asking the same question again after already
being told the answer — done by a friend the book treats as good-natured and
loyal everywhere else (the toffees and clipboard scene earlier in this same
chapter). The rumor is Iyad's doing and is flagged above as deliberate; the
part that's unmarked is Odile's own doubt of Chloe's word.

## Uncertain

- **Ch. 6, "Cool" (above).** Could be deliberate — the scene is long enough,
  and Dad's attention returns immediately after, with real warmth (the
  napkin kept in his pocket). What would settle it: whether "her knee starts
  going, fast and small" right after is meant to register as Chloe noticing
  the lapse, or is just a camera detail with no connection to it.
- **Ch. 16, the professor (above).** What would settle it: whether this
  teacher reappears anywhere else in the manuscript (I didn't find her in
  chapters 1-18) to establish whether "office hours" is her normal register
  or a one-off cold shoulder.
- **Cousin Dana's identical brush-off, twice.** Ch. 1 (`01_before.md`), line
  163: "Dana says nice, then turns to talk to somebody else." Ch. 8
  (`08_the_asking.md`), line 237: "Dana asks whether she's still reading all
  the time, but Chloe says some. Then Dana turns to talk to somebody else, so
  Chloe eats her potatoes." Same cousin, same shape, two Christmases apart.
  Could be a deliberate small note about the family's surface-level interest
  in Chloe; could be the same beat landing twice by accident because nothing
  else was written for Dana to do. What would settle it: whether Dana appears
  a third time anywhere with different behavior.

## Every question in dialogue that never gets an answer

1. **Ch. 3, line 141.** "Is the network the one with cartoons" — Chloe.
   Never answered; see Unintended #1. Looks unintended.
2. **Ch. 5, lines 181-183.** "Did you go in after him? Did anybody? Did he
   have to say it out loud to somebody before they let him go, or did he
   just say it and they let him? How long was it between him saying it and
   his mom being at the door?" — Chloe, to Ruth. Ruth answers only the first
   ("I was going to the bathroom. Why would I go in?"); the other three go
   unanswered. Looks deliberate — part of the Owen mystery `DO_NOT_FLAG.md`
   rules is the point, not a bug.
3. **Ch. 7, line 181.** "When are you actually done thinking?" — Chloe, to
   her mother. Answered with "Go to bed," which answers nothing. Looks
   deliberate, per the Halstead-asking note above.
4. **Ch. 8, line 87.** "Does that mean I can go to Halstead, then?" — Chloe.
   Answered with silence; her mother puts her forehead on the wheel and
   stays there. Uncertain — could be a legitimate camera-only beat (the
   physical gesture as the answer) rather than an oversight.
5. **Ch. 8, line 109.** "Is it Halstead? Is it finally happening?" — Chloe.
   Answered with a description of the school's Thursday program instead —
   a different question answered in place of the one asked. Looks
   deliberate, per the Halstead-asking note.
6. **Ch. 8, line 113.** "Can I go to Halstead instead of it, though?" —
   Chloe. Answered with "Goodnight, Chloe." Looks deliberate, same note.
7. **Ch. 16, line 121.** "What's still missing that isn't just luck wearing
   a good coat?" — Chloe, to the epistemology professor. Answered with
   "Office hours." See Uncertain, above.
8. **Ch. 17, line 265.** "What in God's name is clays?" — Meg. Never
   answered anywhere on the page; the scene moves straight to "Your father
   is looking at his plate when your mother says, 'You knew?'" Looks like a
   genuine oversight — an ordinary, answerable question from a parent that
   the scene simply drops.
9. **Ch. 18, line 45.** "What are the cards?" — Sam. The narration moves
   straight into "It takes her evenings, and what comes out of it is..."
   without showing anyone answer him on the page. Minor; could be
   intentional compression (time skipping past the answer) rather than an
   unheard question.
