# Does it read well: what twelve readers said

Twelve chapters, the ones carrying the heaviest machine editing, each read cold
by one reader asked whether the prose reads like a person wrote it. Individual
reports are in `passes/reads/NN.md`.

The short answer: the prose itself reads as written rather than generated, and
several chapters were called strong. What the readers found instead is that
**each revision pass left a fingerprint, and the fingerprints are now visible.**

## 1. The child-voice pass became a formula

This is the finding, and it is unanimous across every chapter that pass touched.

| chapter | what the reader said |
| :-- | :-- |
| 1 | seven or more times, "borders on visible as a technique" |
| 14 | seven instances, "at the edge of becoming noticeable" |
| 16 | "effective once but becoming formulaic by repetition" |
| 22 | eight to ten times, "becoming formulaic by the middle" |
| 23 | "frequently enough to start feeling formulaic" |
| 25 | "the dominant pattern... becomes formula by repetition" |

The instruction was to show a feeling as a physical action rather than naming it.
It was the right fix for the alien-narrator problem and it is now the only move
the book has. The same thing happened with the negative-space construction, and
was measured and reduced; this one needs the same treatment, which is not to
reverse it but to stop it being the answer every time.

Chapter 25's reader put the cost plainly: technically accomplished, "impressive
*and* lifeless", "like watching a gymnast execute a routine flawlessly rather
than experiencing genuine human confusion or pain". That chapter measures as the
best prose in the book, which is exactly the condition under which this is worth
knowing.

## 2. The expansion pass padded

Three of the four chapters expanded from outlines came back with the same note.

- **Chapter 21.** Nine separate scenes of university representatives observing
  classes, all doing the same narrative work. "This section needs to be halved."
  Meanwhile five students' results are compressed into 35 lines at the end. The
  reader's suggestion is to move the space from the first to the second.
- **Chapter 22.** The 52-line waiting room "serves primarily to demonstrate
  variety rather than advance the narrative", and the reader's verdict is that
  the chapter would be stronger at 600 to 700 words. It is currently 2,471. That
  is a direct challenge to the word-count target I set, and it deserves an
  answer rather than a defence.
- **Chapter 23.** The nine futures at the end "reads exactly as a list". Ruth
  gets one sentence, Kavi one, Theo two, Eli two. Nadia and Priya have enough
  weight to escape it; the others read as check-marks clearing the stage.

## 3. Two of my five clarity fixes over-explain

Worth recording precisely, because the failure mode is symmetrical with the one
it was fixing.

- **Chapter 26, over-explains.** The passage now says the report turns Sam's
  words into something else, *and* that the captain files it as a concern rather
  than a recommendation, *and* then inventories what is in it. "The passage would
  be stronger if it trusted the reader with either statement alone, not both."
- **Chapter 16, over-explains.** The Marek paragraph "reads like an editorial
  addition meant to ensure the reader understands what the fail meant and how
  Chloe felt about it", and carries a stiff phrase, "in January the same as he was
  in December, in the same year".
- **Chapter 19, clean.** Both reworded threads "read naturally and do the
  clarifying work without over-explaining".
- **Chapter 14, clean.** "The rewording, if recent, is invisible."
- **Chapter 34, a seam.** The robbery sentence "fits, but you can see the seam",
  being present tense and slightly narrative against past-tense report prose
  around it.

## 4. Mechanical list structures

Two places where a good sentence repeated becomes machinery.

- **Chapter 24**, the paragraph placing all seven of them as they type. Seven
  sentences, every one Name plus verb plus prepositional phrase. Read singly each
  is good, which is why it survived; read together the reader called it a
  catalogue.
- **Chapter 34**, the incident report: "Seven men / None of the seven / All
  seven" as consecutive paragraph openers.

## 5. Chapter 20 has a real wrinkle, and its reader overstated it

The reader reports a contradiction: they agree to say nothing to avoid being sent
home, and then Ruth "posts a video of it online". She does not. She puts four
lines and a photo in the group chat these students wrote themselves at thirteen,
which is the opposite of public.

What survives is milder and still worth a look. Sixty replies means most of the
year knows by breakfast and argues about it in the dining hall, which sits oddly
against a fear of staff finding out. Students not telling staff is a reasonable
assumption; the chapter currently leaves the reader to make it.

Its other finding stands: six section breaks with widening time jumps, and an
ending that reads as a separate summary rather than part of the chapter.

## What was called good

Not padding. The readers were asked to be blunt and were blunt elsewhere.

- Chapter 19: "reads like it was written rather than assembled", the whole arc
  "hangs together logically"
- Chapter 14: "genuinely written prose, not assembled... strong work"
- Chapter 24: the transcript "captures the rhythm, timing, and dynamic of real
  friends texting across distance"
- Chapter 22: the core of it, Amberg's offer and the phone call home, "genuinely
  well-written and hits hard"
- Chapter 34: Ruth mentioning in passing that she built the box at thirteen,
  "genuinely compelling"
- Chapter 20: the diner is warm, the fight is clear, the voices are distinct
