## Write-up required: misplaced modifier inside protected dialogue

### chapters/26_the_exercise.md, line 131

**Current:** "The man who took you had been in position since twenty-one
hundred, which is seven hours without moving through the entire drop in
temperature that arrives after midnight, and it took him under two seconds
once you finally got there."

**Why the current voice is wrong:** This is not a rule 8 fault; the clause
is active and the agent (the man in the weeds) is named. It fails a
different, adjacent principle: misplaced modifier
(`PROSE_PRINCIPLES_CLARITY.md`, principle 3). "Moving through the entire
drop in temperature" reads, on a first pass, as one unit, as though a
person could physically move through a temperature drop the way he moves
through a doorway. The intended sense is two separate facts run together
with no comma between them: he did not move, and he held that position
through the whole drop in temperature. A reader hits "without moving
through" as a phrase, then has to re-read once "that arrives after
midnight" makes the literal reading collapse. It is the same shape as a
dangling modifier's cousin, a garden path built out of missing punctuation
rather than a misplaced phrase, but the cost to the reader is the same
stop-and-reparse.

**Why it was not auto changed:** This is the major's dialogue, delivered as
one continuous speech, and house rule 1's dialogue protection (never rewrite
what a character says) covers it regardless of how the sentence would score
on any other measure. It is reported per this pass's brief, which asks for
exactly this: a wrong sentence a house rule keeps me from touching.

**Active:** "which is seven hours without moving, through the entire drop in
temperature that arrives after midnight" - the minimum fix, a single comma,
every word kept.

**Active:** "which is seven hours holding still through the entire drop in
temperature that arrives after midnight" - recasts the negative ("without
moving") into a positive verb, so there is no longer a phrase that can be
misread as one unit in the first place.

**Passive:** "the position was held, without a move out of him, through the
entire drop in temperature that arrives after midnight" - technically
available, but a poor fit: it is more formal than anything else in the same
speech, and the sentence's actual problem is a missing comma, not a wrong
voice, so passive does not address the defect either way. Given because the
format asks for one, not as a recommendation.

## Also checked, left alone as deliberate, and why (extends a standing ruling)

Two constructions in the culvert sequence give an inanimate subject an
active verb with intent behind it, the shape rule 8's sibling brief calls
"the book's own recurring fault." `VOICE_25_26.md` already ruled on the third
member of this same family, "the culvert taught him to take it that way"
(line 73), calling it "ordinary English idiom for experience-as-teacher," not
the mechanical fake-agency fault. Re-reading the other two in that light:

- Line 41: "The culvert has him before he has read it properly..."
- Line 43: "...the concrete hands the whole of it back to him at once."

Both personify the terrain in the same register as the line already ruled
on, in the same scene, describing the same ambush: Sam experiencing the
culvert as an active, almost hostile force in the moment before he
understands that four men, not the concrete, are responsible. Read next to
the approved line 73, this looks like one deliberate device spanning the
whole sequence rather than three separate accidents, so I am extending the
existing ruling to cover lines 41 and 43 rather than treating them as new
faults. If the author disagrees with the line 73 ruling, these two should be
revisited alongside it as one decision, not separately.

Given the instruction that a call-out is still worth making when a genuinely
new reason exists, I note the new reason for the record even while agreeing
with the prior ruling: unlike line 73 (a lesson, an abstraction that can
plausibly "teach"), lines 41 and 43 give the concrete a verb of physical
action ("has," "hands... back") rather than a verb of instruction, which is
a slightly harder case for the idiom defense. Replacements in case the
author wants the harder two touched without touching line 73:

**Active:** "He is in under the lip before he has read the culvert properly,
out of sight for the thirty feet it takes to cross, and by the time he
understands that four men are already in there with him, the far end is
blocked and so is the end behind him." / "Somebody fires a blank two meters
from his head, and he gets the whole of it back off the concrete at once."

**Passive:** "He is caught by the culvert before he has read it properly..."
(keeps the personification but marks it honestly as figurative with "by,"
rather than handing the concrete an unmarked active verb) / "Somebody fires
a blank two meters from his head, and the whole of it is bounced back to him
off the concrete at once."

Also checked and left alone, same family, no call-out warranted: line 83,
"The rifle is coming round while his mind is still catching up to it." The
subject is Sam's own weapon in his own hands, and the construction expresses
a reflex outpacing conscious thought, the same device as "his hand shot out"
constructions generally use. This is a different kind of sentence from the
culvert pair: nothing is doing something *to* Sam here, so it is not a
receiver-erasing passive question and not really a fake-agency question
either. Left alone with no replacements offered, since I found nothing wrong
with it.
