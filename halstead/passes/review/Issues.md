Issues



---

### 1. The "Number + Physical Action" Sentence Formula (Severe Overuse)

The single biggest stylistic issue in the entire manuscript is a rigid sentence formula: **`[Character] + does [hyper-specific small physical action] + [exact number of seconds/times/inches] + [before/while/instead of] + [speaking/finishing].`**

Notice how many times sentences are constructed around exact counts of seconds, inches, or repetitions:

* *"She listens through two more sentences before the phone clicks over..."*
* *"Chloe shifts the clipboard half an inch..."*
* *"Chloe had held on to his sleeve a second longer than the door needed..."*
* *"For three full steps she is somewhere else entirely..."*
* *"He waits through a silence he clearly thinks she should be filling..."*
* *"He asks him why the retry ceiling stayed where it was... and is onto the next thing inside a minute."*
* *"Theo finishes his twenty minutes and goes back in..."*

**Why it’s an issue:** Used once or twice, it creates a grounded, observant, Hemingway-esque rhythm. Used fifty times across 36 chapters, it turns into an artificial tick. It makes every character feel like they are moving inside a metronome rather than living naturally.

---

### 2. Syntactical Monotony: The "Comma Splice + Run-on Polysyndeton" Rhythm

Almost every paragraph relies on the exact same sentence architecture: compound sentences linked by strings of `and`, `, and`, `, so that`, and `, which`:

> *"Sam eats standing at the end of the table because his chair is Ruth's now, the result of an argument that started over a spoon in the second week and that neither of them remembers well enough to settle."*

> *"The second prompt asks about a place that changed her, and Chloe cannot get the forge down to six hundred words either, so she writes about the reservoir instead, nine miles from her parents' house, and the hill she has to stand up on the pedals to clear."*

* **The Problem:** The clauses endlessly cascade into one another. There is almost zero variation in sentence cadence—very few punchy fragments, very few inverted clauses, very few rhythmic interruptions. Everything flows at the exact same rolling, mid-tempo drone.

---

### 3. Chronic Physical Restraint Tics ("Hands Flat", "Turning Objects")

When the writer wants to show a character thinking, listening, or holding back emotion, they rely on a tiny grab-bag of 3–4 physical gestures repeated for *almost every character*:

| Overused Gesture | Examples in Text |
| --- | --- |
| **"Hand pressed flat against X"** | Mom's hand flat on forehead; mom's hand flat on counter; Chloe's hand flat on desk; Chloe's hand flat on the wall; Dr. Ammons' hand flat; Nadia's hands flat on the ledger; Kavi's hands flat on the bench. |
| **"Turning an object in fingers"** | Kavi turning a data cable over and over; Kavi turning a pen; Ruth turning a coffee cup a quarter turn; Sam turning a card in his hand; Chloe turning a glass in a wet ring on the porch. |
| **"Ears going hot / jaw setting"** | Chloe's ears hot; Chloe's jaw tight; Chloe pressing thumbnail into desk; intruder's jaw working. |
| **"Heels swinging / feet off floor"** | Chloe's feet dangling at 6; Chloe's feet dangling at the doctor; Chloe's feet dangling at Halstead; Chloe's feet dangling at graduation. |

* **Why it’s an issue:** It flattens the physical diversity of the characters. Whether it's Meg, Chloe, Kavi, Ruth, or a federal investigator, *everyone* puts their hands flat on tables and turns glasses a quarter-turn.

---

### 4. Dialogue Tics: The "X is a different category from Y" Construction

The characters constantly speak using an identical intellectual formula: **`"X is a [noun], and Y is a completely different [noun], and [explaining why the category doesn't apply]."`**

Look at how many different characters speak in this exact rhetorical frame:

* **The Librarian:** *"Then you'd have four... four at a time is a rule, not six."*
* **Sam:** *"That's not lying, that's just being stuck."*
* **Ruth:** *"A square folds, but a triangle can't fold unless something actually breaks."*
* **Marek:** *"A receipt is a different object from work."*
* **Hearn:** *"Ten pages is easy, ten pages is just hours."*
* **Chloe (to her Dad):** *"A hinge is a fact about bread, not a fact about food."*
* **Nadia:** *"That's not an argument, that's an outcome."*

**Why it’s an issue:** This is the primary reason your readers thought all the characters "sound the same." It is not that they are all smart; it is that they all use the **exact same rhetorical sentence structure** to win arguments.

---

### 5. Summary Dumping vs. Scene dramatization (Telling the Aftermath)

The text frequently skips the most dramatically interesting scenes and instead delivers them as a **retrospective summary over a meal or in a chat log**:

* **The 4:00 AM federal breach:** Told in clipped summaries while the kids casually sit on the floor.
* **The Waffle House mugging:** Summarized through dialogue and police report summaries rather than experienced in real-time visceral sensory detail.
* **Priya's 75-man ambush:** Delivered as chat log messages *after* it already happened.

While this works intentionally as a framing device (emphasizing how the kids downplay their own insanity), doing it *every single time* robs the reader of immediate tension and makes the story feel like an extended debrief rather than a lived experience.

---

### 6. The "Clock / Time" Obsession

Almost every single paragraph contains an explicit time measurement:

* *"inside four seconds"*
* *"for eleven minutes, which she counted"*
* *"in about nine minutes"*
* *"with ninety seconds to spare"*
* *"eight minutes early"*
* *"twenty-seven days"*
* *"at eleven forty on the Thursday"*

The writer uses time to create the illusion of precision and hyper-competence. But when every single action in every chapter is timestamped down to the minute or second, it stops being realistic hyper-awareness and becomes an obvious authorial habit.

---

### Summary of What to Fix for the New Book:

1. **Break the cadence:** Mix short, blunt, 3-word sentences with longer descriptive paragraphs. Kill the endless `, and so that` compound chains.
2. **Diversify physical habits:** Ban "hands flat on the counter" and "turning a cup a quarter-turn." Give characters unique somatic nervous habits (biting nails, tugging earlobes, pacing, slumping, cracking knuckles, picking lint).
3. **Diversify argumentative styles:** Not everyone should argue via philosophical categorization ("X is a fact about A, not B"). Let some characters argue through emotional pressure, blunt refusal, humorous ridicule, or passive-aggressive silence.
4. **Show real-time scenes:** Don't always deliver the climax as a chat log or a dinner debrief. Let the reader feel the sensory dirt, blood, adrenaline, and fear in the moment.





# PROSE ANALYSIS: ISSUES, PATTERNS, AND OPPORTUNITIES

---

## SECTION 1: OVERALL IMPRESSION

### What the Book Does Well

The prose has a distinctive, almost hypnotic quality. It's clean, understated, and moves with purpose. The author knows what they're trying to achieve: a sense of quiet observation, of watching someone live their life from a slight distance. The book is never boring, never confusing, never pretentious.

**But it's also uneven.** There are patterns that repeat too often, habits that become noticeable, and a voice that sometimes feels like it's holding the reader at arm's length.

---

## SECTION 2: SPECIFIC PROSE ISSUES

---

### ISSUE 1: The "Understatement Trap"

**The Problem:**

The book consistently understates emotional moments, which works for a while—and then becomes exhausting. Everything is described in the same flat, observational tone, whether Chloe is happy, sad, terrified, or furious.

**Examples:**

- Her offer to give up her birthday (a genuinely devastating emotional moment) is described as: "She says she'll be good, and that she'll do the dishes every single night for the rest of her life. She'll do her dad's jobs as well, all of them, before anybody has to ask her. They can take the bike back..."
- The tone doesn't shift to match the emotional weight. It's the same flat cadence used to describe a math worksheet.
- When Chloe screams with joy about going to Halstead: "She screams, at a volume that surprises all three of them, and then she is up off the floor and onto her dad..." The narrative pulls back immediately. We don't feel her joy—we're told about it.

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Reader fatigue:** After a while, everything starts to feel the same. The emotional high points and the low points are described in the same tone.
- **Emotional distance:** The reader is kept at arm's length. We observe Chloe's emotions rather than feeling them.
- **Lost opportunities:** The most powerful moments in the book are undercut by the refusal to let the prose match the emotion.

**What to Do About It:**

- Let the prose get messy when emotions are messy. Use shorter sentences. Fragment sentences. Break the pattern.
- Match the cadence to the content. Joy should feel faster. Sorrow should feel slower. Anger should feel sharper.
- Use the actual scene, don't add summaries when it gets emotional, say "She screams "AHHHH", so loud that her parents wince, and she is jumping onto her dad..."

---

### ISSUE 2: The "Negative Space" Overuse

**The Problem:**

The book relies heavily on what characters *don't* say or do to convey emotion. This is effective in small doses—and maddening in large ones.

**Examples:**

- Ruth's six-month silence is described through what she doesn't post in the chat.
- Chloe's depression is shown through what she stops doing (reading, eating, talking).
- Priya's fear is shown through what she doesn't say.
- The whole book is structured around the negative space.

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **It's exhausting:** The reader has to constantly infer emotions. There's no release.
- **It's ambiguous:** The reader doesn't know if they're reading correctly. Is Ruth withdrawn because she's scared, or just busy? The book doesn't give enough clues. You can have ambiguity, don't spoon feed readers. But the clues should technically be in there. You can even trick readers, here Ruth's silence could be used, was she kidnapped? Does she not like the group anymore? Is it just natural drift? Then you read Ruth's chapter and find out why. 
- **It's passive:** The characters are defined by absence rather than presence. We learn more about what they don't do than what they do.

**What to Do About It:**

- Balance negative space with positive expression. Let characters say how they feel sometimes.
- Give clearer emotional cues. If Ruth is scared, let her show fear in a small, but unmistakable way—a tremor, a hesitation, a moment of vulnerability.
- Let characters tell each other how they feel. The book is allergic to emotional dialogue, but real people talk about their feelings (especially to the people they trust most).

---

### ISSUE 3: The Flat Dialogue Problem

**The Problem:**

The dialogue is functional but rarely memorable. Characters speak in the same cadence, the same level of formality, and with the same lack of personality. They sound like they're all reading from the same script. This is often 1 sentence as well.

**Examples:**

- Chloe: "Okay, but I already finished the whole book on Tuesday, so waiting for everybody else to get to chapter four just feels like the whole week standing still."
- Sam: "I know, Chloe. Fun's one thing and wanting to be home is a different thing, and he wanted to be home."
- Ruth: "It's a different thing, Sam, and you know perfectly well that it is."

**The problem:** These lines could come from any of the characters. There's no distinction in their voices. Sam should sound different from Ruth. Chloe should sound different from Kavi. But they all speak in the same polished, slightly formal cadence.

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Character confusion:** Without distinct voices, characters blend together.
- **Loss of personality:** We learn about characters through what they do, not what they say. Dialogue should reveal personality, not just advance the plot.
- **Immersion break:** Real people don't talk like this. Real teenagers interrupt, use slang, stumble over words, and express themselves imperfectly.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Give characters verbal tics.** Chloe hesitates and starts sentences multiple times. Sam interrupts and laughs at his own jokes. Ruth uses precise, declarative sentences. Kavi says very little.
- **Use contractions.** The dialogue is too formal. Let characters say "gonna" and "wanna" and "dunno."
- **Let characters talk past each other.** Real conversations are messy. People misunderstand each other. This is used a few times well, like when Chloe and her grandmother misunderstand each other about archery targets.
- **Use subtext.** Characters should sometimes say the opposite of what they mean. The negative space should be in dialogue, not just action, and not mentioned. 

---

### ISSUE 4: The "List" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book loves lists. Chloe lists everything she learned at camp. The author lists everything Chloe did in her classes. The characters list their accomplishments, their fears, their observations.

**Examples:**

- "The first is that she reads constantly, four books a week since she was five, and there's a stack of them by her bed she hasn't touched since August..."
- "Then there's the swimming, where she can do a whole length now, badly... Then dividing fractions... Then Spanish... Then the signing... Then the puzzle... Then the wood shop... Then the bleed... Then the eggs... Then the paintings... Then the computers... Then the robot arm... Then chess... Then negative numbers... Then karate... Then the nerf guns."

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Information overload:** The reader can't process all the information. It becomes white noise.
- **Repetition:** The same structure appears over and over. It feels like a formula.
- **Lost emotion:** The lists are emotionally flat. They tell us what happened but not how it felt.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Pick the best examples.** Instead of listing everything Chloe learned, pick two or three things and really describe them.
- **Use variety.** Some lists are fine. But not every list. Mix up the structure.
- **Show, don't tell.** Instead of listing Chloe's accomplishments, show her doing something impressive.

**Times it's good:**
- When you are purposely trying to overload the reader, or cause them to skim. The summer camp with a list is useful, to make readers miss how much she did. In the school chapters there are some lists that hide how much she covered that year. Don't remove all lists.

---

### ISSUE 5: The "Too Clean" Problem

**The Problem:**

The prose is too polished. Every sentence is correct. Every paragraph is structured. Nothing is messy. Nothing is surprising.

**Examples:**

- "Chloe turns a page she stopped reading two sentences ago, her thumb keeping a place she has already abandoned."
- "The question has already happened somewhere behind her, while she was out in the parking lot with the coffee cup."

**The problem:** These sentences are beautiful—but they're also predictable. The author has a rhythm, and they stick to it. Every sentence has the same careful balance, the same precise observation, the same understated elegance.

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Predictability:** The reader knows what to expect. The prose becomes invisible in a bad way—it fades into the background.
- **Lack of risk:** The author never takes chances with their prose. They never let the writing get ugly or raw.
- **Emotional distance:** Clean prose creates emotional distance. It says "I'm in control of this narrative" rather than "this narrative is happening to me."

**What to Do About It:**

- **Write some ugly sentences.** Let the prose get rough sometimes. Let it be imperfect. Let it be messy.
- **Take risks.** Use unexpected words. Use unconventional structures. Surprise the reader.
- **Let the prose match the emotion.** If the scene is chaotic, let the sentences be chaotic.

---

### ISSUE 6: The "Invisible Verbs" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book relies heavily on neutral, invisible verbs: "is," "has," "says," "does." The action is described in the most basic way possible.

**Examples:**

- "Chloe sits on the living room floor with a book open in front of her."
- "She writes the answer to problem three and then sits with the pencil motionless."
- "He reads it one-handed, still eating, then puts the fork down to hold it with both."

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Lost texture:** The prose lacks tactile detail. We don't feel the action—we observe it.
- **Passive reading:** The reader is watching from a distance rather than experiencing the scene.
- **Repetition:** The same verbs appear over and over.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Use more specific verbs.** Not "sits" but "perches." Not "walks" but "strides." Not "says" but "whispers," "snaps," or "mutters."
- **Add sensory details.** What does the floor feel like? What does the pencil sound like? What does the room smell like?
- **Vary your sentence structure.** Not every sentence needs to be "subject + verb + object."

**Careful:**
- Don't go overboard with this, it should be <10% used, and used with care. Replacing every invisible verb, just makes your texture invisible. 
- **Passivity:** Sometimes you should keep the invisible verbs, to keep the user passive. The book is heavily reliant on controlling readers perspective. Keep that.

---

### ISSUE 7: The "Telling Not Showing" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book often tells the reader how characters feel rather than showing it through action.

**Examples:**

- "She talks through the clearing of the plates and is still going when her mom says okay, upstairs, teeth, so that bedtime takes forty minutes."
- "She works out the corner of her mom's mouth drops and stays down."
- "He says it without any particular feeling about it."

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Reader engagement:** When the author tells us how a character feels, we don't have to figure it out ourselves. We're passive recipients of information.
- **Emotional connection:** Showing emotions through action makes us feel them. Telling us about emotions keeps us at arm's length.
- **Lost opportunities:** The book has many moments where the author could show us something powerful, but instead tells us about it.
- **Spoon Feeding:** When readers are spoon fed, it makes them feel like idiots, and like you don't respect them. It is equivalent to saying, I don't trust you can see this. 

**What to Do About It:**

- **Show emotions through action.** Don't tell us a character is angry—show them slamming a door, clenching their fists, or going silent.
- **Show emotions through dialogue.** Don't tell us a character is sad—have them say something sad.
- **Show emotions through internal state.** Don't tell us a character is afraid—describe their racing heart, their cold sweat, their shaking hands.

---

### ISSUE 8: The "Same Beat" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book has a predictable rhythm. Character does something. Character observes something. Character has a quiet realization. This pattern repeats over and over.

**Examples:**

- Chloe does a worksheet. She spaces out. She's called on. She doesn't know the answer.
- Chloe talks at dinner. Her parents don't understand. She explains. They still don't understand.
- Chloe does something impressive. Someone says "you talk weird." She goes quiet.

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Predictability:** The reader knows what's going to happen before it happens.
- **Stagnation:** The characters seem stuck in the same patterns.
- **Lost tension:** Without variation, there's no surprise. The prose becomes monotonous.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Vary the structure.** Sometimes let the character win. Sometimes let them explode. Sometimes let them give up.
- **Break the pattern.** Have the character make a decision that surprises the reader.
- **Introduce new elements.** Bring in new characters, new conflicts, new stakes.
- **Warning.** Don't replace every instance, it's only predictable, because it is done so much. Replacing all instances makes the new method the predictable rhythm.

---

### ISSUE 9: The "Overly Intellectual" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book presents even mundane moments through an intellectual lens. Everything is analyzed, observed, and filtered through the narrator's precise, analytical mind.

**Examples:**

- "She works out which part was the wrong part, because it is either the Icarus or the saying it a second time louder."
- "She turns that one over while he waits, because the real answer has two halves and the question was built to hold one."
- "She has a very clear idea of what is supposed to happen to a child who does what Ruth just did, so she watches for it the whole rest of the period."

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Loss of spontaneity:** The characters never just *experience* anything. They're always analyzing it.
- **Loss of emotional immediacy:** The characters are observing their own emotions rather than feeling them.
- **Unrealistic narration:** Real children don't think this way. Even smart children. Chloe is six years old in these passages. A six-year-old doesn't analyze their own social failures in this way.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Match the narration to the character's age.** Chloe is six. Let her think like a six-year-old.
- **Let characters just experience things.** Not everything needs to be analyzed.
- **Save the intellectual observation for moments when it's earned.** When Chloe is analyzing a complex problem, that's earned. When she's analyzing her own sadness, it's not.

---

### ISSUE 10: The "Lack of Sensory Detail" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book is surprisingly sparse on sensory details. We know what happens, but we rarely know what it *feels* like.

**Examples:**

- What does Halstead smell like? What does it sound like? What does it feel like to walk down the hall?
- What does Chloe's room look like? What does her desk feel like? What does her bed feel like?
- What does the food taste like? What does the water feel like in the pool?

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Loss of immersion:** Without sensory details, the reader can't fully inhabit the world.
- **Loss of texture:** The book feels thin and abstract.
- **Loss of character connection:** We don't feel what the characters feel because we don't experience their world through their senses.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Add sensory details.** Describe how things look, sound, smell, feel, and taste.
- **Use sensory details to reveal character.** How Chloe perceives the world tells us about her personality.
- **Use sensory details to set mood.** A cold, dark room feels different from a warm, bright one.
- **10 chapters** You have 10+ chapters of them at the school, and we have no idea what the school looks like. Add in descriptions through all the chapters, in different rooms, floors, different senses. Don't flood the details in the 1st chapter. The 1st chapters should have the most when Chloe arrives, but don't overwhelm the reader. 

---

### ISSUE 11: The "Repetitive Sentence Structure" Problem

**The Problem:**

The book uses the same sentence structure repeatedly. Subject + verb + object. Subject + verb + object. This creates a hypnotic rhythm—but also a monotonous one.

**Examples:**

- "Chloe turns a page she stopped reading two sentences ago, her thumb keeping a place she has already abandoned."
- "She writes the answer to problem three and then sits with the pencil motionless, the rest of it being nothing but copying twenty-seven more times while the classroom clock holds at eleven-forty."
- "He listens through two more sentences before the phone clicks over to her mom's other ear."

**Why It's a Problem:**

- **Predictability:** The reader knows what's coming next.
- **Loss of excitement:** The prose doesn't thrill or surprise.
- **Loss of variety:** The sentences all sound the same.

**What to Do About It:**

- **Vary sentence length.** Mix short, punchy sentences with long, flowing ones.
- **Vary sentence structure.** Use fragments. Use inversions. Use questions. Use exclamations.
- **Vary sentence openings.** Not every sentence needs to start with "Chloe" or "She."

---

## SECTION 3: OPPORTUNITIES FOR IMPROVEMENT

### Opportunity 1: Let the Characters Have Distinct Voices

**The Book:** All characters sound the same.

**The Fix:** Give each character verbal tics, speech patterns, and vocabulary that reflect their personality.

**Example:**
- **Chloe:** "Okay, but wait—no, I mean, what if—okay, so, basically..."
- **Ruth:** "That's wrong. Here's why."
- **Sam:** "Dude, that's hilarious. No, actually, wait—"
- **Kavi:** "It's fine. I fixed it."
- **Priya:** "Oh my God, okay, so, listen—"

---

### Opportunity 2: Let the Characters Have More Emotions

**The Book:** Emotions are often told rather than shown. Characters are often described as "quiet" or "calm" rather than experiencing visceral emotions.

**The Fix:** Let the characters feel things deeply and show it.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "She turns her face toward the wall."
- Try: "Her chest tightens. She can't breathe. She presses her forehead against the cold wall and squeezes her eyes shut."

---

### Opportunity 3: Let the Dialogue Be Messier

**The Book:** Dialogue is clean, polished, and often unrealistic.

**The Fix:** Let characters interrupt each other, stumble over words, say the wrong thing, and talk past each other.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "I don't know," she says.
- Try: "I don't—" she stops. "I mean, I don't know. I just. I don't know."

---

### Opportunity 4: Let the Prose Match the Emotion

**The Book:** The prose is consistently clean and understated, even during emotional moments.

**The Fix:** Match the prose to the emotional content. When characters are scared, the sentences should be shorter and more fragmented. When they're joyful, the sentences should be faster and more expansive.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "She screams, at a volume that surprises all three of them."
- Try: "She screams. The sound rips out of her. She can't stop it. She doesn't want to. She's on her feet, and then she's on her dad, and she's sobbing into his shoulder, and she can't get the words out fast enough."

---

### Opportunity 5: Add More Sensory Details

**The Book:** The world is described functionally but rarely sensorially.

**The Fix:** Add details about how things look, sound, smell, feel, and taste.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "The classroom clock holds at eleven-forty."
- Try: "The clock ticks, it's eleven-forty and the hands haven't moved in years. The room smells like pencil shavings and floor wax. Chloe can hear the radiator breathing."

---

### Opportunity 6: Vary the Rhythm

**The Book:** The prose has a steady, hypnotic rhythm that rarely varies.

**The Fix:** Vary sentence length and structure. Use short, punchy sentences for tension. Use long, flowing sentences for description. Use fragments for emotion.

**Example:**
- Instead of: "She sits on the living room floor with a book open in front of her, listening instead of reading."
- Try: "The book is open. The words are there. She can't see them. Her mother's voice is coming through the wall, and Chloe is on the floor, and the carpet is rough under her knees, and she's trying to hear every word."

## SECTION 5: FINAL SUMMARY

### What the Book Does Well

- **Clean, readable prose:** The book is never confusing or pretentious.
- **Strong character arcs:** The characters change and grow over time.
- **Thematic depth:** The book grapples with big ideas about intelligence, isolation, and connection.
- **Emotional honesty:** The book never manipulates the reader's emotions.

### What the Book Could Improve

- **Distinct character voices:** The characters all sound the same.
- **Emotional expression:** The book relies too heavily on negative space and understatement.
- **Sensory detail:** The book could be more immersive and visceral.
- **Sentence variety:** The prose could be more dynamic and varied.
- **Dialogue:** The dialogue could be more realistic and distinctive.
- **Risk-taking:** The author could take more risks with the prose.

### The Bottom Line

The book is good. It could be great. The bones are strong: interesting characters, compelling themes, clear structure. But the prose often undercuts the emotional power of the story by being too clean, too controlled, and too distant. The author is afraid of making a mess—but sometimes, the story needs a mess.

**The fix:** Let the characters speak in their own voices. Let the prose get ugly when the emotions are ugly. Let the dialogue be messy. Let the sensory details flood the page. Let the author take risks.

**The book doesn't need to be rewritten—it needs to be loosened.** The prose needs to breathe. The characters need to be allowed to be human. The author needs to trust that the reader can handle the mess.





# Prose Issues in the Text

## 1. The Sentence Structure Monotony

The dominant sentence shape is **Subject + Verb + Object + comma + conjunction + Subject + Verb + Object**. This repeats hundreds of times. The rhythm becomes hypnotic in a bad way—not the good kind of hypnotic, but the kind where the reader's eye starts sliding because every sentence has the same cadence.

The text rarely uses:
- Fragments for impact
- Inverted syntax
- Sentences that start with a prepositional phrase or dependent clause
- Single-word sentences
- Sentences that begin with dialogue

Almost every paragraph follows the same shape: *She did X. Then she did Y. Then Z happened, which meant W.* The "then" and "and" become load-bearing walls that the prose leans on instead of finding more interesting transitions.

## 2. The "And Then" Chain

A huge number of sentences are actually two or three sentences welded together with "and." The text treats "and" as a universal connector. This flattens the hierarchy of information. When everything is connected with "and," nothing feels more important than anything else. The big moment gets the same grammatical weight as the small moment.

The text would benefit enormously from breaking these apart. Let a short sentence hit hard. Let a long sentence breathe. Right now, everything is medium-length and connected, which creates a flat, even rhythm that doesn't serve the emotional content.

## 3. The "Because" Explanation Loop

Characters constantly explain their own reasoning out loud, in real time, to other characters who didn't ask. This reads as expository rather than natural. Real people don't narrate their own thought process while they're having it. They act, and the reasoning is implicit.

This is especially noticeable in dialogue. Characters will state a position, then immediately explain why they hold that position, then explain why the alternative doesn't work, all in one speech. Real conversation is messier. People interrupt. People say the wrong thing first. People circle back.

## 4. Over-Explanation / Hand-Holding the Reader

The text frequently explains what the reader has already figured out. If a scene shows Chloe finishing a worksheet in two minutes and then sitting for thirty-eight minutes, the text then *tells* us she's bored. The showing was enough. The telling undercuts it.

This extends to emotional moments. When a character is clearly upset through their actions, the text often adds a sentence explaining that they are upset, or explaining *why* they are upset. The reader doesn't need this. Trust the reader.


## 5. Physical Grounding Becomes Repetitive

The text relies heavily on the same physical gestures to ground emotion:
- Hands pressed flat on surfaces
- Turning objects over and setting them back down
- Eyes going to a specific spot instead of a person
- Pressing a thumbnail into something
- Setting something down and picking it back up

These are good instincts. But they repeat so often that they stop functioning as grounding and start functioning as tics. By the fifth time a character presses both hands flat on a table, the gesture has lost its power. By the twentieth time, it's wallpaper.

The text needs more variety in physical grounding. What does the character do with their *feet*? Their *breathing*? Their *jaw*? What's happening in their *stomach*? The text lives almost entirely in the hands and eyes.

## 7. Dialogue Attribution

The text avoids "said" tags, which is good. But it replaces them with a very limited set of action beats that repeat across characters. Characters "set down" things, "keep their eyes on" things, "look at" things, "turn" things. The attribution beats don't vary enough between characters to help distinguish them.

Also, many exchanges have no attribution at all, which works in short bursts but becomes confusing in longer conversations with more than two speakers. The text sometimes has four or five characters talking with no clear indication of who's speaking.

## 8. The "Which" Clause Problem

Many sentences contain long "which" clauses that could be their own sentences. This creates sentences that run three or four lines and force the reader to hold the beginning in memory while processing the end. The prose would be sharper if these were broken up.

## 9. Children Speaking Too Well

The seven-year-olds and eight-year-olds speak in complete, grammatically complex sentences with subordinate clauses and precise vocabulary. Real children this age speak in fragments, repeat themselves, lose their train of thought mid-sentence, use the wrong word and correct themselves, trail off, start over.

The text gives children adult syntax. This is especially noticeable in the early chapters. A seven-year-old doesn't say "That's not a race, that's just being stuck, because stuck is when you can't move." A seven-year-old says "That's not— it's not stuck. Stuck is when you can't move. It CAN move."

## 10. The "Same" Problem

The word "same" appears with extraordinary frequency. "The same way," "the same voice," "the same thing," "the same question," "the same answer." This is a crutch word that the text uses to signal repetition or consistency, but it becomes so frequent that it loses meaning. Often the "same" can simply be cut.

## 11. Lack of Sensory Variety

The text is overwhelmingly visual and tactile. There's very little smell, taste, sound (beyond dialogue), temperature, or proprioception. The reader rarely knows what a room *smells* like, what the air *tastes* like, what the temperature is doing to the character's skin. The text lives in the eyes and the hands and needs the rest of the body.


## 13. The Emotional Beats Are Too Evenly Spaced

The text tends to place emotional beats at regular intervals, which creates a predictable rhythm. The reader starts to expect that every three or four paragraphs, there will be a small emotional moment. Real life doesn't work this way. Sometimes nothing happens for a long time. Sometimes three emotional things happen in one sentence. The text would benefit from more irregular pacing.

## 14. The "She/He [Verb]" Opening

A very high percentage of sentences begin with the subject pronoun followed by a verb. "She reads." "He sets down." "She turns." "He looks." This is the most basic sentence structure in English and it becomes monotonous when it's the default. Varying the sentence openings—starting with the object, with a prepositional phrase, with a participle, with the setting—would immediately improve the rhythm.

## 15. The Text Doesn't Trust Silence

The text fills every gap. Every pause in conversation is described. Every moment of quiet is narrated. Characters don't get to just *be* silent without the text telling us they're being silent. Sometimes a line break or a paragraph break is enough. The text doesn't use white space as a tool.

## 16. The "Though" and "Except" Crutch

The text uses "though" and "except" as connectors very frequently, often at the end of sentences. This creates a pattern where every statement is immediately qualified or undercut. "She did X, though she didn't mean to." "He said Y, except it wasn't quite right." This becomes predictable and flattens the impact of both the statement and the qualification.

## 17. Repetition of Specific Phrases

Certain phrases appear so often they become noticeable:
- "The whole of it"
- "The rest of it"
- "Both hands"
- "Eyes on"
- "Set it back down"
- "The same way"
- "For the rest of it"
- "The whole way through"
- "Before she/he could"
- "Which is the whole"

These aren't bad phrases individually. But they appear dozens of times each across the text, and the cumulative effect is that the prose starts to feel like it's drawing from a limited vocabulary of gestures and transitions.

## 18. The Text Tells You What to Feel

Occasionally, the text will describe a character's emotional state directly: "she was annoyed," "he was pleased," "it made her furious." These are the weakest moments in the prose. The text is at its best when it shows the emotion through action and lets the reader name it. When it tells you the emotion, it robs the reader of the experience of discovering it. It's fine to do occasionally, but it should remain rare. 

## 19. Scene Transitions Are Too Smooth

The text moves between scenes very cleanly, almost too cleanly. There's a tendency to end one scene and begin the next with a simple time marker ("In September," "The following week," "By March"). This works, but it also means the text rarely uses the transition itself as a tool. A hard cut. A jump in time that disorients. A callback to an earlier image. The transitions are functional but not interesting. If you were to listen to the book via audio book, there is no obvious transition, no good merging of scenes. Each scene is stand alone. 

## 20. The Prose Doesn't Change With the Character's Age

The sentence structure, vocabulary, and rhythm of the prose stay roughly the same from age 6 to age 21. But a six-year-old's experience of the world is fundamentally different from a twenty-one-year-old's. The prose could reflect this. Shorter, more sensory sentences for the young child. Longer, more complex sentences for the adult. The text doesn't modulate its voice to match the character's developmental stage.

---

## The Big Picture

The text's biggest strength is its **specificity**. It knows exactly what things look like, exactly how long things take, exactly what a character does with their hands while they're thinking. This is rare and valuable.

The text's biggest weakness is its **uniformity**. Every sentence is roughly the same length. Every moment gets roughly the same prose weight. Every character's physical grounding is roughly the same. Every scene transitions roughly the same way. The prose is *consistent* in a way that reads as *monotonous* rather than *controlled*. It looks like it is trying to be controlled, to hide the students skill level from the reader. 

The fix isn't to rewrite everything. The fix is to **vary**. Vary sentence length. Vary sentence openings. Vary the physical gestures. Vary the emotional register. Vary the pacing. Let the big moments be big. Let the small moments be small. Let the prose breathe in some places and compress in others. Right now, it's all one speed, one temperature, one gear. And that's the main thing that reads as novice rather than accomplished.


# PROSE NOTES — A REVISION CHECKLIST

*Ordered by how much damage each one does. The first four are the ones a reader feels even if they can't name them.*

---

## 1. THE HOUSE SENTENCE

Every character in this book, of every age, background and intelligence, builds dialogue the same way: **statement, comma, subordinate clause introduced by *because* / *so* / *which* / *since*.**

Six-year-old Chloe:
> "Okay, but is it a test, like a real one with a grade at the end, and can you fail it, because what happens if I get all of them wrong?"

Kayleigh Burns, an ordinary child on a playground:
> "That was different, because Maddie kind of scoops it instead of catching it flat, so it's basically a whole other move."

The town librarian:
> "One I can do, since that's a hold rather than a checkout, so it stays outside the four entirely."

Meg:
> "It's a number for how you did next to other kids your age, like if you're the tallest in your class, that's a high percentile for height."

Four people who share nothing, running one syntactic template. This is the single biggest cause of the "they all sound the same" and "they all sound like AI" notes, and it's not a characterization problem — it's a sentence-shape problem, and it's fixable mechanically.

**Fix:** take any page of dialogue and mark every *because*, *so*, *since*, *which*. If more than a third of the lines carry one, cut them until they don't. Most of those clauses can simply be deleted; the reason is usually obvious from context. Assign a few characters a rule: one never explains, one explains in a separate sentence, one explains before the claim instead of after.

---

## 2. NOBODY SPEAKS IN FRAGMENTS

Related, and worse. Almost every line in the manuscript is a complete, grammatical sentence with correct subordination. Real speech is broken: false starts, abandoned clauses, single words, repetitions, sentences that don't finish.

Nobody in this book says *"No — no, hang on."* Nobody says *"What?"* Nobody trails off. Nobody restarts. Nobody says *"I mean—"* and gets cut off.

The children are the acute case. A brilliant six-year-old has a brilliant six-year-old's *syntax*, which is to say short, additive, and full of *and then*. Complexity of thought does not produce complexity of clause structure at that age; it produces a lot of simple sentences in a row.

**Fix:** in the first nine chapters especially, break the syntax and keep the content. Chloe's argument about the four books can be exactly as clever with none of the subordination.

---

## 3. THE NUMBERS

This is the most visible tic in the manuscript and I don't think you can see it anymore.

**Eleven** appears constantly and in every context: eleven days, eleven minutes, eleven pounds, eleven students, eleven languages, eleven filings, eleven jobs, eleven schools, eleven laboratories, eleven seconds, eleven miles, eleven hundred dollars, eleven o'clock, eleven of the eleven. Same for **four** and **nine**, and to a lesser extent **four hundred**.

The problem isn't precision, it's that the *same three numbers* recur. Chloe counting is characterization. A librarian, an admissions officer, a drill sergeant, a bank manager and the narrator all dealing in elevens and nines is an authorial fingerprint, and once a reader notices it they can't stop.

There's a secondary version: **exact numbers hedged with "about."** *About four seconds, about a minute, about nine minutes, about half of it.* Approximation attached to specificity, hundreds of times, which reads as a verbal tic rather than as narrative voice.

**Fix:** search each of *eleven*, *nine*, *four*, *forty*, *four hundred* and count. Replace two-thirds of them with other numbers or with nothing. Where the exact figure isn't doing work, cut it — *he was gone six minutes* is usually better as *he was gone a few minutes*, and you save the precision for the places where it means something.

---

## 4. ANNOUNCED WITHHOLDING

The narrator repeatedly tells the reader that a character is not saying something.

> *which she keeps to herself* · *and Chloe keeps that to herself too* · *with a burn on her wrist she keeps to herself* · *and he keeps it to himself* · *keeps that to himself* · *which she keeps off every form* · *and Kavi had kept it to herself*

This is on nearly every other page. An announced silence isn't subtext, it's text describing the location where subtext would be. It also flattens the characters, because *all ten of them* are described as concealing things in identical language.

The book does this beautifully when it trusts itself — Meg sitting in the dark car after the April visit, never opening her door, with no commentary — and then two pages later explains what somebody isn't saying.

**Fix:** cut two-thirds of these outright. The withholding still happens; the reader just has to notice it, which is the point.

---

## 5. "THE WAY YOU WOULD" SIMILES

> *the way you would report the weather* · *the way you repeat a number to check it the second time* · *the way you look at somebody before you decide whether to believe them* · *the way a form gets read* · *the way scouts do on a prospect* · *the way you buy a lottery ticket and forget you own it*

This is your signature construction and it's genuinely good, which is why it's in the manuscript sixty-odd times. At that density it stops being an observation and becomes a verbal habit, and worse, it's the narrator's habit applied uniformly to every character, so it homogenizes them.

**Fix:** keep the best fifteen. Cut the rest. Prioritize keeping the ones that do characterization work over the ones that do texture work.

---

## 6. "FLAT"

> *both hands flat on the desk* · *hand flat on the counter* · *a hand flat on her back* · *hands flat and still at her sides* · *the flat voice* · *says it flat* · *a voice gone flat* · *a flat wet crack* · *flat and grey* · *lays it flat*

The word is doing four different jobs and is on nearly every page. **Hands flat on surfaces** in particular is the book's universal gesture — every character does it, in every emotional state, for eleven years.

**Fix:** hands-flat belongs to one character. Everyone else needs their own. Ruth knocks things over. Kavi realigns objects. Nadia pushes her sleeves up. Priya reties her hair. Eli drums two fingers. Theo squares paper. If two characters share a gesture, one of them loses it.

Same treatment for **jaw** (set, tight, working) and **the corner of her mouth**.

---

## 7. THE EAVESDROP

Structural rather than sentence-level, but it's the most-repeated device in the book: **Chloe overhears adults.** On the stairs. Through a door left ajar. Through the thin part of the floor. In the back of a car with her eyes shut. In a corridor. From the top of the stairs, at the fourth step. Through a mostly-shut kitchen door.

I count somewhere north of fourteen instances, and it's the primary mechanism for delivering nearly every structural fact in the first half.

It's a legitimate device — she's a child, so fragments are all she can get — but at this frequency it stops being clever and becomes the only tool in the box. It also makes the parents feel like an information delivery system rather than people.

**Fix:** convert a third of them. Some facts she should be told directly. Some she should misread. Some she should find as an object — a form, an envelope, a note on a fridge — rather than overhear. One or two should be things she was *meant* to hear.

---

## 8. THE SCENE-ENDING PHYSICAL BEAT

Almost every scene closes on a small physical action: a napkin folded into quarters, a mug put in the sink, a pen set down, a page turned, a laptop closed with both hands, a stamp put down, a folder squared against a desk edge.

It's an elegant move. It's used roughly two hundred times, and the rhythm becomes predictable enough that a reader starts anticipating the last line of every section.

**Fix:** vary the exits. End some scenes on dialogue. End some mid-action. End one or two abruptly, on a line that doesn't land.

---

## 9. "THAT'S NOT X, THAT'S Y"

> "That's not lying, that's just being stuck." · "That's not an answer, that's the same sentence said louder." · "That's not a number, that's a phrase somebody reaches for." · "It's not one second, Chloe." · "That's not detention. That's home." · "That's not what lying is."

It's in every character's mouth. It's an efficient rhetorical move, occurring perhaps eighty times in your own text.

**Fix:** keep it where the correction is the actual point. Elsewhere, state the positive claim and let the negation be implied.

---

## 10. "AND THAT'S THE WHOLE OF IT"

The rhetorical closer, in many variants:

> *that's the whole of it* · *that's the whole story* · *that's the whole answer* · *that's the entire point* · *that's the whole problem* · *and that's the whole reason* · *that's the whole trade*

Every character uses it. It's a tell for an author who wants to make sure the reader got the point.

**Fix:** cut most. If the sentence before it worked, the closer is redundant; if it didn't, the closer won't save it.

---

## 11. EYES-DOWN DIALOGUE

> *with her eyes on the cards rather than on Chloe* · *eyes on his plate* · *keeps her eyes on the book* · *not looking up from her own laptop* · *his eyes on his tray* · *eyes still on the page* · *with his eyes down*

The book's default gesture for "this character is speaking without full attention," and it's applied to everybody. It also means very few characters ever look directly at each other, which flattens the emotional temperature across the whole novel. It would also be treated as rude to most people. 

**Fix:** let people look at each other. Reserve eyes-down for two or three characters where it means something.

---

## 12. SENTENCES THAT WON'T END

The narration's default is a compound sentence with three or four clauses chained by *and*, *so*, *which*, *then*:

> "There are thirty subtraction problems on the worksheet and all thirty are the same problem, which she works out partway through the second, eight minus three, so the other twenty-eight are that again with different numbers."

Individually fine. As the default for 105,000 words it produces a monotone, and it's a large part of why readers describe the prose as flat or analytical. There is very little short-sentence punctuation in this book — almost no three-word sentences, almost no fragments in narration.

**Fix:** every page needs at least one short sentence. Two on emotional pages. The rhythm change is what makes the long ones land.

---

## 13. MECHANICAL CLEANUP

- **Escaped characters throughout the file**: `1961\.`, `78\.`, `number 10\.`, `room 4\.`, `\-stans`, `\!`, `\---`. Conversion artifacts from a word processor. Global find-and-replace.
- **Inconsistent scene breaks**: the manuscript uses `\---`, `---`, and `________________` interchangeably. Pick one.
- **Chapter headers**: some carry a date range in italics, some don't. Standardize.

---

## WHAT'S WORKING — SO THE ABOVE IS CALIBRATED

- **The child's-eye interpretation.** The coffee cup on the car roof; the NO CELL PHONES sign with the picture for people who can't read; the sticky note under the strawberry magnet. This is the best thing in the book and there's a lot of it.
- **Physical detail as emotion.** Priya turning her sleeve inside out twice looking for a hair tie. Fen finding a rock in the dark by weight. The lemonade grandfather. Meg turning the papers face down on her knees at the last light before their street.
- **Restraint at the payoffs.** Sam's overhang, the first arrow collision, the graduation — each gets a paragraph and then the next thing starts. That discipline is rare and it's why the book doesn't read as triumphalist.
- **The dialectic scenes.** The hot dog, the moon, the four books. These are genuinely funny and genuinely characterizing, and the argument-as-affection thing is a real observation about how clever people love each other.
