# First read: chapters 19–27

Read straight through, in order, as a reader with no other context would. This is
that reader's report.

## The five worst things, worst first

**1. Amberg's job offer is a single sentence a ninth grader would have to read three
times, and it is spoken by a school administrator, not a philosopher.**

> "Everybody on staff signed something close to this same sheet at some point, and
> every single person on that staff is still here, still doing the work, still
> getting the same review everyone else gets, and nobody has ever come back to this
> office asking for the terms to be any different, because the terms are the same
> terms for everybody who ever sat in that chair, and the day they stop being the
> same for everybody is the day this whole arrangement stops meaning anything at
> all, to you or to the next person who sits down in front of this folder after
> you, and this office would rather lose a candidate over an honest answer than
> keep a candidate on terms it isn't willing to give the next person as well."
> (Ch. 22)

That is one sentence, roughly 145 words, built entirely out of "and... and...
because... and... and." It is the exact thing the author complains about by
name: too eloquent, too literary, aimed at a reader who already has a philosophy
degree. It is also the least Amberg has sounded like himself in either chapter he
appears in — everywhere else he talks in short, clipped, mark-scheme sentences
("Question three." "Reason?"). Here he suddenly delivers a TED talk on
institutional fairness without taking a breath. This is the single worst
sentence in the span.

**2. The parking-lot fight has a visible seam where a trim happened and nobody
went back to close it.**

> Sam says, "Hang on." "Let me have this one."
>
> Ruth stays put. "They're adults." "Three guns." (Ch. 20)

Two quoted fragments back to back, no dialogue tag on the second half, no
"he added" or beat between them. Everywhere else in this exact scene, a change
of thought gets its own line or its own tag ("'Left,' Nadia says, flat and
quiet. 'I know,' Sam says"). This looks exactly like what happens when a longer
line gets cut down to hit a length or pace target and the join is never
re-punctuated. It sits in the chapter the brief itself flags as the most
protected in the book, in the exact beat (the decision to let Sam fight) that a
reader needs to be smooth and confident, and instead it reads like a printing
error.

**3. The "3% acceptance rate" exchange in Chapter 21 makes one point six times.**

> "Neither," Ruth says. "You'd think the sign was doing a job that had nothing to
> do with the store. It's written for the people out on the pavement, not for
> anybody who already walked in."
>
> "Ninety-one out of ninety-one bought something, and that's the number the sign
> should say. Nobody is ever going to put that in a window, because a window like
> that stops being an advertisement and starts being an accusation."
> ... "It's on their site," Ruth says, "and it still isn't a real number, because
> both of those can be true at once, the site and the lie on it. Somebody writes
> that sentence for a living and nobody anywhere has to check it." (Ch. 21)

Store sign, then advertisement-vs-accusation, then site-vs-lie-on-it: three
different metaphors, one idea (the statistic is a lie), stated with increasing
cleverness and decreasing return. It's also the most adult stretch of dialogue
in the book — this is two teenagers doing a graduate seminar on statistical
framing, not talking at the end of a hard week.

**4. Chloe's post-fight analysis curdles from "girl explaining what she saw" into
contempt for the muggers' worth as people, and lands on a slur.**

> "If you had one usable skill, any skill at all, you would make money doing
> literally anything other than this... People who fight like that, over
> somebody's phone, at two in the morning; that's retarded. You didn't fight
> seven adults, Sam. You beat up seven people who couldn't tell you their own
> address." (Ch. 20)

The first half (elbow locked, nobody drew, they stood bunched between two
cars) is exactly the "girl explaining what she saw" the brief asks about, and
it's good. Then it pivots into an economic verdict on the muggers as a class of
person too stupid and unskilled to deserve fear, uses "retarded" as a
throwaway put-down, and nobody in the scene, then or later, complicates it.
This is the house rule's "anybody looking down on anybody" problem in its
starkest form in the whole span, and it is put in the mouth of a sixteen-year-old
looking down on strangers she just finished beating unconscious.

**5. A biography gets inserted into the middle of the live two-hander with
Amberg.**

> "She's taught the twelves real analysis out of a room two floors below,
> working through the proofs handed to her at that age, by a teacher always
> making it sound like the ordinary next thing rather than a favor. She's
> dropped flawed hinges of her own into the scrap bin rather than let them
> stand, and gone back to the forge every Thursday for years without anyone
> having to ask her twice. At the start, the whole of this place came down to a
> letter with her name typed across the front of it, in an envelope she still
> has, in a drawer she still checks." (Ch. 22)

This is a straight paragraph of career summary dropped between Amberg's offer
and Chloe's "I don't think so," in a scene that has otherwise been a tight,
live two-person exchange with a clock on the desk. It is summary in the middle
of a live scene, textbook.

## Per chapter

**19 — Sixteen.** The Amberg mark-scheme scene is the best writing in the
span: concrete, specific to contract law, and it never once says what the
lesson "means" — it just keeps handing Chloe the blank rows. The driving
sequence and the porch-step conversation with her parents are good, quiet,
camera-only work ("the pipe rings when a thrown apple hits it" energy, minus
the apple). One thing that reads oddly on a first pass: the chapter is titled
"Sixteen" and dated April 2022 – June 2023, but if her eighteenth birthday is
August 2023 (stated flatly in Ch. 23), she turns seventeen partway through
this chapter's own span, in August 2022 — meaning most of the chapter (the
October retake, the whole of the "home in June" ending) happens while she is
seventeen, not sixteen. Worth checking against the rest of the age-titled
chapters, since the brief specifically flagged this chain as fragile.

**20 — The Parking Lot.** Mostly still works. The fence vault, the dock, the
fight choreography read clean and fast, and Ruth's "Twenty-two seconds" lands
exactly right — deadpan, in character, no adjective attached to it. The count-off
itself (elbow locked, nobody drew, bunched between two cars) is a girl reading
a room, which is what the brief asked for. It breaks in two places: the
tag-less double-quote seam (worst thing #2, above) and the pivot into "that's
retarded" (worst thing #4). Cut the second half of Chloe's speech at "or
they'd be doing something else with their evenings" and the scene is clean.

**21 — The Applications.** Yes, it is the most expository chapter in the
span, and the reviewers are right to flag it twice. The structural problem
isn't any one sentence, it's the shape: rep asks a question, gets a clipped
or mysterious answer, writes it in a notebook, cut to the next rep. That
happens six times in a row (Duke/Sport-Athletics-Recreation, Stanford, Yale,
MIT, Caltech at the table, the Chloe interview, Sam, Ruth). Any one of these
is a nice beat. Six back to back is a checklist of "look how impressive this
school is," dramatized rather than told, but still a list doing a list's job.
The essay-writing scene earlier in the chapter is much better and earns its
space; the visitor parade could lose two of its six stops with nothing lost.

**22 — The Offer.** The 135k speech and its repetition to the parents is the
chapter's real question. The repetition to her mother, then verbatim again
to her father ("say that whole thing again for me... from the start"), is
justified once, thinly, by "she keeps the parts in Amberg's order, because the
order is the only thing about it she can hand over intact" — a genuinely good
character beat. But by the time the reader hits the verbatim block a second
time, they have now read Amberg's near-identical original twice already
(once live, once summarized as "the list comes in the order he gives
everybody" for Nadia's interview a few pages earlier), so what should feel
like numb repetition instead feels like the manuscript being unable to let go
of its own paragraph. Trim it to the first two lines plus "— all of it,
same as before," and the beat still lands.

**23 — The First One.** Priya's Argentina speech is the best-defended thing on
the list of things the audit was worried about. It restates its own point once
("something already decided for me before I got there" / "somewhere that's
never heard of Halstead" is the same idea twice) but closes on a genuinely
sharp, self-undercutting beat — she already let the woman in Bariloche pick her
boots, so the whole "deciding for myself" speech is quietly a little bit of a
lie, and Priya knows it and says so ("Probably," cheerfully). That earns
itself. It is not padding. The grandmother receiving-line bit is a deliberate
list (three near-identical exchanges, "well, that's something") and works as
one. Weakest scene in the chapter is the recruiting-office paragraph for Sam,
which reads like a Wikipedia summary of enlistment paperwork rather than a
scene with a person in it.

**24 — The Chat.** The strongest chapter in the span for voice differentiation
— strip the names off "nadia: youre not annoyed about the memory" versus
"ruth: sam that is not even remotely what tidally locked means" and you can
still tell them apart, which is the actual test the brief sets. Nadia's
"internal. how" thread that goes nowhere (the pill ingredient the school won't
disclose) is a good, quiet piece of foreshadowing precisely because nobody
explains what it means. The one soft spot: "Aurel" appears once, in Iyad's
October email, with no other anchor in this stretch of chapters — a name
introduced and dropped in the same clause. It may be paid off earlier in the
book; inside chapters 19–27 alone it reads as a loose thread.

**25 — Forty Targets.** Good chapter, and the one place in the span where an
adult authority figure (the captain) does not simply fold. He listens, writes
four separate reports, and the chapter ends on real institutional consequence
rather than a clean win for the teenager — a rare and welcome exception to the
pattern below. Sam's three-stage self-diagnosis of why basic training feels
easy (tiredness, then arithmetic, then "this is a screen") is a little
long and analytical for a character established as instinctive rather than
theoretical, but it stays inside his own head rather than being handed to the
reader as narration, so it's a soft flag rather than a hard one.

**26 — The Exercise.** Structurally the strongest chapter in the span. Four
distinct engagements, four distinct lessons, and the AAR scene lets an adult
(the major) win the argument decisively and correctly ("difficulty was never
yours to set in the first place, Marsh"), which is exactly the kind of
correction this cast otherwise almost never receives. This is the chapter to
point to when arguing the book *can* let its people be wrong.

**27 — Nadia.** The scam-fraud material (the deleted accounts, the phone call
to the woman who was robbed) is the best-written material about Nadia in the
span — specific, unsentimental, and it never states what Nadia feels, only
what she does (stays in the dark room with the phone in her hand). The
tire-shop confrontation is well staged environmentally — the locked door, "the
shop underneath you is shut... nobody watched you come up" is genuinely
unsettling, a camera detail doing real work. But Nadia herself never cracks:
no shaking hand, no swallowed breath, no beat of hesitation anywhere in a scene
with four adult men and a locked door. "Hands loose at her sides" is the only
physical note given, and it's a note of composure, not fear. The men fold
completely and immediately once she names the filing weakness; nobody in the
room lands a single point against her. That reads more like a chess
demonstration than a frightening scene, and it is the sharpest example in the
whole span of the "nobody wins an argument they shouldn't win" problem: an
eighteen-year-old alone above a tire shop out-negotiates four adult men running
an identity-theft operation and leaves without a scratch or a single word she
regrets. On the age question specifically: the text is explicit — "Eighteen
since last June" — so she is eighteen, not nineteen, in May 2024, which is at
least internally consistent with a June 2023 birthday.

## What else

**Continuity/age chain.** See Ch. 19 note above (the "Sixteen" title covering
a span in which she is mostly seventeen). Everything else checked clean: the
"seventeen years old" line in Ch. 22 (April 2023) is consistent with an August
birthday; "turns eighteen in August" in Ch. 23 (June 2023) is consistent with
that; the company being "eleven months old" in Ch. 27 (May 2024) tracks
correctly back to the June 2023 founding described in Ch. 23. Nadia's "eighteen
since last June" in Ch. 27 also checks out.

**Dullest stretch.** The rep-parade in Chapter 21 (see above) — not any single
scene, but the fourth and fifth repetitions of the same shape in a row, by
which point the reader is skimming for the next joke rather than reading for
information.

**Hardest to believe.** That every adult authority figure this cast meets,
across nine chapters, either concedes the argument outright (Amberg to Chloe
and to Nadia, the bank officer, the men above the tire shop) or is played for a
punchline (the grandmother, the recruiter). The two exceptions — the captain in
Ch. 25 and the major in Ch. 26 — are both military, both hold their ground, and
both are the best adult characters in the span for it. The pattern makes the
military chapters feel like they belong to a different, better-supervised book.

**Everybody sounding the same.** Not a book-wide problem — Sam, Ruth, and
Kavi are reliably distinct even stripped of tags (see the Ch. 24 chat). The
place it breaks down is Chloe and Nadia in argument mode: both build an
analogy, then land a one-line verdict, in near-identical rhythm. Compare
Chloe's "If a store put a sign in its window saying only a few percent of the
people who walk in buy something, would you think the store was doing well, or
badly run?" (Ch. 21) with Nadia's "So the form isn't reading the person, it's
reading its own listing back to itself" (Ch. 21, a few pages later). Same
move, same cadence, different name on it.

## Answers

**Which chapter is the weakest and why?** Chapter 21. Not because any scene in
it is badly written — the essay-writing scene and the Nadia/Ruth notebook
scene are both good — but because the visitor-parade structure is the same
beat run six times, and it is the chapter reviewers have already flagged twice
for exactly that reason. The passes seem to have polished each individual
visitor scene without anyone asking whether the chapter needed all six.

**Which single sentence is the worst?** Amberg's offer sentence in Chapter 22
("Everybody on staff signed something close to this same sheet at some
point... this office would rather lose a candidate over an honest answer than
keep a candidate on terms it isn't willing to give the next person as well").
It is the single clearest instance in the span of the author's own complaint:
written for a reader who already has a philosophy degree, not a ninth grader.

**Does the parking-lot fight still work?** Mostly, yes. The choreography, the
dock, Ruth's stopwatch line, and the setup all still land. It is damaged in two
specific, fixable places: the unattributed double-quote seam ("Hang on."
"Let me have this one.") that looks like unrepaired trim damage, and Chloe's
count-off sliding from observation into contemptuous verdict, ending on a
slur. Cut both and the scene is close to untouched.

**Which speeches are padded?** The "3% acceptance rate" exchange in Chapter 21
(one idea, three metaphors, six lines of dialogue). The verbatim repetition of
the job offer to Chloe's father in Chapter 22, which is diegetically justified
once but oversells its own device by the second full recitation. Priya's
Argentina speech in Chapter 23 restates its central idea once but earns its
place with a self-undercutting closing beat, and is the one speech on this
list that should be left alone.
