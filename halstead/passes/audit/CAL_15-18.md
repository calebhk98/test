## 1. Spock, Sheldon, AI, mini-adult

Nothing in any character's behaviour hits the four named modes. Everyone in these four
chapters is wrong about something, bored by something, or beaten by somebody. Chloe
fumbles a bolt release for two weeks (`17:5`), teaches seven students a topic backwards
for two months (`17:59`), writes a two-page spec with the load-bearing page missing
(`17:71-77`), files three hinges for three Sundays and bins them (`17:113`), stalls a car
eleven times (`18:161`), and loses a term of game theory to Kavi because he plays the
person and she plays the board (`16:115-123`). That is the opposite of the AI mode and it
is doing a great deal of work.

**1.1 The Marek fail is routed through inference rather than feeling.** `16:47`, the
paragraph the brief asks about directly:

> Marek fails the course, because a blank sheet leaves the mark scheme exactly one
> option, and Chloe is the one who writes it in. He is at breakfast in January as he was
> in December, in the same year, working through the same building, so whatever a fail
> costs a student here, expulsion is no part of it. What it does cost, whether it follows
> him onto anything that matters, whether anybody sat him down about it, stays outside
> her reach for years, and she is careful for a long time about how she asks.

What is right about it, first, because most of it is right. The clause "a blank sheet
leaves the mark scheme exactly one option" removes her discretion, and that single move is
what keeps the paragraph out of the junior-staffer register: she is not exercising
professional judgment over another child, she is applying a rule she has no power over.
"Chloe is the one who writes it in" insists on her hand on the pen rather than on the
institution's. And the two sentences after it are entirely about what happens to Marek,
not about the sheet, the mark, or her own record — a thirteen-year-old worried about a
boy, which is the correct object.

What is thin: every one of her reactions is delivered as a deduction. She looks for him at
breakfast and the sentence turns the looking into an inference about expulsion policy
rather than into relief. "Whether it follows him onto anything that matters" is a worry
about a permanent record, which is the most adult-shaped worry available; the
thirteen-year-old version is nearer *whether he is angry with her, and whether she has to
sit in a room with him on Thursday*. The line "she is careful for a long time about how
she asks" is the one that most nearly gets there and is also the most stately phrasing in
the paragraph.

Sized to a child? Partly, and the sizing lives almost entirely outside this paragraph.
Two beats carry it and they are both good. `16:39`: "Chloe puts the chalk down harder than
she means to, and he waits by the door until she says fine" — real temper, correctly
small, and she loses the exchange. `16:45`: "she reads it twice in the corridor, folds it
into quarters and pockets it rather than binning it" — a child's gesture with a child's
object, and the best thing in the sequence. What `16:45` also holds, and only holds
obliquely, is the unfairness: the grade at the bottom covers all seven, so his blank sheet
marks *her* down, and the text gives the fact and no reaction to it. That is question 5's
"unfairness felt at full size, in a scene that had room for it," and it is the single
place in my four chapters where I would most expect the author to want another half
sentence. Reported, not written: adding felt reaction is invention, not repair.

**1.2 One phrase flirts with the Vulcan register, and it is Marek's, not Chloe's.**
`16:21`: "He says it the way he says everything, with his hands still and his voice
level." Read cold, that is the Spock description. Read in place it survives, because it
is a description of one boy's particular manner rather than of how children at this school
are, and because the chapter immediately undercuts it: `16:37` has him "already halfway
out of the seat," `16:41` has him think for ten seconds before answering. Marek as a whole
reads as a twelve-year-old, not a mini-adult: his objection is that a receipt is a
different object from work (`16:21`), he holds it with total rigidity, he keeps turning up
every Thursday and reading everything anyway, and he lets himself fail over it. No adult
does that. It is stubbornness at exactly twelve-year-old scale wearing a principled
argument, which is the right shape.

**1.3 The seven-student triage in `16:9` is the chapter's closest approach to a
staff-room voice.** "Six of the seven are straightforward: Ivy and Tomas want the answer
handed over, though they can be talked out of it inside a month, Beatriz arrives already
knowing most of the material and so takes eleven minutes of the hour and uses them well,
two more turn up, work, leave." That is a competent teacher's summary of a class. Two
things hold it: "two more turn up, work, leave" is dismissive in a way no adult teacher
would let herself be on paper, and the sentence directly after it has her on the floor for
forty minutes with a crying twelve-year-old, which is not a staff response either. On
balance it lands, and it is a year of teaching compressed into two sentences, which is
where this register comes from. Noted, not actioned.

**1.4 The NDA deliberation, `18:137`, is the best answer to this question in the four
chapters and should be left exactly as it is.** She builds the case against first, makes
that side as hard as she can, then builds the other. That is a formal technique and on its
own would read mini-adult. It does not, because of what brackets it: "She lies awake that
night, and snaps at Sam at breakfast the next morning for no reason she can name, then
apologises for it before he can ask why," and "She is quiet at dinner on Wednesday, and
Sam asks her twice whether she is all right." Sleeplessness, an unexplained snap, an
apology she cannot account for, and a friend having to ask twice. The intelligence changed
what she could do about the problem and changed nothing about how much it cost her, which
is the standard's own sentence.

## 2. Knowing they are exceptional

Nothing to fix. This is the strongest of the five axes in these chapters, and the chat
build and the phone hardening — the two scenes the brief singles out — are the two best
executed passages in the four.

**2.1 The chat build, `16:95-111`, clears section 2 four separate times.**

- `16:99`, Chloe challenges Ruth for writing her own encryption "when there are whole
  libraries of this that people spent careers getting right." Chloe's benchmark for good
  work is professional adults elsewhere, not herself or the table.
- `16:101`, Ruth's answer disclaims originality before she defends the choice: "I read
  three of those before I started, and about half of what's in here came straight back out
  of them."
- `16:103`, "Chloe breaks it in nine days, and the encryption holds the whole way" — she
  breaks her friend's cryptosystem and the narration treats it as an ordinary Tuesday, and
  it also makes clear the cipher was fine and she went round it, which is the un-showy
  version of the beat.
- `16:111`, the load-bearing one: "Amberg could take it apart in an afternoon, and not one
  of them asks him to." An adult in the building would beat all of them. That single
  sentence is what stops the whole sequence reading as children being admired.

And the payoff, `16:111`: a term of network engineering buys them "the eight o'clock bus
the first Saturday in December, and it goes into town with all of them on it." Enormous
capability, entirely trivial want, no comment from the narrator. That is section 3
working, in one sentence, at the exact point where a lesser draft would total up what they
had built.

**2.2 The phone hardening, `18:139-151`, does the same job and adds the designed-in
failure.** `18:139` normalises the whole premise in a subordinate clause — "inside a
building where getting into things is a course with an examination at the end of it" — so
the reader is told the capability is coursework, not exceptionality, and told it as a fact
about a timetable. `18:151` then hands the sequence its deflation: "Sam loses his in
April, which is the failure every one of them predicted out loud and built for anyway, and
eleven minutes with Kavi at a terminal puts him back inside." A clever-children scene that
ends with one of them losing the card and needing rescuing is not a scene that admires
them. And Chloe's closing habit, checking the card "about once a week, the way she checks
a door on her way past it," makes the whole apparatus boring, which is the point.

Kavi's own lines in the scene are checked against `KAVI.md` ("when something he did would
read as impressive, he names the actual cost instead of accepting the compliment"). At
`18:149` he answers Chloe's objection with mechanism and structure, never with a claim
about the work. He does not register his own cleverness anywhere in it.

**2.3 Chapter 18's plateau sequence is the clearest anti-exceptionalism writing in my
range.** `18:63-71`:

> "You're on sixty, everybody's talking about it at breakfast."
> "I know I'm on sixty, so what's your actual point?"
> "I'm on forty, and I sleep fine on it."
> ...
> "Go and be miserable at Ruth, she's on ninety-something."

Sixty out of a hundred at shooting a bullet out of the air is a bad week to these people,
Sam is content at forty, and Ruth is at ninety-one (`18:57`). Nobody in the scene is
impressive to anybody. Sam's turn also matches his sheet exactly: number first, flat
clause, and the one permitted soft register because the subject is whether his friend is
all right rather than what is true.

**2.4 Every comparison in these chapters is scored inside Halstead, and most are scored
against Chloe.** `15:336`, closing the chapter: "By Christmas she can hold twenty-four
seconds in the 10v1, though she is still ranked only thirtieth out of ninety." `16:73`,
Chloe to Eli: "Eleven of us had that set for a week and you did the lot in an evening, so
what did the rest of us miss?" — she puts herself in the group that missed it. `17:67`,
her only self-comparison is against her own earlier languages, and she attributes the
speed to Japanese being fast at the front rather than to herself, then loses the advantage
by November. `18:79`, "two boys who used to come off the mat behind her are ahead by nine
seconds and eleven, one of whom she has beaten every year since they were ten."
`18:105-117`, the investing block ends with Hark telling her "you are up seventeen dollars,
eight percent, and you are barely beating inflation," and `18:119`, "Chloe wipes the board
and sits down."

**2.5 [protected] `15:199` is the one line in four chapters where a character registers
that what they did is not normal for their age, and it is handled correctly.** Sam, after
four twelve-year-olds put down four grown men: "Four grown men. That's not very twelve."
Read alone this is the failure mode. Read in place it is the setup for the rationalisation
that the standard says must follow, and the rationalisation arrives immediately and from
three directions: `15:203` "They didn't even do costumes properly. Look at this. Look at
this guy's boots"; `15:207` Ruth reframing the whole thing as a scoring problem, "It worked
because they took the stairs... and that's minus"; `15:127`, earlier, Ruth already having
disposed of the intruders as defective, "This is a consumer handset with a default code.
My dad's work has better than this and my dad sells insurance"; and `15:215` "we are
losing marks." The contrary evidence appears, is felt for one line, and is explained away
as the other side being bad rather than themselves being unusual. This is the standard's
own worked example and the chapter executes it. Protected, and it should be.

Two notes against `SAM.md` for this line, offered because the brief says to prefer the
manuscript. The sheet's failure mode 1 says "if the joke depends on timing or wordplay, it
is not his joke," and "that's not very twelve" is a coinage. I would keep the manuscript
line: it is number-first, flat, and it names what the thing plainly is, which is his one
permitted figurative channel. The sheet rule reads over-fitted to the wittier lines it was
written against.

## 3. Contrary evidence going unrationalised

**Nothing found that needs fixing.** Two places worth recording, one because it works and
one because it is the one gap a reader might notice.

**3.1 Working.** The intrusion aftermath, `15:127` and `15:201-215`, covered at 2.5.

**3.2 The one place a rationalisation beat is absent, and I believe correctly.**
`18:125-135`, Dr. Sandoval. A professional adult stops a fifteen-year-old in a corridor to
say that her work feeds a project four adults have signed a federal non-disclosure
agreement over, and says out loud, `18:131`, "I would refuse to put a federal agreement in
front of a fifteen-year-old." That is the strongest piece of external evidence about
Chloe's standing anywhere in my four chapters, it comes from outside the student body, and
Chloe never explains it away. What she does instead is engage entirely with the ethics of
being asked (`18:133`, "So you're asking me to keep quiet about it, when there's no rule
anywhere that obliges me to") and then spend a week on whether it is fair to ask her. She
never treats the sentence as being about her ability at all.

I read this as stronger than a rationalisation, not weaker. The standard's failure mode is
a character who notices the evidence and does not dispose of it; Chloe never entertains
the proposition, so there is nothing to dispose of. It is also Sandoval, not Chloe, who
names the age, which keeps the observation out of Chloe's head entirely. Flagged here
because it is the one paragraph in ch18 where an auditor could reasonably ask for a
rationalising beat, and my recommendation is to add none: any line where Chloe explains
away why her work is on a federal project would be the first time in the book she had
thought about it, which is the defect the standard is actually guarding against.

**3.3 Same shape, smaller, twice more.** `17:101`, her name goes into the acknowledgments
of a paper, "at the end of a list of five, on a paper that stays inside the department,"
and Sanders tells her in a corridor "on his way somewhere else, still walking." She stands
there with her bag half off her shoulder and reads the whole paper that night. `18:123`,
her name lands on an author list, "sixth of nine," and "she reads it four times, then
spends twenty minutes looking up the other eight people." In both, the reaction is
excitement plus curiosity about other people, and in neither does she form a view about
herself. The twenty minutes on the other eight is the better of the two.

## 4. Scale announced rather than buried

**4.1 The best example in these four chapters, and worth protecting from a future pass.**
`17:155-157`, the winter-break table:

> Sam grew four inches over the summer and there is a pencil mark on a door frame about
> it. Sam has a girlfriend. She teaches now. She has seven of them and she taught one
> thing to all seven of them backward and had to go in and say so. There is a boy called
> Ferris who does everything the long way round and gets there every time. Japanese, four
> months of it. Abstract algebra, contracts, logistics, the fracture tests, a clamp that
> closes on the wrong axis, three hinges in a bin.
>
> All of it started around September. All of it went past this table in silence. Which one
> was she supposed to have opened with? Why is it the rifle?

This is the camp-arc model exactly. The list is long, it is motivated (she is trying to
work out which item she should have led with, under a scolding), it is not totalled, and
three of its nine items are failures — taught it backward, the clamp, the hinges in the
bin — so it cannot read as an achievement inventory. The last two questions are hers and
they are a fourteen-year-old's questions. A skimming reader takes this as her being
flustered; a careful reader counts nine things and notices that the paragraph never says
how many. Do not touch it.

**4.2 The one narratorial takeover, and my closest call after 1.1.** `18:141`:

> Priya says at dinner that the lock held, which is almost certainly true, and which Ruth
> calls the wrong sentence before Priya has finished saying it. The right sentence is that
> a single lock stood between a lectern drawer and everything the table has said to each
> other since they were thirteen, for three days, and that whether it held is a question
> they are asking on Friday instead of one they had already answered on Monday.

Ruth is named as having the argument and then the narrator delivers it for her, in the
narrator's own construction ("The right sentence is that..."). Two objections. First,
against House Rule 1: it is the narrator doing analysis on the page rather than a person
doing it in a room, and it lands one line above a scene that could have carried it in
dialogue. Second, and this is why it is in question 4 rather than in a prose note: the
narrator here is momentarily the cleverest voice in the scene, which in a
clever-children-doing-clever-things passage is exactly the position the standard warns
about. It is not admiration — there is no valuation anywhere in it — but it is the
narrator stepping forward, and it is the only place in the two flagged scenes where that
happens.

What I would have done if asked: give the second sentence to Ruth as speech, since it is
her argument and her voice does correction-first-reason-second, and let `18:143` ("So the
phone stops holding anything") land off her line instead of off the narrator's. I did not,
because it converts narration into dialogue in a chapter another pass has already measured
for spoken mean, and because the paragraph may be a deliberate compression.

**4.3 One totalling-shaped figure, probably fine.** `17:81`, the no-libraries project:
"the whole of it runs to about nine thousand lines across fourteen files." A line count is
the one measure in these chapters that exists only to say *this is a lot*. Three things
hold it down: it is delivered inside a specification, not a judgment; the sentence it ends
lands on "so on the last day she drags the window around for four minutes in front of Ruth
and Kavi," which is a fifteen-year-old showing off a toy to two friends; and `17:83`
immediately gives Ruth an equally large parallel project, which normalises it as what the
month's brief produced rather than what Chloe produced. Reported as a watch-item only.

**4.4 Buried well, several times.** `15:274`, "Chloe reads it in the corridor and delivers
her whole opinion of it in Russian at volume, and stays annoyed about the C for a week
before she accepts it" — she is fluent in Russian, and the sentence is about being cross
about a C. `17:125`, Ruth on Sam: "He's the only person who's ever got over that wall and
he couldn't think of one," where the remarkable fact is the subordinate clause and the
point of the sentence is that he is bad at asking girls out. `16:133`, Amberg: "at sixteen
you'll take the bar. That's the examination this country uses to check whether a person
knows it, and every citizen in this room ought to pass it" — the bar established as the
ordinary civic minimum, from the mouth of the adult who sets the expectation, which is
where the "they think they are normal" belief comes from in the first place.

## The turret and Voss, `18:5-49`

Taking the brief's third bullet on its own, because all three parts of it come out clean.

**Voss's private view never reaches the page.** He is described only from outside: `18:9`
"Voss lets them look at it for a while, and when he does start he starts with the machine
rather than with them"; `18:11` and `18:15`, delivery only; `18:27`, "on the sixth he is at
her shoulder with his hand out for the rifle." His one moment of apparent knowledge,
`18:45`, "It's a real thing, and you're far from the first person to find it," is about the
plateau at sixty rather than about the drill's difficulty, and it normalises rather than
warns. What he does next, `18:49`, is refuse to explain: "He taps the pages straight
against the desk, hands them back to her and tells her to get down to point six because
her block started four minutes ago." A withholding rendered as an errand. The reader can
suspect there is something he is not saying; the narration never says what, and Chloe reads
it as him simply being unhelpful. This is the correct handling and it is the best-executed
thing in ch18.

**The saying holds up.** `18:15`, "a gun you can only point at a man is a weapon, and a gun
you can put a bullet onto a bullet with is a tool. You have had a year of the first one.
This year you get the second." Then `18:17`: delivered "like a thing he has said four
hundred times to four hundred students," repeated round the year by Thursday and quoted
"with the flat confidence of something printed in a manual," and then the sting, that the
oldest instructor on the staff first heard it from Voss. The reader gets, without being
told, that the institutional wisdom is one man's line. That is inference offered and not
explained, and it is discovered because Chloe went looking, which keeps it in her curiosity
rather than in the narrator's commentary.

**The students take the drill as ordinary, and the zeroes read as normal difficulty.**
`18:13`, Aymar's "why" is asked "in the tone of somebody asking on behalf of ninety
people" — a class asking what the point of the unit is, which is what a class asks, not
whether it can be done. `18:19` is the load-bearing sentence: "the range in October is the
sound of a great many people missing, four sessions a week, ninety of them at it." Ninety
people all missing is a hard unit, not a miracle. The one line in the chapter that comes
near "this is impossible" is put in a child's mouth as a joke — Kavi doing the arithmetic
and announcing "that at this rate somebody will hit something in about March" — and it is
deflated in the same sentence by Priya telling him to eat, and then disproved two
paragraphs later when Odile hits on the eleventh of November. Putting the impossibility
line in a kid's joke and then falsifying it is the neatest structural solve in my four
chapters.

The detail that finally settles it is Ruth at ninety-one (`18:57`). A drill where one
fifteen-year-old is at ninety-one out of a hundred cannot read as near-impossible to
anyone inside the book, and the narration never once compares the drill to anything
outside the building. `SAM.md` carries the outside comparison — "a competent adult
marksman would not get near it" — and the manuscript, correctly, does not.

**One continuity contradiction inside this passage, not a calibration matter.** `18:17`
reads "In February, five weeks into a run of cards that all read zero, she goes looking for
where it comes from." The zeroes run from the first week of October (`18:5`) to Odile's
first hit on the eleventh of November (`18:21`), and by February Chloe is scoring
fifty-nine to sixty-one (`18:27`). Five weeks into the run of zeroes is mid-November, not
February. The month looks like the error rather than the description, and "In November"
would fit both the arithmetic and Odile's date. Not edited: it is outside the calibration
remit, another pass may own chronology, and I would rather the author pick the fix.
