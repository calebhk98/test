# Calibration audit: chapters 6-10

Scope: `06_the_list.md`, `07_the_same_room.md`, `08_the_asking.md`,
`09_february.md`, `10_april.md`. Read against `passes/CALIBRATION_AUDIT.md`,
`passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`, and the sheets for Chloe, Sam, Ruth and Kavi.

Verdict up front: these five chapters are in calibration. I found no instance of
a child knowing they are exceptional, no Spock/Sheldon/AI/mini-adult failure, and
no unrationalised contrary evidence. What I did find is four narratorial
half-steps (three of them in chapter 6) where the narration does a small piece of
work the reader could have done. One of the four was unambiguous and is fixed.
The other three are reported with the strike test applied and left alone.

Line numbers for chapter 7 are post-edit.

## Chapter 6 against the author's own description

The author's model, from section 3 of the standard:

> *The reader sees a few scenes where she is social, the main points of the
> scenes is showing sociability, emotions, her failing, etc. Then at the end we
> have a quick little section where she rambles to her dad about a lot of other
> classes we never see a scene for, and then Dad comments on 2 of them. [...] A
> careful reader sees she did a lot more, and her list says that wasn't even all
> of it, with her just remembering nerf at the end.*

**It still does this.** Piece by piece:

**A skimming reader undercounts.** The only items given any dramatic weight in
the list are CPR (49), the bridge (51) and fractions (55). Dad's phone recap at
229-245 names five: CPR, fractions, Spanish, karate, cooking. So a skimmer
leaves the chapter with four or five things, exactly the number the author
predicted.

**A careful reader finds about twenty.** Counting: Gary/CPR (49), the bridge
plus the written prediction (51), the pool (53), dividing fractions (55),
Spanish (59-65), signing (67), the twenty-sentence unknown-language puzzle (69),
wood shop and goggles (71), wound care (71), eggs (71), the Thursday dish (71),
the paintings (71), text programming (73), the machine room and its water pipes
(73), the robot arm (75), chess (77), negative numbers (79), karate (81), nerf
(83), and wood shop again (85). Nineteen or twenty depending on how the two
computer things are counted, against a skim yield of five.

**The list says it wasn't all of it.** Two mechanisms, both intact. Nerf at 83,
"which arrive out of order because she has only just remembered them," and the
wood-shop repeat at 85, where she loops back to an item she already gave. Both
say the list is not a list, it is her running down.

**Dad comments on two.** At the table he picks up exactly two: the day count
(91-97) and the fractions (105-113). Everything else goes past unremarked. The
later car call (217-257) is a separate beat and does not break the rule, because
it is the parents' bafflement rather than a tally, and because what it recites
is the undercount, not the total.

**The failure and sociability are carried by the list itself, not just by the
scenes around it.** This is the part I would most want the author to know is
holding. Almost every item she gives her father is a place she came second:
"Ours went at eleven, and Ruth's went at nineteen" (51); "I wrote fourteen and
was wrong by three" (51); "the pool, where I can do a whole length now, badly"
and Sam doing two (53); "Kavi got the pattern in the endings, which is still
half out of my reach even though he has explained it four separate times" (69);
"Then chess, which Sam is also better at" (77). The scale is hidden partly
because she reports the whole month as a series of things other seven-year-olds
did better.

### The three restored items, checked specifically

I was asked whether wound care (71), the robot arm (75) and negative numbers
(79) sit at the same depth as their neighbours and whether any has become a
set-piece. **None has.** In detail:

- **Wound care, line 71.** Buried deepest of the three: it is the second
  sentence inside a three-sentence paragraph carrying six items, with no line
  of its own. "Then what you do about a bleed, which is pressure first and the
  dressing second, and the glue they use on a cut too small to need stitches."
  At roughly 29 words it is the longest *single-item* unit in the bare-list
  stretch, because it names two facts rather than one, but it gets no reaction
  from Chloe, no reaction from her father, and no narrator comment, and it is
  bracketed by goggles on one side and eggs on the other. It also never
  resurfaces in the phone call. If anything I would leave it exactly as is:
  clipping it would make it the shortest thing on the page, which is its own
  kind of attention.
- **The robot arm, line 75.** Takes the same "Then X, which..." shape as chess
  (77), negative numbers (79), karate (81) and nerf (83), and at 21 words sits
  inside the band those five occupy (8, 19, 11, 15, 22). Its relative clause,
  "picks a block up and puts it down again for as long as you stand there
  watching it," is deflating rather than impressed: a seven-year-old finding
  industrial robotics slightly boring.
- **Negative numbers, line 79.** 19 words, same shape, same register: "go the
  other way past zero and keep going as far as you want them to." Nothing frames
  it as advanced for her age; it is described as a fact about numbers, not an
  achievement.

One further piece of evidence that all three are load-bearing rather than
decorative: each is anchored elsewhere in the book, so none reads as inserted
filler. Negative numbers is planted backward at `05_behind.md:73`, where a
classmate asks Mr. Baptiste whether the rule works with them. The robot arm pays
off forward at `09_february.md:131`, where she asks in the car whether the arm
on the second floor is the same one or a newer one. Wound care pays off forward
at `12_nine.md:93`, where first aid is a Monday subject run on a clock. Nerf,
for comparison, pays off at `11_eight.md:133` and `11_eight.md:145`.

**Conclusion on chapter 6: it is doing what the author says it does, and the
three restorations have not disturbed it.** The only reservations are the three
narratorial half-steps listed under question 4 below, all of them small and all
of them pre-existing.

## 1. Spock, Sheldon, an AI, or a mini-adult

**Nothing rises to a defect in any of the five chapters.** Two lines are worth
having on the record as the closest approaches.

**`06_the_list.md:113`**, the highest register any child reaches in my chapters:

> "Anybody can do the flipping," Chloe says, putting the pen down, "and you do
> it and it comes out right, but the rightness belongs to him and not to you,
> because all you actually know is that he said so."

Judged in calibration, for three reasons. The content is her mechanism-need,
which CHLOE.md makes foundational rather than a display. The syntax is a child's
("the flipping"), not an adult's. And she states it and stops without checking
whether he followed, which is the sheet's own test. It also opens with "Anybody
can" — she is asserting that the *easy* half is universal, which is the
load-bearing belief in section 2 of the standard, stated by her, unprompted. The
line is marked as unusual only by her parents (233-237), never by the narrator.

**`10_april.md:31`**, Kavi:

> "That's worse than not knowing. If you don't know then it's nobody's fault,
> but he knew and he forgot, and that's a decision about how much it mattered."

An adult formulation, and it is immediately and completely undercut in the same
paragraph: "Kavi sits down on the floor by the door. 'What. I'm allowed to think
that's worse without both of you looking at me like that.'" Sulking on a floor
about being looked at. That is a seven-year-old, and the sequencing is right:
the reasoning first, the childishness on top of it, not the reverse.

For the record on the sheets I was told were corrected today: Sam's chapter 6
lines run longer and more subordinated than SAM.md's "one flat clause" dial
(133, 183, 191), but every character in these chapters speaks in the same
"X, because Y" shape, so this reads as a manuscript-wide dialogue convention
rather than Sam drifting. I would trust the manuscript here. His signature
survives inside it: a number first at 187 ("the bridge log has four entries in
it"), and the repetition-as-comedy at 211, where Ruth corrects him and he says
it again word for word. Similarly RUTH.md:122 cites `06_the_list.md:60` for the
shoes lined up against the wall; the manuscript has it at 121. Line drift in the
sheet, not a problem in the chapter.

## 2. A child knowing they are exceptional

**No instances.** Across all five chapters there is not one comparison in a
child's favour, not one moment of modesty, and not one normal-for-them feat
treated as a feat. A keyword sweep for the whole vocabulary of exceptionality
(smart, genius, gifted, talent, clever, prodigy, exceptional, remarkable,
impressive, special, better than, smarter, faster than, most people, everyone
else, nobody else, unusual) returns nine hits across the five chapters and every
one is either an adult speaking, or a false positive, or points the other way.

Three lines that come nearest, all of which I judge correct:

**`08_the_asking.md:149`**, the highest-risk line in the five chapters:

> She can't work out what's wrong with it, because it's faster than her class,
> everybody keeps telling her it's the fast one, Mrs. Prahl is nice to her every
> single week, and there are nine of them in that room instead of twenty-six.
> She wants to say that this is the wrong thing, out loud, to Mrs. Prahl, on a
> Thursday, in front of the other eight, but the rest of the sentence stays
> stuck behind that one, and she is fairly sure the first half already sounds
> like she thinks she's better than everybody, a thing she has learned to keep
> to herself.

In calibration, and the distinction is the whole thing: she does not think she
is better, she is afraid of *sounding* like she does. The belief attributed to
her is about a social risk, not about her own ability, and the sentence she is
trying to say is "this is the wrong thing," not "this is too easy for me." The
first clause is the proof: "She can't work out what's wrong with it." If she
knew she was exceptional the paragraph would not exist, because she would have
the answer.

**`10_april.md:155`**, the same construction ten weeks later:

> There are people in her year who have been here three years already, and she
> wants to know how long their list is, but every question she tries sounds like
> she is measuring herself against them, so she keeps it to herself.

Also in calibration, and pointed the right way: she assumes their list is
longer than hers.

**The one note worth acting on in chapters 7-10 is that these two are the same
construction.** Both are "it would sound like X, so she keeps it to herself,"
both close their paragraph, and they are two chapters apart. Separately each is
right. Used twice as the book's way of handling the subject, the pattern starts
to imply she has a suppressed self-assessment rather than no self-assessment,
which is the wrong implication. No edit made, because either one alone is
defensible and I would rather the author choose which to vary. If one is to
change, `10_april.md:155` is the weaker of the two, because the thing she cannot
ask (how long is your list) has less at stake than the thing she cannot say
(this is the wrong room).

Peripheral characters are clean. `06_the_list.md:133` has Sam reading a month of
teaching as evidence the place has "plans for the rest of it," not as evidence
anybody was selected. `10_april.md:35` has a boy ask whether Chloe is the one
who did the bridge at camp, take "no" for an answer, and carry on eating.
`10_april.md:67` has Priya deliver "he went to the Olympics" as mom-gossip with
no awe attached.

Worth naming because it is structural: **awareness of the scale lives entirely
with the adults.** `06_the_list.md:35`, "They told us the list is a good list,
which leaves the whole question of good at what." `06_the_list.md:37-39`, a
mother offering "She's top of her reading group at home" and getting "Here it
buys you a paper plate and a seat at this table" back from another parent. That
exchange is doing the work of section 2 in one line: the outside measure is
worth nothing in this room, said by a parent, in front of a child who does not
react to it.

## 3. Contrary evidence that goes unrationalised

**None found, and the mechanism the chapters use instead is better than
rationalisation.**

The contrary evidence in chapters 7-9 is not "you are exceptional," it is "this
room is too easy," and Chloe consistently attributes it to herself or leaves it
unexplained rather than resolving it in her own favour:

- `07_the_same_room.md:131-133`, told by two classmates that she talks weird and
  is weirder than she was. Her response is `135`, "Chloe's hand comes down flat
  over what she has already written," then `137`, a retraction, then `141`,
  scratching the true answer out and writing "I hope you like fifth grade"
  instead. Defect-attribution, not superiority. This is the sheet's "openness is
  her natural state, hiding is a bruise" arriving on the page in three beats.
- `08_the_asking.md:97`, Chloe has the knights-and-knaves puzzle before Mrs.
  Prahl finishes reading it, Dev gets there ninety seconds later and does it on
  the board, "and Mrs. Prahl makes a real thing out of it." Chloe draws no
  conclusion of any kind. She waits, "because you wait."
- `08_the_asking.md:105`, "When does it get harder, though, because I keep
  waiting for the part where it gets hard." She treats the easiness as a
  property of the programme with a hard part still to come, not as a fact about
  herself.
- `09_february.md:127`, at the Halstead assessment day: "No test arrives, and
  she keeps waiting for somebody to hand her one, right through the afternoon
  and out to the car." She assumes there is a bar she has yet to be measured
  against. She never concludes she did not need testing.

The one place where a rationalisation is conspicuously *absent* is
`08_the_asking.md:149`, "She can't work out what's wrong with it." I do not read
this as the defect question 3 describes, because the chapter is explicit that
the failure is a failure to explain, not a refusal to, and because it pays off
directly at `09_february.md:23`:

> Chloe can't answer that one, because school, the sheets and Kayleigh Burns are
> each a piece of it and each too small to be it, and she is seven years old
> with the word for the whole thing nowhere in her.

That is the correct version of the same idea and it is doing the work of a
rationalisation: the gap stays a gap, and the reason given is her age, not her
character.

One thing to note for the record: at `06_the_list.md:219` Chloe is awake and
faking sleep through the entire car call, so she hears her parents say "so what
is that, what's the name for that" and "I don't know what that is, Meg, and I
was standing right there," and the chapter gives her no interior response
whatsoever (`259`, "Chloe keeps her eyes shut the rest of the way home"). I
checked whether this is contrary evidence left lying. It is not, because what
her parents cannot classify is the *school*, not their daughter. Nothing in the
call is about her ability. That is a fine distinction and the chapter is on the
right side of it.

## 4. Scale announced rather than buried

**No defects, but four narratorial half-steps, three of them in chapter 6.** One
has been removed (chapter 7, listed at the top). The remaining three are
reported and left, and I want to be explicit that I applied House Rule 1's own
strike test to each rather than flagging on shape alone.

**`06_the_list.md:95`, the strongest remaining candidate.**

> "Twenty-seven," he says again, the way you repeat a number to check it the
> second time.

The clause names the purpose behind a manner, which is the state half of the
tone construction: the sound is the flat repetition, the checking is what it
means. Strike test: delete "the way you repeat a number to check it the second
time" and nothing is lost, because line 97 immediately dramatises it, "There's
tomorrow as well, which makes twenty-eight, though I'm leaving it out until it
happens." That is the exact pattern House Rule 1 describes, the gloss sitting
directly above the beat that would have proved it. **Not edited**, because
chapter 6 is the model chapter, the clause sits at the pivot the author designed
by hand, and "when in doubt report rather than edit." Recommended for the
author's own decision, deletion rather than replacement.

**`06_the_list.md:85`, second candidate.**

> Then the wood shop again, and the goggles again, in almost the same sentence
> as before, and she keeps going, straight through it.

"again... again" already says repeat, and line 71 is four paragraphs up, so
"in almost the same sentence as before" is largely the narrator confirming the
reader noticed. Argument the other way, and it is a real one: the clause carries
a fact the repetition alone does not, that she used nearly identical words, i.e.
she is on autopilot and running down. Marginal both ways. **Not edited.**

**`06_the_list.md:83`, checked and cleared.**

> Then the nerf guns at the end, which arrive out of order because she has only
> just remembered them.

Same shape as the two above, and I expected to flag it. It fails the strike
test in the other direction: cut the clause and the line becomes "Then the nerf
guns at the end," from which the reader cannot recover that they are out of
order or newly remembered. Something real is lost, and what is lost is the exact
effect the author named. **Keep as is.** Noting it so the author can see the
audit distinguished this from the two above rather than flagging on family
resemblance.

**`06_the_list.md:243-245`, the closest the book comes to a total, and it stays
on the right side.**

> "All of that in four weeks."
>
> "In twenty-eight days, and she counted them."

A character totalling, which question 4 names. It survives for three reasons.
It is in a parent's mouth, on a phone, out of Chloe's sightline. What it totals
is the undercount, five items out of twenty, so the tally itself is part of the
concealment rather than a breach of it. And the exchange resolves into "I don't
know what that is, Meg, and I was standing right there" (251), which withholds
rather than delivers. Nobody in the scene is impressed on the reader's behalf;
two adults are baffled in front of each other.

Two more passages I checked and cleared:

- `06_the_list.md:57`, "Two tables over, another father is sitting through
  roughly the same thing, a pen going on a different napkin while his own
  daughter talks him through her month." This is scale-hiding rather than
  scale-announcing: it makes the whole performance ordinary by showing a
  duplicate of it ten feet away, and it stays inside Chloe's field of view.
- `10_april.md:93`, the library. She takes eleven books the first time and
  "gets three of them read," then six, then settles at two or three. A passage
  that could have totalled her reading instead reports a failure and a
  correction downward.

## 5. Age gone missing

**No chapter is missing its age.** The childish want, the boredom and the
unfairness are all present at full size, and in several places they are the
strongest thing on the page.

Boredom, at full size and never explained: `09_february.md:5`, "Chloe finishes
the words in about four minutes, then colours the diagram very slowly, going
right up to the lines and stopping exactly there." That single clause carries
the whole of the chapter's problem with no narration attached to it.

Childish want, everywhere: `06_the_list.md:167`, up on her knees with one hand
fisted in the leg of Kavi's pyjamas; `06_the_list.md:97`, "There's tomorrow as
well, which makes twenty-eight, though I'm leaving it out until it happens";
`07_the_same_room.md:181`, a column of tally marks on the back of a spelling
worksheet under her pillow, one for every day without a yes;
`09_february.md:75`, "Can I tell Ruth, can I call her right now?" seconds after
being told.

Unfairness felt at full size: the whole of `08_the_asking.md:123-141`, the jacket
fight. "You hate me, that's what it is, you actually hate me" (133), "I hate you,
I hate you so much" (137), then `141`, standing behind the door "until her legs
ache from standing behind the door," waiting for a stair that never gives. Then
`143`, dinner happening normally around it. That is seven, not a small adult.

The body is present throughout: syrup on two fingers before the plate arrives
(`06:5`), the sneaker catching the chair leg, the knee going under the table
(`06:103`), feet that do not reach the floor on Dr. Ammons's couch (`08:11`),
standing on the edge of a cupboard door to reach the tap the way she has been
told not to (`09:133`).

**One passage where the age is thinner than the room allowed**, reported rather
than edited: `08_the_asking.md:145`.

> So is the third, except that Mrs. Prahl puts them in pairs for a game with the
> dice, and Chloe gets a girl called Hana who is careful, slow, and checks
> everything twice before she'll write it down, so they finish before anybody
> else and Mrs. Prahl comes over, says well done to both of them, and gives them
> a word search to be getting on with.

Being handed a word search as the reward for finishing is the most unfair thing
that happens to her in the Thursday sequence, and she registers nothing. Same at
`08:97`, where Dev gets the board and the fuss and she gets nothing. Two
adjacent opportunities for felt unfairness, both passed over. I am reporting
this rather than treating it as a defect, because the flatness is motivated: the
whole chapter runs inside a diagnosed depression, established at `08:15`
(sleeping eleven hours and still tired) and paid off at `09:5-13`. If the author
wants one flicker anywhere in the Thursdays, the word search at `145` is the
place with the most room for it, and it would cost a clause.

## What is working

Brief, so the author knows the audit could tell the difference.

**Praise only ever comes from outside Halstead.** The librarian at
`07_the_same_room.md:99`, "That's a real month, that is." Mrs. Prahl at
`08_the_asking.md:145`, "well done to both of them." Inside the building, across
the whole of chapter 10, not one adult tells her she is good at anything. The
maths teacher reads two lines, says the second is wrong, and hands it back with
forty minutes left (`10:79`). The literature teacher marks the writing and not
the reading (`10:87`). The cooking teacher tells her the onion is a triangle
(`10:71`). This is the sample doing the work structurally, without a word of
explanation anywhere.

**The chapter 10 calibration forms slowly, from evidence, and is not finished
when the chapter ends.** She does not arrive believing everybody does this. She
arrives believing she can be found out, and spends "about three weeks working out
what actually gets somebody sent home from Halstead, checking off a list she
keeps only in her head" (`10:109`). She reads a corkboard twice on two different
days in case she missed a line (`10:111`), asks a teacher and gets "Not for being
bad at something" (`10:115`), then two weeks later asks Kavi and gets an
eyewitness account confirming that Owen was keeping up and went home anyway
(`10:119-123`). That is five weeks, three sources, and a peer finally telling her
to stop asking (`10:127`). And by the last week of June she still has the outside
frame on: `10:155` has her wanting to know how long the older students' lists are
and unable to ask. The belief is forming, not installed.

**Her own failures do the concealing.** Chapter 10 hands her four subjects she is
behind in and one she has caught up in (`10:81`), a wheel problem that takes her
six days and a shower (`10:95-97`), and then Ruth immediately relativising the
win: "Mine's four steps and yours is nine... Do it again from the start and go
slower" (`10:103`). Eleven books carried and three read (`10:93`). Five
`-stans` in a different wrong order every time, against Ruth doing the whole map
including the small islands off Scotland (`10:155`).

**Chloe's three registers are correctly distributed.** Open and running-on with
her father (`06:49-87`) and with the librarian (`07:93-97`); open and bickering
with the four of them (`06:119-201`, `10:9-33`); compressed only where somebody
has burned her, at `07:157` ("I've just got a lot of school stuff") and `07:207`
("The answer, whatever it is, refuses to come out of her"). No scene with Sam,
Ruth or Kavi reads clipped.

**One beat I would single out.** `10:139`: her mother laughs at something Chloe
said, "properly, for longer than the answer deserves, and it's a beat before
Chloe works out that it was funny." A seven-year-old who has just been funny by
accident and is a second late catching up to it. Nothing about that is a genius
and everything about it is a child.
