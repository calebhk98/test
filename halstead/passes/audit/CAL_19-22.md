## 1. Anything that reads as Spock, Sheldon, an AI, or a mini-adult

**Nothing in these four chapters is a defect under this question.** Three places worth naming
because they are where the risk sits.

**20:73**, inside the protected span, is the closest thing to an AI register in my four
chapters:

> Chloe reads it fast: seven grown men who chose this and have presumably done it before.
> Seven, three armed. Every one of them is standing somewhere that makes sense. She puts them
> at her own level until something tells her otherwise. Three ways out: the fence, the gap
> under it, the alley.

It is clipped and it is fast, but it is the shape the author asked for in
`passes/notes/ch20.md` ("Chloe should see 3 ways to get away, and is determining what she would
do, when Sam says he wants to fight"), the earlier talking-to-the-reader tail ("because that is
the only assumption that doesn't get somebody hurt") has been correctly removed, and the beat
that follows it is a sixteen-year-old giving her friend one second of thought and saying
"Fine. We stay close." (20:81). Not a defect.

**20:101**, the triage after the fight, has four teenagers checking airways and putting a
shoulder back in with almost no dialogue. It survives because it stays bodily rather than
procedural: Chloe counts "under her breath the way she'd count her own ribs," and the paragraph
ends on "One has bitten through his lip," which is a detail nobody clinical would end on.

**19:41-55 and 19:73-79**, the two marking scenes, are the mini-adult risk zone and are saved
by the same thing in both: she argues back, she is wrong, and it costs her something physical.
19:79, "a sentence in the middle that makes her jaw set while she is writing it."

**22:67** is not a Spock problem but is worth flagging as narration: "He looks at her the way he
looks at a mark scheme, and says the next part flat, in the register he'd use to correct a
wrong exponent." Two similes stacked to name Kavi's delivery. `passes/notes/ch22.md` flags this
exact clause ("Talking to the reader and interiority"); the revision dropped "and moves on
before it lands" and kept the register comparison. Kavi himself reads as a person, not a
machine, because the coil of cable is going slack and tight in his fist the whole scene, so
this is a leftover to consider rather than a calibration failure.

## 2. Anywhere a child knows they are exceptional, compares favourably, is modest, or treats a normal feat as a feat

### Chapter 20 — the parking lot

**The rationalisation holds all the way through, and it holds in the direction the standard
requires.** Chloe's speech (20:127-131) explains the win four separate ways and every one of
them is a fact about the seven men: they were bunched between two cars so any two swinging
would have hit each other; none of the three had an arm set for the gun; the front one's elbow
was locked out and he was holding it for show; using it means "police in four minutes, for a
phone and some watches off a set of teenagers." Then the categorical claim, which is the
author's own worked example landing exactly as specified: "If you had one usable skill, any
skill at all, you would make money doing literally anything other than this... Which means they
have none." And the close: "that's retarded. You didn't fight seven adults, Sam. You beat up
seven people who couldn't tell you their own address."

There is no clause anywhere in that speech, or anywhere else in the chapter, that attributes
the outcome to the four of them. Audited and unedited.

**The aftermath does not leak.** Checked line by line:

- 20:135-137, Ruth finishing the same argument: "A gun's a prop unless you actually use it...
  They let you walk all the way in. They gave you the wrist. They stood in a clump and waited
  their turn."
- 20:139, Sam accepts the frame and asks the only question it leaves: "Fine, say that one was
  too easy... So who do I actually fight, then?"
- 20:165, the group chat next morning: "an argument about whether it counts, whether seven with
  three guns between them is a real test or just seven people happening to be in the way of
  one." Ninety-one students given the story and the whole school argues in the same direction.
  Note the author's objection in `passes/notes/ch20.md` to the earlier wording ("civilians")
  has been applied; the word is gone.
- 20:167, Kavi's entire response is one laughing face, and Sam "lets it sit every time it comes
  up." Nobody trades on it.

**20:145-153, the waitress, is the strongest calibration passage in my four chapters.** They
seriously rate a fifty-year-old diner waitress above the best fighter in their year, on a
ledger of years-in-the-world: "That's thirty-four years of being in situations, against your
sixteen, eleven of which were spent at school" (20:147). Sam "thinks about it for a good deal
longer than the joke needs, and then answers it straight" (20:149), and Ruth extends it to all
four of them at 20:153. This is the chapter telling the reader what these children think an
average adult is, without one word about what they think they are.

**One borderline, reported not changed. 20:137**, Ruth: "They fought like ten-year-olds, the
whole seven of them, and you could have done that at ten." This is the only line in the chapter
that measures the win against their own ability rather than the attackers'. It stays in
calibration because the yardstick is a Halstead ten-year-old, i.e. their sample's floor, and
the grammatical subject is the seven men's level. It also answers the author's note directly
("IDK if they would call the muggers fighting like a 13 yo?... Maybe a 10 yo?"). Leave it.

Earlier in the chapter, checked and clean: Ruth's brother getting in (20:29-41) is treated as
family news and a question about the food; Sam's "Nine years behind you, and he'll spend every
one of them hearing your name" (20:35) is ordinary older-sibling-in-the-same-school, not a
claim about Ruth being remarkable.

### Chapter 21 — the applications

**Do the students rationalise it?** Yes, and the rationalisation is put in the mouths of three
of them across 21:159-187, after the acceptances, which is where `passes/notes/ch21.md` asked
for it. The mechanism is exact:

- 21:157, Chloe's first move is to distrust her own count, not the count of the world: "The
  arithmetic has gone wrong somewhere, and not in the letters, because the middle of a year
  gets the state school. She has twelve." She has already read the twelfth letter three times
  and checked the name on the envelope against the name inside "in case some office has put two
  files together that both say Kessler" (21:155).
- 21:163, Sam's two words: "They lie."
- 21:175-179, Chloe converts it into a physical scenario, which is her signature move on her
  sheet: "If a store put a sign in its window saying only four percent of the people who walk
  in buy something, would you think the store was doing well, or badly run?" ... "Ninety-one out
  of ninety-one bought something, every one of us, and that's the number the sign should say."
- 21:177 and 21:183-187, Ruth supplies the mechanism: "You'd think the sign was doing a job that
  had nothing to do with the store" ... "a school wants to look exclusive, so it puts a small
  number on the website" ... "it isn't a real number all the same, because both of those can be
  true at once, the site and the lie on it."

**Does it sound like people who genuinely believe they are ordinary?** Yes, and the test it
passes is that the defect is placed on the schools, never on themselves. The obvious wrong
version of this scene ("we're not normal applicants") is nowhere in it, and the obvious
half-wrong version ("the board has been measuring us against the wrong ninety people," which
the author struck in his notes) is also gone from the chapter. What is left is nine days of
letters explained as a marketing number, which is precisely "the outside case was defective."

**Nobody enjoys the reaction, nobody is smug, nobody is modest.** Checked every student beat:

- 21:149, Sam gets eleven of twelve "and wants to know, immediately, what happened to the
  twelfth." The only emotion in the sentence is irritation at the miss.
- 21:151, Odile gets in everywhere, "keeps it to herself, and it comes out three days later
  when Priya finds the letters stacked unopened in date order on her desk." Not modesty:
  modesty would require her to open them. She has not read them.
- 21:121, Chloe walks a rep through night watch "the way she'd walk anyone through a fire
  drill," and does not react when he asks her to say the part about the scoring again.
- 21:131, "The rep asks who Odile is, and Sam points across the dining hall with his fork."
- 21:41, Odile on the six-hundred-word limit: "six hundred is a minimum and every teacher in
  this building takes more and thanks you for it." A Halstead-sized sample applied to an
  outside rule, confidently and wrongly.
- 21:37, Sam: "Ten pages a week for four years, and the thing that decides where I spend the
  next four is a paragraph." Ten pages a week is the unit he measures the outside world with.

**21:125, tested hard, and it passes.** Sam to a recruiting rep: "Fighting. I'm first in my
year, out of everyone, not just close to first." Read cold this is the one line in the chapter
that could be smug. It is not, for three reasons on the page: it is a within-sample rank read
off a board anyone can walk past, said "the way you'd say there's a clock" (21:129); he
immediately downgrades his own measure, unprompted ("It only measures the drill, though, not
anything that happens outside the gym, and the drill's got a clock on it where a real one
wouldn't"); and he hands the rep the near-tie ("Odile's second, close enough it goes her way
plenty of the time"). `characters/SAM.md` backs this both ways: "YES if it states a fact about
himself with no softening around it," and the sheet's own long-form example (25:49) is him
talking a compliment down to a worse number. In calibration.

### Chapter 22 — the offer

**Chloe.** 22:41 and 22:113-117 are the calibration working at full strength. "If you sat the
same exam every year, in the same room, against the same ninety people, and you kept coming out
near the top of it, would you ever actually find out if you were good at the exam, or just good
against that particular room?" And, to her mother's "you're saying no to guaranteed money for
the chance of finding out you're not as good as you think you are": "I'm not saying I think I'm
good, only that I don't know, and I'd rather find out than get paid not to." She refuses both
halves of the trap in one line.

**The boy who turned it down (22:9).** His reason is his mother and "the decade of silence if
he skipped actual college for a paycheck," delivered "in the specific tone of somebody
rehearsing a line he means to use again at home." A family argument, not a status claim. The
chapter also closes the beat correctly: "the table lets it go without asking him the number."

**The girl who repeats the number twice (22:9).** The closest call in chapter 22, checked
carefully, and my read is that she is in calibration. She comes back "grinning" and says "the
whole thing twice, the number, the car, all of it." What she is amazed by is money and a car,
which is outside-world scale a sixteen-year-old has from her parents, not a fact about herself,
and the chapter has already made the offer universal rather than selective: 22:49, "Ninety-one
names go up in all over the nine days," i.e. every student in the year was interviewed and
eleven said yes. Being offered a job in April is the ordinary thing that happens to you here.
Nothing in her beat says "of me." No change.

One narration note attached to her, under House Rule 1 rather than calibration: "as though
saying it again will make it hold still long enough to be believed" names her mental state
after the grin and the repetition have already carried it. The rule's fix would be deletion,
but this is her only characterisation in the chapter, so flagging only.

**Kavi (22:61-67).** He says yes and his reasons are all about the building and the work:
"what I want to do is happening in one building and I'm standing in it"; "It stopped being
school for me a while ago, somewhere around the time the problems stopped having answers
already written down somewhere for someone to check me against"; "It's a lab that also has
children in it." He never once says he is ready for research earlier than other people are.
The subject of every sentence is the work. In calibration.

**22:37, worth naming.** "Mr. Amberg's pen stops moving, and he sets it down instead of writing
with it, a thing she has seen him do perhaps twice in eleven years." This is the one place in
the chapter where Chloe is handed evidence, through her own eyes, that she has just done
something out of the ordinary. It stays inside tolerance because she does nothing with it and
the next line is his "Reason?", so it reads as a fact about him. But it is the exact shape
section 2 warns about, and if any later pass wants a safety margin, this is the sentence.

Also: `characters/AMBERG.md` quotes a superseded version of this beat ("Mr. Amberg writes
something down and doesn't argue with her"). The sheet is behind the manuscript here; I went
with the manuscript.

**22:143, Dave.** "Good for him, actually, since he's the one of the four of you I'd have
guessed the least." An adult ranking the four of them. Adults are allowed to see; Chloe does
not pick it up, and the call moves on.

### Chapter 19 — sixteen and the bar

**Nobody registers the bar exam as an achievement, in either direction.** The author's
driving-licence example is met.

- 19:23-25, the board goes up and she reads her row "once for the verdict and once for the
  margin," then: "Fail. By four points, out of two hundred." Nothing about what the exam is.
- 19:33, "Fifty-two in the year pass and thirty-nine do not." A pass rate, printed like a pass
  rate.
- 19:29, Kavi's "I passed it by nine," followed by his hand coming up over his mouth. The
  etiquette breach is quoting a score at somebody who just failed, which is what it would be
  with any test. Nobody in the scene finds passing the bar itself notable.
- 19:101 is the best single line in my four chapters for this question: "She passes by
  twenty-two, and she reads the number twice before she puts the page away, and then Kavi sends
  back a string of exclamation points and Sam texts to ask what she got on the forklift
  question, so she tells him, asks what he got, and goes back to her book." A number, one
  re-read, one question about one item, and a book.
- 19:103, what does persist is the failure: "she still shuts books harder than she means to,
  though she could not tell anybody why she is doing it," in November, December and February.
  That is the correct asymmetry.

**19:43 is the calibration generating a mistake, which is the best possible use of it.**
"anyone who can read the second line can work out the other four for themselves in about ten
seconds, the same way I did." She assumes the marker's head is her head, and it costs her four
marks and a retake.

**19:49 is the sharpest sentence in these four chapters for question 2, and it holds.** Amberg:
"There was exactly one reader like that in this building in April, and the man marking your
paper was somebody else entirely." An adult telling her, in one clause, that she is singular in
a school of ninety-one. It is safe because it arrives as the diagnosis of a failure, no praise
word attaches to her (the staff test on `characters/AMBERG.md`), and 19:53 shows exactly what
she takes away: "So the four points are for saying the obvious part out loud, in the right
order, one row at a time." She converts a statement about her rarity into a rule about markers.
That is the standard's rationalisation mechanism working on a compliment.

## 3. Contrary evidence that goes unrationalised

**None found in any of the four chapters.**

**Chapter 20.** The contrary evidence is raised by the person best placed to raise it, and it is
raised twice. Sam, 20:117 and 20:121: "That was nothing... Even if it was only seven, it should
have taken something. I should have gotten some experience, gotten hit once, they could have
fired once. I mean, they're seven adults, Ruth. Why was it nothing?" It is answered inside
fifteen lines by Chloe and Ruth, accepted by Sam at 20:139, and then relitigated by the whole
school at breakfast (20:165). Raised, explained away, and the explanation stress-tested by
ninety more people. This is the mechanism end to end.

**Chapter 21.** The largest contrary evidence in the book so far never reaches a student, by
design: the nine offices calling the transcript a formatting error (21:57-59), the ghostwriting
theory (21:61-63), the Penn officer reading the ten-person line three times (21:65), and the
Caltech reading room (21:79-93) are all narrated from outside, with no student in the room. The
one piece that does reach a student in scene is the Duke rep's "What's the hardest thing you've
done here?" (21:135), and Ruth refuses the question's form rather than treating it as a
distinction: "That's not a well-formed question... Hardest by what measure, and compared to
what?" (21:137). Deflection, not acceptance. Then the four percent, handled above.

Worth stating plainly since it is the chapter's whole design: eleven universities send
representatives to camp in the building for a week, and not one student anywhere in the chapter
treats that as information about themselves. They answer questions and go to class.

**Chapter 22.** Chloe's parents supply the contrary evidence directly and hard, which is the
correct place for it: "That's not a decision, that's a mistake, however it's dressed up"
(22:103); "you turned down more money than your mother and I have made in any five years
combined" (22:111); "We still think it's a mistake" (22:145). She never once answers with a
claim about her own ability. Her answer is that the measurement is bad (22:113) and that she
does not know (22:117), and she absorbs the cost rather than deflecting it: "It's not free, and
you and Dad are going to have to figure out how to pay for it... and it's my fault that you
have to" (22:133).

**Chapter 19.** Amberg's 19:49 and Delacroix's 19:91 both hand her outside assessments, and both
get converted into procedure rather than self-knowledge.

## 4. Scale announced rather than buried

This is where all four of my findings are, and none of them is a character. All four are
narration, and three are residues of the author's own notes being applied only as far as the
sentence he quoted.

**(a) 20:83, the flag I would act on first, and cannot touch.**

> The fence drill has been run at this speed every term since they were nine, and from four
> meters away it looks like three people walked backwards out of the world.

The first clause is the buried version and does the job. The second steps outside the scene to
admire the drill for the reader. `passes/notes/ch20.md` flags this exact sentence: *"It is not
quick for them. It is ordinary, done at the speed the fence drill has been run at every term
since they were nine, and from four meters away it looks like three people walked backwards out
of the world." is talking to the reader.* The revision removed "It is not quick for them. It is
ordinary," and kept the clause the quotation ended on. It sits inside the protected fight span,
so it is reported only. **Recommendation if the protection is ever lifted: delete from "and from
four meters away" to the end of the sentence.** Nothing is lost; the muggers' reaction two lines
later (20:87, "Where'd they go?") already carries it.

**(b) 21:63.** "...and two of the ninety-one take opposite positions on the same question with
both worth reading, the one thing a template could not possibly produce." The final clause
finishes an inference the two clauses before it have already delivered. Strike it and read the
page: the theory still dies, because "no two of them argue the same way" is right there. This
is the closest thing to a House Rule 1 / section 3 violation in my four chapters. Not edited,
because the clause is also load-bearing for the plot mechanics of why the committees give up on
the mill theory, so cutting it is a judgment call rather than an unambiguous violation.

**(c) 22:33.** The one place in these chapters where a total of her own work is assembled inside
a single sentence:

> She's taught the twelves real analysis out of a room two floors below this one... She's
> dropped three flawed hinges of her own into the scrap bin rather than let them stand, and
> gone back to the forge every Thursday for five years without anyone having to ask her twice,
> because the standard she was holding the work to was hers before it was anyone else's, and
> all of it sits outside any dollar figure, in a place the number on the desk in front of her
> leaves entirely alone.

The list itself is within tolerance: three items, each framed as ordinary ("the ordinary next
thing rather than a favor"), and it is her own recall at the moment of decision rather than a
narrator's tally. The closing gloss is the part I would look at. It explains the meaning of the
recollection, and it explains it into a reason the scene then does not use: her stated reason
four lines later (22:41) is about the room and the measurement, not about money versus values.
House Rule 1's own test says nothing is lost by striking it. **Not edited**, because
`passes/notes/ch22.md` shows the author revised this exact paragraph, fixed the three things he
objected to in it, and deliberately kept the money-sits-outside content. That makes the cut his
call.

**(d) Smaller, listed for completeness.**

- 21:99, "comparing notes the way scouts do on a prospect." A simile that frames the school from
  outside as a find. Mild; it describes what the reps are doing and attaches no number.
- 21:57, "except there are so many of them." The one point where the narrator names volume
  rather than letting the rows do it, though the same sentence immediately converts the volume
  into somebody's clerical error, which buys it back.
- 19:87, "at a speed where the cup is the more impressive of the two achievements." The only use
  of "impressive" in these four chapters. It points at a driving instructor's coffee, not at a
  child, so it does not touch calibration; it is the narrator being audible.
- 19:17, "an event visiting Sam perhaps twice a year and never on purpose." Same shape, harmless.

**And the places the scale is buried correctly, which is most of it.** 20:7, the eleven-day
preparation split four ways, given as one sentence of logistics with no total. 19:5, eleven
teachers wrote the paper and mark it themselves across twelve hours, stated as room
arrangements. 19:81, "She writes thirty-one of those between May and September... by August the
rate has come down to one in six and settles there, declining to improve" — scale in an aside
with a failure attached to it. 19:83, an entire year of staged emergency driving, each named
once and dropped. 21:73 and 21:93, the escalation to thirty institutions and then to visit
requests, stated flat with no adjective. 22:5 and 22:7, nine days of interviews and the school
timetable carrying on unchanged around them.

## 5. Anywhere the age has gone missing

**Nowhere.** The age is present in every chapter, and in chapter 20 it is doing heavy lifting.

- 20:65, eleven minutes out of a broken shopping cart, Sam going over the handlebar into the
  grass and lying there laughing with his arms out, Chloe laughing hard enough to have to sit
  down on the curb with her head between her knees, the wheel left turning. Four minutes before
  they are attacked at gunpoint, they are sixteen.
- 20:15, Sam eating the crisp top edge of every waffle before he touches the syrup; Nadia
  falling asleep sitting up and then waking and finishing her sentence.
- 20:161, shoes set down by the door "laces still knotted from being kicked off rather than
  untied," and lying on top of the covers a while before getting under them.
- 19:117-129, the summer: sleeping through the first week, four novels in a fortnight she cannot
  afterward tell anybody the endings of, a wasp working the same board every day, and "I could
  put up a shelf. I just haven't wanted to move more than I have to since I got home."
- 19:143, unfairness at full size: "watching her be that cheerful about something this boring is
  genuinely infuriating."
- 21:15, crossing out the same line twice "for no reason she could name"; 21:17, "What happens
  if all fourteen say no"; 21:21, a fourteenth school picked off a phone screen after
  lights-out because it came up on the same page as another one; 21:155, sitting on the floor
  with the letters in two uneven rows; 21:189, the letters going back in the drawer because
  there is an exam on Thursday.
- 22:11, the boy re-reading the same page of a textbook with the words sliding past him and the
  girl sitting perfectly still staring at the closed door, both of them in the chairs half an
  hour early. 22:133, "it's my fault that you have to," which is the childish size the scene
  needed. 22:155, getting up to find something to eat.

One observation rather than a finding: chapter 21 runs from 21:57 to 21:143 with no student
interiority at all, the reps' section being narrated entirely from outside. Nothing is missing
from it and the design is deliberate, but it is the longest stretch in these four chapters with
no childish want anywhere in it, and it is the stretch that most depends on the reader being
trusted.

## What is working

Briefly, so the audit can be seen to distinguish:

- **The waitress argument, 20:145-153.** Four teenagers who have just watched one of them take
  seven armed adults apart, seriously agreeing that a fifty-year-old waitress would beat him,
  and pricing it in years of being in the world against years spent at school. It states their
  baseline for an average adult without a single word about what they think they are.
- **Chloe's speech, 20:127-131.** The author's worked example, executed exactly: four separate
  defects in the attackers, then the categorical explanation, then the conclusion aimed at the
  seven men rather than at the four of them.
- **The four percent, 21:175-187.** Ninety-one out of ninety-one is treated as the true number
  and the schools' four percent as the false one. The rationalisation is in dialogue, split
  across three characters, and every version of it points outward.
- **19:101.** Passing the bar handled in one sentence that ends with her going back to her book.
- **The structure of 22:49.** Making all ninety-one interviewed, rather than eleven selected,
  does more calibration work than any line of dialogue could: a job offer at eighteen is what
  April is, not what you are.
- **21:129 and 21:137.** Sam downgrading his own measure unprompted, and Ruth refusing a
  superlative question on the grounds that it is badly formed. Two different characters, two
  different ways of declining to be exceptional, neither of them modest.
