# Narrative-distance breaks inside dramatized scenes

Read-only audit. Nothing in `chapters/` was changed. Every fix below is a proposal.

All thirty-six chapters read in full (114,177 words), then swept mechanically for the
three shapes the defect takes:

1. a quoted line, a stretch of **reported** speech, a quoted line, in the same scene
2. a live scene that jumps minutes or hours in a clause and comes back to the same talk
3. one side of an exchange quoted and the other narrated

Line numbers are deliberately omitted; quotes are given verbatim so they can be
re-found by search while other agents are editing.

---

## Headline

**Twenty-eight real instances. Six of them hurt. The habit is not widespread, but it
is not random either: it clusters hard in the childhood chapters and again in 15-16,
and it has a strong tendency to strike at exactly the moment a scene reaches its
point.**

That last part is the finding that matters. This is not a book that drifts into
summary out of laziness — chapters 22, 27 and 32 use reported speech with real
discipline, and there are at least four places where a summary paragraph is
unambiguously the right call and does a job no quoted scene could. The problem is
narrower and worse: in about a dozen places the prose reaches the load-bearing beat
of a scene — the plea, the verdict, the counter-offer, the correction nobody
believes — and hands it over in reported speech, and then goes straight back into
quotation marks. The reader is standing in the room, then is told what happened in
the room, then is standing in it again.

Distribution of the twenty-eight:

| severity | count | chapters |
|---|---|---|
| severe | 3 | 5, 9, 31 |
| high | 3 | 8, 16, 25 |
| moderate | 6 | 3, 4, 6, 15 (×2), 24 |
| mild | 16 | 1, 3, 5, 10, 11, 15 (×3), 16, 18, 19, 21 (×2), 23 (×2), 26, 31 |

Chapters with nothing to report: 2, 7, 12, 13, 14, 17, 20, 22, 27, 28, 29, 30, 32,
33, 34, 35, 36. Seventeen of thirty-six are clean.

A note on what was *not* counted, because the count is only honest if the exclusions
are stated: chapters 2, 11, 12 and 13 run long stretches of deliberate montage; the
chat transcripts in 24, 26, 31-36 follow their own rules; the reps montage in 21 and
the graduation sweep in 23 are ensemble passages at chosen distance; and the book's
habit of ending a scene on a reported last line (chapter 19's "Sam goes quiet the
whole length of the corridor, then says he would like to withdraw ten of his pages")
is a style choice, used dozens of times, and is a transition rather than a break.
Those are all left alone. See **Considered and rejected** at the end.

# The ranked list

## 1. Chapter 9 — Chloe's plea on the bedroom floor
`chapters/09_february.md` · **SEVERE**

The emotional climax of the first act, and it is reported.

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Chloe, honey, what are you doing down there on the floor?"

**The summary passage, in full** (the second paragraph is the break; the first is the
hinge into it):
> Then it comes out of her all at once and she can't get one word of it out whole, so
> what comes out is please, over and over, in pieces, in the gaps where she can get
> any air.
>
> She says she'll be good, and that she'll do the dishes every single night for the
> rest of her life. She'll do her dad's jobs as well, all of them, before anybody has
> to ask her. They can take the bike back, because she has no use for it and had none
> for it in July either. Christmas can go, this year and every year after it, and they
> can have her birthday too, the whole thing, cake and candles, since a birthday is
> one more thing she can do without. Please, and please again, and that one thing is
> the last thing she will ever ask anybody for, for the whole rest of her life, if
> they will please just let her go to that school.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Baby, breathe for me, just breathe, because you have to breathe before you can talk
> about any of it."

**Swallowed:** the whole speech — four separate offers, at minimum a minute of a
seven-year-old talking, and the single most important thing she says in the first
nine chapters.

**Why it hurts more than the others.** The narration says she "can't get one word of
it out whole," and then delivers the plea as four clean, ordered, grammatical
sentences. The report contradicts the staging. It also arrives immediately after a
quoted line and is followed immediately by a quoted line, so the reader is walked out
of the room and back in across the exact span where the room matters. And the scene
proves it can be done: three lines later "I can't do it anymore" is quoted, broken,
repeated, and it lands — which is the demonstration that the offers would have landed
harder still.

Two chapters later the mother repeats one of these offers to the father, in quotes,
and it is the strongest line in that scene: *"She said take my birthday, Dave, and
she's seven years old, and she stood there in that gap and offered up her own birthday
like it was money she'd saved."* The book knows the line is good. It gave it to the
wrong mouth.

**Fix: dramatize.** This is the scene's actual content. The exchange is a child
bidding: dishes, then her father's jobs as well, then the bike, then Christmas, then
her birthday, each offer bigger than the last because the last one did not work, all
of it broken by the crying the paragraph above already staged. Keep the mother mostly
silent through it. Cutting is not available here — nothing downstream implies the
offers except the mother's later quotation of one of them, which currently has to
carry the whole beat secondhand.

## 2. Chapter 31 — the professor's office hours
`chapters/31_ruth.md` · **SEVERE**

The hinge of Ruth's entire arc, delivered at balcony distance.

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "It holds because the boundary term cancels once you fix the sign convention, and
> once that's fixed the rest follows directly."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> Somewhere in it he loses the thread, says so, and asks her to start again from the
> top.
>
> From the top, then. Step one, step two, and at step three he stops her again, admits
> he's still lost, and asks whether she'd mind slowing down further than that. She
> slows down. The assumption she'd folded into a single line comes apart into the
> separate claims underneath, and she states each on its own and watches him nod at
> the first and hesitate at the second. It's the second, she realizes watching him
> hesitate, that she has always kept private, checked only against her own head, where
> it always came out fine.
>
> Eventually he gets there, the whole way through, and tells her the result holds. He
> also tells her, setting his pen down, that it took her five tries to explain
> something most of his graduate students would get in two. It's an observation rather
> than a complaint, and he's already moving on to the next question on his list. Ruth
> thanks him, packs the loose pages into her folder in the wrong order, and leaves
> before office hours are officially over.

**The scene does not resume.** Next line: *Ruth walks out and sits on a wall until the
light changes twice.*

**Swallowed:** five attempts at an explanation, three interventions from the
professor, and the verdict — *five tries to explain something most of his graduate
students would get in two* — which is the sentence that ends Ruth's remedial-track
theory and starts everything she does for the rest of the book.

**Why it hurts.** The chapter opens the scene with one quoted line, establishes that
this is a man she likes and the only person who has ever pushed back on her, then
narrates the confrontation and reports the verdict. Everything the chapter has been
building for eight pages arrives as indirect speech. Compare the same chapter's
handling of an incident that matters far less — Devansh's flipped sign — which gets a
quoted line. The scale is inverted.

There is also a smaller cost: the reader never hears the professor's voice, so the
kindness of the delivery ("cheerfully, without embarrassment," "an observation rather
than a complaint") has to be asserted by the narrator rather than heard, and asserting
it is exactly the construction House Rule 1 warns about.

**Fix: dramatize.** The exchange is a proof being taken apart three times by a man who
is not being unkind and is not going to stop. He needs at most four lines: the first
admission that he is lost, the request to slow down further, the moment he hesitates at
the second claim, and the closing observation. Ruth needs the restatements, getting
shorter each time. The verdict sentence in particular should be his, in quotes, and
should be the last thing said before she leaves; the wall outside then does the rest
without a word of gloss.

## 3. Chapter 5 — the fractions lesson
`chapters/05_behind.md` · **SEVERE**

The clearest textbook case in the book: quoted, then two paragraphs of pure reported
classroom, then quoted, all in one continuous hour.

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Good, that's the question," Mr. Baptiste says, capping the marker and uncapping it
> before he turns back to the board. "Okay, so what is dividing actually asking you,
> underneath the flipping part."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> He draws a rectangle, shades half of it, then cuts the shaded half into pieces,
> talking the whole way through about the question underneath the question, and Chloe
> follows him as far as the shading, loses track of what the pieces are pieces of, and
> is left looking at a rectangle with lines across it.
>
> Sam gets it, announcing the fact with one loud grunt; Kavi says he already knew it,
> out of a book, and is told that this is fine but he should listen anyway; Ruth, well
> down the packet, is on to the next question.
>
> Marisol asks him to do the rectangle again from the beginning, and he redraws it
> exactly as before, line for line. Owen asks something further back than that, about
> which of the two numbers the flipping is supposed to happen to, and when Mr.
> Baptiste answers and puts a question back Owen looks at the board and says he has
> lost the thread of it, so he is told that this is fine, that the whole thing gets
> done again tomorrow, and he goes back to his sheet, where he stays, quiet, for the
> rest of the lesson.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Does it work with negative numbers too, or is that a different rule again," somebody
> asks from near the back.

**Swallowed:** at least seven turns of dialogue between four named characters and the
teacher — Kavi's claim and the reply to it, Marisol's request, Owen's question, the
teacher's answer, the teacher's counter-question, Owen's admission, the teacher's
reassurance. The scene then runs on in quotes for another eight lines.

**Why it hurts.** Owen leaves the school two sections later, and this is his only
speaking moment in the book. His line — *he has lost the thread of it* — is the setup
for a departure the whole chapter is about and that Chloe is still worrying at five
chapters later. It is reported. Marisol's request is what Chloe remembers about the
month when Dr. Ammons asks her in chapter 8 what she liked ("Mr. Baptiste doing the
whole rectangle over from the beginning for Marisol, quiet about it") — so the book
later relies on a beat it never staged.

There is a second cost specific to this scene: Chloe is the one who asked the question,
and the point of the passage is that the room has taken her question away from her
("watches a conversation go on over her head about a question she asked first"). That
line only pays if the reader has *heard* the conversation going on over her head. At
present the reader is also outside it.

**Fix: dramatize, selectively.** Owen's exchange is the one that has to be spoken: his
question, the teacher's answer, the counter-question, Owen saying he has lost it. Two
or three lines. Marisol's "can you do it again from the beginning" can be one clause of
quoted dialogue. Sam's grunt and Kavi's claim are fine where they are — they are the
kind of staging the book uses well.

## 4. Chapter 16 — the cipher napkin at dinner
`chapters/16_thirteen.md` · **HIGH**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "A parking system, live, protecting somebody's actual licence plates right now."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> Kavi takes it off him inside a minute, offhand about it, and slides it to Chloe,
> while Ruth reads it upside down and says the key is a date, probably in March. Priya
> wants to know whether they are allowed to be holding it at all, which starts a second
> argument, and Odile says the interesting question is why a parking company bought
> that and what the salesman told them, which starts a third. Sam eats a second dinner
> through all three arguments and contributes twice.

**First quoted line after** (two lines later, same table, same minute):
> "It's not a date, it's a plate."

**Swallowed:** four named characters' speech, three simultaneous arguments, and an
unspecified number of Sam's contributions.

**Why it hurts.** Ruth's reported line — *the key is a date, probably in March* — is
the exact proposition Nadia's quoted line contradicts. The setup is narrated and the
punchline is spoken, so the punchline arrives without a heard antagonist. Then, four
lines further on, Chloe's own quoted line ("It could be a date, since dates have
letters in them too, sometimes") defends a position the reader was told about rather
than heard, which flattens the exchange it opens.

This is also the one dinner-table scene in the book where the whole table is on it at
once, and the table is the book's central social unit. Reporting it costs a set-piece.

**Fix: dramatize the first half, cut the second.** Ruth's line has to be spoken —
it is one clause. Priya's objection is worth a line because it is a Priya move and it
recurs. Odile's "why did a parking company buy that" can go entirely: it starts a third
argument the scene never returns to, and cutting it removes the traffic jam that makes
the paragraph read as a list. Keep "Sam eats a second dinner through all three
arguments and contributes twice" — that is staging, it is funny, and it works.

## 5. Chapter 8 — Dr. Ammons's questions
`chapters/08_the_asking.md` · **HIGH**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Tell me about the program, all of it, from the beginning."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> The questions keep coming after that, and all of them are careful, working down
> through who ran it, and how they found her, and whether Chloe stayed overnight, and
> how far away it was from the house, whether the same adults were there the whole
> time, whether anything happened, whether Chloe came back different, and in what way,
> and starting when.
>
> All the questions go to her mother, and Chloe sits through it, her thread-picking
> gone still somewhere around the question about the adults.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Everything about it was good, she absolutely loved every single day of it."

**Swallowed:** eight questions and eight answers, several minutes, and the entire
substance of a doctor working out whether a child was abused at a residential program
— with the child in the room.

**Why it hurts.** This is the highest-stakes exchange in the chapter and one of the
most charged in the book, and none of it is spoken. The line that follows the break
("I'm sure she did, but I'm asking anyway, because loving a place and something
happening there can both be true at once") is excellent, and it is the *reply* to a
question the reader never heard asked. The mother's answers, which are the whole
tension of the passage — a woman defending the best month of her daughter's life
against an implication she has just understood — are absent entirely.

The one thing the summary does well is the last clause: *her thread-picking gone still
somewhere around the question about the adults.* That is the beat, and it needs the
question about the adults to have actually been asked out loud in Chloe's hearing.

**Fix: dramatize, at half length.** Three or four of these questions, spoken, with the
mother's answers getting shorter, and the question about the adults landing where the
thread-picking stops. The remaining four can stay in a trailing clause. This is not a
case for cutting: the paragraph is currently the only place the reader is told the
doctor suspected something, and it is told rather than shown.

## 6. Chapter 25 — the chow hall
`chapters/25_forty_targets.md` · **HIGH**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Of the shots. There were people in my year who never missed."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> The table takes this for modesty and tells him so at some volume, because the score
> has already gone up on a board in the hallway outside the orderly room. Sam runs the
> correction all the way out anyway. Odile comes out first, a girl who took him apart
> on a mat in front of thirty people, then apologised for it; then the April he lost a
> small object out of his own boot and cost somebody else half an hour of an evening
> getting it back; by the end of it several of them are laughing at him, while another
> has stopped believing a word of any of it.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Boarding school," says the third.

**Swallowed:** the table's response, Sam's entire attempt at correction (two anecdotes,
both of which the reader already knows and would enjoy hearing him tell to strangers),
and the point at which the room turns from teasing to disbelief.

**Why it hurts.** The chapter's whole subject is that Sam cannot get anybody to believe
the true thing about himself. That failure happens exactly once in the chapter at
conversational scale, and it is narrated. It also wastes the best joke available: Sam,
in a mess hall, telling nineteen infantrymen that a girl took him apart on a mat and
then apologised. The quoted line that follows ("Boarding school," says the third) is
the deadpan payoff to a run-up the reader did not get to watch.

Compare the range scene later in the same chapter, where the identical dynamic — Sam
telling the truth, an adult refusing it — is fully dramatized and is the best passage
in the chapter. The book has already shown the right way to do this beat, thirty
paragraphs later.

**Fix: dramatize.** Two of Sam's lines and two from the table. The Odile story is the
one to give him, because it is the one that makes a table of soldiers laugh and then
stop. The lost-object story can stay in narration or go. "By the end of it several of
them are laughing at him, while another has stopped believing a word of any of it" is
a good closing clause and should survive as the bridge into "Boarding school."

## 7. Chapter 15 — the forge, October to November
`chapters/15_twelve.md` · **MODERATE** (cluster of three in one section)

Not one break but a flicker: the register switches five times inside a continuous
exchange, twice within four sentences.

**The passage, in full:**
> Sam picks a hook off her bench in November and says it beats anything else down
> there. "The scroll's wrong, right at the top curve." She takes it back, still warm at
> the thick end, and turns it until her thumb finds where the metal stops giving under
> her nail. "It goes tight and then opens out, right where my thumb is, because that's
> two curves welded in the middle pretending to be one, so hold it up at the window and
> you'll see it." He holds it up with an eye shut, and she tells him the shadow runs
> straight for an inch where it ought to be turning the whole way down.
>
> Sam hands the hook back, tells her it still looks fine to him and takes his coat off
> the peg because it is getting dark...

Sam's compliment: reported. Chloe's reply: quoted. Her second point: quoted. Her third
point: reported. Sam's answer: reported. Five switches, no signal for any of them.

The same section does it again, one paragraph on:
> That month Bell takes the metronome off the post and pockets it in front of all of
> them. "You've had that long enough, and a machine doing your counting for you is two
> years too long, so from today one of you calls the count out loud and the other works
> off the call." **Odile asks which of them is supposed to call it.** "That's the two of
> you to sort out," he says, buttoning the pocket...

Quoted / reported / quoted, inside four lines, with Odile's question — a five-word
question — the only thing narrated.

**Swallowed:** individually, almost nothing. Cumulatively, the reader loses track of
whether they are in the room, which is the complaint the author is making.

**A compounding factor worth flagging separately.** This whole opening section of
chapter 15 runs dialogue *inside* narrative paragraphs rather than on its own lines,
which no other chapter in the book does. That formatting is doing half the damage on
its own: a quoted line buried mid-paragraph reads as reported even when it isn't. If
the section is meant to be a compressed montage, the quotes should come out; if it is
meant to be scenes, the paragraphing should match chapters 14 and 16.

**Fix: pick one register per beat, and it should be quotes.** Sam's compliment is one
clause and should be spoken ("That's better than anything down here" or similar) — it
is the setup for her rejecting it. Her third point can be cut outright: the next
sentence, Sam handing the hook back and saying it still looks fine, already implies
she pressed. Odile's question should simply be in quotes.

## 8. Chapter 15 — the bread test, design argument
`chapters/15_twelve.md` · **MODERATE**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Do it how?" Priya says, rolling onto her front. "Somebody has to know which plate is
> which or you can't score it, and whoever knows is the person who wrecks it for
> everybody."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> They spend an hour on that, most of it Priya taking other people's ideas apart, and
> what comes out is two rounds with the cutting swapped: Kavi cuts both loaves for the
> first, marks the underside of the plates and leaves the eating to everybody else,
> then Ruth runs the second so that Kavi gets a turn, and the key goes onto a folded
> scrap before anybody starts.

**First quoted line after** (one paragraph later, same night, same group):
> "We are not doing thirty slices of toast at midnight, Kavi. Eight is plenty for a
> proper test."

**Swallowed:** an hour, and an argument the clause explicitly says was the good part.

**Why it hurts.** "Most of it Priya taking other people's ideas apart" is a promise the
paragraph then declines to keep. This book's whole pleasure is children taking each
other's ideas apart; here it is named and skipped. The protocol that comes out of the
hour is genuinely necessary information and the summary handles it well — the loss is
only Priya.

**Fix: dramatize one beat, keep the rest.** Two lines: Priya killing one proposal, then
the proposal that survives. Then the protocol paragraph exactly as written, minus the
"most of it Priya taking other people's ideas apart" clause, which will have become
redundant. Do not stage the whole hour; the scene is already the longest in the
chapter.

## 9. Chapter 24 — the bank counter
`chapters/24_the_chat.md` · **MODERATE**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "What size does it take."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> The figure he gives is more than she has ever had at once. Sitting with it a second,
> she gives him the figure she can actually hold across a month, which is a few hundred
> dollars under his, and tells him what holding it costs her, which is that the
> difference sits in his branch instead of in her hand on the fourth of every month.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Three hundred under isn't the number."

**Swallowed:** the pivot of the negotiation — the manager's figure and Nadia's
counter-offer, which is the move the whole scene is built to deliver.

**Why it hurts.** Everything either side of this is quoted, including the closing
("You've spent twenty-two minutes on me"), so the register break is unmistakable. The
scene is one of four negotiations in the book and the only one where the actual offer

**Fix: dramatize.** Two lines. His figure, then hers, with the cost attached — "the
difference sits in your branch instead of in my hand on the fourth of every month" is
already Nadia's voice and only needs the quotation marks. This is her only real
negotiating line in the scene and it is currently paraphrase.

## 10. Chapter 6 — the last-night dinner monologue
`chapters/06_the_list.md` · **MODERATE** (three occurrences, one scene)

Three times in one scene, a sentence of Chloe's own speech is demoted to narration and
set between two quoted sentences of the same speech, with no signal either way.

> "...where a square folds right over." **Everybody had to write down a prediction, in
> pounds, before anything got loaded.** "I wrote fourteen and was wrong by three..."

> "...with everybody's mouth shut." **She and Ruth did that for a whole afternoon, and
> the librarian worked out what they were doing and let them keep going anyway.**

> "...how the entire grammar went from the sentences themselves." **By about the sixth
> sentence she had worked out that the words come in a different order than English
> does.** "Ruth got the plurals before I did..."

**Swallowed:** three sentences of speech, no time at all.

**Why it hurts.** These are not staging. They carry content she is plainly saying out
loud to her father — the prediction rule, the afternoon in the library, the sixth
sentence — and they are in her diction, not the narrator's. Dropping out of quotes for
one sentence and back in reads as a mistake rather than a choice, and it happens three
times in four pages.

Note that this is a different question from the long "Then the wood shop, and the
goggles again..." accelerando later in the same scene, which is deliberate, which is
what the chapter is named for, and which should be left completely alone. See
**Considered and rejected**.

**Fix: cut two, restore one.** The library afternoon and the sixth sentence can go —
the quoted sentences either side already carry them, and the paragraph is long. The
prediction rule should go back inside the quotation marks; it is a fact she is
explaining to him and it sets up "I wrote fourteen and was wrong by three," which is
currently a number with no rule under it.

## 11. Chapter 3 — the call to the school
`chapters/03_the_letter.md` · **MODERATE**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Right, okay, thank you so much for going through all of that with me."

**The summary passage, in full:**
> Then the call goes on a long time after that and her mom's voice changes into the one
> she uses at the pediatrician, asking how many adults sleep in the building, what the
> ratio is at night, and whether that number changes in the daytime.
>
> Whether the staff are licensed, and by who, and whether she can see that in writing;
> what happens if a child gets a fever in the middle of the night, then what happens if
> it is worse than a fever; whether there is a nurse or a person they call the nurse.
>
> What Chloe eats, and whether Chloe can be pulled out on any day, for any reason, or
> whether there is a form, or a notice period, or a fee, and then, near the end of it,
> how many children are going to be in the building.

**First quoted line after:**
> "Over a hundred," her mom says, loud enough that the number carries...

**Swallowed:** most of a long phone call, a dozen questions, and every answer.

**Why it hurts less than the ones above.** The accelerating list is doing real work —
it is a portrait of a frightened woman running out of questions, and Chloe is on the
stairs hearing only one side, which is a POV constraint the book uses consistently and
well. The following line, *Every single question gets answered straight away, immediate
and complete, as though the questions had all been expected*, is the payoff and it
needs the list.

**Why it is still on the list.** It is three paragraphs long, it sits between two
quoted lines in the same call, and it makes the reader wait through a full page of
reported speech before the scene resumes. The three-paragraph run is the problem, not
the technique.

**Fix: compress rather than dramatize.** Cut to one paragraph. The nurse, the fever,
the pull-out, and the headcount are the four that matter; the licensing and the ratio
duplicate them. A single paragraph will read as an accelerando; three read as an
absence.

## 12. Chapter 4 — the hotel room, first night
`chapters/04_pluto.md` · **MODERATE**

**Last quoted line before the break:**
> "Do you want to talk about it?"

**The summary passage, in full:**
> Chloe says no, so her mom gets up, turns the other bed down, sits on the end of it,
> and asks whether Chloe would like her to talk about something instead, and Chloe says
> yes, anything.
>
> So her mom talks, doing the drive down, and the place where they stopped, and the
> woman on the desk downstairs who has a son at a college in a state Chloe couldn't
> find.

**Does not resume.** Next is the hum of the June story and then the section break.

**Swallowed:** three turns of dialogue, including "yes, anything," which is the most
exposed thing Chloe says in the chapter.

**Why it is only moderate.** The drift into summary is defensible in the second
paragraph — Chloe is falling asleep, and the prose blurring with her is a legitimate
effect, and "somewhere in the middle of it the words blur into one long hum, still
going" earns it. The break is the *first* paragraph, which happens while she is fully
awake and which reports a small, precise, painful exchange: a mother asking a
six-year-old whether she wants to be talked at, and the six-year-old saying yes.

**Fix: dramatize the first paragraph only.** Two short lines — the mother's offer and
"yes, anything" — then let the second paragraph do exactly what it currently does. The
drift then has something to drift away from.

# The remaining sixteen, by chapter

These are real but small. Each is a single reported reply or question sitting inside a
live exchange. Listed for completeness; none of them would be noticed on its own, and
some may be deliberate. In every case the fix is the cheap one — either put the line in
quotes, or cut it because the next line already implies it.

**Chapter 1** (1) — the sled-dog dinner. *"She took your book," her dad says, and Chloe
says until the end of the day, but by then everybody had moved on to worksheets, so
having it back barely helped.* Her reply is the only one in the scene not spoken. Fix:
quote it.

**Chapter 3** (1) — the parents after the district call. *Her mom tells him she is only
repeating what the woman told her*, one line after "She said people do this..." and one
line before the scene dissolves into the Petersons. Fix: cut; the dissolve already
carries it.

**Chapter 5** (1) — the bridge load test. *Sam says the number out loud twice, then that
he would have written a lower one if anybody had let him*, immediately before Ruth's
quoted "I told you." Fix: quote Sam; it is the funny half of the beat.

**Chapter 10** (1) — the wheel problem at the board. *Chloe tells her all of it, out of
order and twice in places*, between Ruth's two quoted lines. Fix: leave it. The
seven-step derivation is genuinely not worth staging and Ruth's next line reports the
result better than Chloe could.

**Chapter 11** (1) — the hot dog argument. *On the eighth he concedes on a technicality
about structural intent, and Chloe tells him a concession counts however he gets to it.*
Fix: cut. The next scene has the father telling the mother about it, which is where the
payoff belongs.

**Chapter 15** (3) — Priya on the stairs (*Priya wants to know whether the first round
still counts*); the landing light (*Sam has it at sixty-two and a half before the paper
is open, and says that beats guessing*); Thanksgiving (*her father... turns it a half
turn on the cloth to ask how you catch it*). All three sit directly against quoted
lines. Fix: quote all three; they are one line each.

**Chapter 16** (1) — Marek's oral exam. *Five questions off the sheet get five answers,
the last by a route she had failed to consider.* Fix: leave it. The content is unnamed
mathematics and the clause is the right length.

**Chapter 18** (1) — Voss in the doorway of the range office. Chloe's quoted "So what is
it, then, if you know it's real?" gets *Voss taps the pages straight against the desk,
hands them back to her and tells her to get down to point six.* Fix: quote the
dismissal. It is the beat where the adult refuses her and it should be in his voice.

**Chapter 19** (1) — the back steps. *Her father nods at that and asks about Kavi, so she
tells him about Kavi, and then about Priya, and then about the negotiation board...*
between two quoted questions. Fix: leave it. This is a legitimate conversational bridge
and it sets up the quoted question that follows.

**Chapter 21** (2) — Odile's essay (*when Chloe tells her it is many times the limit
Odile says the limit is a minimum and every teacher in this building takes more and
thanks you for it*), and the transcript phone call (*The registrar reads the
accreditation number back to her, twice, and asks whether she'd like that document
mailed again, and the caller declines and hangs up first*). Both are punchlines
delivered as paraphrase. Fix: quote Odile's line; quote the registrar's offer to mail it
again, which is the joke.

**Chapter 23** (2) — Ruth's mother and Meg (*Meg tells her any day now, that he's already
looking at rooms*, between two quoted lines); Dave and the physics professor (the entire
reply and the discovery of who he is, reported after Dave's one quoted line). Fix: quote
Meg; leave the professor, which is an ensemble sweep and reads as intended.

**Chapter 26** (1) — Ives at the culvert. *"Say the contact rule."* is answered by *Sam
says the contact rule, close to word for word.* Fix: leave it. The rule is quoted at the
AAR twenty paragraphs later and quoting it twice would be worse.

**Chapter 31** (1) — Devansh. *He fixes it, thanks her, and asks, half joking, whether
she's ever actually stuck on anything. No, she says.* Fix: quote both. It is four words
and it is the theme of the chapter said out loud by accident.

# Considered and rejected

Listed so the count above can be trusted, and so a later pass does not re-flag them.

**Chapter 2, the whole testing sequence.** Each section is one subtest, staged then
compressed. The Q&A patterning in the wash-your-hands section (*"Tell me another
reason." / Chloe goes back over what she said... / "Why do people keep their money in a
bank..."*) is the strongest formal candidate in the chapter and is deliberate: the
sameness of her answers is the point, and the section closes on *she stops trusting the
questions and watches the pen instead.* Leave it.

**Chapter 6, the "Then the wood shop, then chess, then negative numbers" accelerando.**
This is a designed effect, the chapter is named for it, and it ends on her repeating
herself while her father goes quiet. Not a defect. The three demoted sentences elsewhere
in the same scene are a separate matter and are item 10.

**Chapter 22, both interviews and the phone call home.** Amberg's offer to Nadia and
Chloe's second telling to her father are both reported specifically to avoid repeating
what the reader has just read verbatim. This is the correct use of the technique and the
best example of it in the book.

**Chapter 27, "She says it again, exactly, word for word and at the same speed."** Same
device, same reason, and here it also characterises her. Leave alone.

**Chapter 30, the twenty-minute list of classes in the Whitaker interview.** Compresses
material the reader has spent twenty chapters acquiring. Correct call.

**Chapter 31, the registrar and the department office.** Both rendered entirely in
reported speech with no quoted dialogue on either side, so there is no switch. The
flatness is the point — Ruth is being processed. Not an instance.

**The anonymous-prompt device** — *"Somebody asks whether you can swap groups"* (7),
*"Somebody down the line says that's impossible"* (11), *"Aymar asks why, out loud"*
(18), *"A man near the front asks whether the laundry bags go out..."* (4). A nameless
voice reported, then a quoted answer. Used at least nine times, consistently, and it
reads as a convention rather than a break. Not counted.

**Scene-ending reported lines.** The book closes scenes on a reported last line
constantly — chapter 19's "then says he would like to withdraw ten of his pages,"
chapter 23's "tells him the supplier has billed him twice for the same case of blades,"
chapter 28's "asks, half joking." These are transitions, the brief excludes them, and
several of them are the best sentences in their scenes. Not counted. If the author
disagrees and wants these quoted too, that is a separate and much longer pass.

**Chapter 24, the "internal" paragraph between two chat blocks.** *When he does, he says
he asked and was told it's internal. Internal how, Chloe asks. That's the word they used,
is all Kavi has for her.* This is the defect's shape in the chat medium — transcript,
reported prose, transcript. It is excluded under the chat-transcript rule, but it is the
one place that rule shelters something the author would probably want to look at.

**Chapter 17, Ruth's twenty replays at one in the morning**; **chapter 13, the essay
plateau**; **chapter 18, the Rustem loan.** All compress hours or months of action, none
of them report speech inside a live exchange. Not instances.

# What the pattern is, if the author wants one sentence

The breaks are not evenly distributed and they are not stylistic drift. They land, over
and over, on the beat where somebody says the hard thing: a child bidding her birthday,
a professor delivering a verdict, a boy telling the truth to a table that will not have
it, a woman naming her number across a counter. The scenes on either side of these
moments are dramatized with real care. It is specifically the moment of highest
exposure that the prose steps back from — which suggests the fix, in most cases, is
nerve rather than craft.
