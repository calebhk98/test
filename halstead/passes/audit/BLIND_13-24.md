# Blind read-through, Chapters 13–24

Method: chapters 1–12 read straight through with no notes, as a reader's memory. From chapter 13 on, one chapter read, then reviewed, before opening the next. Nothing revised in hindsight.

## Chapter 13 — Ten Pages

**1. What happened?** Chloe (now ten) starts a weekly ten-page essay assignment modeled explicitly on Hamilton's Federalist Papers output, and separately starts a self-defense curriculum that escalates from falls to 10-on-1 sparring, plus paintball where she and Ruth reverse-engineer barrel-tracking as a technique, plus a "Defensive Watch" drill against mock building intruders that gets scored and critiqued like a real security failure.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Ten, going by the chapter titles counting up (Eight, Nine, now this one, which the text implies is her tenth year) and the September start date.

**3. IQ?** No number given, still. I'd guess somewhere north of 150 — the ceiling-exceeded testing from chapter 2 plus everything since. My estimate hasn't really moved this chapter because the evidence is the same *kind* of evidence as before (fast pattern recognition, argument sophistication), just applied to older-kid material. It's not more convincing that she's smarter, only that the tasks have gotten harder in step with her.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** The emotional beats do — the "that's a lot of hands" nervousness before the 10v1, needling Ruth, the small-sibling bickering with Sam and Kavi. But the Thanksgiving conversation with her dad about tariffs and rebuilding industrial capacity for a war is not a ten-year-old's conversation in any register I recognize; it's two adults doing policy analysis with one of them assigned a smaller chair. I don't believe a real ten-year-old produces "having the answer doesn't help you at all, does it, since you're already too late either way" as a spontaneous rejoinder, however smart she is.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** Nobody says the word. It stays remarkably unremarked-on inside the story — the paintball trick is treated as something the group worked out together, and when Bex relays it to the teacher and gets credit, Chloe's reaction is quiet resentment, not superiority. That's a consistent choice across chapters: brilliance is normalized to the point of being invisible to the characters, which is either restraint or a way of not having to answer the question I keep asking in item 3.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The essay assignment — a publishable ten-page argumentative essay every week, judged against Hamilton's actual output — is graduate-seminar work. A working adult professional would find sustaining that pace and quality hard. Handed to any real ten-year-old, gifted or not, it would not go the way it goes here. The self-defense material is stranger: multiple adults grabbing a single child for a full minute is a genuinely alarming thing to train fourth-graders to endure, and the chapter doesn't pause on that strangeness at all.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** No. I believed the essay-writing arc as an account of getting better at a hard skill through iteration — that's well observed. I did not believe the combat and Defensive Watch material as something a school does to nine- and ten-year-olds without any character, including the adult narrator's implicit camera, finding it worth a raised eyebrow. It reads like training for something specific, and the story hands it to us as just another elective.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes, and this is the first chapter where it's overt. "Defensive Watch, Scoring" with rules like "they do not learn anything about you" and "you take their comms" is language for repelling a trained infiltration team, not a fire drill. The book gives me the scoring sheet completely deadpan and trusts me to notice on my own that this is not a normal school being cautious — it's a school expecting to be professionally targeted. Nobody in the text asks why. I am clearly meant to be asking why.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same clean, short-sentence, declarative style as the first twelve chapters — I'd put it around 7th–9th grade by ear, maybe pulled up a notch by vocabulary like "viscosity" and tariff economics, but the sentence architecture itself stays simple.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The dialogue tic where two kids argue in short volleys ("It just was." / "How was it different, though?") has been present since chapter one and is still doing a lot of work here (Ruth and Sam on the moon in ch5, now Kowalczyk explaining the 10v1 in nearly the same rhythm). It's a good tic but I'm starting to feel its edges.

**11. What do you expect next?** More escalation on the security-training front, and I expect it to eventually get explained — either the school is preparing kids for something real, or someone in-world is going to ask the question I'm asking and get an answer. I also expect the essay-writing thread to pay off later as a skill the plot needs (something written, argued, and true under pressure).

## Chapter 14 — Sixty Degrees

**1. What happened?** Chloe, now eleven, learns competitive archery to the point of deliberately colliding arrows in midair with a partner across two firing lines, argues with Ruth about the ethics of taking Latin, and gets assigned an essay on whether the Allied bombing of German cities was justified — she has to build the case she disagrees with before she's allowed to knock it down, gets a B for hedging. The chapter ends with a school-wide "Watch" in which every teacher becomes a hostile actor for two hours and the students run a coordinated defense of the building.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Eleven — the timeline (Sept 2016–April 2017) puts her one year past chapter 13's ten-year-old year, and the chapter title pattern (Eight, Nine, [Ten], now this one) tracks.

**3. IQ?** Still no number, and I notice I've stopped expecting one — the book seems to have decided that naming it would be a mistake, which I think is the right call structurally even as a reader who wants the number. My estimate hasn't moved from "very high, ceiling-exceeded" but this chapter's clearest evidence — deliberately building the counterargument she hates until she can't argue with it anymore — is a mark of intellectual honesty and discipline more than raw horsepower, which is a different thing than IQ and a more interesting one.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** Somewhat. The stomach-sickness over the bombing essay, the stubborn defensiveness with Ruth over Latin, the sulking about the B — those read like a real child processing something hard. But "an argument you apologise for is a request. Make it or leave it out" is teacher-marginal-note prose, not an eleven-year-old's internal thought, and the essay's actual content (rebuilding a 1961 apologist's argument from scratch, finding "the hole in the middle of what he says") is the intellectual labor of a graduate student, delivered without strain once she commits to it.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** Nobody frames it that way even now. What's notable this chapter is that she gets outmaneuvered twice — by Hearn, who catches exactly the two sentences she planted to hedge, and by the Watch scoresheet, which faults the group for not counting the teachers while she was pinned to a landing the whole time. The book keeps finding ways to make her wrong or beaten rather than let the accumulating evidence of her ability curdle into self-regard on the page.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The essay is a 28-page paper steelmanning a real historical atrocity and then refuting it — that is hard graduate work, and Hearn's critique (hedging as evasion, an apologetic argument being "a request") is the kind of note a serious writing mentor gives a serious adult writer. The archery timing problem — released blind, on a count, to collide at the top of a sixty-degree-crossing arc — is a genuinely interesting physics/reaction-time problem that I think a skilled adult archer would also find difficult. None of this is what eleven-year-olds do.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The archery collision, once I accepted the premise, made physical sense and I believed the mechanism (arcing arrows hanging at apex, counted release). I did not believe the school-wide Watch as something any real institution would run on children — teachers becoming "hostile" and hunting students with paintball markers for two hours, scored like a live security exercise, with Sam getting a knee between his shoulder blades until he stops moving. That detail in particular — a teacher physically pinning a child down as part of a scored drill — is the first moment in the book that felt like it should have provoked an in-world reaction it didn't get.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes, more than last chapter. The Watch has gone from "a scoring sheet on a corkboard" to "the entire staff spends an afternoon hunting children," and it is delivered with the same flat competence-porn tone as the archery lesson. I am being steered to read this as impressive rather than as the thing it would be reported as anywhere else: an unsupervised live-action assault drill on ten- and eleven-year-olds. The book is banking on me being charmed by "Ruth ran the east stairwell" instead of asking who signs off on this.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same register as before — plain declarative sentences, controlled paragraph rhythm — but the subject matter (rocket equation, the age of the universe measured two ways, area bombing ethics) is pulling the effective reading level up through content even though the sentences themselves stay simple. I'd still call the prose itself upper-middle-grade by construction, with a vocabulary and reference load closer to an advanced high schooler's.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The grandmother scene, where she completely misjudges Chloe's archery ("well, you'll get there, dear, once in a whole year is hardly a lot") is doing the same beat the book has done several times now — an adult outside the school failing to register the scale of what Chloe's describing — and it's starting to feel like a device the author reaches for on a schedule rather than as it arises.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the Watch exercises to escalate again and for someone to eventually get hurt badly enough that the tone can't stay this level. I also expect the bombing-essay skill — building the argument you hate before you're allowed to refute it — to come back as a plot-relevant habit of mind, maybe applied to the school itself.

## Chapter 15 -- Twelve

**1. What happened?** Chloe, twelve now, takes up blacksmithing, keeps refining the archery-collision timing with Odile, and the friend group runs an ad hoc blind taste test on the school's bread (which turns into a minor incident when they light an oven at midnight). Then a real 4 a.m. Watch happens with no warning: four adult "intruders" come over the fence, and the kids -- Chloe included -- capture and drug all four, tie them up, and interrogate them, with Chloe using herself as bait because she reads as small and harmless.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Twelve, stated outright multiple times this chapter, including by her mother.

**3. IQ?** Still no number. This chapter doesn't move my estimate on raw intelligence so much as it moves my estimate of something else entirely -- operational judgment under real physical danger. Volunteering to be grabbed by a stranger's gloved hand because "while they're deciding, they're standing still and they're all facing the same way" is not a smart-kid observation, it's a trained tactician's read, delivered by a twelve-year-old with total calm.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** No, and this is the first chapter where I don't think the book is even trying to make me believe that. Sitting down because "her legs have decided to" after the takedown is a real, human, bodily reaction -- that's the age coming through. But offering herself as bait to four grown men in the dark, then sitting calmly against a wall doing risk-assessment on the mismatched bolt-cutters while a drugged man breathes next to her, is not twelve. Sam actually says it out loud -- "four grown men, that's not very twelve" -- which reads like the book noticing the problem and naming it instead of solving it.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** This chapter is about competence rather than intelligence, and the register shifts accordingly. Nobody calls anybody smart; they call plans good or bad, in the language of professionals debriefing an op ("that's the sensors," "escalation, we've been good"). That vocabulary is not how twelve-year-olds talk even at a strange school, and I think this is the tell that the book itself thinks of them as something other than children -- the register gives it away before the plot does.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** This isn't "hard schoolwork" anymore, it's a live hostile encounter with real drugs, real zip-tie restraints, real interrogation. An adult would find this hard, dangerous, and almost certainly illegal to run against actual pre-teens. Whether the "intruders" are paid actors (as Ruth guesses) or something worse (as Chloe's closing paragraph -- "the direction four real people would get it wrong in if somebody had handed them the wrong list" -- pointedly leaves open), the book has stopped pretending this is a school activity in any ordinary sense.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The bread test and the forge work were completely believable and, frankly, some of the best writing in the book so far -- Kavi getting derailed by the oven's heating curve is exactly how that kind of kid behaves. The Watch sequence I believed as a scene (it's staged competently) but not as a thing that happens to children at a real institution without it being the biggest event in the book's world. Nobody afterward seems shaken. Sam is bragging about it at dinner by the next paragraph. That emotional flatness after handling and drugging four adult men is the least believable thing in the chapter.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Very much so, and Chloe's own closing thought names it without answering it: were these paid actors, or real intruders the school is passing off as a drill? The mismatched equipment cuts both ways and she says so herself. The book is deliberately withholding which one it is, and I don't think that's an accident -- I think the reveal, whenever it comes, is going to recontextualize everything the school has been doing to these kids since chapter 13.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Consistent with the rest of the book -- plain sentences, dialogue-heavy -- but the content (AES-256, key derivation, "run everything a person can type in a hurry," sedation dosing, drug traceability) is now specialized technical and pharmacological knowledge delivered as if it's ordinary school material. The prose itself is still accessible; what it's describing is not.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** Sam's post-op gloating monologue at the tied-up man ("that's a C right there") undercuts the tension of the scene for a joke, which felt like a tonal misstep -- the book wants this scene to be both a real physical-danger set piece and comic relief at the same time, and it doesn't quite hold both.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the actors-vs-real-intruders question to matter later, possibly a lot. I also think the emotional flatness here is unsustainable and expect either a chapter where the toll finally shows on one of them, or an authority figure outside the school (a parent, most likely Chloe's mother, who is already suspicious) forcing the question of what this place actually is.

## Chapter 16 -- Thirteen

**1. What happened?** Chloe, thirteen, teaches a class of twelve-year-olds (the oldest cohort teaches the one below) and gets stuck failing a student, Marek, who does the actual mathematics brilliantly but refuses on principle to hand in paper, so the grading scheme forces her to record a fail regardless of what she knows he can do. Meanwhile her own coursework moves into real analysis, cryptography, contract law, and epistemology, and she and her dinner-table friends build their own encrypted covert channel and traffic-obfuscation scheme just to sneak into town on a Saturday.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Thirteen -- stated in the chapter title and the timeline (Sept 2018-July 2019).

**3. IQ?** Still no number, and by now I've stopped treating that as a gap in the book rather than a choice. What struck me this chapter wasn't her intelligence but her ethical bind with Marek -- forced to punish someone for a correct and admirable refusal because the system she is a small, powerless part of has no other slot for it. That's a much more interesting kind of "hard problem" than another arithmetic showcase, and it's the first time the book has used her intellect to put her in a position with no good move rather than to solve something.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** More than usual, actually. Feeling railroaded into failing a kid she respects, and going to petty, careful lengths (asking the registrar's office "in the flattest words she can find," anonymously) to see if there's a way out before accepting there isn't, both read as true to a scrupulous, powerless thirteen-year-old. The back-steps scene with her father -- caring that a stair riser is four millimetres proud when nobody else will ever notice -- is a nicely observed, age-plausible bit of perfectionism that doesn't require genius to explain. The cryptography-and-covert-channel-building to catch a bus into town, on the other hand, is still not normal thirteen-year-old behavior, even for gifted kids; it's what you'd expect from bored graduate students with security clearances.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** The Kavi/game-theory exchange is the most direct treatment of this yet: Chloe has the correct mathematical strategy and still loses to Kavi repeatedly because he's playing her instead of the equilibrium, and she says so plainly ("I could hand you the payoff matrix right now and you'd still find the one cell that isn't on it"). That's the book finally letting someone else's different kind of cleverness beat hers outright, cleanly, without a moral about humility attached — I liked that a lot, it's the most "people, not chess pieces" the cohort has felt so far.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** Real analysis proofs, contract law aimed at a bar exam at sixteen, live cryptographic system analysis on somebody's actual license-plate database — this is now explicitly bar-exam and graduate-coursework material, stated as such in-text by the teacher himself. An adult professional in any of these fields would find the pace hard to sustain; a lot of practicing lawyers never sit for anything as demanding as "the bar exam by sixteen" implies. It is not remotely what a normal gifted child does, and the book isn't pretending otherwise anymore — it's stating the comparison outright (Federalist Papers rate, bar exam age) rather than letting me infer it.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The Marek plot I believed completely and thought was the strongest material in the book so far — a real institutional dilemma with a real cost, no clean resolution, and consequences (his fail) that the book explicitly says stay unknown to Chloe "for years." The bus-into-town caper I believed as an entertaining set piece but not as thirteen-year-olds' idea of a fun way to spend free time; it's a heist movie wearing a school uniform.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Less overtly than 14 and 15 — no live intruders this time — but Ruth's homebrew camera-defeat device ("goes looking for recorders... about half are still on the password they shipped with") is still troubling in a low-key way: these kids have, and use without any adult sign-off, tools for defeating institutional and presumably real-world surveillance. It's played as a caper, but a device that can compromise a network of cameras is a serious thing to hand a group of minors and then not follow up on.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same plain-declarative register throughout, but this chapter's content vocabulary (differential equations, real analysis, Vigenère, "justified plus true still lets a lucky guess through") is the densest and most specialized yet. The sentences themselves are still short and clear; a reader could sound out every word without knowing what a Gettier problem is, but understanding what's actually happening requires real background.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The dinner-table cipher scene runs long and the rapid-fire, everyone-talks-over-everyone dialogue (Eli, Kavi, Ruth, Priya, Sam, Nadia, Iyad all in the space of two pages) got hard to track without rereading who said what -- the device that's worked well one-on-one (Ruth vs. Chloe, Sam vs. Kavi) strains at seven speakers.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect Marek to resurface, and I expect the friend group's appetite for building tools that beat security systems to eventually be turned toward something with real stakes rather than a bus schedule. I'm also now expecting the "IQ number" to never come, and for that to be revealed as the point rather than an omission.

## Chapter 17 -- Fourteen

**1. What happened?** Chloe, fourteen, moves from archery to live-fire marksmanship, keeps teaching the year below (and has to reverse a term of mistaught material), and her friend group runs a monthslong engineering project to triangulate a single sound on a field to within a hand's width, complete with a synchronization crisis, wired-vs-wireless redesign, and an all-nighter. At Christmas her mother finds out, almost by accident, that Chloe has been doing live rifle fire since September, and it becomes the first real confrontation about how much the parents don't know.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Fourteen, per the chapter title and dates.

**3. IQ?** Still withheld, and I'm fully reconciled to that now — it would flatten what the book is actually interested in, which by this chapter is clearly group cognition (how Kavi, Ruth, Nadia, Priya, and Chloe each hold one piece of the sound-localization problem and none of them alone would get there) rather than any one person's raw score. That's a real shift in the book's engine, and it's a good one.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** The dinner-table confrontation with her mother is the most convincing scene of "real teenager" the book has given me — the guilty, defensive, minimizing "it's fine, Mom, it's completely fine," followed by the genuinely helpless "which one was she supposed to have opened with?" This is the first time I've fully believed a Chloe scene as an ordinary fourteen-year-old's emotional experience rather than a mind performing at a level the scene doesn't earn. The engineering project itself is still not age-typical, but the family scene grounds the chapter in something real.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** This chapter's clearest answer: no, and the book is now actively depicting failure and reversal as often as success. She teaches her own students the wrong method for months without noticing; the sound-localization team carries a known calibration error for two weeks because nobody says it out loud; her own instinct at 1 a.m. to "fix" Ruth's sensor array is simply wrong and Ruth tells her so. Competence here reads as effortful, fallible, and social, not magical, and I trust the story more for it.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The acoustic triangulation project — synchronized clocks over a wired network, temperature-corrected speed of sound, deciding wireless timing wasn't good enough and rebuilding the whole rig with cable in a weekend — is legitimately hard applied physics and systems engineering that professional engineers get wrong regularly (the chapter even shows them getting it wrong for two weeks). This is beyond what any child does, gifted or not, but I found it the most credible "impressive" set piece in the book because the difficulty is shown through real failure, not narrated as effortless.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** I believed the acoustic project completely, technically and emotionally. I did not, even slightly, believe live rifle marksmanship as a standing elective for early-teens with a "man behind the line" as the only safeguard, and the book seems to know that too — the confrontation with her mother is the book finally staging the reaction a real parent would have, which retroactively makes me trust that the earlier no-reaction chapters (the four drugged intruders, in particular) were choices, not oversights.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes — very pointedly this time, and named outright by Chloe herself: "All of it went past this table in silence. Which one was she supposed to have opened with?" That's the book admitting, through its own character, that it has been curating what reaches the parents (and by extension, me) for chapters, and this is the first time a character calls that out rather than me having to infer it. It's a strong choice — the narrator's habitual omission becomes a plot point instead of just a stylistic tic.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same accessible sentence-level style, but this is the most technically dense chapter yet — synchronization jitter, speed of sound as a function of temperature, font hinting and glyph rendering within a 16ms frame budget. A reader without background in any of those fields will follow the sentences but not really absorb the substance, which is a widening gap between surface readability and actual comprehension.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** None that stood out to me this chapter — this might be the tightest chapter of the book so far. If I'm reaching for a nitpick, the group-scene dialogue (five or six kids trading half-lines rapid-fire) is again dense enough that I occasionally lost track of who said what, same issue as chapter 16's cipher scene.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the parents to start pushing harder for information now that the seal has been broken, and I expect at least one more "the school does something genuinely dangerous to minors" reveal before this arc resolves. I'd also bet the sound-triangulation skill set (arrival-time differences, calibration honesty) gets reused later for something with higher stakes than a field exercise.

## Chapter 18 -- Fifteen

**1. What happened?** Chloe, fifteen, hits a hard plateau in marksmanship that months of analysis can't break (Voss just tells her "your block started without you"), gets outpaced in self-defense sparring by boys who are finally growing into their size, runs a small entrepreneurial-loan experiment with real money and comes out barely ahead of a classmate who took real risk, gets asked by a researcher to informally keep a secret she has no legal obligation to keep, deals with a false rumor about a boy she's never met, learns to drive with her father, and helps build a two-factor physical-token system after a friend's phone is confiscated.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Fifteen.

**3. IQ?** Unstated, as ever, and this is the first chapter that gave me a genuine, uncomplicated data point against genius-as-the-whole-story: she hits a wall in marksmanship that "her own numbers" cannot solve, Voss won't explain it, and Ruth — checking her form from behind — can't find the problem either. A plateau that resists analysis, that just requires more reps and time, is the most true-to-life thing that's happened to her in several chapters, and I liked it a lot for the same reason I liked the sound-triangulation failure last chapter: it costs her something instead of resolving into another quiet triumph.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** Yes, more consistently than any chapter yet. Snapping at Sam for no reason after the NDA conversation and then apologizing without being asked why; refusing to investigate the Aurel rumor even though it would be trivial to find out who he is; getting quietly outclassed on the mats and needing a full corridor conversation with Kowalczyk just to be told "you have to learn faster than they do" — none of this required a genius, just an adolescent under normal adolescent pressures. This is the chapter where the book's "person, not chess piece" instinct is strongest.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** The Hark investment-block scene answers this cleanly: she has "the right numbers and the wrong answer" and finishes barely ahead of a boy who took real risk and actually learned something from his losses. Hark's line to her — "you are barely beating inflation" — is the most direct any adult has been about the limits of cleverness without nerve, and it lands as a genuine correction rather than a beat the book quickly walks back.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The marksmanship-on-a-moving-round drill (shooting a bullet with a bullet, on a mechanical cycle) is an extraordinary ask for anyone, let alone teenagers, and Voss's own framing — "you will spend most of it missing by the width of a dime" — concedes as much. The two-factor security work (separating the login from the device, distributing physical tokens) is realistic professional practice, well described, and would be reasonable work for an actual security engineer, just not for a fifteen-year-old doing it casually between classes.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** Mostly yes, more than any chapter since 13. The plateau, the investment block, the sparring gap opening up against bigger boys, the driving lessons — all of it reads as true and well observed. The one thing I didn't fully buy was Dr. Sandoval's NDA conversation: a researcher explicitly telling a fifteen-year-old "I would refuse to put a federal agreement in front of you because it would be wrong" and then asking for the same secrecy informally is a strange, legally fraught thing for an adult to say out loud to a minor, and it reads more like the author wanting to stage an ethical dilemma than something a cautious institution's counsel would ever let happen.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Less than the last several chapters — no live intruders, no rifle-range confrontation with a parent this time. If anything the book is opening up: the NDA scene and the phone-security scene both foreground exactly the kind of institutional opacity I've been flagging, and have a character (Sandoval, Ruth) name it out loud instead of leaving it to me. That feels like the book meeting its own pattern halfway.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Consistent — plain sentences, still technically dense (bearing sounds, sutures, two-factor authentication, live-fire ballistics) but the emotional material this chapter (the plateau, the rumor, the NDA) is more accessible than the acoustics or cryptography chapters, so this reads as an easier chapter overall despite the same prose style.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The Aurel rumor subplot is thin — it's introduced, escalates off-page ("it is still going in the summer"), and resolves into nothing but her avoiding a staircase. It reads like a placeholder for a plotline that either needs more room or shouldn't be there at all; as written it doesn't do much besides tell me rumors exist at this school.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the marksmanship plateau to break at some cost, and I expect the physical security tokens and the NDA-adjacent secrecy to matter later — probably together, if the book's pattern of "skill introduced quietly now, paid off dramatically two or three chapters later" (the essay-writing, the sound triangulation) holds.

## Chapter 19 -- Sixteen

**1. What happened?** Chloe, sixteen, sits the state bar exam the school has been building toward since chapter 16, fails it by four points out of two hundred because she wrote correct but under-explained answers, spends the summer working through why ("thinking it and writing it down are not the same action"), retakes it in October and passes comfortably, and starts advanced driving instruction that includes staged mechanical failures. At home for the summer she is listless, sleeps constantly, and mostly talks to her parents about her friends rather than herself.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Sixteen, and the bar-exam framing (set up explicitly back in chapter 16, "at sixteen you'll take the bar") pays off exactly on schedule.

**3. IQ?** Unstated, and this chapter is maybe the strongest argument yet for why the number never mattered: she fails a real exam by a real margin for a real, ordinary, fixable reason — not showing her work — and the fix takes her a full summer of deliberate practice with peer marking. That's the most convincing "smart person learning a skill" arc in the book, because it's the first time cleverness alone visibly wasn't enough and she had to build the missing skill the slow way.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** Yes, and this chapter's home scenes are some of the most true to life in the book. Sleeping through the first week home, riding to the reservoir every day, reading novels she can't later summarize, sitting on the back steps refusing to move — that is exhausted-teenager behavior, rendered without any need to invoke her intellect at all. The detail of her answering every question about her own summer by talking about Sam, Ruth, Priya, and Nadia instead of herself is a sharp, quiet piece of characterization — deflection, not analysis.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** This chapter finally lets failure sit without a quick recovery. She fails the bar and it visibly, repeatedly haunts her — "she still shuts books harder than she means to, though she could not tell anybody why" — well after she's passed the retake by a wide margin. That's a real emotional consequence, not a plot beat that resolves in the same scene, and it's the most convincing evidence in the book that she does not experience herself as simply, effortlessly smart.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** Sitting and passing an actual bar exam, even a fictionalized one, at sixteen is obviously not what any real teenager does, gifted or not — most licensed adult attorneys took years of law school first. But the specific skill she's missing (translating known reasoning into a fully explicit written answer for a reader who doesn't share her head) is a completely ordinary, real skill gap that trips up plenty of very smart adults, and Amberg's explanation of it is about the clearest, most useful piece of writing instruction in the whole book so far.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The failure, the summer of practice, and the "still shutting books harder than she means to" aftermath — completely. The staged driving failures (a sabotaged gearbox, a sabotaged brake pedal, indistinguishable from real failure, at highway speed) are the one element I balked at: doing that to a sixteen-year-old driver, even in a controlled setting, is a genuinely dangerous thing for an institution to do and the chapter doesn't register it as remarkable at all, which is a pattern by now.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Less than earlier chapters. If anything this chapter is unusually candid — showing a real failure, an unresolved emotional aftertaste, and a mother directly asking whether her daughter is actually resting or just avoiding the house. That question ("that's not the girl who left here for that school in the first place") is the sharpest the book has let a parent get, and it isn't answered, which reads like the book building toward something rather than deflecting from it.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Consistent plain-sentence style; the bar-exam and mark-scheme material is more accessible than the cryptography or acoustics chapters because it's about writing and reasoning rather than specialized technical content, so this reads as one of the more approachable chapters despite the sophistication of what's being discussed.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The Priya/Nadia negotiation-board aside at the very end, relayed secondhand through a conversation with her father, is compressed almost to the point of opacity — I don't fully understand what Priya did or why, and it's dropped in as a coda rather than developed, similar to the Aurel rumor in chapter 18. The book seems to like planting these compressed social mysteries and not following up in the same chapter; it's a real technique but it's starting to feel like a habit rather than a choice made fresh each time.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the summer listlessness to matter — either as ordinary burnout that passes, or as the first sign of something the book has been building toward with all the psychological-appointment history from the early chapters. I also expect Marek to reappear again given how deliberately the book keeps returning to him.

## Chapter 20 -- The Parking Lot

**1. What happened?** Chloe, Ruth, Sam, and Nadia use their accumulated hacking, surveillance-defeat, and infiltration skills to sneak off campus at night for waffles at a diner, and on the walk back are confronted by seven armed muggers. Sam asks to fight them alone, does, and dispatches all seven in twenty-two seconds without a scratch; the group then gives first aid to the unconscious men and leaves without reporting it, reasoning that a police report would expose the whole excursion.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Sixteen — the chapter falls inside chapter 19's dated span (April 2022–June 2023) and no age is disputed.

**3. IQ?** Not a factor in this chapter's central set piece at all — the fight is entirely physical competence, and Chloe's actual intellectual contribution is her post-fight read of the muggers ("if you had one usable skill... you'd be doing something else with your evenings"), which is a sharp, cold, and slightly unpleasant piece of analysis rather than a warm one.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** The diner scene beforehand is the best "these are teenagers" writing in the whole book — the waffle ritual, the shopping-cart stunt, the pedantic argument about "hither," Ruth's brother's letter landing quietly in the middle of banter. It's genuinely funny and warm and reads true. Then the mugging happens and nothing about the response is age-plausible in any direction: no fear, no adrenaline crash, no shakiness afterward, just competent triage and a debrief about tactics, followed by walking home arguing about a waitress. Real sixteen-year-olds who fight off seven armed adults do not process it this calmly by the next paragraph.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** This chapter is one of the more openly unflattering ones about how the group thinks of outsiders. Chloe's assessment of the muggers — calling them, in the text's own word, "retarded" for not having a "usable skill" — reads as callous and classist in a way I don't think the book intends to flag as a character flaw; it's delivered as simply correct, and nobody at the table pushes back on either the word or the sneer behind it. That's the ugliest the protagonist has been allowed to sound in the whole book, and I don't think it was meant to land that way.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** Beating seven armed men, three with guns drawn, in twenty-two seconds without injury is not hard for Sam in the text — it's presented as almost insultingly easy, which is the point of the scene, but it means the "how hard is this" question stops making sense here. This isn't hard schoolwork anymore; it's action-movie choreography, and no real person of any age or training does this to seven-to-one armed odds and walks away clean.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The night-out logistics (camera loops, badge cloning, patrol timing) I believed as an extension of skills the book has spent six chapters establishing. I did not believe the fight itself as a physical event — three drawn guns against one teenager ending in twenty-two seconds with zero shots fired and zero injuries strains credulity past where "these kids are extraordinary" can carry it — and I did not believe the group's total emotional flatness afterward, giving first aid and bantering about a waitress minutes after nearly being shot.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes — the group's calculus for not reporting an armed robbery to the police ("that's home, and not coming back") is presented as obviously correct, and I'm meant to nod along, but it's actually a chilling thing for four teenagers to decide on their own: seven armed men are now free to rob someone who won't be able to fight back. The book doesn't examine that cost at all, and I think it's steering me toward admiring the tactics rather than questioning the ethics.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same clean, plain-sentence style as ever; this chapter is dialogue-heavy and easy to read, probably one of the more accessible chapters simply because it isn't loaded with technical vocabulary.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The use of "retarded" as a casual insult stood out badly to me — not because a teenager wouldn't say it, but because the narrative voice doesn't seem to register it as a character note at all, and in a book this controlled about word choice everywhere else, it reads like an oversight rather than a deliberate character flaw being shown to the reader.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect this incident to either come back (the muggers, the missing gun parts, someone noticing) or to be quietly dropped, and I'm watching for which. I also expect the pattern of "no consequence, no fear response, immediate joke" to eventually break, because emotional flatness this consistent across this many escalating chapters is starting to feel like a structural choice I don't yet understand rather than an accident.

## Chapter 21 -- The Applications

**1. What happened?** College applications go out, and the school's transcripts and essays are so far outside normal that admissions offices assume clerical error or ghostwriting, until published research papers under students' names force a slower, stranger process: representatives from top universities visit campus to verify the students in person, escorted everywhere except the fighting hour and the range. Nearly the whole senior class gets in almost everywhere; Chloe gets twelve of fourteen and is still stunned by that ratio against the schools' stated acceptance rates.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Sixteen going on seventeen, per the October 2022–March 2023 span, consistent with chapter 19's "Sixteen."

**3. IQ?** Unaddressed as ever, but this chapter puts a number on something adjacent and more concrete: the outside world's verification process (a Caltech rep spending a full day fact-checking one paper by hand) functions as the most rigorous, credible confirmation yet that the abilities described across twenty chapters are real and not narrative exaggeration — which paradoxically makes me trust the earlier chapters' claims more even as it makes the premise harder to believe as something that could exist in the world I live in.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** The essay-writing scenes are the best character work in the chapter — everyone in the room procrastinating differently (Sam bending a card, Kavi finishing in twenty minutes flat, Ruth stuck on the meaning of "failed"), and Chloe unable to compress the true, complicated Marek story into a college-essay anecdote and resenting the form for demanding it. The decision-day scene, sitting on the floor with letters in rows and recounting them three times because the number doesn't match what she expected, is exactly how a real seventeen-year-old would react. This is one of the most emotionally legible chapters in the book.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** The store-window analogy at the end — "if a store put a sign in its window saying only a few percent of people who walk in buy something, would you think the store was doing well or badly run?" — is the clearest moment yet of the group collectively realizing their own abnormality from the outside, via statistics rather than ego. It's a smart scene about smart kids figuring out they're outliers, done without anyone saying "we're geniuses" out loud, which the book has now sustained for twenty-one chapters and I have to give it credit for the discipline of that.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The chapter's real point is that the work was never comparable to what any age group does — a Caltech postdoc-level representative spends a full day and still can't independently rebuild the methods in a published paper by a Halstead student. That's adult professional work being verified by other adult professionals and found to hold up, which is a different and higher bar than anything the book has staged before.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** The admissions-office confusion, the transcript-rejection phone call, and the on-site paper verification were all completely believable and well-imagined — that's exactly the kind of skepticism a real institution would apply, and I liked that the book finally shows an outside system reacting with appropriate disbelief instead of shrugging things off. The "Sport," "Athletics," "Recreation" runaround about what's happening in the gym is a nice, controlled bit of stonewalling that raises exactly the right kind of question without over-explaining it.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes, precisely and deliberately: the school will not release its research papers and instead insists on a chaperoned, notes-only reading room — the same pattern of controlled disclosure the book has used on me as a reader since chapter 13. This is the first time that pattern is depicted as visible and strange to an outside professional adult, which validates that I've been reading the withholding correctly rather than imagining it.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Consistent plain style; this chapter is unusually free of jargon compared to recent ones (no cryptography or physics deep dives), so it reads easily despite being emotionally and structurally one of the densest chapters, juggling six or seven characters' application arcs at once.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** None really — this might be the best-constructed chapter since 17. If anything, Nadia's vocational-track subplot (skipping college for a trade job, faring badly against automated hiring systems) is a strong, underused thread that I wish got more room; it's introduced and mostly wrapped in the space of a few pages.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the "three percent" statistical gag to be the emotional hinge the book uses to make the cohort finally reckon with what they are, out loud, to each other and eventually to their parents. I also expect at least one of them (Nadia, given her thread here) to choose a path the school's success metrics don't reward, and for that to matter later.

## Chapter 22 -- The Offer

**1. What happened?** In exit interviews, the school offers thirteen students staff research jobs on the spot — $135,000 to start plus housing, food, car, medical, and a paid degree on their own schedule. Chloe turns hers down, wanting to be measured against a wider world than the ninety-one people she's competed against her whole life; Nadia turns hers down too, to build her own hiring startup; Kavi accepts, to Chloe's surprise. That night Chloe tells her parents, and her father calls it a mistake to her face while both parents make clear they'll find the money for college anyway.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Seventeen (April 2023), matching the end of chapter 19's span.

**3. IQ?** Never given, and this chapter makes the clearest case yet for why: Chloe's own reasoning for declining the offer is explicitly about not knowing her real number — "I don't know what I'd be if I got ranked against anyone else" — which reframes the entire missing-IQ-score question as the book's actual subject rather than an omission. That's a satisfying piece of structure, eleven chapters in the making.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** Yes, more than in almost any chapter before this one. Turning down guaranteed six figures and a paid degree for the "chance of finding out you're not as good as you think you are," as her mother bluntly puts it, is a very teenage kind of principled recklessness, and the phone call afterward — reciting the offer twice, verbatim, because "the order is the only thing about it she can hand over intact" — captures exactly how a kid explains a huge decision to parents by hiding behind procedure instead of feeling. Her father's blunt "that's a mistake" and refusal to soften it for her is the most convincingly adult, unresolved parental reaction the book has given us.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** This is the chapter that finally answers the question directly, and the answer is: she doesn't know, and that not-knowing is presented as her defining trait rather than false modesty. Kavi's answer is the inverse and just as clarifying — he stays specifically because the problems in that lab don't have answers already written down anywhere, which is the first time a character has explicitly named "having the answer checked against a key" as the thing that makes school school, and its absence as the thing that makes his choice not staying in school at all.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** The chapter doesn't dwell on task difficulty so much as career and financial reality, which for once makes it directly comparable to what an adult professional faces: turning down a guaranteed six-figure job with full benefits and a paid graduate degree, at seventeen, for the uncertainty of an unranked wider world, is a decision plenty of actual adults never have the nerve or the option to make. That the book treats this as the harder, scarier choice than any exam or drill so far is a real and interesting inversion.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** Yes, almost entirely, and it's the most grounded chapter since the driving-lesson and reservoir material — no live intruders, no impossible physical feats, just a job offer, a hard conversation, and a family working out what they owe each other. The one beat that stretched credulity slightly was the sheer procedural uniformity of the offer script (Amberg giving the identical terms, in the same order, to every one of ninety-one seventeen-year-olds) — efficient, but almost too clean, like corporate onboarding copy rather than something adapted per student.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Somewhat — Kavi's line, "it's a lab that also has children in it," is the sharpest indictment of the whole institution any character has voiced, and it's delivered almost approvingly, as the reason he wants to stay. I'm being steered to read that as thrilling rather than as the disturbing thing it plainly is: a research lab employing minors on unchecked, high-stakes, live problems. The book keeps handing me the uncomfortable phrase and trusting me not to flinch at it.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same plain, controlled sentence style; no technical jargon this chapter, which makes it one of the most accessible so far, matching its more domestic, emotional subject matter.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The Nadia interview scene runs very close in structure and rhythm to Chloe's interview scene just before it (same office, same clock, same "reason?" prompt, similar cadence of numbers recited), and while the parallel is clearly intentional, back to back it felt a little mechanical, like a template being filled in twice rather than two distinct scenes.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the book to jump forward past graduation into whatever life outside Halstead looks like, and I expect the "ranked against a wider room" question Chloe raised to become the actual test of the back half of the book. I also expect Kavi's choice to stay, and what actually happens in that "lab that also has children in it," to come back as a significant plot thread.

## Chapter 23 -- The First One

**1. What happened?** Graduation, the school's first ever. All ninety-one graduate; families mingle, the ceremony is run with the same rehearsed precision as everything else at the school; and the chapter fans out to show what everyone chooses next — Sam joins the Army, Ruth goes to MIT, Kavi stays on staff, Nadia negotiates a year to build her hiring startup while working her father's counter, Priya leaves for South America with no return ticket, and Chloe reveals she's chosen Georgetown specifically because it sets her on a path toward the Foreign Service exam, which she can't sit until twenty and be sworn in until twenty-one.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Seventeen at graduation, turning eighteen in August.

**3. IQ?** Still never given, and by now the omission has become fully thematic rather than a gap — this chapter's real interest is in what each of these evidently exceptional kids chooses to do with an open future, and the choices (a trade, the military, a startup pitched door to door, a solo trip with no address arranged) are far more individuating than a number would ever be.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** The small domestic details land well — reaching up for a doorframe she used to have to jump for and finding her arm goes straight, driving up to college alone rather than sit in a car working out what to say, the grandmother's flatly identical "and what are you doing next" to every graduate in turn. The five-year forward-planning of Foreign Service eligibility stages at seventeen is not typical of the age even for an ambitious kid, but by now that kind of forward-engineered life plan feels consistent with who the book has built her into rather than a new leap.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** The chapter's structure — cutting rapidly between nine or ten characters' very different post-graduation choices, none framed as better than another — is itself the answer: this is a group that has stopped measuring itself primarily by cleverness and started measuring by what each person actually wants, which is the payoff of the "ranked against a wider room" question raised last chapter.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** Nadia's storyline is the standout here and doesn't require genius framing at all: her account of the modern job-application funnel — software that "matches itself against itself," subscription job boards designed to almost-but-never-quite work, two hundred applications yielding fourteen automated non-answers — is a bitterly accurate, adult description of the real labor market that plenty of un-gifted adults live through daily. It's the most true-to-the-real-world material in the whole book and doesn't lean on the school's premise at all.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** Yes, almost completely, and it's the most grounded chapter since the applications chapter. The ceremony details (three gown sizes stretched across ninety-one heights, an applause track landing a half-beat late, a grandfather selling lemonade out of habit) are warm, specific, and unforced. Sam's Army enlistment scene, reading the pay-grade form twice while signing everything else at drill speed, felt exactly right for his character.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Less overtly than most chapters — this one reads like a chapter meant to let the reader (and the characters) breathe after twenty-two chapters of escalating strangeness. If there's a steer, it's a soft one: Kavi's staying on, unremarked here after the sharper treatment it got last chapter, and I notice the book doesn't revisit the "lab that also has children in it" line at all, which either means it isn't important or means it's being deliberately let go quiet before it returns.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** Same accessible, plain-sentence register; no jargon-heavy material this chapter, so it reads easily — the most novel-like and least classroom-like chapter in a while.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** The grandmother's repeated, near-verbatim "and what are you doing next" / "well, that's something" to three different graduates in a row is clearly intentional (showing how little she actually absorbs), but landing it three times in a row back to back felt slightly overdone as written — twice would have made the point as well.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the book to jump into Chloe's Georgetown years and the long run-up to the Foreign Service exam she's already planned around, and I'd bet on Nadia's startup, Sam's Army career, and Kavi's research work all resurfacing as the group scatters. I'm curious whether "the chat" (the group's years-long encrypted channel, referenced repeatedly since chapter 16) becomes the thing that keeps them connected once they're no longer at the same table every night — which the next chapter's title suggests directly.

## Chapter 24 -- The Chat

**1. What happened?** A year-in-the-life of the group's five-year-old encrypted group chat, now scattered across MIT, an Army basic training program, a federal intelligence agency, a Halstead research post, a struggling startup, and South America. Small updates accumulate — a bug-bounty joke, a laptop purchase, a bank negotiation, a review-inflation observation — until the final scene, where Chloe can't identify a medication she and Sam both took regularly at school; no doctor, pharmacy, or search turns it up, and when Kavi asks the school directly, he's told the formulation is "internal" and gets nothing further.

**2. How old is the main character now?** Eighteen, per the September 2023 dateline and her turning eighteen "in August" as stated in chapter 23.

**3. IQ?** Unaddressed, permanently it seems, and this chapter makes the withholding pay off in a completely different direction than I expected: the closing "internal" medication reveal retroactively puts a question mark over everything the book has shown me about these kids' abilities. If Halstead was administering an undisclosed, unfindable substance to children for years, then "how smart is Chloe, really" stops being an innate-ability question and becomes a question about what was done to her, which is a much darker and more interesting turn than a number ever could have been.

**4. Is she behaving like a real person of that age?** Yes, very much — the format itself (a group chat, half-jokes, incomplete sentences, people replying to a message three days late) is the most naturalistic writing in the book, precisely because texting doesn't require the elevated prose register the essay and dialogue chapters kept slipping into. The laptop-memory mix-up, the "im going to go do my reading" deflection, the bank negotiation Nadia runs cold and precise — all of it reads true without leaning on any "gifted" framing at all.

**5. Does she think she is smart? Do her friends?** Kavi's aside about performance reviews — "you tell people they're exceptional early so they work harder and don't leave... it's in the same chapter as the acceptance rate thing" — is the most self-aware the group has ever been about being managed rather than simply excellent, and it directly echoes the "three percent" statistical unmasking from chapter 21. That's a real throughline: this group is slowly learning to be suspicious of every number and every compliment they've ever been handed by an institution, which is a mature and hard-won kind of self-knowledge, not arrogance.

**6. How hard is the work, compared to what someone that age normally does? Would an adult find it hard?** This chapter is mostly about ordinary post-graduation adult friction — first-job confusion, bank bureaucracy, a failing startup's brutal unit economics, basic training — rendered at a level of specificity (the five-day-hold policy, the forty-one candidates and thirty-seven false claims, the "lorem ipsum" bug sitting unfixed for months) that reads like observed adult experience, not a teenager's guess at it. It's the most convincingly adult chapter in the book, ironically, despite everyone in it being freshly eighteen or nineteen.

**7. Did you believe everything in this chapter?** Yes, entirely, up to and including the unsettling final beat, which I believed as a piece of writing even as it alarmed me as a reader. A pharmaceutical that a real doctor has never heard of, that a pharmacy search returns nothing for, administered daily to children for years by an institution that answers formulation questions with the single word "internal," is the single most concerning thing revealed in the entire book — more concerning to me than the armed muggers or the drugged intruders, because those were external threats being handled, and this is the school itself, quietly, for years, putting something unidentified into these kids' bodies.

**8. Is the book hiding something from you, or steering you? What?** Yes, unmistakably, and this is the chapter where the hiding becomes the explicit subject rather than a background hum. "Whatever internal means, it's a wall each of them keeps running into" is the narrator naming, for the first time, that there is a wall — not just for me as a reader, but for the characters themselves. I don't think I'm meant to read this as a throwaway detail. Chloe's own restraint here — she can feel the shape of the version of the afternoon where she keeps pulling at it, and chooses not to — reads like the book flagging its own withholding as a live wire it intends to return to.

**9. What reading level is the prose?** The chat-log sections are the most accessible writing in the entire book — lowercase, unpunctuated, plainly conversational — while the connecting narrative prose stays in the same register as the rest of the book. Overall this is one of the easiest chapters to read, which makes the unsettling final beat land harder by contrast.

**10. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that felt like a novice wrote it?** None — this chapter's format shift (chat log interspersed with narration) is the freshest structural choice in the book and it's well executed, distinguishing each character's texting voice (Eli's lowercase run-ons, Ruth's fast precise replies, Sam's economy, Priya's days-delayed posts) without ever needing a dialogue tag.

**11. What do you expect next?** I expect the "internal" medication to be the actual center of whatever the book has been building since chapter 13 — I would be surprised now if it turns out to be nothing. I also expect Kavi's position inside the school, and Nadia's teetering startup, to be the next threads pulled, and I'll be watching for whether Chloe's restraint here ("she goes and does her reading instead") turns out to be a mistake the book eventually holds against her.
