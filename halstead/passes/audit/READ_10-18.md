# First read: chapters 10-18

Read straight through, as a reader, after roughly forty automated passes.
This is not a measurement report; it is what the book feels like on the page.
Every claim below is quoted from the actual chapter files, not from a script
output.

## The five worst things, worst first

**1. Ruth's own surname turns into a stranger, twice, without anybody
noticing.** Chapter 10 spells it out on purpose, in a scene built entirely
around Chloe collecting names correctly:

> "Aymar. A, Y, M, A, R. Why bother, when everyone at camp already said it
> wrong and I let them?"

That is Ruth Aymar, established as a bit for Chloe's habit of getting names
right. Two years later, at the range, "Aymar" is a different, male student
standing next to Ruth in the same scene:

> "It's seven and about a fifth," Aymar says, with the stopwatch out on the
> bench where he can see it. "Because I've timed thirty of them and the
> mechanism resets slow every time."
>
> "The card says what it says, Aymar," Voss says, keeping his eyes on the
> range.

And again the next chapter: "Aymar asks why, out loud, in the tone of
somebody asking on behalf of ninety people." This is not a coincidence a
reader can absorb the way two Johns exist in real schools; Ruth's surname was
made a plot point on page one of the span specifically so the reader would
remember it, and then a pass minted a new character on it. This is exactly
the kind of break the audit exists to catch: a name-bank collision from
automated work that nobody read back against chapter 10.

**2. A named emotional state, flatly stated, right where the chapter had
just earned the alternative.** Chapter 14, after two full paragraphs of
Chloe being physically sick over an essay she doesn't want to write and
grinding through the opposing case line by line:

> "By Thursday afternoon she has stopped being angry and started being
> interested."

This is rule 1's flat ban in its purest form: a stated feeling with no
camera behind it, dropped in as connective tissue between two paragraphs of
real, physical, earned work. The preceding material (being sick, sitting on
the bathroom floor, writing the missing piece of the opposing case herself)
already shows the turn from resentment to engagement. This sentence throws
that away and tells the reader the plot beat instead.

**3. The same failure opens the book's biggest scene in the span.** Chapter
15's intrusion sequence, the one built to be read at a run, starts like
this:

> "Chloe is annoyed when the alarm wakes her, the small one that sits in her
> ear and means watch, at four in the morning."

"Annoyed" is a named state doing the reader's interpretive work for them at
the exact moment the scene should be establishing pure sensory alarm. Compare
it to how the same chapter earns tension two pages later purely through
action ("Kavi already there," short clipped exchanges, Ruth's hair
"everywhere") — the scene knows how to do this without naming feelings, and
then opens by naming one anyway.

**4. A structural seam left unstitched, chapter 15.** The file has a section
break repeated twice in a row with nothing between them:

```
The library gets her about once a week now, because most evenings there
are people in the third floor common room with a game spread over the
table and a space kept for her at it.

________________

________________

DEFENSIVE WATCH - SCORING
```

`style_report.py` catches the symptom (section breaks: 7, target 2-6) but
the real problem is what it signals: this is the exact place where the
chapter's tone gears down from a slow, domestic first half (forge, bread
test, tomato count) into a thriller. The seam between those two halves was
never actually smoothed, just double-ruled. It also happens to be where the
`DEFENSIVE WATCH, SCORING` numbered list from chapter 13 gets restated
almost verbatim but reformatted (comma vs. dash in the header, numbers
dropped), which reads as material pasted in from a different draft state
rather than a deliberate echo.

**5. Bex's conflict with Chloe is real exactly once in nine chapters, and
everywhere else it is just her personality.** The brief for this pass calls
for Bex as someone Chloe actively dislikes for taking credit. The strongest
instance, chapter 12:

> Bex has it before Chloe has her mouth open, the whole fortnight in order
> and every part of it right, and she says we in her first sentence but I
> in the rest of them.

That lands. But everywhere else in the span — organizing a bus trip
(chapter 16), bringing a physics disagreement to a teacher (chapter 14),
narrating a self-defense round after the fact (chapter 17) — Bex is doing
the same confident, talks-over-the-table thing Ruth does constantly and
sympathetically. "Bex comes in on the end of that, and when the next person
through the door asks what happened Bex tells her, fifty seconds and the
third round and the knee across the shin at the finish, all of it in order
and all of it right, and then what Chloe should have done with that shin.
By the week after, the round the year has is a round Bex called from the
edge of the mat." That is credit-taking again, but the sentence-level voice
is indistinguishable from Ruth handing out corrections in the cooking class
or the bread-test scene. Iyad's material (below) is sharp and specific
enough to dislike; Bex, outside chapter 12, reads as mild social friction
wearing the label of a rival.

## Per-chapter notes

**10, April.** The arrival chapter does its job — the notebook of names, the
"February is allowed to change its mind" exchange, Fen's rocks. Reading
grade (F-K 8.0) is the highest in the whole span, which cuts against the
house rule's own stated design ("the book gets harder as she gets older"):
a chapter for a girl who is six or seven reads harder than chapter 18, where
she is fifteen (F-K 6.9), and harder than chapters 11-15 combined. That may
still clear the chapters-1-10 band average, but it is worth someone actually
checking that band together rather than trusting that each chapter clearing
its own floor is enough.

**11, Eight.** The strongest chapter for parent voice in the span — the hot
dog argument and the curfew fight are both genuinely funny and genuinely
sharp, and the parents sound like two different people who have had this
argument before ("You let her have it, like you let her have everything
once she gets that look on her face"). The back half (Margie, French,
Nerf, wood shop, choir) is a fair catalogue but thin; choir in particular
("choir turns out to be a different activity wearing the same word") gets
one good line and then nothing.

**12, Nine.** The weakest chapter in the span as a piece of reading, not by
the scripts but by feel. Mandarin tones, statistics, chemistry, first aid,
textiles, and the fish-tank subplot run back to back, and five of those six
follow the identical beat: Chloe or a friend struggles, an adult delivers
one terse correction, cut. Vasquez alone: "Water. That's the only thing
that turns it that particular grey, and it's nowhere on your reagent list,"
then two more rounds of the same shape with the same rhythm. Bex and Iyad,
who matter for the rest of the book, are introduced as thumbnail
descriptions rather than scenes, so neither lands with any real weight the
first time. The chapter recovers with Priya's riding arc at the end, which
is the one place stakes and character overlap.

**13, Ten Pages.** Strong chapter. Hearn's essay grind, the 10v1 self-defense
math, and the first Defensive Watch drill all have real shape and a real
cost (Chloe standing in front of failure #6, certain it names her). The
house-rule example this repo cites as fixed shows up fixed here: "she reads
it under a light that throws everything back off the glass" now stops at
the comma instead of continuing into the banned tail. Good sign that at
least one specific repair held.

**14, Sixty Degrees.** The archery collision sequence is the best pure
"camera" writing in the span — the sound, the two arrows coming down wrong,
nobody hearing the other over the noise. The essay assignment (area
bombing) is the most morally serious material Chloe has been given and she
is allowed to be wrong and be told so plainly by Hearn, which is exactly the
"Chloe never wins an argument she shouldn't" rule working as intended. Marred
only by the named-state sentence flagged above (worst thing #2).

**15, Twelve.** Read as a reader rather than a scorecard, this is close to
the best chapter in the span. The forge apprenticeship, the bread test, and
the intrusion sequence all work, and the intrusion sequence in particular
does the child-not-Vulcan test correctly: "Four grown men," he says, to
nobody in particular. "That's not very twelve." Ruth's monitoring, Sam
apologizing for missing a vein, Chloe sitting down because her legs decided
to — none of that reads as miniature adults, it reads as extremely
well-trained twelve-year-olds who are still twelve. The 5.3 reading grade is
mostly the honest cost of the clipped rhythm the scene needs and not a
defect on its own; the two real defects (the opening "annoyed," the doubled
section break) are both fixable without touching that rhythm.

**16, Thirteen.** Marek is the chapter's real achievement. Every note the
brief asks for lands: warm ("he leans in over it instead of back, already
talking before she can say anything else"), apologetic ("an apology thrown
in for her ruler, which he still has not given back"), fast, and impossible
to actually be angry at, right up until the blank column costs him the
course and Chloe is the one who has to write the zero in. "Marek does the
work and keeps it. The sheet has a column and the column is for paper." is
about as clean an ending to that thread as the book has. The chapter is also
the widest in the span — proofs, French, materials lab, cryptography, law,
epistemology in one sitting later, plus the bus trip and the back-steps
scene — and starts to feel like a list of subjects with scenes attached
rather than a chapter with subjects in it.

**17, Fourteen.** This is the group-project chapter and it earns its place.
The task is genuinely undoable alone: Kavi owns clock sync and gets it
wrong for two weeks without saying so, Ruth owns onset detection and is the
one who calls that failure out, Nadia runs logistics and keeps redrawing the
plan, Priya is the one who spots the calibration-bias problem nobody else
sees coming, and Chloe's contribution is the geometry — she needs all four
of the others' numbers before she can do anything. She is corrected and sent
to fetch food in her own scene: "Do your own end again, or go to bed, or go
and find me something to eat, and leave mine alone." / "Chloe puts it back,
and goes and finds her something to eat." That is about as far from "stays
in the lead" as a protagonist gets. The dinner-table reveal at the end
(rifles, the summer's silence) is the best parent scene in the span outside
chapter 11.

**18, Fifteen.** Iyad's material here is the sharpest conflict writing in
the span and the one place the brief's "one false item" shows up cleanly:
"In February the thing he has about Chloe is a boy in the language block
called Aurel... She has never heard the name in his life." A real cost
follows it ("She stops using the language-block stairs") that runs the rest
of the chapter. The plateau at sixty hits, the ledger scene with Hark, and
Dr. Sandoval's NDA request are all good, adult-facing material appropriate
to a fifteen-year-old band. Reading grade (F-K 6.9) sits just under this
band's own floor of 7.0; whether the band average clears it depends on
chapters 19-22, which are outside this span.

## Does the group project earn its place?

Yes. Chapter 17's sound-localization project is the one place in the span
where the interdependence is load-bearing rather than decorative: each of
five people (Kavi, Ruth, Nadia, Priya, Chloe) holds one piece nobody else
can supply, the project fails partway through because of a real, specific
mistake (Kavi sitting on a known bad number for a fortnight), and Chloe is
put in her place by Ruth on-page rather than complimented for her math. The
B grade and the named failures at the end ("the group carried a known error
for a fortnight without putting it on the board") make the imperfection feel
earned rather than decorative.

## The four questions

**Which chapter is the weakest and why?** Chapter 12. Not because any single
sentence is bad, but because five of its six subject vignettes (Mandarin,
statistics, chemistry, first aid, textiles) run the identical beat —
struggle, one terse teacher correction, cut — back to back, and the chapter
introduces both Bex and Iyad, who matter for the rest of the book, as
thumbnail descriptions rather than scenes. It is the chapter in the span
that reads most like a checklist with dialogue attached.

**Which single sentence in the span is the worst?** "By Thursday afternoon
she has stopped being angry and started being interested." (chapter 14). It
is a flat, named emotional state dropped directly on top of two paragraphs
of real, physical, camera-visible work that had already shown the same turn
without needing to say it.

**How many of the eleven could you identify with the tags hidden?** Five
reliably: Ruth (precise, corrects people, ends arguments by fiat), Sam
(deflects with food or a joke, never actually defensive), Kavi (counts
things, argues fairness, keeps lists), Nadia (short, flat, reads tells off
other people), and Marek (interrupts himself, apologizes mid-sentence,
already three thoughts ahead). Priya and Odile are recognizable by subject
matter (horses; physical bravado) more than by sentence shape. Bex and Iyad
are recognizable by function (credit-taker; information-hoarder) but their
actual lines could be swapped with each other, or with Ruth's, without a
reader noticing from the words alone. Eli and Fen have too little dialogue
in this span (two lines and one scene, respectively) to judge fairly. Call
it 5 of 11 on voice alone, 9 of 11 if behavior and running plot function are
allowed to count as identification.

**Does the group project earn its place?** Yes — see above.
