# Blind Full Read — Chapter-by-Chapter Log

Method: one chapter read, one entry written, no reading ahead, no revising earlier entries.

---

## Chapter 1: Before

**1. What happened?** A montage of Chloe's kindergarten year — overhearing her mother's frustrated phone calls to the school, arguing library rules with a librarian, dominating dinner-table book reports, getting corrected on the playground, getting caught reading ahead in class — ending on a scheduled evaluation appointment in March.

**2. How did this chapter change the main character?** This is her baseline, not a change — but within the chapter she moves from freely showing her whole answer (the "ocean going somewhere else" thought in full) to deliberately truncating it to one word, "water." She has learned, by November, to hide the size of an answer on purpose. That's the first acquired skill: self-editing for the room.

**3. Is she behaving like a real person of her age?** Mostly yes — the tennis-ball rule argument, the crust-peeling, the swinging heels at the grown-up table, the pretending-to-sleep-in-the-car are all convincingly six. The library-card logic argument and the Icarus comparison are precocious but plausible for a bright six-year-old who reads constantly; nothing yet reads as adult-shaped in a costume sense.

**4. How intelligent is she, judged only by what she does?** Estimate: notably gifted verbally and pattern-wise (finishes 30 near-identical subtraction problems after one), reads at least 3-4 grade levels up, reasons in analogies (Icarus) unprompted. Call it a rough 8/10 for a six-year-old, mostly on reading/verbal and inference, not yet tested on anything harder. No strong signal yet on math beyond a trivially easy worksheet.

**5. Does she think she is smarter than other people?** Not framed as superiority — she seems bored and lonely more than contemptuous. But there's an early data point: she works out the "true" reasons other kids get away with rule inconsistencies (Kayleigh's catch) and is unable to let it go, suggesting she assumes her reasoning is right and others' is arbitrary, without yet generalizing that into "I am smarter."

**6. How do the teachers and other adults behave?** Mrs. Aldana treats sitting still and pacing with the group as the priority, and correctness as secondary — she confiscates the book, not for wrongdoing but for being out of step. The doctor is notably careful to say "none of this is medical" while still recommending evaluation — a nuanced, non-alarmist adult voice. The librarian enforces the letter of a rule against a clever technicality, unbothered by the argument's logic.

**7. How much does the chapter work to make things seem normal?** Very much so — deliberately mundane framing (subtraction worksheets, cereal boxes, a pediatrician visit) around what the mother clearly experiences as a crisis. The gap between the mother's alarm ("somebody in that building to be as bothered about that as I am") and the flatly reported classroom incidents is the chapter's whole tension.

**8. Is the book hiding something, and HOW?** Yes — it never names what's being tested for beyond one mention of "attention," and it never lets Chloe articulate what she's experiencing internally; we get behavior and adult dialogue but not her interior diagnosis of herself. The technique is withholding via strict close-third camera: we see what she does and says, never a narrator's summary of what she "is."

**9. Is the narration plain and neutral, or does it address you?** Plain and neutral, close third, no editorializing. Even loaded moments (crust peeling "working out which part was the wrong part") are delivered flatly rather than interpreted for the reader.

**10. Anything that contradicts something earlier, or does not make sense?** Nothing yet — first chapter.

**11. Was anything a surprise?** The ending beat — that the appointment is so unspeakably significant to her that she "cannot say it" (the name on the sticky note) — lands with more weight than expected from the flat prose leading up to it.

**12. The prose specifically.** Simple-to-moderate, plain sentences, present-tense internality avoided, long comma-spliced sentences used for cumulative effect (the recitation of the library week) rather than complexity of vocabulary. Right register for a story trying to stay inside a six-year-old's plain experience while an adult crisis unfolds around her.

**13. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that reads like a novice wrote it?** The section-break device (a plain horizontal rule) chops the chapter into many vignettes; effective but slightly mechanical by the sixth or seventh occurrence. Otherwise clean.

**14. What do you expect next?** The March 4th evaluation appointment itself, presumably a diagnosis (ADHD is suggested, but given the "not medical" line and reading precocity, possibly giftedness or twice-exceptional territory) and its fallout.

## Chapter 2: March 4th

**1. What happened?** Chloe undergoes a full psychoeducational testing session with a psychologist, Ben, who finds she is off the charts on reasoning/verbal subtests (some ceiling out) but only ordinary-to-below on processing speed/fine-motor copying; afterward, told nothing changes, she teaches herself over the rest of the year to finish fast, stay quiet, and disappear.

**2. How did this chapter change the main character?** This is the hinge chapter. She now has direct, if incomplete, evidence that she is measured as exceptional ("at the top of it") and that the finding will do nothing for her ("the rest of them put it in the folder"). By June she has converted that knowledge into a survival strategy: minimize visible output, never finish early where it's noticed, isolate herself, and let adults believe "meeting expectations" is the truth. This is the birth of her masking behavior, not an add-on — an earned, causal response to a specific disappointment.

**3. Is she behaving like a real person of her age?** Yes, remarkably well-calibrated for six going on seven. The physical tells (stomach growling, switching legs, gripping the pencil till her hand aches, not knowing she's allowed to say she's tired) are age-real. Her testing answers are precocious but the book shows the work: the boat/car and hour/year analogies are the kind of thing a verbally gifted six-year-old produces, not a thirty-year-old's insight in a child's mouth. The one place it strains slightly is the sustained multi-turn theory-of-mind reasoning in the corridor ("she can't work out which fast enough" whether to ask for the number) — but this is more an authorial narration of an internal conflict than a claim she articulated it, so it's defensible.

**4. How intelligent is she, judged only by what she does?** Raised sharply this chapter. She solves the block-design (matrix reasoning) subtests faster than the tester can reset, ceilings two verbal subtests (the form runs out, not her), and reasons correctly that "an hour" and "a year" are both arbitrary human conventions — a genuinely abstract insight most adults wouldn't produce unprompted. Her weakness is exactly localized: visual-motor coding/speed and copying, not reasoning. Estimate: 9-9.5/10 on reasoning/verbal, closer to 5/10 on processing speed — a real, specific split (consistent with a plausible profile of giftedness plus a mild processing-speed weakness), not blanket superintelligence.

**5. Does she think she is smarter than other people?** Still no overt self-rating, but new material: she "stops trusting the questions" when every answer she gives seems to be accepted as correct — a flicker of noticing she may not be gettable-wrong the normal way. She also is competitive/hungry for the tasks ("Is there another like it?") in a way that suggests she enjoys succeeding at hard things, not just completing them.

**6. How do the teachers and other adults behave?** Ben is careful, honest with Chloe within limits, and bluntly cynical with the mother about institutions ("bad numbers move a system," "the rest of them put it in the folder") — an unusually frank adult voice for this genre. The mother is earnest and overwhelmed, translating jargon for Chloe in real time and protecting her from the raw number. The school afterward is inert: Mrs. Aldana's single check-in ("is everything all right at home?") entirely misreads the situation and gets a flat "yes."

**7. How much does the chapter work to make things seem normal?** Heavily — the framing device is banal (ice cream on a Wednesday, a waiting-room fish tank joke, a report card with a form-letter comment) wrapped around what is actually a life-altering piece of information being deliberately withheld from a child by a caring adult. The last line — the report card literally papering over the sticky note where the appointment date used to be — is the chapter's thesis image: official paperwork replacing/erasing the real record.

**8. Is the book hiding something, and HOW?** Yes, doubly: (a) the actual number/percentile is withheld from both Chloe and the reader — Ben refuses to give it, so we as readers are put in the same epistemic position as the child; (b) the technique is the closed door — we get the psychologist's conversation with the mother only in overheard fragments, filtered by what a child in a hallway could catch, deliberately incomplete. This is a controlled, effective structural withholding (not summarized-and-skipped, but eavesdropped and fragmentary).

**9. Is the narration plain and neutral, or does it address you?** Still tightly close-third and neutral. One near-exception: "But a word like that always comes with a number stuck to it" reads as narratorial commentary rather than pure observation — a small step toward telling the reader what to conclude, the first sentence in the book that isn't purely camera.

**10. Anything that contradicts something earlier, or does not make sense?** Nothing contradictory. It does retroactively clarify chapter 1: the "not medical" framing from the pediatrician and the mother's insistence something is wrong are both partially right — Ben confirms no attention/behavior disorder but a processing-speed gap plus giftedness, which recontextualizes chapter 1's "trouble focusing" as boredom/mismatch rather than ADHD, though this isn't fully certain yet.

**11. Was anything a surprise?** Yes — I expected (per my chapter-1 guess) a possible ADHD-adjacent diagnosis; instead it's closer to a twice-exceptional/gifted profile with a processing-speed gap, and Ben's flat "she's completely fine" undercuts the reader's expectation of a medicalized turn. The "the form ran out before she did" detail is a well-placed narrative surprise beat.

**12. The prose specifically.** Simple in vocabulary, but the sentence architecture is doing more work than chapter 1 — long cumulative sentences track her hands working through the block-design puzzle in real time, giving a kinesthetic, almost technical precision (the corner-to-corner block description) that reads as controlled/literary in structure while staying lexically plain. This is the right choice: it lets the reader feel the mismatch between her fast reasoning and slow hands without narrating it directly.

**13. Anything annoying, repetitive, or that reads like a novice wrote it?** The section-break vignette structure continues from chapter 1 and works better here because each break corresponds to a discrete subtest — structurally motivated rather than just a scene-collage. No real missteps.

**14. What do you expect next?** A time jump forward (the chapter title pattern and "after the appointment, nothing changes" suggest the story moves in leaps); likely the third-grade gifted program mentioned as "the only lever," and probably a chapter showing the isolation from this year hardening rather than resolving.
