## 2. A child who knows they are exceptional, compares favourably, is modest, or treats a normal-for-them feat as a feat

### Chapter 30

**The one item I would put in front of the author before anything else in these four chapters.**

`30_cleared.md:23`:

> Around her, other candidates chew pens and glance up at the clock every few minutes; her own eyes stay on the page from the first question to the last.

The comparison is built by the narrator, semicolon and all, and the reader is invited to score
it. Chloe never looks at the other candidates and draws no conclusion, which is correct for her
— but that is precisely the problem: the noticing she is not allowed to do has been moved one
level up, to the narration. It is also the House Rule 1 pattern in its usual position, sitting
two lines above the beat that would have proved it ("She reads it as having sat an exam,"
line 25).

Recommendation, not applied: cut the first clause and keep "Her own eyes stay on the page from
the first question to the last," or keep the other candidates and drop the contrastive framing.
Either costs a little grade, which is why I have left it.

`30_cleared.md:7`:

> The summaries start getting picked up on their own, once people notice hers runs the length of a normal article and holds more in it than the article does

A favourable comparison in narration, attributed to third parties. Weaker than the exam item: it
is causal (it explains why the summaries travel) rather than admiring, and the judgement is
lodged with readers rather than with her. Reported for completeness.

`30_cleared.md:63`, borderline:

> it takes a full twenty minutes to get through the list

"A full" is the narrator's thumb on the scale. "Twenty minutes" alone does identical work and
lets the reader do the arithmetic. One word; not worth the grade.

**Working, chapter 30, and worth the author knowing the audit could tell:**

- Line 9: "it's just her, it takes a day, at school she was doing ten pages a week alongside nine other subjects, so this is well under half of what she's used to." An accurate account of an extraordinary CV with the wrong conclusion drawn from it, by her, in her own voice. This is the standard's target register exactly.
- Line 15: "none of it registers as a load, because her actual timetable at fourteen was heavier than this and included getting hit." Buried scale and rationalisation in one clause, with the most extraordinary fact in the sentence thrown away at the end of it.
- Line 65: "It's the school's own, not accredited anywhere outside it. It wouldn't hold up in an actual court." **This is not modesty and the distinction matters.** Modesty would require knowing there is something to be modest about. She is deflating a credential's legal standing, which is a fact she has, not her own ability, which is a fact she doesn't. She volunteers it before he asks. Line 69, "You're recording something with no legal standing," presses the same point against her own interest.
- Line 53: "I started that April, a few months after I turned seven." Flat. When Whitaker says "That's young," she agrees and immediately hands the strangeness to her mother, who objected at the time. She never once treats her admission as a selection.
- **The allocation of outside astonishment is the single biggest reason the scene survives.** Whitaker reacts exactly once, at line 55, and it is about her age, not her ability. He does not react to eleven languages, to a twenty-minute curriculum, to two published papers already pulled and highlighted in his folder, or to three compartmentalised projects. `WHITAKER.md` rules surprise off his page and the manuscript holds that line under maximum temptation.
- **The cut of "She tells him everything" was correct.** It was a total. What stands in its place, line 45's "she gives him more than the minimum from the first page onward, because he's already shown her what happens to it," is a reason rather than a total, and it is about him, not about the volume of her history.
- **The April fix reads clean.** "I started that April, a few months after I turned seven. There was a four-week programme the summer before, which is how they found me, or how I found them, depending which end you look from." The camp is downgraded to a programme, the discovery is made ambiguous in her favour of neither party, and no line in the answer flags that a seven-year-old being found is unusual.

## 3. Contrary evidence that goes unrationalised

**No unrationalised encounter found in any of the four chapters.** This is the standard's hardest
test and the stretch passes it deliberately. Detail, since the author asked for everything:

### Chapter 30

Line 11 is the whole test and it is the cleanest execution in the book that I have read:

> Another third turn into a second email asking the same question in different words, as if the first answer got lost somewhere between her and them, and she types the same three sentences again and sends them again. The pattern repeats often enough, same question, same drop-off, that she decides it's people being funny about credentials and stops turning it over between emails.

The evidence is repeated, the pattern is noticed as a pattern, the explanation is wrong, and the
loop closes ("stops turning it over"). It also does the author's example a second way: she says
the same three sentences twice and concludes nothing from having to.

Whitaker's "That's young" (line 55) is rationalised at line 57 by handing it to her mother's
objection, a fact she already holds. The one place nothing is explained away is line 109, the
joke she misses — and that is a social miss, not evidence about her ability, so there is nothing
for her to explain.

### Chapter 28

- Lines 95-104: a stranger tells her, in writing, that he cannot make the arithmetic work; she answers with the arithmetic; he doesn't reply; "She has told him the truth. It's just arithmetic." Rationalised, and "arithmetic" is borrowed straight back out of his own email, which keeps the sentence hers rather than the narrator's.
- Line 14: the loading-dock question is rationalised by behaviour rather than by thought. She shortens to match and concludes nothing about him. That is the more sophisticated version.
- **Lines 112-270, Ruth's four statistics, are the largest single block of contrary evidence in my four chapters, and the rationalisation is structural rather than verbal.** Each block ends "And it moves." Twice the deflection lands on a member of the group's own failure (Theo's parallel parking, Sam's failed reading placement), which re-anchors the sample back onto Halstead. Once it lands on arithmetic about Chloe at four or five, which Sam and Nadia both score against themselves ("five is still insulting to the rest of us," "five is generous, some of us didnt learn that until nine"). Nobody ever engages with the number. The rationalisation rule has been turned into a shape instead of a sentence, and Ruth stopping in April is the payoff for a reader who noticed. This is the best piece of construction in the four chapters.
- One line to watch, and it belongs to chapter 31 rather than to me: "kavi: 21 out of what denominator, adults total or adults actually tested" is the only line in the sequence that treats a statistic as real. He gets his answer and drops it, which is enough here.

### Chapter 29

The file is the largest single block of contrary evidence given to any character in my stretch,
and the handling is right: Theo rationalises what he can ("Theo had assumed, for nine years,
that the guy just quit," line 55) and does not manufacture a rationalisation for what he can't.
His twelve-year-old frame survives at lines 107 and 110 without adult correction.

**Judgement call flagged for the author, not a defect.** Line 113, "He puts his hand over his
mouth and sits like that," is a realisation landing at chapter 29. It stays inside the standard
because of what it lands *on*: that the school was under state observation, that an intrusion
happened, that he was inside it and did not know. He never thinks a sentence about what the four
children in that corridor were, and neither does the narrator. If a later chapter converts this
moment into "we were extraordinary," the defect will be there rather than here.

### Chapter 27

- Four hundred candidates, a test lowered twice, a pass rate under a third. Rationalised in action (make it easier), then verbally by Ruth. The bar moving is `NADIA.md`'s own account of her blind spot, and the manuscript matches it.
- The board's monthly line about burn and headcount (line 92) is contrary evidence about her hiring, and she "reads [it] all the way through and answers by posting two more roles the same afternoon." - Line 73: a professional adversary tells her flatly "We read the client wrong." She takes it as the end of it and leaves. No conclusion drawn about herself.
