# Calibration audit: chapters 11-14

Scope: `chapters/11_eight.md`, `12_nine.md`, `13_ten_pages.md`, `14_sixty_degrees.md`.
Chloe eight to eleven, all four fully inside Halstead. Read against
`passes/CALIBRATION_AUDIT.md` and `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`, with
`characters/CHLOE.md`, `SAM.md`, `RUTH.md`, `KAVI.md` and `_CALIBRATION.md` read first.

**Verdict: no calibration defects found in these four chapters.** The load-bearing
section (2, they think they are normal) holds without exception across all four. Every
item below is either something working, or a borderline case tested and cleared with the
reasoning shown, so the author can overrule me on any of them.

## The three named tests

**Chapter 13, the ten-pages chapter: passes.** No child, teacher or narrator anywhere in the
chapter registers the volume as unusual. `grep` for "ten page" returns nine occurrences across seven lines, and every one outside the
chapter title is a unit of work, never a quantity worth remarking on. Hearn's own framing at `13:11` does the
opposite: *"It has to be as good, and that's the part that's actually hard, because ten pages is
easy, ten pages is just hours."* Volume is dismissed in the same breath it is set. Chloe's
frame at `13:13` makes it prior-normal before the reader can be surprised by it: *"Chloe has
written ten-page reports before, three in Mandarin last year and one in French the year before
that."* What is new to her is the cadence and the abstraction, never the page count. She reads all
fifty-one of Hamilton's papers by mid-December (`13:23`) in a subordinate clause with no total
attached, and the chapter never counts the roughly thirty essays she writes over the year.
Hearn's deflations are the whole method: *"That's Madison," he says, and starts the hour without
adding anything to it* (`13:21`). The chapter closes on `13:186`, *"I don't know," Chloe says, and
goes back to eating*, which is the calibration in one line: she cannot account for her own
improvement because she has never thought of it as anything to account for.

**Chapter 12, the three differentiated teaching beats and Kavi's converted objections: passes.**
Kavi at `12:17-25` argues like a clever nine-year-old who has been wronged, not like a lawyer.
The test I applied: what is he actually angry about? Not that he deserves harder work, which
would require knowing he is exceptional, but that a form lied to him and that the answer was
fixed before he was handed the paper. *"the thing that stopped me is my age, which they had
written down before they handed me anything to fill in"* (`12:21`) is a child's grievance about
adults, exactly sized. *"They kept the request eleven months and gave me back a date that was
fixed before I wrote it down"* (`12:25`) is the same. He never once claims he could do the
material better than the tens or the elevens; he says the classes exist in the building and his
birthday is the only bar. The enumeration ("The first one is", "The second one is", "That's all
three of them") is the closest thing to lawyer register, and it is defused twice: by his own
object-fiddling (*"He squares the sheet against the edge of the table"*, `12:25`, his tic per
`KAVI.md`), and by Sam cutting across it at `12:23` with *"Is there a third one, or can I go."*
The scene refuses to find him impressive, which is the technique working. Ruth's `12:19` is a
nine-year-old's cynicism, not an adult's: *"They read it. They counted how many people wanted
each thing, and then they did what they were going to do anyway."*
The three beats themselves are properly differentiated and all three are calibrated: chemistry
runs on **three Fridays of wrong turns** to isolate one variable (`12:83-91`), textiles on **two
failed shirts and a traced cheat** (`12:99`), the overhang on **Sam losing to a girl in the year
below and going to ask her how** (`12:113`). None is a display of ability; each is a record of
being wrong first.

**Chapter 14, Sam and the ninety seconds: reads as a boy enjoying a story against himself.**
Confirmed, and I can say why it does not read as modesty about competence. Modesty requires a
success to be modest about; the story he is telling is a **failure**, and he is the only person
out in the first ninety seconds. The narration gives it deadpan and lets the failure land as the
punchline: *"he goes over the second floor bannister onto three teachers coming up, which works,
then lands badly, which does not"* (`14:203`). He never says the bannister move worked; the
narrator does, in half a clause, and moves on. What he does is repeat it: *"he talks about it from
there all afternoon, again at dinner, and he is still talking about it in June"* (`14:203`),
which is the stubborn-repetition comedy `SAM.md` names as his only permitted kind. The closing
line seals it: *"Sam is describing the bannister for the fourth time to two people who were
standing underneath it"* (`14:213`). Telling it a fourth time to eyewitnesses is not information
and not humility, it is a bit he is running, and it matches `SAM.md`'s Embarrassment entry
exactly ("Being the joke and being humiliated are different things to him, and the difference is
who is telling it").

## 1. Spock, Sheldon, an AI, or a mini-adult

**Nothing found.** A keyword sweep of all four chapters for `impressive|genius|prodig|gifted|
brilliant|amazing|remarkable|extraordinar|talent|most people|smartest|show off|proud` returns
**zero hits in all four files.** The vocabulary of exceptionality is simply absent.

Against the AI failure mode specifically, these chapters are unusually well armoured. Wrong turns
and dead ends carried on the page, in order: the mallet handle that comes out oval and stays oval
(`11:147`); ninetieth of ninety in drawing and in dance in the same week (`11:159`); choir
abandoned in the sixth week (`11:199`); *"she cannot make the difference happen in her own
mouth"* on Mandarin tones, unfixed for a year (`12:53-61`); grey instead of white and three
Fridays of experiments to find out why (`12:83-91`); two shirts that drag on the same arm
(`12:99`); riding dropped out of boredom (`12:141`); *"Chloe loses the first three games, because
she keeps counting the same card twice"* (`12:161`); four seconds against a minute (`13:49`); a
December plateau of essays that *"are the same essays they have been since October"* (`13:164`);
eleven attempts at one joint over three weeks (`14:93`); being sick at ten o'clock and going back
to it from the bathroom floor (`14:131`); and a B with eleven lines of criticism (`14:137-139`).
No perfect recall, no clean first attempts, and boredom said out loud repeatedly.

Three passages I tested for mini-adult and cleared, with reasoning:

- **`11:171-175`, Chloe negotiating with Mrs. Okonkwo.** *"Then can I move teams on the Wednesday
  instead, because that's a swap and not a drop."* The move (refused A, ask for B) is a child's
  move, and the reasoning is her signature concrete-parallel shape, not procedure. `11:175`,
  *"I've done two whole terms of Wednesdays with them before coming in here about it,"* is the
  closest to an adult framing her own patience as standing, but it lands as a kid saying "I put
  up with it for ages first," which is a fairness appeal. Cleared. Note also that she loses,
  flatly and twice, and the chapter does not compensate her for it.
- **`12:163-187`, the vendor contract.** The scene is built so she arrives through confusion, not
  audit: *"What does that one actually mean? I've read it three times"* (`12:171`), then
  *"That's what I couldn't get. I couldn't work out what breaking it would look like"* (`12:181`).
  She frames her own contribution as a failure to understand. Her father does all the
  generalising and all the acting, and the chapter ends with him telling her about the outcome
  *"at some length, while she eats a sandwich"* (`12:187`), which puts her back where she belongs.
  This is also planted: logic at `11:123` is *"an hour of being shown a sentence and asked what it
  is actually claiming."* She is doing her homework at the kitchen table without knowing it.
  Cleared, and it is one of the best-calibrated scenes in the four.
- **`12:107-109`, Bex and Iyad.** *"Iyad agrees with you loudly in a way that means he stopped
  listening around the second sentence"* is the most adult-sounding social diagnosis in the four
  chapters. Cleared on the grounds that the two grievances underneath it are child-sized (someone
  talks over me, someone repeats my idea as his) and her response is petty rather than measured:
  she gets to the joint question *"before Bex can get a word in."* Flagged here only because it is
  the closest call.

## 2. A child knowing they are exceptional, comparing favourably, or being modest

**Nothing found.** The cohort is the yardstick in every single measurement in these chapters,
without exception. The pattern is visible in the grep: *"everybody in the year can do it at
will"* (`11:143`), *"everybody is bad at it and knows why"* (`13:67`), *"most of the year is in
the six-to-ten range"* (`13:61`), *"most of the year has managed a collision at least once"*
(`14:47`), *"by Christmas most of the year is past where they ever were with darts"* (`13:89`).
Every advantage is transient and gets absorbed by the room within a paragraph.

The single best instance of section 2 working is `11:69`, Chloe using Halstead as leverage in a
curfew fight: *"At school I can walk down to the library at eleven at night if I feel like it.
There's nobody on the door, nobody asks me anything, and that's a whole building of
eight-year-olds, and the roof hasn't come off it yet."* An eight-year-old deploying the strangest
school in the country as an ordinary precedent, and losing anyway.

Second best, `12:5-13`. Handed an eighth academic subject at nine, her concern is clerical:
*"No, I wanted to know whether somebody had put it on there by mistake"* (`12:11`). The office
answers in the same register: *"It goes up every single year, because that is supposed to be the
whole idea of a school, so is there a problem with it?"* (`12:9`).

Two items tested and cleared:

- **`13:126`, the music comparison.** *"in the third week he plays eight bars and she gets the
  whole of it while the girl next to her gets four notes."* This is the only sentence in four
  chapters that scores Chloe favourably against a named other. Cleared: it is narration reporting
  a classroom fact rather than her thought; it is bracketed by *"She is a mediocre player of
  everything they put in front of her"* in the same sentence; and the conclusion she draws is a
  preference, not a verdict ("she keeps the block all year"). Worth the author's eye anyway, since
  it is the one of its kind.
- **`13:146`, "it's thin, I know it's thin."** Tested for modesty and it is not modesty: she is
  rating a rushed argument, not deflecting a compliment, and the reason she gives is *"it was
  Tuesday and I had to put something down on the page."* The reverse case at `12:141`, *"It's
  boring. It's easy, I can do all of it,"* is the correct calibration stated outright: things
  being easy is a fact about the thing, not information about her, and she says it in the same
  breath as conceding Priya is better at all of it.

No character in these chapters treats a normal-for-them feat as a feat. The one place it could
have happened, `14:181-185`, is handled precisely: patronised by her grandmother
(*"once in a whole year is hardly a lot, is it"*), Chloe corrects the **count** and never the
difficulty: *"October was the first one, and I've done it a dozen times since then, more than a
dozen."* She insists on the record and lets the valuation stand.

## 3. Contrary evidence going unrationalised

**Nothing found, and nothing needed rationalising**, because in the two places contrary evidence
appears the text withholds the knowledge rather than handing it over and explaining it away.
That is the stronger move and I want to flag it as deliberate-looking rather than as a gap.

- **`12:169-187`, her father misses what she asks about.** A working adult reads a commercial
  proposal and does not see the hollow promise until a nine-year-old asks him what breaking it
  would look like. She never registers this as evidence of anything. She registers herself as the
  one who could not follow the paragraph, and at `12:185` disclaims the rest of it outright:
  *"No idea, I only got as far as that one."* No rationalisation is required because no
  comparison is made.
- **`14:161-167` and `14:177-187`, two adults applying an ordinary scale.** Her mother meets a
  fourteen-thousand-word essay on area bombing with *"Well, a B's a B, and it's still a good
  grade"* (`14:167`), and Chloe accepts it without correction, having already given up on the
  explanation at `14:163`. Her grandmother says the arrows *"all sounds very complicated for a
  school."* Both are contrary evidence pointing the same way Chloe already believes, so both
  confirm her frame rather than threaten it. Correct.

## 4. Scale announced rather than buried

**Nothing found.** The camp-arc model is being run properly in all four chapters: the dramatised
scenes carry feeling and failure, and the scale sits in subordinate clauses that are never
returned to. Inventory of what is buried and never totalled:

- `11:121`, a Spanish novel in nine days, dropped into a sentence whose actual subject is Ruth
  doing Portuguese *"badly."*
- `11:123`, the day going to nine hours after Christmas, stated as a schedule fact.
- `11:143`, an entire year of eight-year-olds shooting foam darts out of the air at will, given
  as the setup for what she actually cares about, *"the sound two darts make when they meet and
  burst."*
- `12:51`, ten-page end-of-term reports written in Mandarin inside one year of study, normalised
  in five words: *"on the same question everybody else has."*
- `13:23`, fifty-one Federalist papers by mid-December, in a clause, then *"and then she starts
  on Madison's."*
- `13:124`, two eleven-year-olds trading insults across a dinner table in Russian and Arabic while
  Kavi complains in a third, played entirely as sibling teasing.
- `14:63`, five languages and two more queued, buried inside a playground argument about whether
  Latin counts as cheating.
- `14:123`, *"I've been sitting in the same ethics class as you since we were ten,"* delivered as
  a snap at Sam.
- `14:93`, `14:105`, `14:77`: a Japanese corner joint, a seven-thousand-word essay in seven days,
  eleven pages on the age of the universe, each mentioned once and dropped.

Two places the narrator comes closest to speaking to the reader. Neither is a totalling of
achievement, so neither is a section-4 defect, but both are House Rule 1 candidates and I am
reporting rather than editing:

- **`13:160`**: *"A mark with no line under it leaves her only her own doubt to argue against,
  and that's the harder version of being told she's wrong."* The trailing clause names what the
  preceding clause already carried. Strike test: strike from "and that's" and nothing is lost.
- **`13:164`**: *"A plateau and a beginning look exactly alike from inside the same week; only
  which way the next one goes tells you which one you were in."* A general aphorism in the
  narrator's voice, glossing the situation the paragraph has just given. This is the one sentence
  in four chapters that reads as written at the reader.

I also tested **`11:29-33`**, the father on the hot dog argument (*"Nobody's ever put the hinge to
me, not in twenty years of having that argument"*) and cleared it. Chloe is upstairs in the
shower and cannot hear it, so it gives her nothing; it is an adult noticing, which the design
needs; and the scene's real subject is the parents noticing she has started singing and started
fighting back, which is where the next four lines go.

## What is working, briefly

The four chapters use the same three mechanisms consistently and they all hold.

**The cohort as the only yardstick.** Every number is reported against ninety other people and
every advantage is absorbed within a paragraph. `13:85-89` is the clearest run of it: Chloe and
Ruth solve the paintball problem, hold eleven against everybody else's four for one hour, and
within three sentences Kavi has it on his own, Nadia has it off her own back, Priya gets it out of
Ruth in ninety seconds, and the whole year is past it by Christmas.

**Teachers who never remark.** Hearn's *"That's Madison"* (`13:21`); Vasquez sending her back to
run it wet on purpose instead of praising the white result (`12:89`); Bell explaining in advance
why darts out of the air are the easiest thing in the building (`11:141`); Kowalczyk explaining
ten as a geometry problem rather than a demand (`13:43-47`); Okonkwo refusing twice in four flat
words (`11:165-169`). Not one instance of an adult inside the school marking a student out.

**The dinner table doing the deflating.** Sam cutting Kavi's objections short (`12:23`), Sam
accusing Chloe of losing the same argument every Monday (`13:73`), Sam telling her she will get a
C and deserve it (`14:117`), Ruth's *"So what you're telling me is he's right"* (`14:149`). The
group never lets a good result sit as a good result.

And the single strongest calibration beat in the four chapters is `14:163`: handed a chance to
explain the essay to her mother, Chloe works out that the answer starts with the hat, then the
seven days, then why hedging counts against you rather than as manners, and gives up: *"That it
was wrong," she says. "I got a B."* The scale is entirely visible to the reader and entirely
unspoken by her, and the reason it goes unspoken is that she has no idea any of it is worth
saying.
