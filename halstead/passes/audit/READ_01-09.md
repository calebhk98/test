# First read: chapters 1-9

Read straight through, as a reader, after roughly forty automated passes. This
is not a measurement report; it is what the book feels like on the page.

## The five worst things, worst first

**1. With the tags off, the parents are the same person.** This is the
complaint the author names first (#5) and it is worst in the two adults, not
the kids. Compare chapter 9:

> "She said take my birthday, Dave, and she's seven years old, and she stood
> there in that gap and offered up her own birthday like it was money she'd
> saved."

> "I heard her, because I was standing right there in the doorway with a
> dishcloth in my hand, and I've been standing somewhere in this house every
> night since, trying to work out what I'm supposed to say back to that."

> "You haven't said it back, though, which is exactly what I'm telling you,
> and I have been the only one saying anything about any of this since
> August, and I am so tired of being the only one who has to."

> "What do you want me to say, Meg, when you already know what I think? I've
> thought it since the car ride home in July, I've thought it every day
> since, and you've known that the whole time without me having to say it
> out loud at this table."

Meg and Dave are supposed to be an anxious controller and a passive avoider,
and the content of what they say does still sort that way. But the
*sentence*, independent of who's speaking it, is the same sentence: name the
listener, state the grievance, land on "I've been [verb]-ing every [time
unit] since [month]." Ben the psychologist, Mr. Baptiste, Dr. Ammons, and the
librarian all build the identical three-clause cascade joined by "and" /
"because" / "so." Cover the dialogue tags on this argument and you cannot
tell which parent is talking from the shape of the sentence alone, only from
the content. That is the specific failure mode the author is complaining
about, and it reads like a single narrator's rhythm imposed on every mouth in
the book, adult and child alike.

**2. A live scene collapses into a synopsis and climbs back out, chapter 8.**
Rule 8 by the book. Dr. Ammons is questioning Meg, in the room, in real time:

> "Did she stay there overnight."
> "The whole time," her mom says. "Every night of it."
> "How far is it from the house."
> "Half a day in the car."
>
> Every question goes to her mother, and Chloe sits through it.
>
> "Were the same adults with her the whole time, or did they change over."
>
> Chloe's hand stops on the thread.
>
> "The same," her mom says.
>
> The questions keep coming in that voice, working down through who ran it,
> how they found her, whether Chloe came back different, in what way, and
> starting when.
>
> "Did anything happen to her there."

The scene is quoted, then it is minuted, then it is quoted again, and the
minuted middle throws away exactly the exchange ("whether Chloe came back
different, in what way") that a scene this important should be dramatizing
line by line. It reads like a cut made for pacing that nobody went back to
rebuild as dialogue.

**3. Two named states in nine chapters, and both are doubled up with a
trailing tail.** The house rule allows roughly five uses in the whole
thirty-six-chapter book, spent deliberately. Two are already gone by chapter
7, and neither is a deliberate high point — both are throwaway sentences that
would be fine without the naming:

> Chapter 5: "Chloe does eight problems in the packet and gets them all
> right, while the reason the turning-over works stays shut to her, and the
> gap between those two facts is making her furious in a way she can feel
> along her jaw."

> Chapter 7: "Every answer matches what she wrote the first time, so she
> puts her hands in her lap and waits for the room to catch up around her,
> relieved to find it still works like it worked in first grade."

Both sentences already show the fact (jaw tension; hands in lap, waiting).
Naming "furious" and "relieved" on top of the physical beat is the exact
double-dip Rule 1's tone-construction fix warns against — give the sound,
then give what the listener does, never the state — except here it's the
narrator doing it to Chloe's own body. Strike "relieved to find it still
works like it worked in first grade" from the second one and nothing is
lost; the hands going into the lap already said it.

**4. The "finishes first, checks it, waits" beat has become a tic.** It runs,
close to unchanged in shape, in chapter 1 (the subtraction sheet: "the
pattern is already found, she writes the answer and then sits with the
pencil motionless"), chapter 2 (the block designs, three times running),
chapter 5 (the fifteen-minute writing sheet, the fractions packet), and
chapter 7, where it returns almost beat-for-beat:

> "The first math sheet comes around midmorning, and she squints at it
> before she can tell that it's adding with carrying... Having done all
> thirty she goes back to the top and checks them all over. Every answer
> matches what she wrote the first time, so she puts her hands in her lap
> and waits for the room to catch up around her..."

This is the same move as chapter 1's subtraction sheet, restaged with a
different arithmetic operation and nothing new discovered by it. By chapter
7 the reader has already been shown that Chloe finishes early and is bored
four separate times; the fifth doesn't earn its page.

**5. The daily-asking scene is run three times with diminishing returns**
(chapters 3, 7, 8), and chapter 7's version is where it goes slack.
Chapter 3 earns the device — it is new information, the parents are
actually deciding. Chapter 8 earns it again because it ends in the jacket
fight and a diagnosis. Chapter 7's version —

> "At breakfast she asks, standing at the end of the table instead of
> sitting down to it; she asks in the car on the way to school and again on
> the way home... by the end of September it is several times a day."

— adds nothing chapter 3 hadn't already established (the parents are stuck,
Chloe won't let it go) and nothing chapter 8 doesn't do harder. It's the
weakest stretch in the span for exactly that reason: a repeat of an already-
used construction, in the chapter that most needs new material because it's
also carrying the second-grade classroom scenes and the phone-number scene,
none of which have room to breathe as a result.

## Per-chapter notes

**Chapter 1 (Before).** The strongest opening chapter available to this
book: the folder Chloe doesn't know about, the appointment she can't say
the name of, twelve months compressed into discrete, well-chosen scenes.
The "short version" strategy (holding back the full answer to "water") is a
real, specific behavior, not a stated trait. Minor: "which she is good at"
(the eye-tracking test) and "The short version works every time she uses
it... so she has to hold it back on purpose, every single time" both edge
toward narrating the point rather than trusting the two demonstrated
instances (clouds, water cycle) to make it. Cut, the chapter loses nothing.

**Chapter 2 (March 4th).** The best chapter in the span, and the answer to
the specific question asked: does the IQ test land without being told a
number? Mostly, yes — through mechanism rather than pure inference. The
reader is shown two real facts (ceiling effects — "on a couple of them she
got to the end of what the form has for her age and I kept going with a
different one, and none of that counts"; and a composite that averages
speed against reasoning) and told, correctly, that this kind of averaging
produces a number that "reads as good on the page" while undercounting her.
That's a true and well-chosen mechanism, but it is delivered as expository
dialogue from a professional to an adult, not inferred by the reader from
what's on the page the way the brief's "without being told" implies. It's
the right kind of telling — Ben is allowed to talk shop — but it's telling,
and a sharper cut would trust the coding-test scene (the neatness question,
timed out mid-row, "which hasn't happened yet today") to do more of that
work on its own.

**Chapter 3 (The Letter).** Strong. The two competing parental positions are
distinct in content even where the syntax repeats (see worst-thing #1). The
library negotiations continue to characterize Chloe correctly — she loses
every one of them, which is the right call given Rule 7.

**Chapter 4 (Pluto).** Good introductions; Sam, Ruth, Kavi land as distinct
personalities in this chapter specifically (the room-shaped-object joke, the
dwarf-planet argument, the volcano scene) even if their sentence shapes
converge later. Owen is planted well and quietly — worksheet folded small
under the table, still copying the word off the board — for a payoff two
chapters later.

**Chapter 5 (Behind).** The bridge and the fractions sequences are the
strongest pieces of "smart kid, still a kid" writing in the span — the
answer arrives at night, alone, on the floor, by trial rather than by
insight handed down, and Chloe sorts "the ones that came out right for the
reason from the ones that came out right off the trick" without anybody
telling her to. The Owen conversation is the emotional spine of the chapter
and is handled with real restraint — nobody explains what Chloe is supposed
to feel about a friend leaving over something that felt, to her, like pure
good fortune. See worst-thing #3 for the one sentence that breaks the
control established everywhere else in this chapter.

**Chapter 6 (The List).** The dinner monologue is a deliberate list (per the
two protected exceptions, not flagged as a rule violation) and it earns its
keep on the emotional beat at the end — the dad's "Cool," said to a room he's
stopped really seeing, and then, alone in the car, "I don't know what that
is, Meg, and I was standing right there." That said, the back half of the
list ("Then chess... Then negative numbers... Then karate... Then the nerf
guns") is the one passage in the span I had to push through rather than read
straight; it stops accumulating meaning around the wood-shop mention and
starts just being an inventory. The parking-lot hallway argument among the
four kids about the enrollment form is well done — Ruth is petty and right
at the same time, which is exactly the note Rule 7 wants for a child arguing
with other children rather than adults.

**Chapter 7 (The Same Room).** The weakest chapter in the span; see
worst-things #4 and #5. The phone-number scene (nobody has anybody's last
name) is the one real new idea in the chapter and it's a good one — it's
specific, it's sad without being told to be sad, and it sets up the
isolation that chapters 8 and 9 pay off. But it's surrounded by two
recycled beats (the early-finisher tic, the daily-asking loop) that have
already done their work.

**Chapter 8 (The Asking).** Recovers well. Dr. Ammons is a genuinely
different adult voice from Ben and the parents — clinical, unhurried,
willing to let silence sit — until the summary-collapse flagged as
worst-thing #2. Mrs. Prahl's thinking-corner scene, and "some children get
there in October and some get there in May, both of those are the same
speed as far as I'm concerned," is a good, quietly cruel bit of mismatch —
an adult being kind in a way that fails to help, without anybody saying so.
The jacket fight ("You do, you hate me, and Dad says yes, but you're the one
who says no, and I hate you, I hate you so much") is the correct register
for a seven-year-old losing an argument she can't win any other way — loud,
unfair, and immediately regretted by the silence that follows it at dinner.

**Chapter 9 (February).** This is the chapter the brief asks about
specifically. The floor scene works. It is built almost entirely out of
things Chloe is willing to give away in ascending order of what they cost
her (dishes, chores, the bike, Christmas, her own birthday), each one a
concrete noun rather than a stated feeling, and the only narrator
intervention is physical: "Chloe's fists are still in the carpet," "her
face is wet all the way to her chin." No sentence tells the reader what to
feel about it. It earns the claim that it's meant to be the strongest page
of the first act — it is. The parents' argument that follows it is the
scene where worst-thing #1 is most visible (see above), which is a shame,
because it sits directly against the strongest scene in the span and makes
the contrast in craft obvious. The chapter's other strong choice: Chloe's
mother sitting in the driveway with the engine and lights off, not yet
willing to come inside, closing the chapter on the parent's cost rather than
the child's win.

## Continuity

Nothing that breaks outright. Ben's "two years between now and the only
lever the school actually has for her" (chapter 2, March 2012, third grade
being the gifted-program floor) is closer to a year and a half by the
book's own school-year math; minor enough that it reads as adult
approximation rather than an error, but worth a look. Ages track correctly
across the span — six from chapter 1 through the camp, seven by chapter 9
("she's seven years old"), with no birthday scene needed to make that work.
The Owen departure gets two different accounts from Sam and Ruth in chapter
5, which per the brief is deliberate and not flagged.

## The four questions

**Would you keep reading after chapter 1?** Yes, without hesitation. It ends
on an object (a name on a sticky note she can't pronounce) rather than an
explanation, and that's a real hook.

**Which chapter is the weakest, and why?** Chapter 7. It sits between two
strong chapters (6 and 8) and does the least new work of any chapter in the
span — its two main engines, the early-finisher beat and the daily-asking
loop, are both repeats of scenes the book has already run once (worst-things
#4 and #5), and its one genuinely new idea, the phone-number scene, is good
enough that it makes the recycled material around it more visible by
comparison, not less.

**Which single sentence in the span is the worst?** Chapter 7: "Every
answer matches what she wrote the first time, so she puts her hands in her
lap and waits for the room to catch up around her, relieved to find it
still works like it worked in first grade." It names a banned state, tells
the reader what the already-shown gesture means, and restages a beat the
book had already used four times — three violations in one sentence.

**Is Chloe a person or a device?** Mostly a person. The grounding details
carry it: holding a sleeve a second too long before a door she isn't sure
she's allowed through, keeping a hair tie for a roommate crying over a
missing one, pressing a thumbnail into a seatbelt rather than asking a
question she's afraid of the answer to, and the floor scene in chapter 9,
which is a child's bargaining, not a philosopher's. But there are stretches
— the fractions-on-a-napkin recitation in chapter 6, the tidal-locking
debate in chapter 5, both built to demonstrate the premise rather than to
happen because the scene needed them — where she is running a demo of "160
IQ" for the reader's benefit rather than living a scene, and in those
stretches she is closer to a device. The therapy and begging chapters pull
her back into a person every time, which is the right rescue and the
reason the book as a whole reads as more than its premise.
