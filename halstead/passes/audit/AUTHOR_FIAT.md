# Author fiat audit

Read-only. Nothing changed. Every fix below is a suggestion for the author.

The test applied throughout: not *is this implausible*, but **does the book pay for
what it spends**. A thing is fiat when it happens because the plot needed it and
the text has not earned it. Verdicts are one of four:

- **earned** — the page does the work, and a careful reader can find it
- **cheap but survivable** — the page half-does the work; a reader will shrug
- **held card** — deliberately unexplained, marked as such, answer owed later
- **fiat** — the plot needed it and nobody paid

**Headline: nothing in the eight items is fiat.** One clause in chapter 20 is a
genuine defect and it is the source of the biggest complaint in both reviews.
Two of the eight are misreadings of the text. The most exposed item in the book
is not on the reviewers' list at all; it is Chloe's clearance.

Ranked by how much a reader would notice.

---

## 1. The footage erasure — chapters 16, 20, 34

**Verdict: held card, with one cheap clause inside it. Not fiat.**
Both reviewers call this the clearest fiat in the book. Both audited chapter 20
against chapter 34 and **missed chapter 16**, which is where the device is
actually specified. With chapter 16 in hand most of the objection dissolves.

### What the box is actually described as doing

Ruth builds it in April of her thirteenth year, in a scene ostensibly about
getting out of Saturday tomato-counting (16):

> "It goes looking for recorders, anything on any network in range that's
> recording video, and about half are still on the password they shipped with,
> **so it tries those first**."

> "Then it does everything else at once, cracking, sniffing, brute force, and
> others besides. It runs the lot together and takes whichever arrives first, so
> it's **eight ugly things in a box** rather than one clever thing, and one of
> them is always working."

> "Then it **writes noise into the recording** every so often, over part of the
> file. Delete a file and somebody notices a file is missing. Make it noisy for
> a bit and that's a camera being a camera."

Three things follow that the reviewers did not have:

1. **Default passwords are the first of eight attacks, not the whole device.**
2. **The output is corruption, not deletion.** Which is, word for word, chapter
   34's finding: *"unrecoverable wherever the students appear, and intact
   everywhere else."* The method was specified eighteen chapters before the
   consequence. That is the book's best piece of long-range payment.
3. **"No known method" is now defensible.** Eight different attack paths across
   a mixed population of recorders leaves no single signature to name. A
   forensic team looking for one method would not find one. The federal file is
   not being stupid; it is being precise.

### Range, which is the part that is actually unpaid

The box is portable and it is running the whole night — chapter 20 has Ruth
carrying a bag over the fence and *"Ruth shuts the box off in the corridor"* on
return. So it is not a school-proximity device; it walks the route. Two hours
sitting in a booth is more than enough dwell time for the Waffle House and the
two units either side, all of which would be small-business DVRs on wifi that
bleeds through a party wall. Those three are covered by what is on the page.

**The traffic camera and the municipal camera are not.** The students pass them
on foot, twice, so the box was physically near them — but a pole-mounted
municipal camera is normally on cellular or fibre backhaul, not a wifi network a
box walking past can join. "In range" is doing unshown work here. This is the
one genuine gap, and it is one camera type, not the whole paragraph.

### The observation unit's own recordings

The largest sub-claim — a federal surveillance team's own cameras eaten by a
child's box — is the one the book has **already paid for and not cashed**.
Chapter 15 establishes, in dialogue, that this exact class of federal team runs
consumer equipment on factory defaults, and the author has ruled that canon
(deniability). Ruth's box tries factory defaults first. The two facts sit nineteen
chapters apart and nobody on the page has ever put them together. One line does
it, and it strengthens both chapters at once.

### Why this reads as a held card and not a cheat

- Ruth does not understand it either, and says so on the page: *"they had a team
  on us and my box got their cameras too and i didnt know their cameras
  existed."*
- The chapter's emotional payload **is** the inexplicability. Kavi's *"read that
  back"*, Nadia's *"and four years later the federal government wrote 'no known
  method'"*, Eli's *"i have never once been at the top of a room in my life."*
  If the box were explained, chapter 34 would have no engine.
- The closing image is explicit that the debt is outstanding: *"still on that
  page, exactly as unexplained now as it was the first time she read it."*
- `BETA_NOTES.md` §6.3 confirms the book is a middle, with open threads listed.

### The actual defect: chapter 20's summary clause

> Ruth brings the box she built at thirteen **for finding video recorders on the
> networks around it and logging in with the passwords they shipped with**

This is a reductive restatement of the device chapter 16 built. It deletes seven
of the eight attacks and the entire noise-writing mechanism. A reader arriving at
chapter 34 with only chapter 20 in mind is looking at a toy that logs into
DVRs with `admin/admin` — and correctly concludes the ending is a cheat. Both
reviewers did exactly this. **The clause is doing active damage to a payoff the
book already bought.**

### Smallest fixes

- **Required.** Rewrite the chapter 20 clause so it matches chapter 16 — one
  extra sub-clause is enough: *"…the box she built at thirteen, eight ways into
  anything on a network that records video and noise written over whatever it
  finds."* Costs six words, recovers the whole payoff.
- **Recommended, one line.** Somewhere in the chapter 34 chat, let Kavi connect
  the observation team's gear to the drill team's gear. *"kavi: they run consumer
  kit so nothing traces back. thats why your box ate them."* This converts the
  hardest sub-claim into a consequence of a rule the book already set.
- **Optional, one clause.** The municipal camera is the only remaining stretch.
  Either drop it from the list in chapter 34, or have Ruth flag it herself as the
  part she cannot account for — which costs nothing and sharpens the mystery:
  *"ruth: the diner i understand. the pole camera up the road i do not."*

## 2. Chloe's clearance — chapters 33, 34, 36 *(not on the reviewers' list)*

**Verdict: cheap but survivable. The most exposed unpaid item in the book.**

Chloe holds a federal security clearance obtained across ten weeks of
investigation dramatised in full (ch 30). She then:

- co-authors the operational rules for a §1030 intrusion into a classified unit
- reads the stolen file **at her federal desk**, *"five months into a job whose
  clearance she is stretching a long way past its purpose to do this"*, with the
  badge still clipped to her bag
- runs a search at work in chapter 36 *"with an entirely different name in the
  box"*, cross-referenced against a message from Theo

Three separate acts, all on monitored government systems, all with zero friction
and zero detection.

**Why it stands out specifically in this book:** everyone else pays. Sam's range
score generates four documents that travel upward. Nadia's confrontation costs
her a licence plate read aloud twice and a till counted three times. Eli's
five-vulnerability afternoon gets him a meeting with a lawyer. Theo's nine days
of refusal are dramatised. Chloe's exposure is the largest of any of them and
is the only one that generates nothing.

The text notices, but notices as characterisation rather than payment: *"What
that actually costs her she works through exactly once, on the drive home from
the office… and the thought ends in about as long as it takes a light to
change."* That tells us she is not thinking about it. It does not tell us the
system is not thinking about it.

**Smallest fix — one sentence in ch 36.** Give her an audit-log awareness and a
cover for the query, or let her know there isn't one. Either direction pays:

> *The search is one of eleven she runs that morning, and the only one she could
> not defend to anybody who pulled the log.*

That single line converts the chapter's last movement from oversight into
suspense, and costs nothing structurally.

## 3. Theo receiving the file — chapter 29

**Verdict: held card, probably. Cheap but survivable if it is not.**

The reviewers assess this as a plausible-but-convenient coincidence and grade the
mitigation as adequate-ish. The text does more than they credit, and there is a
plant they missed.

**What the text does earn:**

- Theo's job is established as reading and summarising, with a specific
  competence (paired Pashto/Dari) and a specific track record.
- The box is a routine artefact of a 26-year retirement, handed over with a
  reason that is not about him: *"Somebody has to read it eventually. Might as
  well be the new guy."*
- The Halstead folder is buried: ninety minutes of embassy postings, shipping
  manifests and a dead currency dispute first, and the folder is *near the
  bottom*.
- It takes him **a day and a half** to work out what he is holding.

**What is genuinely unpaid on its face** — and this is the more interesting
problem than the coincidence: the file the box contains tracks all ninety-one
graduates by *"Address, employer, family, where their parents work, where
everybody banks"*, and Chloe's entry specifically records *"that she has recently
accepted employment requiring a background investigation."* So the unit tracks
its subjects into federal employment. Theo is one of the ninety-one, cleared and
sitting inside the agency that holds the file, and he is handed the box. That is
not a coincidence problem; it is a counterintelligence problem, and nobody in the
book notices it.

**Except that the book may already have answered it.** Chapter 34:

> Theo's has a note on it that makes him close the laptop with both hands, the
> careful two-handed close of something that might spill, and go outside for
> twenty minutes. **That's the whole account anyone else gets of it.**

An unspecified note on Theo's own file, withheld from the reader, placed in the
chapter that reveals the unit tracks employment. If that note is what it looks
like — that his employment was known, or permitted, or arranged — then the
retirement box is not a coincidence at all, it is the setup, and item 4 is the
strongest held card in the book rather than its weakest coincidence.

**Recommendation:** the author should decide now whether that is what the note
says. If yes, nothing needs changing and this item comes off the list entirely.
If no, then one line somewhere in ch 29 or 34 should acknowledge that a graduate
of the school works in the building — from Kavi, who annotates everything, or
from Chloe, who reads the file as a translation problem. The gap is small; the
silence around it is what draws the eye.

## 4. Seven students find the founder in sixteen weeks — chapter 32

**Verdict: earned, one line short of airtight.**

The mitigation the reviewer describes is real, visible, and better than they say.
The chapter pays in five separate currencies:

- **Named mechanism, two parts.** Eli's worm designed to be *boring* — "sitting
  in the places where a badly maintained reconciliation job would sit" — and
  Kavi's watcher whose only job is to notice when the worm stops looking bored.
- **Visible failure and iteration.** *"every version he throws at his own test
  environment behaves like exactly what it is, a thing that goes looking… The
  polling interval gets rewritten again and again before it finally reads as
  tired instead of curious."* Committed at 3:14 a.m.
- **A month of rehearsal on a live system they own** (Nadia's books) before it
  goes anywhere, with one correction in the month: a timezone.
- **The grind, not a montage.** Kavi's whiteboard rewritten on both sides, index
  cards, Ruth on the floor for a night walking a paper strip on her hands and
  knees, posting *"the version she has already checked and rechecked, not the
  version she found first."*
- **The argument is made, not asserted.** *"the government is arguing from
  absence… im arguing from presence."* Then three concrete tells: three-to-five
  day gaps with no operational cause (*"committees dont get flu"*), a seven-hour
  decision band drifting forty minutes across two decades (*"thats a person
  getting older"*), and an instrument preference held since 2003.

That drift detail is the single best-argued thing in the thriller half of the
book. It is a claim only a private party holding the full time series could make,
which is precisely why nineteen years of federal work did not make it.

**The one line missing:** the book never says why the government could not do
this. The answer is on the page in the next chapter — Ruth quotes §1030
sentencing at Eli — but nobody connects it. Eli succeeded because he committed a
felony against private financial systems, and the government cannot.

**Smallest fix, one chat line in ch 32,** after Sam's *"twenty years and were the
first people to look? that cant be right"*:

> **kavi:** theyre not the first to look. theyre the first who could do it
> illegally.

That closes the last gap in the item and it costs one line.

## 5. Seventy-five men for one person — chapter 36

**Verdict: earned. The number is paid for; the aftermath is not.**

The accounting works, and it works because the text does five things:

1. **Priya raises the absurdity herself** and refuses to accept the flattering
   reading: *"either they are lying about who they work for, or somebody has
   spent a lot of money on people who are not very good at it."*
2. **Capture, not kill, is demonstrated rather than asserted** — guns held from
   the start and unused until she was through the line, then darts, then
   *"small dose. it is for slowing you down rather than putting you out."*
3. **The escalation is a staircase with a stated basis**: two in March, six in
   May, about a dozen in July, seventy-five on the Tuesday. Ruth supplies the
   sizing logic and then corrects it: *"three vans that came back empty gets you
   thirty" / "it gets you thirty if you think you are collecting a person."*
4. **The quality is downgraded, in her calibration, not the narrator's**: *"they
   came in like thirteen year olds. a couple of fourteens. one of them was a
   fifteen."*
5. **Ruth prices the geometry privately** — seventy-five across a field is a
   hundred and ten metres, a distance she has walked a thousand times — and
   keeps it to herself, which is the book's house style working correctly.

The one obvious counter-question the reader will raise (why not dart her from
range at the start?) is answered in dialogue: *"you do not put those in a
person, because a person watches you raise it and steps left."*

**What is unpaid is the aftermath, not the number.** Seventy-five men, three
vans and two disabled engine blocks in a foreign country produce no local police,
no diplomatic noise, no second visit, and no attempt to retrieve the two men
Priya is holding. She keeps two American federal prisoners in a room over a feed
merchant's and the only consequence in the text is that they eat more than she
budgeted for. That is a lovely beat and it is also the whole account.

**Smallest fix:** one line, from Priya, noticing the silence. *"priya: nobody has
come for these two. that is the part i keep coming back to."* It converts an
absence into an observation, which is the technique the book already uses
everywhere else.

## 6. The eight-foot chain link fence — chapter 20

**Verdict: earned. The reviewer misread the sentence.**

The text does not say the fence is eight feet tall. It says:

> There is chain link **eight feet behind them** and a loading dock on the other
> side of it

"Eight feet behind them" is a **distance**, not a height. The fence height is
never stated anywhere in the manuscript — I grepped every fence reference in all
thirty-six chapters. There is nothing to lower.

The vault is separately paid for in the same paragraph, and paid well:

> The fence drill has been run at this speed every term since they were nine

That is the book's standard move — name the drill that makes the feat ordinary —
and it does the whole job.

**But the note is still worth acting on**, because a professional reader parsed
that sentence as a height and built a fiat complaint on it. If one careful reader
misparsed it, others will. The problem is prose, not plausibility: "eight feet
behind them" sits immediately before a sentence about people going over the
thing, and the two readings collide.

**Smallest fix:** disambiguate without adding a height. *"There is chain link a
few strides behind them"*, or *"There is chain link three metres behind them."*
Either kills the misreading and neither commits to a height the book has been
wise not to specify.

## 7. The admission rate — chapter 21

**Verdict: earned. The reviewer's headline number is not the claim.**

*"Ninety-one out of ninety-one bought something"* is not a 100% admission rate.
The chapter establishes the application pattern in its second paragraph: the
eight Ivies bought like lottery tickets, then a parent's college, **then the
community college an hour from home, quietly listed by everybody**, then
something strange. Every one of the ninety-one has a guaranteed admit on the
list. Ninety-one of ninety-one placed *somewhere* is arithmetically
unremarkable, and Chloe's store-sign line is about the sign, not the rate.

The real load is the hit rate at the top: Chloe 12 of 14, Sam 11 of 12, Kavi 3
of 3, Ruth and Odile everywhere. And the chapter pays for that at length, from
the other side of the desk, which is the correct place to pay:

- Eight offices write back asking for the *real* transcript.
- The ghostwriting theory is raised, and killed by the standard method: *"they
  lay them side by side… no two of them argue alike, and a pair of the ninety-one
  take opposite positions on a question with both worth reading."*
- The Penn officer reads a sentence three times and picks up the phone.
- Thirty-plus institutions open files by January.
- The paper-in-a-room offer, and the Caltech representative's entire day — eight
  to half past three, five sheets both sides, lunch gone cold, *"Is the third
  author available to talk?"*
- The campus visits, with three separate reps failing to get past the same wall:
  Sport, Athletics, Recreation.

Roughly sixty percent of the chapter is admissions-side conviction, dramatised
rather than narrated. This is payment, and it is generous.

**One thing the author should note.** The "broken selection process" argument the
reviewer wants to check *is* on the page — but it is delivered by children, and
it is a **rationalisation**, not the book's thesis. Per `CALIBRATION_AUDIT.md` §2,
these characters must explain away contrary evidence, and Chloe's store-sign
analogy is exactly that: the outside case was defective. If the author intends
readers to take it as the book's own argument, it is under-supported; if he
intends it as the cohort being wrong in a characteristic way, it is perfectly
calibrated and should not be strengthened. **I read it as the second.** Nothing
to change; worth being deliberate about.

Two minor residuals, both cosmetic: no standardised testing appears anywhere in
the application material, and the chapter shows offices *convinced* but never
shows one *deciding*. Neither would trouble a reader.

## 8. The default pairing code on federal radios — chapter 15

**Verdict: earned. The break is a gift, and the gift is deliberately worthless.**

Taking the author's ruling as given (federal investigators running black
operations on civilian equipment for deniability), the question is whether the
speed and ease read as a present to Kavi, and whether any cost or risk shows.

The text handles this better than the review credits, in four moves:

1. **The characters diagnose the amateurism themselves and treat it as
   evidence.** Ruth: *"They're almost certainly actors, look at this. This is a
   consumer handset with a default code. My dad's work has better than this and
   my dad sells insurance."* The equipment quality is not a convenience the
   reader is asked to swallow; it is a clue the characters are actively reading.
2. **The payload is a dud.** *"The problem is that they barely talk. Two
   transmissions in six minutes, both of them position checks… One of them said
   he's at the corner and one of them said copy. That's it."* Kavi wins
   instantly and gets nothing. The plan still has to be built from scratch, and
   Chloe still has to walk into the corridor as bait. **This is the payment**,
   and it is the technique the author should be conscious of reusing.
3. **The marketing claim is named as a marketing claim.** *"Because the box says
   AES-256. The box always says AES-256."* That is a correct real-world
   observation about a real class of consumer radio, and it removes the
   cryptographic objection entirely.
4. **The wrongness is planted as a held card.** Chloe: *"All of it wrong in the
   same direction, which is the direction a school would get it wrong in if a
   school were building this, **and also the direction four real people would
   get it wrong in if somebody had handed them the wrong list**."* The book has
   already told the reader the second reading is available, fourteen chapters
   before chapter 29 confirms it. The default pairing code is not a hole in the
   fiction; it is the fiction's own foreshadowing.

**Residual, small.** The break itself carries no risk and no resource cost worth
naming — the campus cluster does it and nobody is billed. If the author wants one
more increment, the cheapest is on the grade sheet, which already itemises four
failures. A fifth line noting campus compute used for a four-second problem
would cost eight words and be entirely in the register of that document. Optional;
the item is fine as it stands.

**Loose thread noticed while reading, worth a look.** Ruth loses the argument
about the sedative — *"If they get a work physical in three weeks they're going
to fail it and they're going to have to explain why"* — and they use the
traceable agent, and the grade sheet dings them for it. So four federal operators
went home with a traceable school-issued drug in their blood, and the government
never picks that thread up in chapter 29 or 34. It is exactly the kind of thread
this book normally pulls. Either it is a held card, or it is the one detail in
chapter 15 that goes nowhere.

## 9. Marek failing with no visible consequence — chapter 16

**Verdict: held card / deliberate withholding. Agreed with the second reviewer.**

This is not fiat, and the distinguishing sentence is right there:

> What it does cost, whether it follows him onto anything that matters, whether
> anybody sat him down about it, **stays outside her reach** for years, and she
> is careful for a long time about how she asks.

Three things make this a withholding rather than a gap:

- It **names the gap** rather than papering over it.
- It **attributes the not-knowing to a character's position** — Chloe is a
  thirteen-year-old teacher with no standing to ask, and the text says so.
- It **shows the cost persisting**: "careful for a long time about how she asks"
  is a decade of a person carrying something.

The whole Marek sequence is also the book's best small argument, delivered by the
person on the losing side of it. *"A receipt is a different object from work"* is
correct, and Chloe's grade suffers for his being correct, and she writes the
failure in herself. The unfairness is the point and it is fully paid.

**One caution.** The debt is only a held card if it is eventually paid. As of
this volume it is not, and `BETA_NOTES.md` already flags the book as a middle. If
there is no second volume, this becomes a dropped thread rather than a withheld
one. Worth noting on whatever list tracks the outstanding promises.

# Sweep: other unpaid spends, chapters 29–36

Beyond item 2 (Chloe's clearance), which belongs in the ranked list above:

### The nine minutes — chapter 35
**Held card, correctly marked.** Someone stops Eli's tool, restarts it byte-for-byte,
and cleans *three mutually blind logs* including a watcher purpose-built to
distrust clean logs. This is a larger capability claim than anything in the
footage complex, and the chapter is entirely constructed around its
impossibility. It is paid in the only currency available: the characters treat it
as impossible, and their conclusion is *"hes very slightly wrong about how good
we are."* The one thing that could have been cheap — Eli not invoking the stop
clause — is armoured in advance by having Eli himself argue two of that clause's
sub-sections into their final shape. Nothing to fix.

### Three "no forensic trace against federal systems" events
The box (ch 20/34), the ch 33 exit tool that *"erases the traces of the entry as
it happens"*, and the nine minutes are the same capability class appearing three
times, and only the third — the one done *to* them — is presented as
astonishing. Not a fiat finding; a texture note. The author may want to vary how
the first two are described so the third lands as the escalation it is.

### The founder's two portraits
He is caught by a personal preference held for twenty-three years and a circadian
band, and he is also good enough to be invisible inside Kavi's watcher. These are
reconcilable — a preference is only a tell to someone holding the whole time
series, which required a felony nobody else committed — but the book never says
so. If the tension is intended, it is the best held card in the book. Worth the
author confirming to himself which it is.

### The social leak nobody addresses
Nineteen years, ninety-one graduates with families, employers and banks, and the
government's file still lists *method unknown*. The chat's technical security is
argued for at length (ch 16's traffic-shaping, ch 24's five untested years). The
*human* channel — ninety-one people, none of whom has ever said anything to
anyone — is never addressed. This is adjacent to the reviewer's metadata note and
is the larger of the two holes. Costs one line if the author wants it closed.

### The drill/raid boundary, chapter 15 → 29
Twelve-year-olds sedate and hogtie four federal operators; an adult tells them
*"an attack on a school in the United States would not be accepted, and that they
were free to leave"*; and *"There is no reply on file."* Both sides then behave
as though nothing happened. Marked as a held card by the three blacked-out lines
Theo cannot read — *"more than he's seen redacted from anything else in the box."*
The redaction is doing exactly the job it should. Nothing to fix.

### Nadia and the men above the tire shop — chapter 27
Checked because it looks like it should be fiat and isn't. Four adult criminals
let an eighteen-year-old walk out after she names them. Paid: the leverage is
argued out loud and is genuinely the better deal for them (a Secretary of State
complaint kills Hanley and takes twenty-six other clients' paperwork with it);
the intimidation is real and costs her (the latch, the plate read aloud twice,
the till three dollars under and counted three times, working the counter for the
first time since November); and the consequence is confirmed off-screen months
later — *"Hanley's filings all go dead in July… which she spots in August and
keeps to herself."* **Earned, and one of the best-constructed sequences in the
book.**

# The reverse: where the book pays, and how

Both reviewers note the book is unusually disciplined about this. It is, and the
discipline is not luck — there are about ten repeatable techniques in play.
Listed so they can be reused deliberately.

**1. Give the win a worthless payload.** Kavi breaks the comms in four seconds
and learns *"one of them said he's at the corner and one of them said copy."*
The gift is granted and immediately made useless, so the scene's problem is still
a problem. This is the single most transferable move in the manuscript.

**2. Spec the capability years before you spend it, in a scene about something
else.** Ruth's box gets its full specification in chapter 16, inside a scene
whose stated business is avoiding a Saturday of counting tomatoes. Three chapters
of thriller in the thirties draw on it. **This is the technique the chapter 20
summary clause accidentally undoes** — see item 1.

**3. Show the drafts that failed.** Eli's polling interval *"gets rewritten again
and again before it finally reads as tired instead of curious."* Chloe's essay
cut three times. Nadia's hundred and ninety applications against twelve replies,
eight of them machine-written. Ruth posting *"the version she has already checked
and rechecked, not the version she found first."* Nothing in the book arrives
first try except things that are supposed to be alarming.

**4. Let the institution generate paperwork.** Sam's range score produces four
documents, itemised, each with a named purpose, and the memo lands three floors
up in front of a woman who pulls out a folder older than the captain's
commission. Ch 26's safety incident goes onto a form with a statement stapled
behind it and a medic's card behind that. Bureaucratic consequence is the
cheapest realism available and this book buys a lot of it.

**5. Put the objection in the mouth of the character who benefits.** Priya on the
seventy-five. Sam's *"Why was it nothing?"* in the parking lot — an entire page
of the book spent arguing down its own action scene. Nobody has to defend the
book against a reader's objection because a character has already made it,
harder.

**6. Argue from presence, not absence.** Ruth's three tells — the flu gaps, the
forty-minute drift, the worse instrument since 2003 — and she names the
methodology while she does it: *"the government is arguing from absence… im
arguing from presence."* The reader is handed the epistemology along with the
conclusion.

**7. Prove it from the other side of the desk.** All of chapter 21's admissions
work. The Caltech representative's day, minute by minute. The Penn officer
reading a sentence three times before she picks up the phone. Duke's escort
answering *Sport, Athletics, Recreation* and the rep writing all three down, in
order. The school is never described as impressive; people are shown being
unable to explain it.

**8. Name the drill that makes the feat ordinary.** *"The fence drill has been
run at this speed every term since they were nine."* One clause, and a vault over
a fence stops being athleticism and becomes homework.

**9. Price the rule out loud before anyone breaks it.** Ruth quoting §1030 at Eli
— *"five years first offense, ten if theres a financial angle attached"* — and
Eli's *"then i go away for a very long time, so im not going to be wrong."* The
stakes are numeric and stated by the person who least wants to do it.

**10. Let the character be wrong about why.** Ruth's remedial-track year. Sam's
*"This is a screen."* Chloe's store sign. In every case the reader gets the true
explanation for free by watching a very intelligent person build a wrong one, and
the book never has to state the true one. This is the calibration audit's §2
requirement and the book's best-executed constraint.
