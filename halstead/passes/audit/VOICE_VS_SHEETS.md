# Voice vs. sheets

Read-only audit. Method: read `_SHEET_RULES.md`, `_TEMPLATE.md`, `_CALIBRATION.md`,
`_DIFFERENTIATION.md`, `_ALLOCATIONS.md`, `CALIBRATION_AUDIT.md`, then all ten
cohort sheets, BEX, IYAD, MAREK, and the nine adult sheets named in the brief.
Then grepped every chapter for `<Name> says` / `<Name> said` (the manuscript's
near-universal tag), read the surrounding scene for anything that turned up,
and read several full chapters (01, 04, 13, 16, 21, 22, 24, 29, 30, 31, 33) end
to end where the tags clustered. This catches the large majority of tagged
lines but not every untagged line in a fast exchange, so treat the negative
findings (silence, thin sampling) as "not found in this pass," not as proof of
absence.

Chat transcripts (ch. 24 and the chat-run chapters) are judged as their own
register, per the brief, not against the speech dials.

---

## CHLOE

**VERDICT: MATCHES, with one signature collision.**

The register word is "unmoderated" and the signature is the concrete parallel
scenario. Both are on the page correctly and often. The exit-interview scene
(ch. 22) is the best single piece of evidence in the book for her sheet:

> "If you sat one exam every year, in a single room, against the same ninety
> people, and you kept coming out near the top of it, would you ever actually
> find out if you were good at the exam, or just good against that particular
> room? ... I don't know what's outside this building, and everyone I've ever
> been measured against my whole life is inside it. Staying keeps the question
> open. It just makes the room permanent."

That is the sheet's defining move — reframe the abstract claim as a physical/
structural scenario and argue the scenario — executed exactly, at the exact
age (twenty) where the sheet says her rhetorical polish should have caught up
to her perception. It also passes calibration cleanly: she never claims to be
unusual, she treats the exam-question as a real open question about herself,
not a boast.

Chapter 1 at age six is equally good: short, concrete, reasoning-in-public
("The rule is about at a time, though. It's fine. It is still four at a time,
just twice over.") without a hint of the "small professor" or the alien/AI
readings the sheet warns against. She still says "I don't know" under
pressure, still gets in trouble, still can't connect her sadness to its cause.
This is the calibration working as intended — flag it as a positive, per the
brief's request for the opposite case.

**The collision.** Ruth's signature — "drops the name of the person she is
correcting into the middle of the correction" — is described in
`_DIFFERENTIATION.md` as exclusive: *"if a line could carry another
character's signature, it is the wrong line."* Chloe uses it twice in one
scene, teaching Marek (ch. 16, `chapters/16_thirteen.md`):

> "Because that's what the hour is for, Marek, and I need something with your
> name on it at the end of the year, like everyone else."
>
> "I can't take this in pencil on the back of Ivy's Tuesday, Marek, I need
> your name on it."

Chloe's own signature — the physical parallel scenario — is entirely absent
from this scene; a correction-with-name-inside-it is Ruth's construction, not
hers, and doing it twice in the same exchange makes it read as a habit rather
than an accident. Fix: give Chloe her own move here (a parallel about paper
records vs. actual finished work would fit her better than it fits anyone) or
drop the vocative.

**Distinctiveness:** high. Five unattributed lines from her would be
identifiable more often than not, because the parallel-scenario construction
is rare enough in the cast that it functions like a fingerprint. The Marek
scene is the one place she would be mistaken for Ruth.

## SAM

**VERDICT: DRIFTED, once, in the one place that matters most for him.**

Register word "unbothered." The sheet's single hardest rule for him is under
Axis 3 and repeated on his own sheet's "Would never" list: *"Hedge about a
fact, a plan, or himself."* Across the speech-tagged corpus he holds this
rule almost everywhere — "Sixty-two and a half. That beats guessing,"
"Noted. It's still the plan," "Five weeks. ok" — number first, no qualifier,
subject closed. The board-fact line in ch. 21 ("Fighting. I'm first in my
year, out of everyone, not just close to first... There's a board, right
outside the gym, and anyone can walk by and check it.") is a good example of
calibration working: he states a real, checkable, publicly-posted fact with
zero self-congratulation, which is exactly what the audit asks for — flag it
as a positive.

The violation is in the chat (ch. 24), which the brief says to judge as its
own register, but even by that lower bar this is a plain contradiction of the
one rule his sheet treats as absolute:

> sam: i dont think its measuring anything
> sam: i think its meant to be fun

Two hedges in three lines, both about a claim rather than himself, but still
squarely the construction "no hedging, ever" was written to rule out. This is
the character's most load-bearing negative rule and it breaks on the page.

**Distinctiveness:** high everywhere else. The number-first habit and the
deflating-literalism jokes ("A lid, on a whole entire pool?" ... "the pool
that offered nothing," etc.) are unmistakable. This is one of the two or
three most reliably identifiable voices in the cast.

## RUTH

**VERDICT: MATCHES, with one dated defect and one overuse risk.**

Register word "loud," signature the name-drop-mid-correction. Both are
strong and frequent: "sixteen doesnt even compare to what everyone actually
runs, chloe," "some of us dont get to pick our own calendar right now, eli,"
"That's not the same as accusing you of anything, Dad" (paraphrased — the
actual line is "That's not what the policy says, Dad. It says you need one
within thirty days...", ch. 31), "That's not wrong because the idea's bad,
Devansh. It's wrong because you dropped a negative on line six" (ch. 31).
That last chapter — her own — is the strongest evidence in the book that this
character survives growing up: the nine-year-old who says "I told you, I said
that on Friday" (ch. 4) and the twenty-year-old rebuilding a proof for a
professor are recognizably one person, and her arc there (privately holding a
belief for months, then going and getting the evidence herself, then telling
the group in short flat chat lines rather than a speech) is calibration and
character sheet working in lockstep — the best single chapter for any
character in this audit.

**The defect:** `_DIFFERENTIATION.md` states plainly that Ruth "currently
hedges and should not." That defect is still in the text. Chapter 10
(`chapters/10_april.md`), age roughly nine:

> "He probably forgot, because he's been telling everyone for a week, so it
> stopped feeling like news to him before it ever actually happened," Ruth
> says.

"Probably" is a hedge in front of a claim, which her own sheet's "Would
never" list rules out categorically ("Hedge a claim she has finished
testing"). It is early in the book and isolated in this pass's sample, but it
is exactly the artifact the differentiation doc already flagged and it has
not been fixed.

**The overuse risk:** in ch. 33, four consecutive Ruth lines in one exchange
each end by naming Eli — "thats not the same, eli" / "section 1030, eli" /
"and if anything we pull touches something classified thats a different
statute and it doesnt start at five, eli." The signature note warns that
three or four uses across the *whole book* is enough before it reads as a
catchphrase; four uses in one exchange is close to that line already.

**Distinctiveness:** very high. Volume, the name-drop, and the refuse-until-
the-measure-is-named move are all distinct and frequent enough to identify
her from a single unattributed line most of the time.

## KAVI

**VERDICT: DRIFTED. His signature scene borrows someone else's move.**

Register word "level," signature "corrects without naming anyone... does not
register that the mechanism belonged to somebody." Most of his tagged lines
hold this exactly: "The worst. You do this every single time" (to Sam, no
name used in the correction), "Because the box says AES-256. The box always
says AES-256," the long unbroken technical run in ch. 33 about the
government's own logging. Good, level, mechanism-only.

Chapter 10 breaks it twice in one speech:

> "You already have all of it, Chloe, because his mom came and that was the
> whole of it. You keep asking about this, and you asked Ruth in April, at
> the trays, while I was standing right there," Kavi says.

This is Ruth's exclusive construction (the addressee's name dropped into the
middle of the correcting sentence), and it is the specific thing Kavi's own
sheet rules out by name: *"He corrects without naming anyone... An
unaddressed that-is-wrong-because-X is his move, not hers."* Two pages
earlier in the same chapter he also says, defending himself: "I'm allowed to
think that's worse without both of you looking at me like that" — a line
that registers and reacts to social judgment in the room, which is the
opposite of "does not track the conversational layer running beside it."
Both lines are in the same short scene, so this reads as a real, localized
drift rather than scattered noise.

**Distinctiveness:** good when the sheet is followed (the AES-256 exchange,
the long technical run in ch. 33 are unmistakably his), compromised in the
one scene sampled where he sounds like Ruth.

## NADIA

**VERDICT: MATCHES. The strongest execution in the cast.**

Register "flat," one delivery for everything. The bank scene (ch. 24) is a
model instance of the sheet's "laying out numbers for someone whose decision
they will change" condition and should be pointed to as the standard other
scenes are measured against:

> "Five days is the policy on a new business account. It's the same for
> everybody who walks in here and it isn't mine to move."
> ... "It's the policy on a new account with no history. This one has three
> months on it, steady deposits and zero returns. Two days on nine named
> payers. I'll write them down for you."

No figurative language, no hedge, short independent clauses, a want stated as
a fact, negotiation scaled exactly to what's needed. The chat scenes hold the
same register under a completely different subject (hiring, her failing
company) without ever widening it — "its tedious, not brutal. brutal implies
effort," "thats the number, eli. i can read it." The sheet's ban on
figurative language holds throughout every sample pulled.

**Distinctiveness:** very high. No hedge, no image, near-imperative sentence
shape, and the flat literalism of her humor are all present and none of them
overlap with anyone else's assigned territory.

## PRIYA

**VERDICT: MATCHES.**

Register "run-on." Camp-era and later dialogue both hold the tumbling,
un-hedged, animal-adjacent, staying-power register: the pony monologue in
ch. 4 ("Okay, so the first thing is a pony isn't a baby horse... A pony is a
separate thing, anything under fourteen two."), and the plate-scoring
argument in ch. 15 ("Do it how? Somebody has to know which plate is which or
you can't score it, and whoever knows is the person who wrecks it for
everybody... so Sam has to be in the room and not knowing"). The ethics
objection in ch. 16 ("Are we allowed to be holding this at all, though? ...
and nobody writes anything down") is exactly the "can take a plan apart under
scrutiny for forty minutes" staying-power the sheet asks for, not the
scatterbrained reading the sheet explicitly warns against.

**Distinctiveness:** very high. She is the only character who runs a turn
past its first complete thought as a matter of course, and the tag could be
removed from most of her lines without loss.

## ODILE

**VERDICT: DRIFTED, once, badly.**

Register "bare" — a number and nothing else, or a short observational
fragment. Most of the sample holds this: "The far end is colder," "Sixty,"
and goes back out," "Which of us calls it?" all match the sheet exactly.

Chapter 21 does not:

> "A limit like that is a minimum," Odile says, without coming off the bed.
> "Every teacher in this building takes more than they asked for and thanks
> you for it. The question wanted what it wanted. If they meant to be handed
> less than that they should have asked something smaller."

This is a four-clause, fully reasoned argument with a built premise and a
conclusion — the shape belongs to Ruth or Kavi, not to a character whose
sheet says "No figurative language, ever," "She never explains her own
motives or feelings in words," and whose signature is *the absence* of a
sentence around a number. It is her single longest quoted line in the whole
sample and it is the one place she sounds like someone else. Fix: cut it to
the observation ("A limit like that is a minimum") and let the room supply
the reasoning, or hand the longer version to Ruth or Kavi, who are also in
that scene.

**Distinctiveness:** good elsewhere, undermined by the one long line, which
happens to be the line a reader is most likely to remember from her.

## ELI

**VERDICT: MATCHES where it appears; the book is wrong by omission.**

Register "straight-faced," and where Eli actually speaks — almost entirely in
chat, ch. 24 and ch. 33 — the fit is excellent and in places reads like the
sheet was written from these very lines: "so thats nobody" (the plain
unwelcome count, said the moment the room has decided to be accommodating),
"im not being harsh about it. its just the actual count," "everyone speeds...
its exactly the same. its a rule with a known enforcement mechanism and you
learn where the cameras are" (reframing a real risk as a mechanism), "youre
one bad month off the plumbing supply on route 8" (the one permitted hazard
image, aimed at someone else's exposure, never his own). This is some of the
most sheet-accurate writing in the whole manuscript.

The problem is coverage, not voice. Per his own sheet's continuity note, Eli
appears in chapters 16, 23, 24, 27, 32, 33, 34, 35 — eight chapters across the
back half of the book — and in every one of them apart from the chat
exchanges he is narrated *about* rather than quoted. Chapters 32, 34, and 35
(the ones about the tool he builds) contain no directly quoted speech from
him at all outside two short chat lines. A character whose sheet spends two
pages on his exact joke mechanics is being tested almost nowhere in
dramatized prose dialogue. This is a gap in the draft, not a contradiction of
the sheet — but it is the reason the final distinctiveness question below
comes out as low as it does for him.

**Distinctiveness:** high in chat, effectively untestable in prose dialogue
because there is almost none of it to test.

## THEO

**VERDICT: MATCHES on everything except the one rule the reference docs
already flagged as unfixed.**

Register "unhedged" (he is supposed to be the cast's *only* hedger — Axis 3
calls this a deliberate reversal the book still owes him). The chapter-29
sequence (the file on the school) is the best-executed adult-Theo material in
the book: the hand over the mouth, the routine kept exactly on schedule after
a shock ("Fine," Theo says, and takes his hand down — the folder comes back
up because leaving it open is worse than finishing it), the twenty-minute
call to his grandmother where neither of them mentions the actual subject.
This is the sheet's "keeps the schedule exactly... more care than usual
rather than less" instruction followed to the letter.

What is still missing, and was already named in `_DIFFERENTIATION.md` before
this pass: actual hedge words in his dialogue. Across the sampled lines
("Most of it's dead weight. A folder's going to need another day," "i want it
on record that this is the stupidest thing any of us has ever done," "still
the stupidest thing any of us has ever done") there is no visible hedge
construction. The chat line "apparently im skipping steps every time??" is
the closest thing to one in the whole sample, and it reads as exasperation
rather than the sincere accuracy-hedge the sheet asks for. The reversal Axis
3 calls for — Theo hedging more than Sam, Ruth, or Nadia — has not happened
in the dialogue; his hedges are still, as the reference doc already
diagnosed, living only in narration ("he does not lie awake," "he would
resist being told otherwise" — these are the narrator's qualifiers, not his).

**Distinctiveness:** high when he is on the page in prose (the file chapter),
thin everywhere else because, like Eli, most of his book-specified chapters
(13, 23, 24, 29, 32, 33, 34, 35) give him narration or chat rather than
quoted speech.

## FEN

**VERDICT: SHEET MOSTLY UNTESTED, BOOK IS THIN.**

Register "literal," practical fact first, feeling second and shorter. The
handful of tagged lines available — "Nearly," Fen says, not opening her
eyes,' and the radiator line — are consistent with the sheet but there are
only two or three of them in the entire book. Fen's own book-specific
continuity note already says this: she appears in chapters 10, 11, 18
(named only), and 21 (named in passing), which is the thinnest presence of
any of the ten cohort sheets. This is not a contradiction so much as an
honest acknowledgment that her sheet cannot be checked against the dialogue
because there is almost no dialogue to check it against. Nothing found
contradicts the sheet; nothing found confirms most of it either.

**Distinctiveness:** cannot be assessed on five lines — there are not five
tagged lines of hers in the book.

## BEX

**VERDICT: MATCHES.**

Register: interrupts, finishes other people's sentences, no formal register
change for rank. "That was me. And I'd start off it next time instead of
finishing on it. We finished on the part that was going to move the number,
and we finished on it in April, with the run already over" (ch. 21) is
exactly the "assembled account" signature — full, accurate, first-person,
delivered to an outsider about work she watched somebody else do — and the
sheet's own "Would they say this" YES list ("if it is the assembled account,
accurate, in the order it happened") is satisfied line for line. "Chloe's are
half up already, and the rest of hers are tomorrow, Bex says, over her
shoulder and out to the room, before Chloe has got to the bottom of the
list" is the completed-ending signature in narrated form. Good fidelity for a
character with a comparatively small speaking role.

**Distinctiveness:** good on the material sampled, though the sample is
small (roughly four scenes).

## IYAD

**VERDICT: MATCHES, with one small self-crediting slip worth a look.**

Register: loud unspecific agreement, restatement at length, addresses the
table by name. The read-out scene in ch. 16 is the signature move exactly:

> "A plate," Iyad says, over the end of it, at the volume of the far end of
> the table. "That's what it is, that's a registration off the system itself,
> sitting right in the middle of the thing, and everybody's been reading the
> ends of it, but nobody looked at the middle... And Chloe's had it a while
> and she'll not say, because she never says until she's certain, and she's
> never certain."

This is the "read-out" and the addressed-to-the-table register done
correctly. The one thing worth flagging: "nobody looked at the middle, which
is where I went first" (context around this line, same scene) is a small
claim of personal priority — not attribution theft exactly, since he isn't
taking someone else's idea, but it is a beat of *his own* credit-taking that
sits slightly against the sheet's insistence that he never tracks ownership,
including of his own ideas. Minor, and arguably defensible as him reporting a
sequence of events rather than staking a claim, but worth a second look.

**Distinctiveness:** good; the loud-agreement-then-longer-restatement shape
is distinct wherever it appears.

## MAREK

**VERDICT: BOOK IS WRONG. His largest scene contradicts his sheet's most
explicit rule.**

His sheet states, twice, in different sections:

> "Would never: Argue that a requirement is beneath him. He has no quarrel
> with the requirement."
>
> "Would they say this — NO if it argues that the rule is stupid. He has no
> quarrel with the rule; the rule simply does not reach him."

Chapter 16 (`chapters/16_thirteen.md`) is built entirely around him doing
exactly that, at length, across a whole scene:

> "Copying it out afterwards is a receipt for something I've already done,
> and a receipt is a different object from work. You want the receipt so
> somebody can look at a page and see I did it. Ask me anything off that
> sheet and I'll prove it without the paper."
>
> "There is no sheet. You asked me for a problem and I brought you back the
> problem, solved. That's the problem and the whole of it. Not a piece of
> paper with your marks on the corner."
>
> "Nothing would [make me hand something in]. A mark on a sheet stays exactly
> as true whether it's written down or not, and the work is just as real
> whether I hand in a page or leave it blank."

This is not a boy who is "somewhere else" and loses track of a form by
accident — the sheet's explicit correction section ("Do not write them as:
The cold monk... a still, flat, contemptuous boy making a principled stand
against bureaucracy... giving him lines about integrity"). It is a coherent,
repeated, three-beat philosophical case that the record-keeping requirement
is conceptually empty. The scene even ends with him walking out ("He is
already halfway out of the seat... he waits by the door until she says fine,
then goes") after making the case, which reads as someone who has made his
principled stand and is now enforcing it — precisely the "cold monk" failure
mode the sheet names and rules out by name. The one thing the scene gets
right is the physical stillness ("he says it the way he says everything,
with his hands still and his voice level"), which does match the sheet's one
carve-out (he goes still and courteous specifically in front of an adult
holding a form) — but the content of what he says in that stillness is the
opposite of courteous evasion; it is argument.

This is the single clearest sheet-vs-book contradiction found in this audit.
Fix: keep the stillness and the outcome (blank sheet, failed course, no
larger consequence), replace the argued case with the sheet's actual
mechanism — the substitution offer ("ask me anything off the sheet and I'll
prove it without the paper" is close to correct and should be built out
further) — and cut the two lines that make it a stance ("There is no sheet,"
"A mark on a sheet stays exactly as true whether it's written down or not").

**Distinctiveness:** the scene is memorable, but for the wrong reason — a
reader who has read the sheet would flag this exchange as someone else's
voice before they flagged anything else in the book.

"Eleven years. Anything for the file" is used verbatim, unmodified for age or
relationship, on two different students (Chloe and Nadia, ch. 22) — exactly
the sheet's claim that he does not adjust register for the listener. The pen

"That's Madison," he says, and starts the hour without adding anything to

no adjective, no encouragement.

No fully quoted extended Bell line turned up in this pass's grep (his

checked.

### MEG — MATCHES, mostly; her signature escalating question is present but

"Over a hundred... Coming from where?" (ch. 3) is a clean hit for the

names as her defining shape — not wrong, since a mother calming a six-year-old

### DAVE — MATCHES.
"Well, how would anybody" and "You already know what you're going to say"
(ch. 3) both read as the flat, low-commitment closing token the sheet

decided to end) the sheet predicts.

### WHITAKER — MATCHES precisely.
"It goes into your file, and it stays sealed there. Access stops at the file
itself..." is the real-answer-on-the-first-try the sheet names as his single

### ALDANA — Insufficient dialogue sample to verify beyond ch. 1, which
matches. Her one scene ("Chloe." ... "What was the question?" ... "That's
what I thought.") is flat, short, closes rather than opens, and carries no
adjective about the child — correct per the sheet's dials. She does not
otherwise speak on the page per her own continuity note, so there is nothing
further to check.

### GRANDMOTHER (Dorothy Halloran) — MATCHES excellently.
The graduation scene (ch. 23) is a near-perfect enactment of the sheet: "What
are you doing next?" (the identical opening question the sheet says she asks
everyone at a gathering), "That'll do you good. Teach you some discipline,"
and the pat on the arm that closes the exchange without waiting for more.
This is one of the cleanest matches in the whole audit.

## If a reader removed every dialogue tag

Six or seven, reliably: **Ruth** (volume, the name-drop, refuses-until-the-
measure-is-named), **Sam** (number-first, deflating literalism, terse to a
fault), **Nadia** (flat, unhedged, near-imperative, no figurative language),
**Priya** (the only run-on), **Chloe** (the concrete-parallel-scenario move,
when it's present), and **Kavi** (level, mechanism-only, when the sheet's
being followed).

Two more — **Theo** and **Odile** — are identifiable only when their
signature construction happens to be on the page (Theo's briefing-shaped
disclosures, Odile's bare number), and blend into generic terse-genius filler
the rest of the time.

The remaining four or five — **Eli, Fen, Marek, Bex, Iyad** — are recognizable
in the handful of scenes actually built around their signature, and
invisible everywhere else, including, in Marek's case, in what is nominally
his own signature scene. Eli and Theo's chat voices are genuinely distinct
from each other and from the rest of the cast, but that is one register out
of two, tested in three chapters out of eight for each of them.
