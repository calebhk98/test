## The gestures

**hand(s) flat, 12 down to 2.** The two survivors are both the mother, and
they are the same gesture ten chapters apart: hand pressed flat against her
forehead at the counter in chapter 1, and in chapter 6 the same picture inside
Chloe's arithmetic about the drive. That gesture now belongs to one person in
this stretch of the book instead of to five.

Everything else went to a body that is actually six years old:

- Ch1 library: "Chloe has both hands flat on the desk, up on her toes" became
  "Chloe is up on her toes with her chin over the edge of the desk." A child
  too short for the counter does not brace on it, she gets her chin over it.
- Ch1 classroom, after the Icarus line: "her hand stays flat on the desk even
  when she knows the answer" became "she sits on her hands even when she knows
  the answer." The one substitution I deliberately used twice: chapter 4 has
  Chloe reverting to the identical posture after Ruth is not punished, so
  "she sits on her hands through the next class" now reads as the same body
  doing the same thing, which is the point of that beat.
- Ch2 with Dr. Prentice: "both hands flat on the table" became "She hooks her
  heels on the rung of the chair." The chapter already has a chair too tall for
  her feet and feet nowhere near the floor; the heels put her in the same
  furniture problem in a new way.
- Ch4 hotel: "one hand pressed flat against the spare pillow" became "her face
  turned into a spare pillow." At six the whole face goes in.
- Ch4 corridor: "puts a hand flat on the wall and laughs until she has to bend
  over" became "laughs until she has to put her forehead against the wall."
- Ch4, Priya measuring a pony in hands: "puts her hand flat on the top of her
  own shoulder" became "lays the edge of her hand across the top of her own
  shoulder," which is also more accurate to how you measure a horse.
- Ch6, last dinner: "talks the rest of it out with one hand flat on her dad's
  sleeve" became "leaning in far enough that her shoulder ends up against his
  arm." Standing too close is the six-year-old version of holding on.
- Ch5's "the square goes over flat on the table" was scoring on the gesture
  meter without being a gesture. It is now "the square folds over," which puts
  the chapter's own verb where the tic was.

**eyes on / eyes down, 14 down to 2.** The two kept are the mother handing the
letter across the table with her eyes still on it, and Sam saying "Ruth" from
two seats over with his eyes on the board, a warning delivered without looking.
The replacements traded gaze for sound, action, or grammar:

- Ch1 librarian: "stamping cards with her eyes on the cards rather than on
  Chloe" became "and the stamp keeps coming down, card after card." The not
  looking is now audible instead of described.
- Ch3 dinner: "both of them keep their eyes on the letter instead of on her"
  became "but the letter has both of them." Shorter, and the letter does the
  taking.
- Ch3 kitchen: "her mom says, her eyes on the stack of bills clipped to the
  refrigerator instead of on him" became "her mom says, to the stack of bills."
  She is now talking to the bills, which is worse for her and better for the
  page.
- Ch3: the standalone line "Her mom keeps her eyes on the screen" became "Her
  mom scrolls," which sets up "Her mom keeps scrolling" ten lines later.
- Ch4 dining hall: "Sam says, with his eyes on his tray" became "and pushes his
  tray a couple of inches away."
- Ch4: "still doing her shoes and not looking up for any of this" became "still
  down over her shoelaces for all of it."
- Ch6 breakfast room: "eyes on the table" became "and folds his paper plate in
  half."
- Ch6: "I had yet to lay eyes on" became "I had yet to see," which makes that
  mother's sentence the exact sentence Chloe's mother says in chapter 3. Two
  women in different rooms reaching the same words is worth more than the idiom.

**turning an object, 6 down to 1.** Kept the one that is physically necessary,
Chloe turning the napkin over to work the fractions again on the clean side.
Sam weighing Chloe's six against his seven and a half is now "setting the six
down beside the seven and a half and weighing them," which keeps the scales and
loses the tic.

## The closers and the withholding

**"the whole of it" / "the rest of it", 11 down to 2.** Kept "Her mom waits for
the rest of it," because two beats later Chloe answers it with "It says Miss,"
and Sam's "has plans for the rest of it," where the rest of it is the school
year and the sentence is about that. Chloe's matching "Chloe waits for the rest
of it" in chapter 5 lost it, since one of the two had to; Ruth's "that's the
whole of it" after the bridge test was pure closer and simply came off.

The one I am proudest of is chapter 5's fractions epiphany. "how many eighths
fit inside the thing you started with, which is the whole of it and all it ever
was" became "and that is all it has ever asked." The sentence is about a
question, so the closer should be about the question.

**announced withholding, 4 down to 1.** Chapter 3's "keeps it to herself until
dinner, four hours later" became "brings it back down at dinner, four hours
later," so the four hours does the withholding instead of the narrator naming
it. Chapter 4's "she keeps that to herself too" was the pure form, and the
whole sentence collapsed to "Chloe is on day six of it herself."

**Kept, and over target because of it:** chapter 1, Chloe explaining the train
station book at dinner. "And she likes having him there by the end, but she
keeps it to herself, and you just know anyway," answered by "How do you know,
then, if she never says so?" and then the locker key. This is not a narrator
announcing a silence; it is a six-year-old articulating subtext, and the scene
exists to show she can do it. One instance puts these chapters at 4.9 against a
target of 3.0. I varied the father's echo, which used to repeat her exact
phrase, so the construction appears once rather than twice.

**"that's not X, that's Y", 1 down to 0.** The father on "usually pretty good":
"because that's not a number, that's a phrase somebody reaches for instead of
giving you a real one" became "because a phrase is what you reach for when you
haven't got a number." The positive claim, with the negation left to the reader.

**"the way you would", 7 down to 3.** Two of the seven were the same simile:
Kayleigh reporting the weather in chapter 1 and Sam agreeing about the weather
in chapter 5. Kayleigh keeps hers, since "You talk weird" delivered as weather
is the injury the whole chapter turns on; Sam's came off entirely, and eight
lines earlier he already has "it comes out flat, like a fact." The remaining
two do work: copying Mr. Baptiste's rectangle, and the CPR hand position.

Chapter 2's "the way you look at somebody before you decide whether to believe
them" also went, because the next clause, filing what he said with everything
adults say when they want you to sit still, is the deciding. Chapter 6's
"'Twenty-seven,' he says again, the way you repeat a number to check it the
second time" is now just "'Twenty-seven,' he says again." The bare repetition
was always doing it.

## The numbers

**four, 75 down to 28.** This was the bulk of the work. The library limit could
not move: chapters 7, 8 and 10 all name four books at a time, so the number is
load-bearing outside my six. Instead the two library arguments were compressed.
Chapter 1's "just four and then four again" became "just twice over," which is
also a sharper thing for her to say. Chapter 3's "I'm going away for four weeks
... if I don't take any out for four weeks, that's four weeks of four" became
"I'm going away for a month ... if I don't take any out the whole time I'm
gone, that's four weeks of four," which leaves the arithmetic she needs and
removes the two that were only echo.

Where the number was arbitrary I moved it and usually improved the beat:
Bryce Dolan now counts problem two on his fingers, not problem four, which puts
him further behind Chloe, who is already at three. The doctor's referral wait
went from four months to six. Kayleigh's "like four minutes ago" became "like
two minutes ago," which is how a seven-year-old argues. Ruth's "I can show you
in four seconds" became two seconds. The bridge log has two entries in it now
instead of four, which is funnier about how fast Ruth quits. December's writing
assignment is three to six sentences and Chloe writes three, against the six
she wrote in November, so the shrinking is one number wider.

Three "four weeks" in a row in the parents' argument became "none of it is
being paid for by the summer, because the summer is free," and the school's age
kept one "four-year-old" and lost the second to "a school that age."

The four hours of the drive, the four books, chapter four of the sled dog book,
the four of them in Pluto, and "All of that in four weeks" answered by "In
twenty-eight days, and she counted them" all stayed. Those are the ones the
book is counting with.

**eleven 10 to 8, nine 11 to 9, forty 6 to 3.** The classroom clock at
eleven-forty was scoring on two meters at once for no reason and is now twenty
past ten. Chapter 3's mother checking the front desk at eleven at night moved
to midnight, which is also more like her, and freed chapter 5's "at eleven at
night" to stand alone as the hour Chloe gets the fractions. Priya's monologue
now runs "in one go" in the narration and lands as "nine minutes end to end"
in Chloe's count, so the number arrives from the child who was timing it rather
than from the narrator. Three separate "forty minutes" became an hour, most of
an hour, and one kept for the bridge build; Ben's "that's where the forty
minutes are" stays, since it is the sum of the two and the thirty-eight he has
just said.

**hedged exact, 8 down to 2.** Mostly the hedge came off and the number stayed,
or the whole measure went: "it goes fine for about ten minutes, until Kayleigh
Burns catches the ball" is now just "it goes fine until." The two kept are both
doing something the bare number would not: Chloe measuring an answer that runs
to about five minutes against a car two streets from home, and Sam's mock
precise "you've said so about two hundred times," which is how children do
hyperbole.

## The two big words

**"same", 62 down to 25, almost all by deletion.** Kept every instance where
sameness is the subject: thirty problems that are one problem, "everything
stays the same," the same worksheet on March 5th, "same day" underlined twice
on the back of the map, the moon's same side, "one wall's the same as any other
wall," and Chloe and Sam independently reporting the same paper for a month,
which is the same sentence from two schools and has to stay twice.

The rest were adjectives doing nothing. A chair "the same size as hers" is now
"no bigger than hers." Ruth is in "that group," not "the same group." Marisol's
rectangle is redrawn "exactly as before, three lines again." Chloe's third pass
at the two facts lost "the same two facts" and reads better for it.

**"flat", 28 down to 7.** The gesture pass took most of them. Of what was left,
"It comes out flatter than she meant, flat enough that her mom looks up" lost
the second one. Mrs. Aldana saying her name "in the flat voice" is now just
Mrs. Aldana saying her name, and "We're on chapter four." carries the tone
without help. Kavi's voice "gone flat" became "worn down." Kavi's goodbye,
hand held out flat, is now palm up, which is the actual gesture.

Kept: the mother pressed flat against her forehead, "She's been flat since
Christmas," Sam's flat and wide bridge and the flat wide bridge it produces,
the pool cover done with one hand flat over the other, and "it comes out flat,
like a fact." Those are the word for the thing.

## The openings

**She/He + verb, 32 down to 23.** Varied the opening, never the sentence.
Chapter 1's gerbil now reads "Most days he gives it up halfway." "She knows
what the teacher is talking about almost all of the time" became "Almost all of
the time she knows what the teacher is talking about," which puts the qualifier
where the sentence is actually arguing. Chapter 2's "She glances at the door"
became "The door gets a glance," and "He tells her a story about a boy and a
wagon" became "There is a story about a boy and a wagon that she has to repeat
back," which also fits the list-of-tasks paragraph it sits in. Chapter 3's "She
tries two more houses, then gives up" became "Two more houses, then she gives
up." Chapter 5 had two She-openings inside one paragraph of fraction work; the
second is now a participle, "Shading three quarters of a rectangle, she rules
the eighths straight down across it." And the third pass at the Owen problem
now opens on the subordinate clause, "Long after Ruth has gotten up and taken
her tray," which is also one more subordinate opening for a chapter that is
short on them.
