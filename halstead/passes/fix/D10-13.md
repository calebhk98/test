### Method for the "specific reason" bar
I re-examined every one of my first-pass "protected" calls against the
stricter standard and dropped most of them:
- Dave's "It's one piece of bread" / "Then it's a different food" — expanded.
  General terseness is not a moment-specific reason; he now argues in full
  clauses ("folded around itself, which is a different thing entirely from
  two").
- Chloe's "It's the same food, but I cut it." — CHLOE.md cites this exact
  line verbatim, and I expanded it anyway, per the coordinator's ruling that
  a sheet citation is not a preservation order. Now: "It's the same food. I
  cut it, that's all, and a cut is just a cut, not a transformation." Flagging
  explicitly: CHLOE.md's citation of the old wording is now stale and should
  be refreshed by whoever owns that sheet.
- Ruth's "That's a hand" exchange, Priya's "Yeah," the Vasquez lines, the
  vendor-scene lines, the curfew scene, the grandmother's weight remark, the
  10v1 scene, the confession exchange, the Thanksgiving argument, the
  paintball scene — all expanded further or expanded for the first time.

Three lines I'm still defending, but now for a moment-specific reason rather
than a general one, named here explicitly so it can be argued with:
1. **Hearn's "That's Madison," he says, and starts the hour without adding
   anything to it.** (ch13) The very next clause of narration states, in the
   narrator's own voice, that he adds nothing. Expanding the quote would
   contradict the sentence sitting next to it, not just underuse a
   terse-but-generally-talkative character.
2. **Dave's "Tariffs. Huh."** (ch13) His sheet's signature section is built
   entirely around this one word being his sole cross-book identifier
   ("appears only in his dialogue across the whole book, a full stop that
   belongs to him alone"). This is the one case in these four chapters where
   the sheet isn't citing an example of a register, it's naming the specific
   token that makes him legible as himself.
3. **Chloe's closing "I don't know," Chloe says, and goes back to eating.**
   (ch13, last line of the chapter) Expanding this would require giving her
   a reason she doesn't have — the whole point of the beat is that she
   genuinely doesn't know why Hearn's unchanged sameness stopped being
   miserable. An invented reason would falsify the moment, not lengthen it.

Two further specific-moment defenses, both physical rather than
psychological:
4. Fen's "Nearly," (ch10) — she is drifting off mid-sentence one line before
   this, "one arm already off the side of the mattress." The shortness is
   drowsiness at that exact second, not her general economy.
5. Chloe's "Four." after the 10v1 drill (ch13) — the whole quartet is
   sitting on the corridor floor having just been physically beaten up by
   the drill, one of them bleeding. Immediate post-exertion breathlessness,
   not a character setting.

One comedic-mechanism defense, flagged rather than silently kept:
6. Sam's first "I liked dance," Sam says. (ch12) is the setup half of a
   two-beat escalation joke — bare assertion, doubted, then the elaborated
   stubborn version two lines later ("I liked dance, and I was good at
   dance, and I'm going to keep saying it."). I left the first half short
   and put the expansion in the second half and in Ruth's reply, since the
   gap between the two is the mechanism his sheet documents as his only
   comedic register. Also left "She says a lot of things," Sam says, into
   his sleeve. (ch13) alone for the same reason — his sheet is explicit that
   he has no other channel for humor than flat, unadorned repetition, and
   this line is exactly that.

Everything else — every line that was short because a character is
generally terse, generally clipped, or "wouldn't have more to say" — got
rewritten. That includes lines from Sam, Kavi, and Dave, all three of whom
have sheets describing a terse default; the brief and the coordinator both
say the floor moves for them too, not that they stay exempt.
