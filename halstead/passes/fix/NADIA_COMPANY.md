## Note 3, scale: what four to six people actually produces

This was done first and it resized the whole chapter. Everything the old draft
used for texture was a function of forty people, so all of it went.

**Gone:** the fastener warehouse, the half-wall, the loading dock and cinder
block, the resurfaced slab, forty coffee orders, the break-room machine, the
four-nights-a-week cleaning contract, the dentist and chiropractor on the same
lot, the five-minutes-on-a-chair morning address, the windowless office, four
hundred interviews, and the board that emails her about burn and headcount. A
bootstrapped five-person company does not have a board, so the competitive
pressure that paragraph carried was moved onto the two rival outfits' job
postings, where it already half was.

**What replaced it:** three rooms over a laundromat, a hundred yards up the
same street as her parents' shop. Concretely, and all of it load-bearing rather
than decorative:

- Dryer heat comes up through the floor from ten in the morning, so the windows
  stay open into November.
- The one bathroom belongs to the laundromat; the key hangs on a paint stirrer
  behind the change machine.
- The building came with two working outlets a room, so there is a run of surge
  strips down the baseboard and an orange extension cord stapled over a door
  frame. She taped and stapled it herself on a Sunday in February. This is the
  Saturday-with-a-visible-end-state habit doing company infrastructure because
  there is no money to do it any other way.
- The kitchen is a folding table with a kettle and one unopened jar of instant
  coffee. It pays off in the closing chat: "six people and a kettle."
- No morning meeting. She says what went live yesterday from wherever she is
  standing and it takes under a minute, because there are four people to say it
  to and three of them were there when it happened.

Headcount arithmetic, kept consistent with the surrounding chapters: chapter 24
has her at two staff in September 2023. The chapter opens on **four people
besides her**, names three of them (Tomas, Bev, Manny), and closes with her
hiring a **fifth** in place of buying screening software. That is five to six in
the building across the chapter, inside the 4-6 band, and it grows toward
chapter 36's "four more people on the floor than she had in June" two years
later without a jump.

`characters/NADIA.md` still carries "Forty staff and three counties of employers
by the following spring" in its continuity block. That line is now wrong, but
the sheet is another agent's file this session, so it is flagged here rather
than edited.

## Note 2, the tests are generated

She has never written a test. She wrote the thing that writes them, over two
weeks of last July, before there was anybody to test. It reads requirement text
off live listings, pulls whatever public documentation and question bank a skill
has, and produces short problems. By May it covers eleven hundred skills, of
which she has read perhaps forty.

The device that makes her wrongness durable is that her only calibration is
herself. She has sat and taken four sets: Python, Excel, Spanish, and one on
brake systems, which she took to see whether it would catch what her father
catches by ear. Full marks on all four; the Python ran eleven minutes. She wrote
the times down the margin of the pad.

Then "almost everyone fails" survives intact and now has a cause. She makes it
easier by moving a difficulty figure, and the whole eleven hundred sets rebuild
overnight while she sleeps. She moves it again in September. By November it is a
version she would have handed a twelve-year-old at Halstead and the pass rate is
still under a third, so she takes the November Python set herself, finishes it
in four minutes, and writes that in the margin under the other four. Nobody
comments on any of it.

## Note 10, interviewing

Four hundred hand interviews became **thirty-one**, all but one of them herself,
against a stated intention of twelve.

The bottleneck is shown rather than described. In February she builds a pass
over the submissions so the reading gets done for her, tuned to put ten in front
of her a week, and it puts ten in front of her a week. She then reads the other
sixty as well, at the kitchen table before eight, because the ten it picks are
not always the ten she would have picked. She calls that wanting to know how
often, which is not a thing anybody argues with her about.

Both hand-interview scenes are kept whole, because other chapters lean on them:
the payment system and "that's an edge case," and the scheduling tool where she
asks him to name the person who does the review and he cannot. Chapter 24's "i
ask them to tell me where the thing they built breaks" still lands on the same
question.

## Note 9, her hires

Three named people, each doing something past the edge of the job, none of it
framed as impressive:

- **Tomas.** A board starts rate-limiting her submission traffic on a Saturday.
  He catches it off a graph well outside anything he was ever asked to watch,
  rewrites the back-off, ships it himself, and tells her Monday, by which point

## Note 5 and note 6, the confrontation

### How she walks in

Note 6 is carried by three things she does before anybody speaks, and one thing
she says.

She parks in front, where the upstairs window can see the car, and leaves the
doors unlocked, because ten minutes is what this is going to take. She tells
nobody where she is going. Then, out loud in the room:

> "You aren't going to do anything to me. There are eleven filings with a real
> agent's name on them, and a shop full of people underneath us who watched me
> come up those stairs."

The belief is stated so it can be taken apart in front of her, which is the
whole point of putting it in dialogue:

> "It's twenty past five. Chuck locks the bay at five, so the shop underneath
> you is shut and those stairs were yours alone, and you want to be thinking
> about that."

She does not re-evaluate. The next line is "She goes on to the next thing on her
list." Nothing in the prose says she is wrong, and nothing says she is brave.
The reader gets to see the belief is thin and she does not.

### How it reads now

No concession, no apology, nothing polite. The sequence:

1. **Confusion.** "Whoa, hey, hold on." "You're after the tire place. That's the
   door at the bottom of the stairs."
2. **Dismissal.** She states the charge with the dollar figure on it, and gets
   "Get out of here."
3. **Containment.** The one in the doorway walks around behind her, pushes the
   door shut with the flat of his hand, and stands against it with his arms
   folded. She hears the latch and keeps her eyes on the desk.
4. **The age, used against her.** "How old are you." "Eighteen since last June."
   "Jesus Christ. She's eighteen years old, she's a kid, she's somebody's
   daughter." Then "Does your father know where you are this afternoon?" and she
   answers it honestly, which is worse than a lie, and the man behind the desk
   sits back in his chair for the first time since she came in.
5. **The threat.** The one with the coffee photographs her, crosses to the
   window, photographs the car, and reads her registration out loud, all seven
   characters, slowly, and then again.
6. **Her one mistake.** "You can't prove any of it. There's not a name on a
   single one of those accounts, and you've got no idea who you're standing in
   front of." Nadia laughs. It is one sound and it is out of her before she has
   decided anything about it. The man at the folding table stands up, takes his
   time about it because he can afford to, comes around the end of the table,
   and stops close enough that she has to tilt her head back to keep his face in
   view. "Say that again. Slower, so everybody in the room gets the benefit of
   it." She says it again, exactly, in the same order and at the same speed.
7. **The leverage, delivered from under him.** Nine of eleven filed in one week
   in September through one agent; the agent is Hanley; Hanley carries
   thirty-one filings on that address and twenty-six belong to somebody else.
   She is not calling the police, because a county detective gets eleven form
   submissions and a Tuesday he already had plans for. She is sending it to the
   business division at the Secretary of State with nine time-stamped copies and
   a dollar figure attached, which is the only kind of complaint anybody up
   there opens before lunch. They come for Hanley, not for them, and everything
   filed through him goes back on the street the same week. "Then twenty-six
   other people find out who cost them their paperwork, and it will be the
   notice telling them, rather than me."
8. **The offer, sized exactly.** The eleven are already deleted, a twelfth stays
   off the site, nothing else of theirs comes near it, and she never makes the
   drive again. "That's the whole offer, and I'm not improving it while I stand
   here."
9. **Compliance without concession.** Nobody agrees to anything. The man behind
   the desk looks at the one standing over her until he sits back down. "Get out
   of my office. That's the second time I've said it to you and I'm finished
   saying it." She takes it.
10. **The exit stays ugly.** The one at the door says three words with sweetheart
    on the end of them and another laughs. Somebody comes out onto the landing
    and watches her cross the lot and is still up there when she reaches the car.

Afterwards, no gloating and no relief: she gets the key in on the second try,
drives back, and counts a till her mother has already counted. The threat she
made is never carried out, and it never needed to be. Hanley's filings go dead
in July, thirty-one of them inside the same fortnight, which she spots in August
and keeps to herself. The chapter does not say she won.

The chat afterwards used to be "one of them apologised." It is now Sam getting
nothing out of her twice and saying so, and Eli asking for the agent's name and
being refused, which is the would-never about escalation doing work on the page:
"eli if you go anywhere near it, it stops being over."

## Note 7, emotion

Nothing is narrated. All of it is in what she says and does.

- **Plainly upset.** The third victim call runs twenty minutes and the woman
  apologises four times, twice for taking up the evening and twice for having
  answered at all. Nadia stays in the back room afterwards with the light off
  and the phone still in her hand. Then, in the chat: "she apologised to me /
  the woman. four times in twenty minutes, i counted them."
- **Admits confusion, and asks for help.** "i dont know what youre supposed to
  say to that." Ruth tells her. "i said that. she said it twice more."
- **The knife edge.** In the closing chat, and the figure of speech is Sam's,
  because she does not use them: payroll is the 22nd, employers pay on the hire,
  hires land between the 5th and the 20th, two slipped in April and she covered
  everybody out of her own account and put it back on the 9th. Sam: "thats a
  knife edge on a tightrope." Nadia: "its eleven days." "eleven days of what."
  "of everyone still getting paid if nothing at all comes in."
- **Laughs at something stupid.** In the scam room, at confidence that has
  outrun competence, played completely straight, and it costs her.
- **Wants something for no strategic reason.** Ninety dollars of white vinyl
  letters she puts on the street window herself off a ladder sinking into the
  verge, with the second word half an inch low and unliftable. "Who is ever
  going to read that," Bev said. "I read it every morning," Nadia said, and went
  back down the ladder for the squeegee. Nobody who matters to the business ever
  walks past that window and nothing in the prose points that out.
- **Gets something socially wrong and has to recover.** The laugh, and the
  recovery is her repeating the sentence exactly rather than backing off it,
  which is the only recovery she has.
- **Fear as recounting** (per the character sheet's body language): the till
  comes out four dollars under, she counts it again, gets the same number, then
  runs the whole tape through a third time from the top of the day. Her mother
  asks where she was and gets a true, useless answer. She works the counter
  until close, on a Thursday, for the first time since November.
- **"im FINE. im insulted"** is kept deliberately, as her first and wrong
  answer, undercut sixteen lines later by the thing she actually minds.

## House Rule 1

The two places the old draft explained itself are gone. "The one behind the desk
looks at the other three before he looks back at her. That's fair" was doing
narrator work through a character's mouth. Two beats added during revision that
named an effect ("Nobody in the room has an answer for that") were replaced with
physical facts: one of the two at the folding table turns a printout face down,
and the man behind the desk puts both hands flat on the desk.

No sentence anywhere says the social good is a social good. She takes the
scammers down because they came into her building, she calls the three victims
because it happened on her site, and she declines to reimburse anybody. Her one
stated reason for anything is proportion.
