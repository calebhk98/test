## Per number

### one: 237 found, 106 deleted, 131 left

Two kinds of *one* in these chapters. The numeral (*one page*, *one key*, *one
word*) is mostly load-bearing and mostly stayed. The pronoun (*the next one*,
*a different one*, *that one*, *the right one*) is the actual habit, and that
is where the 106 came from: it was almost always replaceable by the noun it
stood in for, which is shorter and clearer. *he asks one that stops her cold*
became *he asks a word that stops her cold*; *a different one out of the
drawer* became *a different booklet out of the drawer*; *the third one to bed*
became *the third book to bed*; *the one you said built the better bridge*
became *the girl you said built the better bridge*; *goes back up to the fourth
one* became *goes back up to the fourth stair*.

Kept, and why:

- **The locker key, ch1.** *There's one key to the locker.* The whole of
  Chloe's argument about how you know the brother matters rests on there being
  exactly one.
- **The bounce rule, ch1.** *caught it on one bounce* four times across the
  playground argument. It is the rule two children are arguing about; the
  phrase has to stay the same phrase each time or the argument stops being an
  argument. One instance thinned to *off the bounce*.
- **The single word, ch1.** *takes the entire thing down to one word.*
- **One room and one worksheet, ch1.** The pairing is the sentence.
- **The composite score, ch2.** *one big number on the front of it* /
  *averages two very different pictures into one score.* Ben is arguing a
  figure.
- **One over zero, ch5.** Mathematics. Three instances in four lines, all
  kept.
- **One second, ch8.** *it takes one second to say it* / *It's not one second,
  Chloe.* The number is what the fight is about.
- **One hand flat on it, ch7.** The worked example in HOUSE_RULES. Untouched.
- **One thing, and it is small, ch8.** The hinge of the chapter.

### Two honest flags

1. **ch1, *like two minutes ago* became *like a minute ago*.** This is the one
   place in the pass where the fix is close to a substitution. *A minute ago* is
   idiom for *just now* rather than a count, and it carries no tracked number
   word, but the reader may still hear a number in it. It stayed because
   Kayleigh and Chloe are arguing about something that happened seconds ago and
   *just now* flattened the line.
2. **Five places where *four weeks* / *twenty-eight days* became *a month*.**
   ch3 (*It's free, the whole month*), ch6 (*All of that in a month*), ch7
   (*the whole month*, *a whole month*), ch8 (*a month, away from home*). This
   swaps a counted form for an uncounted one of the same duration rather than
   losing precision, so it is a weaker move than the rest of the list. It was
   worth doing because the camp's length was being restated in counted form six
   separate times, and the one that matters is kept intact and unchanged:
   *There's tomorrow as well, which makes twenty-eight, though I'm leaving it
   out until it happens*, answered later by *In twenty-eight days, and she
   counted them.*

## Where the line was drawn

The brief's distinction, *Chloe counting is characterisation, the narrator
counting everything is the habit*, did most of the work. Two rules of thumb
came out of applying it:

- **A number in Chloe's mouth or in her head usually stayed; the same number in
  the narrator's mouth usually went.** *for eleven minutes, which she counts*
  stayed. *Chloe waits for the phone for eleven days* went, because nobody in
  the scene is counting the days, the narrator is.
- **A number told twice is a number told once too often.** The bridge holding
  at eleven pounds is stated in ch5 as the event, retold in ch6 to her dad and
  again in ch7 to the librarian. It now appears once. The camp's length, the
  eighteen-inch span, the four hours' drive, *seven and a half*, *twenty-eight
  days*: in each case the pass kept the instance where the number is doing
  something and deleted the restatements.
