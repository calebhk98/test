## 2. ch12 — Kavi lists his objections in order

*Finding (section 7): "Kavi lists his objections in order; none is given." Author: "This should
also be a dialogue, not a summary."*

**Before:** "Kavi got none of the three he asked for and lists his objections at breakfast in
order, starting with the first and working down, while something on Sam's sheet is different from
everyone else's and he keeps it to himself, so that by Thursday there are four theories."

**After:** the summary clause is replaced by the breakfast itself. He puts three objections in
order, each attacking a different joint of the same mechanism:

1. "The first one is that the form does nothing, because it asks you for three and it gave me
   zero of three."
2. "The second one is that two of the three are running in this building this year. Programming
   is on every ten-year-old's sheet and astronomy is on the elevens', so both of them have a
   teacher and an hour and a room, and the thing that stopped me is my age, which they had
   written down before they handed me anything to fill in."
3. "The third one is cryptography, and cryptography happens at thirteen, which was already true
   in October when I asked for it. They kept the request eleven months and gave me back a date
   that was fixed before I wrote it down."

The Sam clause survives verbatim at the end of the beat.

**Voice.** Against `characters/KAVI.md`: conclusion first, never a lead-in; disagreement aimed at
the mechanism and never at a person; outcomes stated as settled fact; no questions; zero
figurative language; length set by how much the mechanism takes to state, never padded. He is
handling the object in the room the whole way through (the sheet flat by the tray, turned over,
squared against the table edge), which is his documented way of occupying a scene.

**Facts.** Checked against `CURRICULUM_GRID.md`: programming is age 10, astronomy age 11,
cryptography age 13, so all three of his requests are real courses in the building and none of
them is available to a nine-year-old. That is what makes the second and third objections true
rather than sulking, which is the difference between Kavi and a complaint.

**Who answers.** Ruth answers the first one, at mechanism level, the way she answers everything:
"They read it. They counted how many people wanted each thing, and then they did what they were
going to do anyway." It is a real answer and it does not help, and he takes it as read and keeps
going. Sam tries to leave. The third objection gets nothing: "Ruth takes the sheet off him and
reads the back of it twice, and hands it back when Chloe gets up with her tray." No narration
tells the reader that nobody had a counter.

## 4. ch12 — the three identical beats

*Finding (section 9): the "fails once, adjusts one specific technique, succeeds" beat runs three
times with little differentiation: chemistry, textiles, the overhang. Author: "Yes, the beat needs
variation, but we need to keep those classes, show she does fail, and she needs to have success in
all of them afterwards."*

All three instances are still here, each still contains a real failure, and each still ends in a
real success. What changed is the shape, because the problem was never any one of them: it was
that all three ran fail / one adjustment / clean success at the same tempo.

**What I rejected.** Cutting one of the three (breaks the author's first constraint). Softening a
success into a partial one (breaks the third). Turning the overhang into Chloe's beat so the
author's "she" applies to all three: the overhang is Sam's whole year and the chapter's loudest
scene, and taking it off him would cost more than the redundancy does.

### Chemistry — the adjustment arrives fast and the understanding is made to cost extra

**Before:** the beat ended at "She runs it again on the Friday, drying every piece of glass twice
before she starts, and it comes out white, and she grins at it before she catches herself and
carries it up to the front."

**After:** that success stands, and then Vasquez refuses to let it stand as knowledge:

> "That one's white, so tell me which of the three it was."
>
> "The glass, because the glass is the one I changed."
>
> "You dried the glass, you took a fresh bottle off the shelf, and you tied your sleeve back out
> of the way, which is three changes and one result." He is already two benches down. "Run it wet
> next Friday and find out which of them you fixed."
>
> So the Friday after that she runs it wet on purpose and gets the grey back, and the Friday after
> that she runs it dry off the old bottle and gets white, and it is November by the time she
> writes glassware in the back of the notebook with a date beside it.

This is the one that costs her something: a clean white sample in week five, and three more Fridays
spent producing a failure deliberately before the result is worth anything. It also inverts the
shape the other two use, because here the success comes before the last failure rather than after
it.

### Textiles — the first adjustment is wrong, and the fix comes from outside the method

**Before, in one sentence:** "...so it drags every time she lifts her arm, until she takes the
sleeve out, redraws the armhole, and builds the shirt over from the armhole out, and the second one
fits."

**After:** the armhole rebuild happens and fails. The second shirt drags in the same place on the
same arm. In January she stops redrawing, brings in a shirt from home that already fits her, lays
it flat on the brown paper and traces round the top of it, and finds her drafted shoulder line an
inch and a half out. The third shirt fits, at the end of March, and she wears the second one all
spring anyway, because the second one is the green one.

Two failures instead of one, self-found instead of handed over, six months instead of a week, and a
success she does not fully collect on. No sentence explains why the armhole was the wrong suspect;
the second shirt dragging in the same place says it.

### The overhang — the success is public, second-hand, and paid for

**Before:** "He goes up in one piece and gets a hand over the lip on the second try, because of the
left foot, and the noise underneath him is loud enough that a teacher comes out of the side door."

**After:** "He comes off the first attempt a foot under the lip, in front of all thirty of them,
and stands under the wall shaking his right hand out for a while before he goes back at it. He gets
a hand over the lip on the second try..." and, after the crowd beat, "Sam eats left-handed for three
days and keeps the other hand under the table."

The failure is now on the day, in front of the audience he assembled himself, and the win costs him
his hands for three days that he says nothing about. "because of the left foot" is gone: Sam had
just said it twice in his own dialogue, so the narration restating it was House Rule 1 in its
purest form.

**Net effect across the three:** the adjustment is handed to her by a teacher / found alone outside
the classroom's method / obtained from a rival by asking; the timescale is one week / six months /
one school year; the success is clean-then-audited / late and partly abandoned / public and paid
for in skin.

## 5. ch9 — the two overheard arguments

*Finding (section 9): two set-pieces of Chloe passively overhearing her parents argue about her.
Author: "Needs to change one, but keep the reader aware. We can change how, or if needed, use a
floating 3rd camera POV."*

**Chosen: change the second one, to a detached camera on Dave and Meg with Chloe out of the house.**

### Which one to change

The first (Chloe in bed, the kitchen table under the thin part of the floor, "she said take my
birthday, Dave") has to stay overheard. It arrives four paragraphs after she has begged on her
bedroom floor and been told nothing, and the whole point of the placement is that the decision about
her is audible and out of reach on the same night. Convert that one and the chapter loses its
strongest use of the device.

The second (the house offer) was the weaker overhearing on its own terms. Its content is adult
reasoning Meg would never say in front of a seven-year-old, which is why the draft had to muffle it:
Chloe "listening to none of it," then catching three nouns, "house, and roof, and nowhere." The
scene was already paying to keep her in the room.

### What changed

**Before:** "The house comes up in April, in the living room, with Chloe on the rug in front of the
television and listening to none of it." ... and closing: "On the rug, Chloe has stopped moving. She
catches enough of it to string together house, and roof, and nowhere, then gets up once in the
middle of it to check through the window that the car is still in the driveway, before sitting back
down in front of the television and letting the rest of it go."

**After:** "The house comes up in April, on a Saturday, with Chloe up the road at the Petersons' for
the afternoon. Her dad has the letter open on the kitchen table in front of him, and her mother
stays standing at the counter for the whole of it with the mug in both hands." ... and closing:
"Her mother puts the mug in the sink and runs water into it. Her dad folds the letter back along the
crease it came in, and at four he walks up the road for Chloe, who talks the whole way back down it
about a dog that is allowed on the furniture."

Every line of the argument itself is unchanged except one stage direction: "Dave looks down, at
neither of them" becomes "Dave looks at the table," since there are now only two people in the room.
Meg's mug moves from a dialogue tag to the framing sentence so the detail is not stated twice. The
reader gets more of the offer than before, not less: the furnished house, the ten minutes from
campus, the payment for as long as she is enrolled, the single condition, the nine weeks a year,
Meg's actual reason, and July.

### Why this costs the chapter least

The chapter is already willing to leave Chloe's head. It does it twice more in the same file: her
parents listening to her cross the ceiling for ten minutes, and Meg alone in the parked car with the
engine off, seen through a kitchen window, which is `characters/MEG.md`'s own cited picture of her
processing privately. A detached camera on the two of them is therefore not a new instrument in this
chapter, only a longer use of one it already owns.

Chloe learning it some other way was the alternative I rejected. There is no plausible route: Meg's
whole argument is that she might one day pull Chloe out, and neither parent is going to say that to
her, before or after. Making her a participant would require Meg to argue a censored version of her
own case, which is the version the draft already had.

**Character check.** `characters/MEG.md`: flat and unhedged wherever anyone can hear her (Dave is in
the room, so no hedge), occupies rooms at counters with her hands busy, states things flatly even
when they are hard. `characters/DAVE.md`: no hedge, no image, the reversal-adjacent register where
he keeps building past where "Huh" would close it, amusement and distress as physical beats rather
than spoken ones ("rubs a hand over his face," "looks at the table"). Both keep the "her dad" /
"her mother" naming the book uses for them in Chloe-absent scenes elsewhere, including ch11's
detached kitchen exchange.

**Kept out.** No sentence anywhere in the new staging tells the reader that Chloe missed this, or
what it will mean later.
