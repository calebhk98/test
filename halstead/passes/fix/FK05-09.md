The job here was 143 words over the then-ceiling without losing the 9.0, plus a
check of the inherited rewrite. It came out shorter and slightly higher.

**How the words came out.** Not by shaving adjectives: shaving alone drops
words-per-sentence and takes the grade with it. Ten pairs of sentences were
merged with their joint written in, which removes a sentence and a few words at
once and holds the mean up.

> He draws a rectangle, shades half of it, then cuts the shaded half into
> pieces, talking the whole way through about the question underneath the
> question. Chloe follows him as far as the shading, loses track in the middle
> of what the pieces are pieces of, and is left looking at a rectangle with a
> number of lines across it.

became one sentence joined on `and`, four words shorter with the same content.
Same move on the Owen-in-maths paragraph (three sentences to two, eleven
words), the bridge-load paragraph, the packet paragraph, and the last line.

**Two defects found in the inherited rewrite and fixed.**

1. *A joke lost its antecedent.* The teacher's line had been expanded from
   "You can keep going if you're mid-sentence... Not mid-paragraph" to "if
   you're in the middle of a sentence... but not if you're in the middle of a
   paragraph." Sam's next line is "How mid is mid," which no longer had a "mid"
   to refer to. Reverted; six words back as well.
2. *A comma splice.* "There is a pool, swimming is at four every afternoon, and
   because Chloe arrived unable to swim..." is two short independent clauses
   welded with a comma. Rewritten as "Swimming is at four every afternoon, and
   because Chloe arrived unable to swim she is put in the group at the shallow
   end of the pool..."

**Negative space.** The one narration hit went: "and Chloe, without running the
sentence past herself even once, says" became "and Chloe answers him a half
second later with". The four that remain are dialogue in the Owen scene, where
"he wasn't having fun" is the thing being said and cannot be said another way.

## Chapter 6, The List

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| Flesch-Kincaid | 4.8 | 8.5 |
| words / sentence | 14.2 | 23.5 |
| 7+ char words % | 10.3 | 10.4 |
| spoken mean (tool) | 12.9 | 23.4 |
| spoken mean (per quoted sentence, see note) | 10.6 | 15.3 |
| negative space % | **12.1** | **1.4** |
| word count | 3,048 | 3,324 |
| quoted share % | 49.6 | 51.4 |

The hardest of the five: the worst negative space in the book, the talkiest
chapter in the book, and at the time only 352 words of headroom.

**What went wrong first, and the correction.** My first draft of this chapter
hit 9.2 and came in at **3,910 words** - the exact defect the addendum names.
The cause is worth recording, because it is a trap in the method: spelling out
a logical joint costs four to six words a sentence, and this chapter had 215
sentences, so treating every sentence adds 900 words before you look up. The
lever that actually works is **merging**, not adding. Merging two sentences with
their connective costs about two words and removes a sentence, so it raises
words-per-sentence from both ends at once. The finished chapter is 215
sentences down to 141 with 276 words added rather than 862.

**Negative space, 12.1% to 1.4%.** Twenty-six negative constructions down to
three. Most were curable by saying the positive thing, which is usually the more
specific thing:

> "Nobody put theirs on the bus." -> "Every one of us drove, and the letter offered a bus."
> "It doesn't mean anything here." -> "Here it buys you a paper plate and a seat at this table."
> "Sam never got it, and he said the click was broken, but it's not broken" -> "Sam gave up before his and decided the click was broken, but the click works fine"
> "You weren't even on the phone call" -> "You were on the wrong side of a door for that call"
> "so nobody else at the table can tell what we're saying" -> "so the rest of the table has to sit there and guess"

Three were kept deliberately, and they are the chapter's spine: the mother's
"- I still don't know what it is", and her dad's "I don't know what that is,
Meg" four hundred words from the end. The chapter is about adults who cannot
name the thing; those sentences are the naming failing.

**Note on the spoken mean, 23.4.** This number is not what it looks like.
`dialogue_study` concatenates all quoted spans and then splits on terminal
punctuation, so a line that ends `...both times," the woman says.` runs
straight into the next speaker's line, because neither quote contains a full
stop. Four of the "spoken sentences" it counts here are three consecutive
speakers glued together, the longest at 83 words. Measured per quoted sentence
instead, this chapter is at **15.3**, up from 10.6, against chapter 10 - the
chapter the addendum called inflated - at **17.3** on the same measure. The
dialogue here is less lifted than the line that was flagged. The longest real
spoken sentences are Chloe's recitation at 37 to 51 words, which is the
chapter's premise, and the originals in that passage were already 28 to 44.

Same effect in the other four: tool 16.9 / 23.4 / 24.5 / 22.2 / 20.6 against
per-quoted-sentence 11.3 / 15.3 / 16.9 / 14.4 / 14.9. **If the 16 floor is
meant to describe how long a spoken line actually is, the tool is the wrong
instrument for it** and the second row is the one to read.

## Removed under the no-talking-to-the-reader rule

Everything below was introduced by this pass and has been taken out again. The
coordinator is right that it is a side effect of the method: once you are in
the habit of writing the logical joint in, the next clause after "which is"
starts wanting to be the point rather than the reason.

**Chapter 5** (two of these were inherited from the earlier run, not mine)

- "the pleasure arrives with a habit attached: she cannot stop checking where everybody else has got to on the page" -> "though she cannot stop checking..." The colon named the feeling and then showed it.
- "which is more than can be said for Ruth's" -> "better looking than Ruth's". The narrator scoring a point.
- "giving him the beat a person takes before the part that explains the first part" -> "giving him a beat to add to it". Explaining what a beat is for.
- "out of habit, the way she would have all through first grade, and the sentence comes back clean" -> "out of habit, and the sentence comes back clean". The gloss told the reader what the habit was for on the chapter's last line, where the reader has just watched eleven pages of it.

**Chapter 6**

- "cutting his waffle into squares and stacking them at the side of the plate, **which is what he does with a question he intends to ask properly**" - the clearest case: the gesture already carries it, and he asks the question in the next line.
- "and two people take that up gratefully, **because a route is something an adult can be certain about**" -> "and two people take that up". The scene is four parents failing to say what the school is; the reason did not need supplying.
- "Chloe keeps her eyes shut and her breathing slow and even on purpose, **because a person who is asleep gets told things**" -> "...slow and even, on purpose." "On purpose" was doing it.
- "her mom at the counter with her hand flat on her forehead, and the woman at the district office saying be careful, **both turning up in the sum uninvited**".
- "she keeps going straight through **the repetition as though it were new**" -> "and she keeps going, straight through it."
- "another father is sitting through roughly the same **performance**" -> "the same thing".
- Mother's line: "She's top of her reading group at home, **which is a sentence that means something there and lands in this room as weather**" -> "She's top of her reading group at home." The next speaker answers it; the answer was the point.
- Speaker's line: "a whole family meeting over it, **which is a strange thing to convene about a summer camp**" -> "a whole family meeting over it, about a summer camp".
- Ruth: "what I hate is homework, **which is a different activity entirely**" -> "what I hate is homework."
- "he checks the screen **to see whether the call has dropped**".
- "the way **a person repeats a figure to see whether it holds up**" -> "the way you repeat a number to check it the second time".

**Chapter 7**

- "It works on the first day of second grade exactly the way it worked in April, **which is to say the arithmetic is finished by twenty-five past and the remaining thirty-five minutes belong to whoever can invent something to do with them**" - the paragraph above it has just shown all of that.
- "she has finished two of them, **the first occasion in two years that a library date has arrived with anything left unread**".
- "By the end of September **the asking has organised itself into a routine**, several times a day, at the same points in it" -> "By the end of September, she is asking several times a day." The catalogue that follows is the routine.
- "Whatever comes back takes a long time, **and Chloe counts the interval in pen taps**".
- "the tone **she reserves for a conversation whose answer she already knows and has to hear delivered again anyway**" -> back to the draft's own, plainer version.

**Chapter 9**

- "so she stops waiting for them to, **which is a decision she can feel herself making, deliberately, the way a person puts down something heavy**".
- "lets the rest of it go, **on the understanding that whatever the argument underneath it is about, the driveway still has the car in it**" - she checks the driveway two clauses earlier; the reader can do this.
- "right through the corridors and out to the car, **because in her experience a building decides about a person with a sheet of paper and a pencil, and this one has apparently decided some other way**".
- "said some of it had, **which was accurate about the arithmetic and inaccurate about everything else**".

**Also reverted: syllable padding.** The addendum's second defect ("long words
have to be words the thing is actually called, not longer synonyms for short

## What is still failing, and why it is left

Three corpus-median measures fail across the five and are a direct consequence
of the technique rather than something to grind further:

- **"and" as a share of words, 3.9 to 4.3 against a median of 3.3.** Merging two
  sentences with their connective is what raised the reading grade, and the
  commonest true connective in this narrator's register is "and". A pass over
  the worst offenders swapped fifteen of them for "so that", "while", "then",
  "before" and a full stop, which took roughly half a point off each chapter.
  Pushing it lower means either reaching for connectives the narrator would not
  use or putting the short sentences back.
- **Sentences opening on a subordinate clause, 4.3 and 4.5 in chapters 6 and 7
  against a band of 0.5 to 3.** Almost every hit is a false positive: the regex
  matches "When is the form due, and who signs it," and "Since when do you skip
  an entire week," which are questions, not front-loaded clauses. The genuine
  ones ("While one of the fathers looks at his plate,", "After eleven days she
  stops sitting in the kitchen,") were in the draft before this pass.
- **7+ character words, 9.8 to 11.3 against a median of 13.2.** This one is
  deliberate after the revert described above. The chapters are seven- and
  eight-year-old Chloe, her parents and her teachers, and the honest long words
  available are the ones the things are actually called - transportation,
  prediction, enrichment, registered, arithmetic, percussion. Reaching past
  those produced "telephone directory" for phone book and "conducts them around"
  for takes them around, which is the addendum's second defect, and it is gone.
