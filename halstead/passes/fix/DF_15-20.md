## 2. ch16, Amberg on locks. Put in his mouth.

Finding (D_FINDINGS 8, ch16 L69): "because you would be guessing about what a
lock is for," glossing Amberg's metaphor. Author: *"IDK about this one, it's
written poorly, but also it is something you would say to kids."*

Before:

> In cryptography the first term goes entirely on breaking things other people
> built, substitution, then Vigenere, then a fortnight on why a key you use
> twice stops being a key, so by November they are working real messages off
> real systems with real mistakes in them. Amberg's line from the first day is
> that you cannot build a lock until you have opened one somebody else built,
> because until then you are guessing what a lock is for.

After (the first sentence unchanged, the second replaced by a new paragraph):

> Amberg gives them the order of it on the first day, before a single cipher
> goes up on the board. "You will not build a lock this year; you will open
> locks other people built, and the sequence is deliberate. One, a lock fails in
> the one place the person who designed it stopped looking. Two, you find that
> place by opening the lock, and by nothing else. Three, after thirty of them
> you know where people stop looking, and that is the whole of what you carry
> into the first lock you build yourself. The other way round, you are guessing
> what a lock is for."

The content the author wanted kept is all there, including the gloss itself: it
is now the last thing he says to a room of thirteen-year-olds rather than the
narrator turning round to make sure the reader followed the metaphor.

Against `characters/AMBERG.md`: enumerated clauses, each adding one concrete
fact ("One... Two... Three..."), the rule and the reason for the rule in the
same sentence, no hedging, no praise, no joke, and no shortening for the age in
the room, which the sheet names as the thing that is his rather than a generic
teacher's. I did not give him a third instance of the put-the-document-down
signature; the sheet counts its two clean instances as the whole of it, and a
cipher class has no governing document to set down. "The sequence is deliberate"
is his procedural register; "the man who built it" was my first draft and is now
"the person who designed it".

Cost: +75 words (chapter well inside the band at 3484) and two tenths of reading
grade, discussed above.

## 4. ch15, Chloe and Pruitt on the hook. Both kept; his confirmation arrives from the other side of the work.

Finding (D_FINDINGS 9, ch15 L11-13 vs L19). Author: *"This is supposed to show
that Chloe is not imagining it, but only Pruitt sees it. But can be modified a
little."*

**The answer to the author's note first.** Both halves are intact: Chloe is
right, Sam looks straight at the fault and cannot see it, the girl at the next
bench admires the wrong thing, and Pruitt is still the only adult who finds it
on his own. What changed is that he is no longer saying her sentence back to
her. Chloe's diagnosis is of the finished shape, read at the window: where the
two curves meet, and how the shadow runs. Pruitt's names a moment in the making,
the second turn, which the finished hook cannot tell you and which she had no
way to recover, and he gets it off a thumb run down the scroll as he walks past.
His line is unchanged, word for word, and he is given nothing else to say.

Before:

> He hands the hook back, tells her it still looks fine to him and takes his
> coat off the peg because it is nearly six, and then the girl at the next bench
> comes over with a punch in her hand and admires how straight the hammer marks
> run down the shank. Three weeks later Pruitt passes the bench, where the hook
> is still sitting. "You lost the curve on the second turn," he says, and keeps
> going.

After:

> ... Three weeks later five hooks sit on the bench with the same fault in all
> of them, and Pruitt comes past with a bar on his shoulder, running a thumb
> down the scroll of the nearest as he goes. "You lost the curve on the second
> turn," he says, and keeps going. She has the sixth in the fire that evening
> and stops at the second turn, scroll half open, and puts it back in the coals.

Three weeks of hooks with the same fault in each is what makes his eight words
new information rather than agreement: she has been unable to fix a thing she
can describe exactly. The last sentence is the whole of the reader's evidence
that he told her something she did not have, and it is an action, not a gloss;
what she does with it is put the metal back in the fire at the point he named,
which also lands against his first correction, three chapters of his speech
being about heat and a curve. No narration says any of that.

Against `characters/PRUITT.md`: he never stops and never demonstrates, the
correction is one flat clause naming a checkable fault, no name, no instruction
on what to do about it, and the hand is on the work before the eye is
("running a thumb down the scroll"), which the sheet gives as the mechanism
behind his whole method. He gains no line and no sentence of speech.

Also trimmed nine words of restatement from Chloe's own diagnosis a paragraph
earlier: "where it ought to be turning the whole way down, the same curve from
one end to the other" now ends at "the whole way down".

Word count: ch15 goes 4626 to 4668, still 668 over the ceiling. I could not
honestly shorten it here. The chapter's length is in its scenes and the brief
forbids cutting those; the nine words above are the only genuine redundancy the
passage contained.

## 5. ch18, the driving. Answer: yes, it can be dramatized, and it should be.

Finding (D_FINDINGS 8, ch18 L169): the father summarises her driving for the
reader instead of the reader watching it. Author: *"If you can somehow have a
reader watch her driving, without narration, then yes? IDK though?"*

**The answer.** Yes, and it costs nothing the summary was doing. Three reasons I
did not leave it:

1. The summary's two halves are already the two halves of the driving section.
   The junction reading was dramatized eleven lines earlier, in full, with the
   truck. The clutch was dramatized in the church lot, eleven stalls in the
   first half hour. So the sentence was not carrying information the scenes had
   withheld; it was restating both of them and adding a verdict.
2. What it did have that the scenes lacked is the two arriving *at the same
   moment*, in July, when everything else has come good. That is a scene, and a
   short one: one junction where she is expert and then a child inside ten
   seconds.
3. The simile is attributed to her father, and `characters/DAVE.md` has
   figurative language under **not allowed, none, ever**. "Reads a junction like
   somebody who has been doing it fifteen years" was out of his mouth on the
   sheet's own terms as well as the house rule's.

Before:

> Her father drinks some of the coffee and tells her to carry on. By the end of
> July she can drive, and the clutch is the part that takes her longest, and at
> dinner he tells her mother, laughing, that she reads a junction like somebody
> who has been doing it fifteen years and pulls away from a stop sign like a
> child.

After:

> Her father drinks some of the coffee and tells her to carry on.
>
> On the last Sunday of July she comes down to the stop sign at the bottom of
> the reservoir road with the whole junction called before the car ahead of her
> has finished clearing it: the van on the left, the cyclist tucked in behind
> the van, the width of the gap after him. Then she brings the clutch up and the
> car bucks twice and stalls square in the middle of the road, and she has it
> started and moving again while the driver behind her is still deciding about
> the horn. Her father laughs into the cup.

It does not duplicate the reservoir-road scene: that one is him teaching and her
answering a question he asked, and this one is her driving with nobody prompting
her. The junction read is reported in a list rather than quoted, so the chapter
does not get a second "That truck, what's he doing?" exchange. The father's
pride and amusement, which were the only other content of the dinner beat,
stay: he laughs into the cup, which `characters/DAVE.md` gives as his register
for it ("amusement shows up as a suppressed physical beat, a laugh into a
glass"), and he says nothing, which is his other one. Nothing in the paragraph
tells the reader what the contrast means. The grandmother's "a week later" in
the next paragraph still lands correctly against the last Sunday of July.

The passage near the top of the chapter where Voss introduces the turret and
gives the tool-not-a-weapon saying was not touched.

Cost: +49 words, chapter at 3700, inside the band; reading grade unmoved at 8.9.
