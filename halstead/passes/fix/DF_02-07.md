## Item 1. ch5, "three questions backed up behind it"

Author: *"that is actually bad questions, why are those the questions a 7 yo has in
this situation?"*

**Before**

> Chloe's own fork stops halfway to her mouth, three questions backed up behind it:
> what he looked like, how long he was in there, who found him.

**After**

> Chloe's own fork stops halfway to her mouth, three questions backed up behind it:
> whether anybody went into the stairwell after him, whether he had to say it out
> loud to somebody before they would let him go, and how long it was between the
> saying and his mom being at the door.

The old three were a reporter's questions: appearance, duration, discovery. Nothing
in them is a six-year-old, and nothing in them is this six-year-old. What she wants
in that chair is the mechanism of leaving, which is what CHLOE.md says she always
wants, plus the one thing a child at that table actually feels, which is whether
Owen was left alone in the stairwell.

The second question does double work: saying the true thing out loud in front of
people is exactly her own injury from chapter one, so wanting to know whether Owen
had to do it is her asking about herself without knowing she is.

**Payoff.** The third question gets answered by the chapter, quietly, at the
Owen-becomes-a-pattern beat. I changed one clause there so the corridor carries the
elapsed time:

> Before: a door standing open in the afternoon, a mattress with the sheets gone.
> After: a door that was shut at breakfast standing open in the afternoon, a
> mattress with the sheets gone.

Nothing points at the answer. The first two need no payoff: they are the kind a
child holds at a table and never gets to ask, and Ruth has just been told off for
answering the last one.

## Item 3. ch2, the sign and the picture

Author: *"This should be something Chloe thinks as funny. Shows her intellect."*

**Before**

> There is also a sign that says NO CELL PHONES with a picture of a phone beneath it
> and a red line through the phone: if you can read the sign the picture is
> redundant, and if you can't read the sign the picture won't help you either.

**After**

> There is also a sign that says NO CELL PHONES with a picture of a phone beneath it
> and a red line through the phone. The picture must be there for the people who
> can't read the sign, and the people who can't read the sign are looking at a phone
> with a line on it. Chloe puts her chin down on the arm of the chair. It is still
> funny a minute later, when she is looking at something else.

Three changes. The colon-and-aphorism shape is what made it the narrator's joke, so
it goes. "Must be" is the tell that a mind is inferring rather than a narrator
asserting; the sentence is free indirect and the mind is hers. And the joke lands
physically: she puts her chin down, and it keeps being funny after she has looked
away, which is what a private six-year-old laugh actually does. The word "redundant"
also went, which was never her word.

I did not have her explain why it is funny. CHLOE.md: she says a thing once and
lets someone else argue with it.

The thought now runs first, unattributed and in the present tense, landing as her

she does when somebody's answer and their voice are telling two different stories"

lie now is her mom talking to the windshield, Chloe watching the side of her face,
the thought about the number, and the thumbnail. Four pieces of evidence, no verdict.

## Item 5. ch6, "Then the arithmetic arrives, unbidden"

Author: *"Kinda, that is a bad phrase I agree, but Chloe still does the math. It
needs to show that she is aware of the consequences already, but she is accepting
them."*

**Before**

> Then the arithmetic arrives, unbidden: four hours in the car each way, [...]

**After**

> Chloe runs the numbers again, the same ones she has been running since the drive
> down: four hours in the car each way, a whole day of driving to put her somewhere
> and a whole day to collect her at the end of it, twice over in a year, before
> anybody counts a visit in between, and her mom at the counter with her hand flat
> on her forehead, and the woman at the district office telling her to be careful.

The list is untouched; only the frame changed. "Arrives, unbidden" made the sum an
event that happened to her, and made it new. "Runs the numbers again, the same ones
she has been running since the drive down" makes her the one doing it and makes it
old news, which is the awareness the author asked for, stated as a fact about her
habit rather than as a gloss on her state.

The acceptance is already dramatized and needed nothing added: Sam says
"obviously," she says "obviously," and she holds on to the pyjama leg until the
lights go out. I resisted adding a line to the effect that she wanted it anyway.
That line would have been House Rule 1 exactly.

## Item 6. ch7, "the tone that she uses when she already knows the answer"

Author: *"Chloe hears the tone, and needs to describe it, but that is also a bad way
to describe it. But I also don't know how to do it better, but I notice that in the
book all the time. IDK if it can be done well?"*

**Before**

> Coming down for water, Chloe gets the end of one of the calls, and her mom is at
> the counter with her hand flat on it, her voice gone careful and extra polite, the
> tone she uses when she already knows the answer and has to hear it again anyway.

### Diagnosis first

The construction is "the tone she uses when X," and X is always a mental state. It
fails three times over:

1. It hands the reader the conclusion before the evidence. Nothing is left to hear.
2. It is unfalsifiable inside the fiction. Chloe cannot perceive "she already knows
   the answer"; she can only perceive sound. Any sentence in which she perceives a
   mental state directly is the narrator wearing her as a glove.
3. Here it is also duplicated. Four lines later the mom says, out loud, "No, I know
   what the policy is, you've told me what the policy is." The gloss was telling the
   reader something the scene was about to say in dialogue.

Point 3 is worth keeping in mind book-wide, because it may be the general case: this
construction tends to appear directly above the beat that would have proved it. If
so, the fix is usually deletion, not replacement.

### Attempt A, physical signal only

> Coming down for water, Chloe gets the end of one of the calls, and the sentence
> she arrives on has two pleases in it. Her mom is at the counter with one hand flat
> on it.

Delete the reading and print only what reaches Chloe's ear. Two pleases in one
sentence is the whole of "careful and extra polite," rendered as a count, and
counting is a thing a seven-year-old does. The meaning arrives four lines later from
the mom's own mouth.

Cost: it is thin on its own. Nothing marks Chloe as having heard anything, so on a
fast read the detail can pass as scene-setting.

### Attempt B, recognition by precedent

> Her mom is at the counter with one hand flat on it, in the voice from the stairs
> in July, the one that spelled Halstead twice for the district office.

Chloe identifies the tone by matching it to an instance, not by naming a state. This
is closest to how a child actually reads a parent, and it is honest about her
epistemics: she has no theory of her mother's mind, she has a catalogue of times she
has heard this. The July call in chapter three is the perfect antecedent, since it
ended in "thank you, and I do appreciate it" and produced nothing.

Cost: it spends reader memory from four chapters back, and it makes the beat about
chapter three for a second instead of about this call. Good technique, wrong spot.
Worth keeping in the toolbox for the later instances the author says he notices,
especially anywhere the antecedent is within a chapter or two.

### Attempt C, reading proven by action (adopted)

> Coming down for water, Chloe gets the end of one of the calls, and the sentence
> she arrives on has two pleases in it. Her mom is at the counter with one hand flat
> on it, and Chloe stops in the doorway with the glass still empty.

A plus behavior. The narrator asserts nothing about anybody's state; Chloe's reading
is proved by what she does with it, which is come down for water and not get any.
The scene already ends with the mom turning and finding her there, so the empty
glass frames the whole call and the reader has her standing through it.

I changed the closing line by four words so the frame closes cleanly rather than
repeating the staging:

> Before: until she turns and Chloe is in the doorway with the glass.
> After: until she turns and finds Chloe still in the doorway.

### The general answer, since the author asked whether it can be done at all

Yes, and there is a rule that comes out of these three:

**Give the sound, then give what the listener does about it. Never give the state.**

The tone is a fact about the air; the meaning is a fact about the listener; the
mental state behind it belongs to nobody in the room and is the narrator's alone.
Two of the three are available in POV and are enough.

Practically, when the phrase "the tone she uses when..." appears, one of these will
be true:

- The proof is already nearby in dialogue or action. Delete the gloss, change
  nothing else. This was the case here, and I suspect it is the common case.
- Chloe has heard the tone before in this book. Name the earlier occasion (B).
- Neither. Then find the physical fact she is reading it off, and give her one small
  action she takes because of it (C). If no action suggests itself, the beat may not
  need the tone at all.

A useful test: strike the clause and read the surrounding page. If nothing is lost,
it was talking to the reader. In this instance nothing was lost.
