# Flesch-Kincaid pass, chapters 19-22

Owner scope: `chapters/19_sixteen.md`, `20_the_parking_lot.md`, `21_the_applications.md`,
`22_the_offer.md`. Following `passes/FK_BRIEF.md` including its addendum, and
`passes/WHY_HIGHER.md` including the appended corrections.

Method, in order of how much each moved the number:

1. **Sentence-count reduction, not word addition.** The derivative is brutal: at
   ch19's starting shape, adding a word buys 0.002 grades and removing a sentence
   boundary buys 0.037. Everything below is merges first.
2. **Spelling out the logical joint** (because / so that / whereas / which meant /
   since), which is what makes a merge legitimate rather than a splice.
3. **Cataloguing** where the draft named a category and left the members implicit.
4. **Precise nouns** where the draft reached for the general word: proctoring,
   invigilating a paper, ignition, sabotage, sodium lamp, numerical marks, corridor,
   photograph, duplicated, interval.

No em dashes or curly quotes introduced. The pre-existing em dashes in ch20 and ch22
sit inside protected or untouched spans and were left alone.

The chapter is 42% dialogue and its spoken mean was already at the floor of 16 when I
started, so per the addendum I left every spoken line over 16 alone. The narration
carried the pass: 88 narration sentences down to 66 in the first round, mostly by
merging dialogue-tag beats into the surrounding action and by writing in the joint
the draft had left to inference.

Representative merges:

> He lays her own paper next to the scheme. A tick sits by her second line. The four
> rows below it are empty.

> He lays her own paper down beside the scheme, where a single tick sits against her
> second line and the four rows underneath it are as empty as they were in April.

> Sam is up in half a second and talks for four minutes without stopping for air, but
> Chloe goes up the following week and stops twice inside the first thirty seconds.
> Both times she can hear the scene three lines out, she wants to be at the good line

> Sam is up in half a second and talks for four minutes without stopping for air,
> while Chloe goes up the following week and stops twice inside the first thirty
> seconds, because both times she can hear the scene three lines out and wants to be
> standing at the good line already instead of walking through the ordinary one to get
> to it.

(The second sentence of the original was a comma splice; the merge fixes it and
removes a boundary at the same time.)

Six dialogue joins, all of them places where the second sentence was already an
elaboration of the first and the joint was merely unwritten:

> Whoever marks this already knows all of it. It's contract law, not a mystery, and
> there's exactly one rule that could possibly apply...

> Whoever marks this already knows all of it, because it's contract law and not a
> mystery, and there's exactly one rule that could possibly apply...

The spoken mean went 16.0 to 17.5, inside the corpus HIGH band (12.7-18.0) and well
short of the 22.8 the addendum flags as the failure case. No line that was already
over 16 was lengthened.

**Word count is the tight constraint here, not the grade.** 3,383 against the 3,400
ceiling. Spelling out joints costs words; I paid for some of it by deleting a repeated
motif (the "hands flat and still at her sides" beat appeared twice within forty lines
and is now folded into the second instance only) and by trimming the flabbier of my own
additions in round three. There is no room left in this chapter.

**Known cost:** relative-clause rate went 22.5% to 33.1%, over the corpus band of
14-26. Some of that is mechanical (the metric counts any sentence containing
who/which/that, so it rises with sentence length alone), but not all of it: round one
produced ten "which is" clauses in a 3,400-word chapter, which is a tic. Round three
converted six of them to appositives and participials. Four remain, which I judged the
right number. "and" rate is 3.5% against a 3.3% median; I removed seven coordinators
in round three and stopped short of hunting the rest, because the alternatives were
worse sentences.

**Did not reach 9.0, and cannot.** The number below is the arithmetic, not an excuse.

### What the protected zone costs, exactly

`passes/fix/D19-22.md` fixes the protected span as everything from
`"Phones. Watches. Come on."` through `"You beat up seven people who couldn't tell you
their own address."` I measured that span separately, before and after, and did not
touch a character of it. I also left the paragraph immediately before it (`The men come
out four blocks later...`) alone: the named boundary excludes it, but it is the fight's
opening frame and four sentences are not worth the risk of editing prose the author
rewrote by hand.

| | words | sentences | w/sent | syl/word | own F-K |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| protected span (frozen) | 1,194 | 104 | 11.5 | 1.261 | **3.76** |
| open remainder (now) | 2,153 | 80 | 26.9 | 1.327 | **10.56** |
| chapter | 3,347 | 184 | 18.2 | 1.303 | 6.87 |

The frozen span is 36% of the chapter's words but **57% of its sentences**. It reads 3.8
on its own. That is the whole problem: F-K is words-per-sentence, and more than half of
this chapter's sentence boundaries are nailed down.

**How far the remainder can actually go.** It is now at 26.9 words per sentence. For
scale, Black Beauty — the corpus book that reaches 8.0 on sentence length alone — runs
22.9, and Wharton runs 20.1. The open two-thirds of this chapter is already written
longer than any book in the corpus and reads 10.6 in isolation. Continuing to merge
there produces sentences that are long because they were welded, which is the exact
failure the brief names in rule 1.

**What that leaves the chapter at.** Holding the frozen 104 sentences and 1,194 words
constant, and letting the open remainder run to the 3,400-word ceiling:

| open remainder written at | chapter w/sent | chapter F-K |
|---|---|---|
| 27 w/sent, 1.33 syl (where it is now) | 18.2 | 6.87 |
| 30 w/sent, 1.36 syl, at the word ceiling | 19.3 | 7.35 |
| 35 w/sent, 1.40 syl, at the word ceiling | 20.4 | 8.00 |

The last row is not a plan. 35 words a sentence sustained across two thirds of a chapter
is longer than anything in the corpus by half again, and 1.40 syllables per word is
Wharton's vocabulary in a scene about waffles and a shopping cart. **The honest ceiling
for this chapter, with the fight and the speech frozen, is about 7.3, and 6.9 is where
it lands without straining.** If the protection is ever lifted, that span at even 16
words per sentence would put the chapter at 8.4 on its own.

### What was done in the open two thirds

Sentence count in the open zone: 118 to 80. Word count there: 1,727 to 2,153.

The four-way preparation was a category with its members already listed as four separate
sentences; it is now one catalogue sentence, which is the Black Beauty / Sea Rat move:

> Over eleven days, Nadia stands next to a maintenance supervisor... and walks away with
> his badge copied. Chloe gets into the corridor cameras and sets them looping. Ruth
> brings the box she built at thirteen... Sam clocks the night patrol's second pass four
> nights running...

> Over eleven days the preparation divides itself four ways: Nadia stands next to a
> maintenance supervisor in the dining hall on a Tuesday, talks about the weather for
> forty seconds, and walks away with his badge duplicated; Chloe gets into the corridor
> cameras and sets them looping on a stretch of empty hallway; Ruth brings the box she
> built at thirteen, the one that finds video recorders on the networks around it and
> logs in with the passwords they shipped with; and Sam clocks the night patrol's second
> pass four nights running, until the interval between it and the first is regular enough
> to walk through at an ordinary speed.

Dialogue in the diner and the walk home took thirteen joins, all of them a claim and its
reason sitting as two sentences with the "because" unwritten:

> "He tells anyone who asks, straight out. It's not a secret to him, it's just a dent."

> "He tells anyone who asks, straight out, because it's not a secret to him, it's just a
> dent."

Nadia's "Go back and fight the waitress" was left verbatim, as in the previous pass.

Because sentence count is dominated by the frozen span, added words are unusually
valuable in this chapter (each one buys three times what it buys in ch19), so this is the
one chapter where I deliberately spent the word budget: 426 words, all of it subordination
and two closing generalizations of the kind `WHY_HIGHER` section 6.4 asks for at the high
points. 3,347 against the 3,400 ceiling.

**Two metrics moved the wrong way and both are mechanical.** Spoken 1-3-word lines went
16.8% to 18.8% and the spoken mean is still 12.8, because sixteen of the chapter's short
spoken lines live inside the frozen span; removing longer spoken sentences from the open
zone by merging them raises the frozen span's share of what is left. Negative space went
4.1% to 4.3% for the same reason — eight of the ten negative-beat sentences are frozen,
and the denominator shrank. I repaired the two that were mine to repair (one I had
introduced in round one, one pre-existing in the hither argument) to keep it under the
cap; it is 4.3% with no further headroom available outside the frozen span.

**At target, and back under the ceiling.** This chapter was 267 words over the 3,400
limit before the pass started, so it is the one chapter where the arithmetic pointed the
same direction as the brief: cutting a sentence boundary and cutting words both raise
words-per-sentence, and this chapter needed both. 205 sentences to 151, 3,667 words to
3,396.

The chapter is only 18% dialogue, so the narration carried almost all of it. The
strongest single move was cataloguing paragraphs that were already lists written as
fragments:

> Everybody applies the same way. The Ivies first, eight of them, the way you buy a
> lottery ticket and then forget you own it. Then the college one of your parents went
> to. Then the community college an hour from home... Then one strange one...

> Everybody applies the same way: the Ivies first, eight of them, the way you buy a
> lottery ticket and forget you own it; then the college one of your parents went to;
> then the community college an hour from home, quietly listed by everybody and named
> out loud by almost no one; then one strange one, picked for whatever reason a
> seventeen-year-old picks things, so that Bex applies to a place in Vermont because her
> cousin says the food is unbelievable, and Marek to one in Arizona because a woman he
> watched lecture online teaches there.

Five sentences to one, three words shorter.

**I overshot and corrected back down, deliberately.** After the merge pass the chapter
read 10.4 at 25.8 words a sentence, and the damage showed in the metrics that are not
F-K: sentences-per-paragraph had fallen to 1.5, meaning nearly every paragraph had become
a single sentence, and the 20-35-word band had collapsed to 14.6% because everything was
now longer than 35. I restored thirteen sentence breaks at the places where the merge had
been a bare "and" rather than a real logical joint, which put the chapter at 9.09 with
sentences-per-paragraph back at 1.8 and the 20-35 band at 22.5%. The merges that stayed
are the ones carrying "because", "while", "so that", "since" or a colon.

**Where the 271 words came from.** Mostly compression of phrasing rather than deletion of
material: "in about nine minutes after lights-out" to "after lights-out", "polite and a
little embarrassed, the way people get when they think they've caught somebody else's
mistake" to "...a mistake", and so on across roughly forty places. Three real cuts:
the Chicago locked-door paragraph lost its second half (the Duke rep finding the same
door), which was a third statement of a point the Sport/Athletics/Recreation runner
already makes twice; Ruth's "That's not an answer" pushback in the essay-Sunday scene came
out, folding Kavi's two turns into one; and Sam's list of who got what lost two
appositives. The essay-Sunday scene was where the previous dialogue pass had added the
most, so it is the right place to give some back.

Spoken mean went 13.0 to 15.1, just under the floor of 16, and 1-3-word spoken lines fell
from 7.8% to 2.5%. The institutional-terseness thread ("Sport." / "Athletics." /
"Recreation." and the escort's "No.") was left alone, as in the previous pass.

**Known cost:** relative-clause rate 28.8% to 34.4%, and "and" rate 3.1% to 3.6%. Both
are consequences of 22.5-word sentences; I took the thirteen sentences back apart partly
to hold them down, and stopped there.

"Here?" / "Here." / "Reason?", Dave's "Huh." and his triple question, Chloe's enumerated
read-back of the four-part offer, the repeated "Staying keeps that question open", and

Because sentence count fell by 43 while the word budget had room, this is the chapter
where I spent words on the second technique rather than only on merges: about 300 words
of spelled-out joints and one added catalogue member, e.g.

> His own contracts class still meets four times that week, the interviews slotted into
> whatever's left of the day around it.

> His own contracts class still meets four times that week, the interviews slotted into
> whatever's left of the day around it, because the timetable was set in September and a
> timetable set in September is the one thing in this building an offer of a hundred and
> thirty five thousand dollars a year cannot move.

Following `WHY_HIGHER` section 6.4, three of the additions are the narrator generalizing
at a high point rather than only reporting: the folder tab that is "either a symmetry or
a warning and she has fifteen minutes to decide which", the interview ending "in the time
it takes the runner to reach the top of the stairs", and the last line's "a decision can
be finished and still take up the whole of the evening behind it." I wrote and then
removed a fourth ("the entire ceremony eleven years earns") because it duplicates an
existing line in chapter 23, which I do not own.

Negative space actually improved, 3.8% to 2.8%: two of the six negative-beat sentences
were rewritten to the same content without the trigger (Kavi's "nobody in the building
could tell me... because nobody had checked it yet" became "the whole building had to
wait to find out whether it was wrong, because it was still unchecked").

**One metric moved the wrong way.** Spoken 1-3-word lines went 17.0% to 18.6%, for the
same mechanical reason as ch20: merging longer spoken sentences shrinks the denominator
while the protected one-word lines stay. I removed one of them ("Research." folded into
"Research, full time, ..."), and stopped, because every remaining one is on the previous
pass's list of lines backed by a character sheet or a deliberate callback.
