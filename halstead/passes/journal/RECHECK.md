# Re-check of issues against the author's replies

Method: I read all 36 chapters in order, then tested each reply against the text. Verdicts are one of: Author right, drop it / Author partly right / Still stands / Intent not on the page / Explained. "Still stands (author agrees)" means the author conceded the issue, so it still needs a fix. Chapter numbers are the chapter files. No rewritten prose is offered, only descriptions of fixes.

**1. Two camp goodbyes** - Still stands
Ch 6 ends with the hall goodbye ("see you at the thing in the fall", Kavi's hand) and the drive home. Ch 7 then reopens on the same last morning with "Sam left early" and Kavi gone, plus a second Ruth hug and a second "we're all getting in" promise at the flagpole. Two consecutive chapters disagree about one morning, so it is not minor; the cheapest fix is to cut ch 7's opening beats or move the whole farewell into one chapter.

**2. Mrs. Prahl at camp** - Still stands
The issue is not two people sharing a name. In ch 7 (mid-September 2012) Chloe tells the librarian "Mrs. Prahl kept ours on the windowsill", but the camp teacher was Mr. Doyle, and Chloe does not hear of any Mrs. Prahl until her mother names her in ch 8. A reader will take it as a slip or as the same person, so change the name in ch 7.

**3. Camp size** - Intent not on the page
Ch 3 repeats "over a hundred" and ch 4's narration says "a hundred-odd children", then ch 10 says "of the ninety who were at camp", with nothing saying the first number was the school's boast. Ch 3 does give a sense that something is off (no news, no forums, a website only the school wrote), so a reader can feel a lie but not see one; note too that the page says the school is "open four years", not three. The minimum is one noticing beat (Kavi, who counts everything, saying the real count differs from the letter and the phone call) and ch 4's narration should not state "hundred-odd" as fact. Your "most come back eventually" fits ch 11's "ninetieth of ninety" but ch 10 says only "twenty-some came back that first fall", so a clause saying more arrive over time would help.

**4. School size** - Author partly right
Growth from one class to twelve is a sound idea, but the page numbers do not fit it yet. By ch 15 (2017) there should be several younger cohorts, so "two hundred of us" reads low beside ninety-one in Chloe's year, while "eight hundred children who are not graduating" in ch 23 averages about 73 a year, which is plausible. Also ch 12 (2014) has ten- and eleven-year-old sheets "running in this building this year", which clashes with ch 16's claim that her cohort is the oldest. Fix the 2017 number and ch 12's older-year classes together.

**5. Owen** - Intent not on the page
Ch 5 shows your intent well: Owen writes two sentences, "I've lost the thread of it", and is short of the span. Ch 10 then undoes it: Kavi says Owen "was keeping up", doing "the water thing and the bridge" fine, and left on a Saturday after crying by the kitchens, while ch 5 has him leave on a Tuesday after crying in the stairwell, and no water thing exists in ch 5. Nothing marks Kavi's and Ruth's versions as the children's wrong guesses (Kavi says "something I saw with my own eyes"); if Chloe, who watched Owen struggle, noticed the accounts did not match, the reader would get your point.

**80. Children talk like essayists** - Still stands (author agrees)
Six-year-old Chloe says "it's exactly like sending a package to a person you have yet to meet" (ch 7), and by the late chapters nearly everyone argues in the same form: "That's not an answer, that's..." turns up in ch 13, 15, 24 and 26. Keep a few strong lines and break the shared formula.

**81. Mandarin and "all around 180"** - Author partly right
Mandarin novels in five months fit the pace the book sets everywhere (Latin, German, Russian), so that claim is not the problem; 180 is. On the usual SD 15 scale 180 is about 1 in 20 million, so the US (about four million births a year) would expect roughly 0.2 a year and the whole world six or seven, not ninety-one in one cohort, and tests do not measure reliably above about 160. Ninety-one a year drawn "not one town, not one state" (ch 3) is about 1 in 44,000, around 160, close to your "dropout range" (1 in 31,000, roughly 125 US children a year), so the premise works at that level and not at 180. The book need not explain where they come from, since ch 31 has Ruth ask the question herself and ch 21 has admissions officers puzzle over it, but keep 180 off the page and do not commit to an explanation the book has not given.

**82. Laptop joke** - Author right, drop it
Ch 24 carries your explanation on the page: "the MEMORY is meant to be 1tb", "sixteen doesnt even compare to what everyone actually runs", "the guy thought i was doing a bit at him", and "they'll only sell to current students or staff". One small snag: "i just needed something to type on" sits oddly with her shock.

**83. One-note outside world** - Author partly right
There is more variety than the reviewer said: the Army major who corrects Sam, the registrar and bank manager who push back, Deb, and hostile skilled people (the scam crew, the nine-minute intruder). What is still missing is an outsider who is clever, not on their side, and wins even one exchange on the page: the registrar and bank manager give way, the scammers lose, the 75 men are "fine" but outclassed, and the intruder is never seen. One such person would give the gap an edge.

**84. Ch 10-19 as a map** - Author partly right
Your purpose is achieved: by ch 12 a reader knows the school is languages, sport, craft and weapons. But after about three examples a year the extra items do the same job, and many follow one shape (she misses something, isolates the cause, writes it in a notebook: chemistry gray and white, the textile shirts, the Mandarin tones, the wood joint, the forge scroll, the clamp spec). Compress within chapters rather than cutting chapters: keep what pays off (the Nerf, paint, archery, rifle chain; the 10v1; the bread test; the forge; the chat's origin) and shrink one-offs such as the text renderer, Ruth's synthesizer, a third failure-and-fix in a chapter and the art-history digression. Ch 10 to 19 total about 43,500 words, a third of the book, so a 15 to 20 percent cut there is roughly 6,500 to 8,700 words.

**85. Bex taking credit** - Author partly right
Your point (status goes to whoever announces first) lands by ch 13, when Bex tells Bell the paintball discovery and he writes it "under her name". By my count the beat then recurs in ch 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 23 and 30, more than the seven or eight reported. Keep the origin (12), the Bell board (13), the bar list (19, the cruelest), the confrontation (21) and Ruth's tally (22), and fold ch 14, 16 and 17 into one-line references; ch 14 also muddies the pattern by having her credit Sam.

**86. Iyad's rumour beat** - Author partly right
Iyad's beats in ch 12, 13, 16, 17 and 18 (the ledger and Aurel) are staged almost identically: far-end volume, the table laughing, Chloe silenced. The Aurel rumour in ch 18 is the one that changes her behaviour (she "stops using the language-block stairs"), and ch 17's backwards teaching is next; the versions in ch 12, 13 and 16 could shrink to one. His roster use in ch 22 is a different job and fine.

**87. Retellings** - Still stands (author agrees)
Camp is retold at length in ch 6 and again in ch 7; ch 22 gives the offer's list from Amberg, then to her mother, then word for word to her father; ch 19's mark-scheme point is made four or five times (Amberg, Kavi, Nadia, the forklift answer, the filled-in paper). Trim each to one full telling and a short echo.

**88. Duplicate scenes** - Author partly right
Several repeats do different work: Dana asking "How's school?" in ch 1, 8 and 23 is a three-step build, the three library arguments set up the no-card library, and "Sport / Athletics / Recreation" in ch 21 is fresh. The weaker ones are the near-identical family-explaining scenes (the ch 14 Christmas and ch 15 Thanksgiving), Voss and Kowalczyk both saying "work it out yourself" in ch 18, the second printing of the Watch sheet in ch 15, chat statistics rounds three and four in ch 28 (which ch 31 then lists again), and Nadia's bank scene in ch 24.

**89. Dead weight** - Author partly right
Several have a payoff you are not counting: the stock count is the punishment ch 15's Thanksgiving and ch 16's "next Saturday of tomatoes" use, Eli's bounty pays off in his five vulnerabilities in ch 34, the waitress joke is Sam's "who do I actually fight" beat, Hark's lending leads into Iyad's ledger scene, and Sam's girlfriend is brief and fine once the wall line is fixed. The ones with no payoff are the choir, the text renderer, the bank scene, the third night in ch 26 and the third gap in ch 35. Cut those and leave the rest.

**90. Chloe sidelined** - Author partly right
The chapter titles in the last third ("Nadia", "Ruth") and the order (Sam, Nadia, Theo, Ruth, then Priya) show the ensemble shift, so the intent is visible. The cost is that Chloe's one decisive act, writing the stop clause and "noticed" definition in ch 33, is what the group leans on in ch 35 while she sleeps, so the reader loses their anchor at the climax. One active choice for her in ch 35 or 36 would make the outgrowing read as design.

**91. Genre shift at ch 24** - Explained
The "conspiracy" is the set of open questions the last third rests on: who funds Halstead, who has watched it since 2013, who stops the worm for nine minutes, and why the government now hunts Priya. It is signposted earlier (ch 3's school with no footprint, ch 15's intruders, ch 16's encrypted network, ch 18's NDA and phone lock), but ch 24 spends most of its length on workplace comedy and reaches the pill hook only in its last two sections. Move a hook earlier in ch 24 so the reader sees the turn coming.

**92. Ending resolves nothing** - Author partly right
An open ending is fine for a first book if the reader leaves with one thing closed. Chloe's belonging arc does close: ch 36 ends with the whole group offering Priya practical help, which answers ch 1's girl who could not be known. But Chloe's last on-page act is a deleted paragraph and an unseen one-line message, so the last chapter reads as Priya's, not the book's. One quiet beat for Chloe, or a sign this is book one of two, would fix that.

**93. Needs more room** - Still stands (author agrees)
The biggest gaps are the break-in itself, which falls between ch 33 and 34, and any reaction to the 4 a.m. night after ch 15. Chloe's first night at Halstead is shown in ch 10 but briefly, thinner than the camp's first night in ch 4, and the first whole-school Watch (ch 14, "People say afterward...") is told secondhand.

**94. Gap spelled out four times** - Author partly right
The scenes do use different people and metrics (acceptance rates in ch 21, the fitness test in ch 25, hiring in ch 27, the chat statistics in ch 28, MIT in ch 31), and they build toward Ruth's thesis. The repetition is in the recaps: ch 31 lists ch 28's three statistics again and quotes Nadia's and Sam's moments, and ch 21 and 28 state the conclusion in narration. Trim the restating and keep the scenes.

**95. Ch 2 tells her exceptional** - Author right, drop it
The item itself says keep it, and you agree.

**96. Last third lacks weight** - Still stands (author agrees)
Ch 9's weight comes from a face-to-face scene with something at stake. After graduation the friends never meet in person (the ch 24 plan for a weekend before December is never mentioned again), and the strongest later beat, Ruth who "sits on a wall until the light changes twice" in ch 31, is alone. You do not need to match ch 9; one in-person scene between two of them with something to lose would carry more than another chat.

**97. Ch 26 repeats Sam's "real one" beat** - Still stands (author agrees)
Ch 25 has Sam ask Ruiz when the real one starts and get "Tuesday"; ch 26 has him ask the major the same thing and repeat it in the chat. Cut one of the two; ch 25's is the stronger.

**98. Unmarked jumps back** - Author partly right
The chapters carry datelines, so they are marked, but only in small italic headers, and the first lines do not re-anchor. The real jumps back are ch 5 (to week one after ch 4's second week), ch 7 (to the last camp morning after ch 6 ended with the drive home), ch 20 (June 2022 after ch 19 ends in 2023) and ch 31 (October 2023 after ch 30 reaches 2026); ch 25 is a small step back, and ch 36 is a skip forward with a short flashback. Open each on a line that names the time through something in the scene.

**99. Ch 29 cliffhanger dropped** - Explained
Ch 29 ends on Theo's hypothetical and Ruth's "bad", the first thing she has posted in six months. Ch 30 is Chloe's blog, exam and Whitaker interviews (September 2025 to January 2026) and never mentions the chat or Theo's question, and ch 31 goes back to Ruth at MIT in 2023. Ch 32 opens "The financial one takes sixteen weeks" and ch 33 opens "Eli proposes the second worm, but Theo says no", so the decisions to hunt the money and to break in are never shown. A short scene where Theo says what he found and the group decides would close the gap.

**100. Chapters open on place or procedure** - Explained
Ch 14's first paragraphs describe the long field and the building at its end, and the first person (Coach Bell) arrives in paragraph three; ch 15 opens on the forge, ch 17 on marksmanship drill, ch 22 on how Amberg runs the exit interviews, and ch 23 on the graduation rehearsal. A reader returning to the book after a break gets description before there is a person or a want. To fix one, start on a person already in motion at a specific moment (Chloe missing her release on the field, Chloe in the corridor waiting for her slot, Chloe in the stage line), then let the place or procedure arrive in the next lines. Ch 28 and 29 do begin on a person, though in summary, which is a different problem (telling, not showing).

**101. Catalogue blocks without breaks** - Still stands (author agrees)
Ch 12 and 18 are the heaviest: ch 12 runs chemistry, first aid, textiles and biology as one long block, and ch 18 puts surgery, accountancy, Hindi and topology in one paragraph. Breaks between topics, or fewer topics, fix it.

**102. Summary tails** - Still stands (author agrees)
Examples: ch 17 ends with "The calls home run twice a week through January..." after a strong scene, ch 21 with her counting letters and putting them in a drawer, ch 25 with "On the Thursday he puts it to somebody else", ch 30 with the clearance and "sworn in that August", and ch 35 with texting Ruth before lunch. Each could end one beat earlier.

**103. Flash-forwards** - Still stands (author agrees)
They are ch 16's "stays outside her reach for years", ch 27's Hanley filings "all go dead in July", ch 30's "sworn in that August, her birthday", and ch 33's "Chloe turns twenty-one in August". Delete as planned.

**104. Roll-call paragraphs** - Explained
A roll call is a paragraph that places each friend, where they are and how they type, before the chat starts: ch 24 does it for eight people, and shorter versions open ch 32, 33, 35 and 36. One is standard and useful when the cast has just been scattered (ch 24 is the first post-graduation chat) or after a long gap. It becomes a problem when it repeats before every chat and when each entry is a tic (Kavi turning an object, Eli tapping, Nadia's sleeves, Theo at a window with the blinds down), because the reader already knows them. Place only the people speaking in the scene, in a clause attached to their line, and only when where they are changes what they say.

**105. Stairs and car devices** - Author partly right
A child listening in is natural, and ch 1's car echoing in ch 6 reads as a deliberate rhyme. But listening recurs in ch 1, 3 (three times plus the fourth stair), 8 (twice) and 9, which is more than a rhyme. Keep the ch 1 and 6 car pair and trim three or four of the listening scenes.

**106. Ch 23 viewpoint drift** - Still stands
In ch 23 "Her mother knows this campus" turns into "Meg says" and "Dave strikes up a conversation" with a stranger, scenes Chloe is not in, with no break or signal. Either end the Chloe scene and mark the shift, or keep the parents' talk to what Chloe sees and hears.

**107. Narrator explains** - Still stands (author agrees)
Examples to cut: ch 21's "because a window like that stops being an advertisement and starts being an accusation", ch 25's "because a test everybody who comes through the door has to take must be one most of the country can pass", and ch 31's "Reading is free. Answering commits her to a version of herself...". Each names what the scene already showed.

**108. Register** - Still stands (author agrees)
Examples: "p-value" (ch 12), "Idempotency key" and "registered agent" (ch 27), S-2 (ch 25). The terms need plain-word equivalents or cuts for a ninth-grade reader.

**109. Long sentences** - Author partly right
Nobody wants Spot-level sentences; the problem is chains, not length. Ch 3's opening mailbox sentence runs about sixty words through six clauses, and ch 22's corridor paragraph starting "Early, she arrives" stacks sight after sight before anything happens. Keep long sentences for continuous action and break the ones that carry reasoning through "so", "which" and "because".

**110. Tics** - Still stands (author agrees)
Cutting about 80 percent is right. "exactly where it was" and its cousins recur (ch 18, 19), quarter turns turn up in ch 19, 20, 21 and 30, sleeves going up in ch 21, 22, 23 and 33, "yet to" in ch 7, 9, 14 and 16, and the Bex and Iyad "volume" phrases in ch 12, 13, 14, 16, 18 and 19.

**111. Inverted openers** - Author partly right
They are few, not pervasive, but the odd ones stand out: "On she goes to the next thing on her list" (ch 27), "Anyway she writes the names down" (ch 24), "Into the drawer of the lectern he puts it" (ch 18) and "Sitting down, in a chair, she pushes her sleeves up" (ch 22). Fix those and leave the rest.

**112. "but" without contrast** - Still stands (author agrees)
Examples: "in under an hour, but then it's Friday" (ch 8) and "a tissue out early, but her father keeps a hand on the back of her chair" (ch 23). A read-through for "but" will find the rest.

**113. Too many names** - Author partly right
Your core cast is about fourteen, but the book names far more, often for one scene (teachers, classmates, staff, relatives). The sharper problem is reused names for different people: two Debs (ch 5 swimming, ch 28 coworker), two Fens (see 11), two Tomases (ch 16, ch 27), two Vances (ch 7 teacher, ch 26 corporal) and the two Aymars. Rename those and cut names that appear once.

**114. The slur** - Author partly right
A sixteen-year-old using it in 2022 is realistic, and ch 36's callback only works if the reader remembers ch 20. But nothing in ch 20 signals that the book sees it as a lapse by a careful character (nobody reacts), and in ch 36 Priya repeats it as a joke at Chloe's expense in a book aimed at ninth graders. If the lapse is meant to be seen, give it a reaction in ch 20, and let ch 36's callback carry the meaning without repeating the word.

**115. Dialogue too finished** - Still stands (author agrees)
Ben in ch 2 ("a composite that averages two very different pictures into a single score") and Dave in ch 22 (the long number-and-pension speech) give polished speeches where a doctor or parent would speak in pieces. Split or trim, and add interruption and false starts.
