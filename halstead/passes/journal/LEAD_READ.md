## Smaller things worth a look

- "an whole" appears six times (ch 6 x3, ch 11, ch 14 x2) in the mouths of different characters, so it reads as a typo rather than one person's voice.
- Ch 8: the sentence "So is the third, except that Mrs. Prahl puts them in pairs" sits after the jacket fight and has nothing for "So is" to refer to. The Thursday sequence there reads like a paragraph got moved.
- Ch 8: "I keep getting the bridge and the apples". Apples have not been mentioned yet at that point.
- Ch 17: "He's got a lake, Kavi. A lake and a boat shed." does not connect to anything around it.
- Ch 15 says the 4 a.m. watch is "the first one nobody posted beforehand"; ch 13 already said unannounced drills happen "every few weeks".
- Ch 34 uses "car park" and "shopping trolley" inside an American government report. Fine if the analyst is British; otherwise it reads as the narrator's word, not the file's.
- Ch 30: the Foreign Service path in ch 23 lists an oral assessment; ch 30 goes from written exam straight to background check to clearance.

## A prose pattern the measures may have caused

A lot of sentences have been turned around so they do not open with "She/He + verb", and some came out stiff:

- "Anyway she writes the names down, a name to a line" (ch 24)
- "Again she squares the statements against the edge of his desk." (ch 24)
- "On she goes to the next thing on her list." (ch 27)
- "Into the drawer of the lectern he puts it" (ch 18)
- "Filing the edge." as a whole sentence (ch 14)
- "Flipping to the back of the invoice, she finds that hand has already done the arithmetic." (ch 23)
- "Sitting down, in a chair, she pushes her sleeves up." (ch 22)

CLAUDE.md says an earlier pass pushed `sentence opens She/He + verb` way over target. These look like the fix for that going too far the other way. Inverted openings are as easy to spot as repeated "She" openings, and they sound less like a person talking.

There is also a "but" that joins two clauses with no contrast between them, e.g. ch 23: "she asks sixty-one employers, in person, ... but nine of them agree".

## Overall, briefly

- The first nine chapters are the strongest stretch. The library-limit arguments, "Nobody was mean", and the "please, take my birthday" scene in ch 9 do the most with the least.
- The best structural move in the book is ch 15 (the 4 a.m. drill) paying off in ch 29 (Theo's file). It works because the only hint in ch 15 is Chloe's unease about the wrong bolt cutters and boots, and the chapter lets her drop it.
- The Bex-takes-credit beat happens at least six times (ch 12, 13, 14, 17, 19, 21) with the same shape each time and no change in how Chloe handles it. Iyad's public-gossip beat repeats in a similar way (ch 12, 13, 16, 17, 18). The fifth and sixth instances need to add something or go.
- Chapters 10-19 lean on lists of classes and activities. A teenage reader will likely skim those.
- From ch 24 on, Chloe is mostly a reader of other people's chapters (Sam 25-26, Nadia 27, Theo 29, Ruth 31, Priya 36). That may be the design, but it changes whose book it is.
- Chloe's "that's retarded" (ch 20) is quoted back by Priya in ch 36, so it is clearly on purpose. For a 9th-grade readership it is worth being sure it is a choice the author wants to keep.
- Open threads at the end: who "he" is, the nine-minute gaps, Priya's attackers, the sinus pill, Theo's "name I already know", Sandoval's second agreement, the knuckle set "in a corridor at midnight", Owen, Ruth's brother starting at Halstead. Some are clearly meant for a sequel; Owen and the knuckle read as forgotten.
