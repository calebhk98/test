# Pacing read

Lens: where each chapter moves or drags, scene versus summary, what can be cut, and what later chapters depend on.

## Chapter 01: Before

Proportion: about 85% scene, 15% summary (the reading-everything paragraph, the "get good at sitting there" paragraph in January).

Moves: the phone call opener, the library four-at-a-time argument, the train-station dinner monologue, the Kayleigh "You talk weird" beat. These are short, specific and carry character.

Drags: this is a long chapter for an opener, and it is built from many small episodes (worksheet, library, dinner, tennis-ball game, Peanut/Icarus, November/December shrinking answers, conferences, doctor, Christmas, sled-dog book, sticky note). Six or seven separate "she is too much for the room" demonstrations make one point. The tennis-ball rules argument and the Icarus/Peanut beat both end in the same lesson (Kayleigh, nobody minds, she goes quiet). The Christmas at grandmother's scene (Dana, the quiet car ride) is the thinnest: it mostly repeats "she stops herself mid-sentence".

Cut/compress candidates:
- Tennis-ball game: could be trimmed by a third or merged into the Peanut beat, since both set up Kayleigh and "you talk weird". Cost later: Kayleigh Burns as a recurring name; check. Small risk.
- Christmas at grandmother's: compress to a few lines. Its one asset is the parents' split ("She was quiet tonight" / "She was fine"). Could be kept in two lines.
- The doctor visit is functional but slightly procedural (pencil line, food-group poster); the referral logic could be shortened.

Too fast: the parents' tension is shown well. Nothing needs more room, though the sticky note ending is a good stop.

Load-bearing: the shrinking of answers to "one word", the "short version", the four-at-a-time rule, the books as comfort, the March 7 note. These set the mask that later chapters have to break.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

Proportion: about 90% scene, 10% summary (the closing "After the appointment, nothing changes" section and June).

Moves: the overheard door conversation (Ben telling her mom the number is "good" and that a bad number is what moves a school) is the best part and the real hinge. The car ride ("What's a percentile?") is tight and quiet. The ice cream on a Wednesday is a good small beat.

Drags: the testing itself, roughly the middle 55% of the chapter. Blocks, similarities, digit span, picture concepts, coding, vocabulary, letter-number sequencing, matrices, comprehension, symbol search, cancellation each get a paragraph or several. For a 9th grader this reads as a run of puzzle episodes with one shape: Chloe is fast, asks a sharp question, then one subtest (coding) makes her feel slow. Only two moments differentiate: "They're the covers for the ends of you" (boot and glove) and the coding page that she is bad at. The "Tell me another reason" sequence is slightly repetitive with the same joke of her giving more of the same.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Roughly a third to a half of the subtest sequence could go: keep blocks (first and the knocked-over block), one similarities question (hour and year), the coding failure, and one "tell me another reason". Drop or fold digit span, letter-number rule, matrices, symbol search, cancellation. Savings: about 600 to 800 words.
- The waiting-room opener (fish tank, handwashing poster, NO CELL PHONES sign) is charming but is two jokes about signs; one would do.

What later depends on it: the diagnosis machinery and "good number is the trouble" logic likely pays off in the next few chapters (the letter, the report). The coding-page shame (slow hand) may pay off later with handwriting or speed; I will check. Dr. Prentice/Ben may recur.

Too fast: the aftermath. "After the appointment, nothing changes" covers March to June in a few paragraphs; given what the test just told her, a single scene of March 8th would earn the summary. Acceptable, though.

## Chapter 03: The Letter

Proportion: about 90% scene, 10% summary (the nine-day stretch, the packing, the drive).

Moves: the envelope with "Miss" on it, the parents arguing at the table, her mom's cross-examining phone call (heard from the stairs). The "It says Miss. I think that's why." exchange is the emotional center and is short.

Drags: the middle, about 40% of the chapter, is the parents' due diligence: district call, the Petersons, the website, the second phone call, then the teacher bios and her mom's solo search, then the nine-day argument. The same information (this is odd, nobody has heard of it, it is free, the staff are unusually good) is delivered four or five times in different rooms. The back-and-forth about whether free things exist ("People give away scholarships") appears twice almost verbatim.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Merge the district call, the Petersons, and her mom's Sunday search into one beat: about 400 words saved. Keep the postmark and "usually pretty good" if they pay off later (the odd list and the "networks" answer feel like seeds for the later files and money chapters; check).
- The librarian scene: it echoes Chapter 1's four-at-a-time argument and works as a callback, but it could be half the length. The "hold, not a checkout" is a cute payoff for ch1.
- The packing/shoe-joke paragraphs are warm but long; one can go.

What later depends: the teacher bios (Olympic swimmer, SEAL) probably name real later characters; the postmark and "four states off" and the "list is usually pretty good" line will matter if the book later pays off who selected Chloe. Parents' suspicion sets up their trust arc.

Too fast: the farewell. Chloe's actual leaving (the drive, the goodbye) gets two compressed paragraphs, and her mom staying at the hotel is summary. For a book that is going to put her away from home, a first goodbye with an actual exchange would earn more than another phone-call.

## Chapter 04: Pluto

Proportion: about 85% scene, 15% summary (the hotel nights, the fourth-night phone call and her mom leaving are semi-summary).

Moves: the Pluto door scene (body reaction, "room-shaped object", the dwarf-planet argument) is the best passage: it introduces Sam, Ruth and the class's argumentative texture in one go and answers the "you talk weird" wound. The volcano/viscosity scene introduces Kavi cleanly and funny. The Ruth interruption scene introduces Mr. Doyle and Chloe watching for punishment that does not come.

Drags: the orientation (laundry bags, fruit bowls, nurse, lights out) is a procedural list, but it also sets the "same day, no argument" promise, and her mom underlining it keeps it alive. Priya's horse monologue runs long; it is a good gag for the first paragraph and then repeats. The first hotel night (her mom talking about her work in June) is a sleepy beat that works.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Orientation: trim by about a third (laundry bags, fruit, lights-out detail). Keep the go-home promise and the nurse.
- Priya's monologue: cut by about half. Cost later: Priya is her roommate and probably recurs; the pony-versus-horse bit may be a callback. Check.
- The fourth-night phone-not-called beat and the mom leaving are both good but overlap; could be one scene.

Too fast: Owen is introduced twice (worksheet under the table, copying the word) but the reader barely learns who he is. Kavi is glimpsed for one scene. The first day is crowded with named kids; Priya, Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Owen in four chapters of space is fine for the book because these are the cast, but the reader may not yet hold them.

Load-bearing: Pluto as a room name, Sam's "and a half", Ruth correcting, Kavi's viscosity, Chloe sitting on her hands in Ruth's wake (the mask still on). All of it seeds the Pluto group.

## Chapter 05: Behind

Proportion: about 80% scene, 20% summary (swimming paragraph, "more leave before the end of the month", the opening on speed).

Moves: the bridge test (square folds, Ruth's triangle snaps at nineteen), the fraction-division night with the rectangle ("Six." then the dark room), and the moon dinner where Chloe notices she has stopped checking her sentences. These are the model for how the middle chapters should work: a small concrete problem, a failure, a private payoff.

Drags: the essay timing paragraph at the top and the extra dowel/glue-gun logistics. The Owen leaving scene is the emotional center but the dinner argument that follows with Ruth runs about one exchange longer than needed ("She asked the table. I was sitting at the table").

Cut/compress candidates:
- The opening speed/timer paragraphs and the in-class essay ("what the man in the story believes"): trim by a third. Marisol appears here and may recur; check.
- The "how mid is mid" exchange with the teacher is cute but optional (about 80 words).
- The Owen dinner exchange: trim Chloe's rapid-fire questions.

Load-bearing: the fraction epiphany (the book's first example of her getting something alone at night, and it is the template for later study chapters), the bridge (square versus triangle, Sam ignoring Ruth, Chloe not saying her idea; later chapters probably replay Chloe finally speaking up), Owen leaving (the cost of the program), tidally locked and "check on every sentence since November" (the mask coming off). All four are payoff material.

Too fast: swimming. Chloe cannot swim; later swimming is likely to matter (Deb, bubbles). It gets two small paragraphs and one Ruth moment. If swimming recurs as a skill arc, it will be summary-only in the first stretch. Note for the later check.

## Chapter 06: The List

Proportion: about 60% scene, 40% summary (the dinner monologue is summary delivered as speech).

Moves: the hallway scene outside Pluto (the form, "a day where they look at you", Ruth's weekly letter, Sam's "you're going to do it twice") is lively and sets the next stakes. The closing car call, with Chloe pretending to sleep and her dad saying "Meg, they taught her CPR", is excellent and short.

Drags: the breakfast room scene is a second dose of "nobody knows what this is" that Chapter 3 already gave, and the adults are unnamed. The dinner is the problem. The author has made it a deliberate list ("Then... Then... Then...") and it reads like one: Gary the CPR dummy, bridge, pool, fractions, Spanish, signing, language puzzle, wood shop, eggs, painting, computers, robot arm, chess, karate, nerf guns, wood shop again. The reader recaps what they already watched in Ch 5 (the bridge, the fractions), then gets about ten new activities named in half a sentence each. This is exactly the "list" complaint, in miniature, and here it is intentionally comic ("goggles again, in almost the words she used the first time").

Cut/compress candidates:
- Breakfast-room scene: cut to a few lines or fold into the car call at the end. Saves about 300 words. Cost later: the "list is a good list" and "bus stayed a rumour" echo Ch 3 only; I do not expect a payoff. Check Chapters 21 and 22 for it.
- Dinner: the bridge and fractions replay can be cut to one line each, since the reader just saw both. Keep the napkin line "the rightness belongs to him and not to you" because it is her thesis about learning. Cut about 500 words of the replay. Keep a shortened version of the list (the comic effect is real) but drop four or five of the items.
- The hotel line about "twenty-seven days": keep.

What later depends: CPR (Gary), Spanish, signing, the language puzzle with Kavi's endings, computers (counting to a hundred, skip every seven), chess, karate. Any of these may be paid off later; the list names them so the later book does not appear to be a time skip. Check each.

Too fast: nothing. The goodbye is brief but well pitched.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room

Proportion: about 65% scene, 35% summary (the waiting for the phone, the library drop-off, the asking-every-day paragraph).

Moves: the last-names scene at the kitchen counter ("First names were the only names anybody used there") is the best thing in the chapter: the reader and Chloe realize at the same moment that she cannot find her friends. The time capsule scene with Kayleigh and Bryce is tight and cruel and gives the chapter its turn. The ending, with her crying at her desk, lands.

Drags: the library scene. Chloe tells the librarian about Gary, the bridge, and the fractions for the third time (dinner in Ch 6 was the second). It is the same sentences. The "Mrs. Prahl kept ours on the windowsill" detail is new but the rest is replay. The waiting-by-the-phone section is long on mechanism (first ring, second ring, the registered-owner-of-the-vehicle call) before getting to one real beat (asking the school to call back). The final September sequence (call about third-grade floor, then the crying) has two good ends but runs three scenes in a row on one idea: Chloe is stuck in the wrong grade.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Library scene: cut the retelling entirely and keep "It's been five weeks, not four" and the four-at-a-time callback. About 200 words saved. Cost later: Mrs. Prahl (a name; check ch 8 onward), and the "the corner, only the corner" detail, which is already in Ch 5 and Ch 6.
- Phone-waiting: compress to a short paragraph plus the day-ten request. About 150 words.
- Second-grade first math sheet (carrying, green marker boxes): could merge with the time capsule intro. It exists to say "I checked all thirty, same as last year", which is Chapter 1's idea again.

What later depends: the time capsule (it is promised to open in fifth grade, so any later reappearance would be a major callback; check), the third-grade policy call (the "only lever" from Ch 2 gets a name; it must connect to the year-school decision), Ms. Vance, and the library card in the battery drawer.

Too fast: the decision. Chloe asks for Halstead "several times a day" through September and the chapter ends with her crying. The parents' reasoning is entirely off-page. Probably fine because the next chapters ("the asking") show it.

## Chapter 08: The Asking

Proportion: about 65% scene, 35% summary (the weeks of asking, the Thursdays after the first one, the bike, January).

Moves: Dr. Ammons asking "Were the same adults with her the whole time" and Chloe echoing "The same" is sharp. The car afterward ("Does that mean I can go to Halstead, then?" and her mom's forehead on the wheel) is a well-turned short beat. Mrs. Prahl's first Thursday ("When does it get harder?") is the chapter's thesis scene. The jacket fight ("I hate you so much") and the "Nobody was mean" bedside talk are the emotional hits.

Drags: the weeks between. The weekday run ("Is it today?" Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday) is a countdown that repeats Chapter 7's asking-every-day paragraph. Dr. Ammons's long list of questions to her mom is slightly procedural before it turns. The Hana-and-dice Thursday and the fourth Thursday are quick but add little beyond "the program is easy and nice". Christmas at grandmother's with Dana is a near duplicate of Chapter 1's Christmas (same cousin, same "How's school", same potatoes).

Cut/compress candidates:
- Christmas at grandmother's (Dana, potatoes): cut. About 60 words. Its only function is to show the mask on again. The bike paragraph already has the parents watching her.
- The weekday countdown after Mrs. Prahl's first Thursday: compress to two lines. About 150 words.
- Dr. Ammons's intake: trim by a quarter. Keep the "same adults" beat.
- The first Thursday logic puzzle (knights and knaves) could be shorter; it only needs to show she finishes fast and waits.

What later depends: Mrs. Prahl and the Thursday program (named in Ch 7, may return); Dr. Ammons (check); the strawberry magnet; the envelope under her socks (likely paid off when she gets the real acceptance); "Bryce thinks I talk weird. That's different from bullying. It's only true."; the bike. Dev and Hana are minor names; check.

Too fast: January. "She stops asking in January" and her parents quietly changing course arrives in three short paragraphs. The decision that is the pivot of the whole first act (the parents agreeing to apply) happens off-page, in an overheard "yeah, all right." That is a legitimate choice, but it makes the whole slump (Oct to Jan) feel like it ends without a turn. Next chapter should supply it.

## Chapter 09: February

Proportion: about 75% scene, 25% summary (the opening February paragraph, the drive home).

Moves: the floor scene ("Take my birthday... Please just let me go to that school") is the best sustained scene in the first ten chapters and does not need a word cut. The living-room yes ("Is that a true thing you're saying, or is it a thinking about it thing?") with the bike coming into the living room is a perfect release with a sharp turn (her dad: "You cannot buy this off us"). The parents' overheard kitchen argument and the house-offer argument are real adult conflict, concise, and keep the stakes honest.

Drags: the opening February paragraph is one long summary sentence-chain (lunch, plant diagram, Dr. Ammons) that restates Chapter 8's flat mood. The visit day in April is mostly a tour; Chloe asking about Sam and Mrs. Okonkwo correcting Ruth's rumor is useful but long for what it carries. The drive home monologue is pleasant but repeats the "she talks for the whole drive" idea used in Chapter 6 and again in 3.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Opening February paragraph: trim to two sentences. About 100 words.
- The tour (Mrs. Okonkwo walking backward, the nurse questions): compress by a third, keep the Ruth-rumor correction and the man with the gray beard who asks "an entire day".
- The long second act of the drive home (robot arm, room questions): trim; keep the final image of her mom still sitting in the car.

What later depends: the house offer and the lease clause "if she leaves it stops" (the parents' refusal is a major plot choice; check Chapters 22, 32 for the house and the money); Mrs. Okonkwo (name, may recur); the man with the gray beard (probably a later staff member; the beard recalls Dr. Prentice, which may be a connection); Ruth's wrong rumor about the fall evaluation (a small character fact about Ruth: she says things); the bike under the sheet.

Too fast: nothing. The parents' real decision, which Chapter 8 deferred, is given room here.

## Chapter 10: April

Proportion: about 50% scene, 50% summary. This is where the survey starts. The arrival, Fen and the rocks, the wheel problem and the Sunday phone call are scenes. The subject-by-subject paragraph run (Spanish, math, literature, swimming, cooking, history, geography) is summary.

Moves: the arrival (Sam running down the hall, Ruth hugging her, Kavi "I have been through this whole building since lunch") is the payoff of three chapters of wanting, and it moves fast. Fen's rocks and "the whole row's off" is a small, exact character. The wheel problem is the first time in the middle that she solves something and brings it to Ruth, who corrects her method, and "After that it is the hour she gets up for" is the one line that makes the grind earn its place. The Sunday call ("Are you happy there, actually happy") is quick.

Drags: the middle section. Fen's night talk about the rocks is the second helping of her rock rule. The second-hour subjects are listed in a row: the swimming teacher (who went to the Olympics), cooking (the onion is "a triangle", a callback to the bridge that is pleasant but thin), Spanish (one word in nine), math (reads two lines), literature (no list), library, history and geography (the -stans). Each gets one tidy paragraph and then the next begins. This is a sampler of "what school here is like", repeated, and the reader has already had a month of this at camp in Chapters 4 to 6. The "what gets sent home" section is Owen again, and Kavi says "You already have all of it, Chloe", which sounds like an author comment on the repetition.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Cooking and the onion triangle: cut or fold into one line. About 100 words. The triangle joke is a callback to Ch 5; it is not needed for any later payoff that I can see yet. Check Ch 11 to 19.
- Geography/-stans paragraph and the Marcus "seven subjects, never more than ten" paragraph: compress. About 150 words. The seven-subject rule may matter later (workload), so keep one clause.
- The "what gets sent home" section (corkboard, writing teacher, Kavi on the stairs): it restates Owen. Cut by half. The writing teacher's line "a school rather than a prison, and certainly rather than a competition you can be knocked out of" is a thematic promise; keep that line.
- Fen's second night conversation: fold in with the first.
- Swimming teacher: keep the Olympian as one sentence, since the teachers' bios in Ch 3 set up an Olympic swimmer.

What later depends: the library with no card (the direct payoff of Ch 1's four-at-a-time fight and Ch 7's battery-drawer card; it should be kept in full); the writing teacher (who asks why she put things in that order, probably recurs); the math teacher who reads two lines (a method that must pay off in Ch 11 to 19); the notebook with names and last names (callback to Ch 7); Fen; Defensive Watch on the corkboard (a named thing, check that it comes back); the envelope in the desk drawer.

Too fast: Chloe's first night away at the year school. She phones nobody, no homesickness, no hotel. The reader has to accept her as fine in one paragraph, which the book chooses deliberately; it is a quiet payoff of the Ch 4 phone arc. It could use one beat.

## Chapter 11: Eight

Proportion: about 35% scene, 65% summary. A full year (June 2013 to June 2014) in one chapter.

Moves: the hot-dog-is-a-sandwich fight with her dad (the cut argument, then the parents' scene under it about her humming in the car) is the best scene: funny, short, and it shows Chloe recovered. The curfew fight ("a number too, because somebody picked ten") shows her using school logic at home, and her mom's kitchen read ("she always stops the moment she knows") is a real observation. The office scene with Mrs. Okonkwo (ninetieth of ninety in dance, "Ask me in June and it'll be no in June") is a proper scene with a clean refusal. The apple/drainpipe bracket rule with the four friends is the only friend scene in the school year and it is a joy.

Drags: the school-year core. After the home summer the chapter shifts to a list of subjects and activities that each get one or two paragraphs: Margie as new roommate, Fen's quartz, French replacing Spanish, reading in Spanish, Ruth learning Portuguese, logic ("like taking a lid off"), politics (leaders, spouses, in-laws on the wall), Nerf, wood shop (the oval mallet), team sports (the teammates who do not pass), drawing (forty left hands), dance, elective sheet, choir with Amara, climbing (Sam), electronics (Kavi), beekeeping. This is exactly the "classes, subjects and activities that feel like lists" the earlier readers complained of. It is a catalog of tasting things: some succeed, some fail, and the failures (dance, choir, the team) are described with the same affect as the successes.

Cut/compress candidates (ranked by safety):
- Choir and Amara: whole subplot cut or compressed to one sentence. About 180 words. It is a failure with no consequence found so far.
- Team sports plot (two teammates who do not pass, counting passes, asking to swap): compress; merge with the dance office scene so that Mrs. Okonkwo's "you're the third in here about a team this week" is the only mention. About 100 words.
- Grandmother visit ("She's put on weight") is 150 words that quietly confirm the previous winter's weight loss. Cut unless a later chapter needs it. It is a nice but unnecessary beat.
- The elective sheet scene (Sam talking about climbing, Kavi electronics): compress; the list of electives is the sort of passage readers called a list.
- Politics paragraph and the Piraeus teacher speech: the idea (everyone is connected; learn children, then who the children married) is very likely load-bearing for a later chapter about files, names and chains (Ch 21, 25, 29). Do not cut before checking.
- French/Portuguese/Spanish paragraph: compress but keep "It carries on being yours", a thesis of the school.
- Wood shop mallet: keep one sentence; the mallet may return (check). Drawing forty hands and Ruth's "mittens" line: keep unless drawing never returns.
- Nerf: the idea of "the easiest thing in this building to hit" is a good scene, but the rest of Nerf probably pays off later (Ch 11's title is "Eight"; age eight). Check.

What later depends: dance (ninetieth of ninety, a continuing failure arc; check), drawing hands, Nerf (Coach Bell), politics wall, French/Spanish reading, logic, the bracket rule (apples), Margie. At least some of these are the "watch her acquire skills" setup the author wants. The question is whether each needs a full paragraph in this chapter.

Too fast: the transition from "she is flat and sleepy" in Ch 8 and 9 to "she is singing in the shower" in the first page is sudden but justified. The summer at home is the only time the parents see the change, and it could use a little more room: one scene about the hugging goodbye in June and one about her mother after the first night. Not necessary.

## Chapter 12: Nine

Proportion: about 40% scene, 60% summary. A school year plus a summer.

Moves: Kavi's three objections at breakfast, "the form accomplishes nothing", is funny and quick. Sam finally getting over the overhang on May 11th (left foot first, the screaming, the torn corner of her book) is a real climb with a real payoff after a year of falling, the best scene in the chapter. The cribbage on the balcony and the vendor proposal at the end are small, warm, and show Chloe seeing a trick in language that her father misses.

Drags: the subjects. Mandarin (tones, Mrs. Sun, Ruth on Sundays), statistics (Kavi and the eleven-person study), chemistry (the gray precipitate and the three changes), first aid (the dark room and the man asking about his wife), textiles (the shirt that does not fit), biology (the bullied striped fish) are all one or two paragraphs, back to back, with different teachers and different small lessons. Each has an interesting kernel, but run together they are the long-stretch-of-classes problem the earlier readers named. Add Bex and Iyad (two unpleasant peers, each a paragraph), then Sam's overhang, then riding, and the chapter has about twelve threads. The weakest: riding (she quits, Priya is hurt, and the conversation says "it's boring"), the biology fish arc (Kavi picks a favorite; "a name for that from statistics"), and first aid.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Riding with Priya: compress to a short paragraph plus the dinner confrontation, or cut the stables description and the blind-spot lesson. About 250 words saved. It is the only horse scene since Priya's monologue; check whether Priya's horses recur.
- Biology and the fish tank: cut to two sentences. About 120 words. Check whether "the man with the fish tank" returns.
- Textiles paragraph (the shirts): trim; the lesson (trace a shirt that fits, stop redrawing) is the same lesson as chemistry (change one variable). Keep one of them in full. About 120 words.
- First aid paragraph: keep as a short seed if the drill pays off later (a midnight scene with a person asking questions). Check.
- Iyad Mansour: either shorten or cut; check whether he returns (Chapter 12 uses him as a rival and nothing else yet).
- The dance and drawing "gone from the sheet" scene is a good bit but is the third time we hear that she was ninetieth of ninety; it pays off Ch 11 properly and can stay short.

What later depends: Bex and the we/I injury (Chloe stops saying "we" for the rest of the year, which should pay off in a later collaboration scene; check), the chemistry "three changes and one result" lesson (a method; check), Mandarin and recordings (check), the vendor contract (the idea that a sentence can be written so it cannot be checked is probably the book's key skill for the files and money chapters; very likely load-bearing), the cribbage board (check).

Too fast: Bex. The "we in her first sentence, then I" insult is a strong and subtle scene but it gets one paragraph and Chloe's one conversation with Ruth. If Bex matters later, she needs the room; if not, the scene is a stand-alone gem and still worth it. Iyad's public reading of Chloe's flaw ("a stranger loses her by the second sentence") is another small injury with no room to land.

## Chapter 13: Ten Pages

Proportion: about 45% scene, 55% summary.

Moves: Mr. Hearn's opening (Hamilton's rate on the board, "ten pages a week") and the first returned essay ("whether you are right or only comfortable") is a clear, strong start to a skill-building arc, and the arc pays off at the end with the Hearn essay that finally works ("all the pages do what only the third used to"). The Defensive Watch scoring sheet, the F, and "Nobody scores the drill" is the tightest piece of the book's world-building so far and the most plot-bearing. The Thanksgiving tariffs conversation lets the reader see Chloe's thinking from outside, quickly.

Drags: the middle. Self defense (mat room, ones, twos, Kowalczyk, the 10v1 minute, Sam's fourteen seconds) is three long paragraphs of rules and counts. Paintball is another long run: the barrel-and-hose insight, then Kavi timing the pump, then Bex getting the credit from Bell, then Chloe's retelling at the sinks. Then comes Russian, music and algebra in one paragraph, almost as an afterthought. After the drill sheet there is a long dinner coda with Iyad turning her line into a joke. A seventh thread is the December blank note and the grandmother's floor.

The key structural problem: Bex steals credit again (Ch 12 the bridge "we" to "I", here the barrel-and-hose insight). Two nearly identical injuries in two chapters, each followed by Chloe telling Ruth and Ruth being unhelpful. The second helps establish a pattern, but the sinks scene repeats the first Ruth scene beat for beat.

Cut/compress candidates:
- Paintball: compress by about 30% (cut the tacky floor, Chloe getting hit more times than she counted, the "luck" boy impressions, Kavi's solo practice). Keep the hose insight and the Bex theft. About 250 words. Cost: the "barrel is a hose" idea and the nineteen-hit evening may be a callback; check.
- Self defense: trim the explanation of 10v1 ("Five people can reach you at once") by half. About 150 words. Check whether 10v1, ten on one, or the minute return (it very likely does; the book has later chapters named "Nine Minutes", "Seventy-Five", "Forty Targets"). The rule explanation may be load-bearing to later fights.
- Russian/Arabic, music, algebra paragraph: cut to one sentence. About 120 words. Cost: Russian and Arabic may be a payoff later (insult runs in two languages) and Ruth's Arabic. Check.
- Sinks scene with Ruth after Bex: compress to a line; the pattern is already established.
- Iyad's dinner joke ("Kessler was standing in it") is cruel and funny; cut or merge into one line.
- Swimming "gets harder" paragraph: cut to a clause.

What later depends: Defensive Watch (the six scoring rules, "response time," F grades; this is the book's engine and must stay in full), Hearn and the Federalist reading (likely recurs; "right or only comfortable" is the thesis of her later reasoning; check), Kowalczyk and the 10v1, Nadia (named in passing; Ch 27 is titled "Nadia"), Wes, Theo, Bex, Bell's board.

Too fast: Chloe's own reaction to the first Watch F. She decides number six is her, Ruth says it's everybody, and the chapter moves on in two exchanges. For a moment that shifts the book from school to training, this is the place where the reader should feel the stakes. Also too fast: Hearn's blank note. It is one of the most interesting beats (a mark with no line under it) and gets one paragraph before the book skips to the February essay.

## Chapter 14: Sixty Degrees

Proportion: about 50% scene, 50% summary.

Moves: Bell's metronome speech and the two firing lines meeting at sixty degrees is a clear, concrete setup with a strange goal (hit an arrow with an arrow), and Chloe's first collision with Odile (the dry click, the broken arrow, the shouting) pays it off at once. The area-bombing essay is the book's best study scene: the hat, "it has an answer", the sick-on-the-bathroom-floor night, the other side built on a 1961 writer, and the B with the hedge note. ### D. Can the middle be shortened without breaking the later book, and by how much?

Yes, but not by half, and the author is right about why.

What the later book depends on is a specific, short list of acquired things, and I can name each as it is used: the arrow-on-arrow count and the turret and the plateau (Ch 14, 18, 25, 31, 34); the bar exam's lesson that the reasoning must be written down for a stranger (Ch 19, 33); Marek and the receipt (Ch 16, 19, 30); the Defensive Watch and the four a.m. night (Ch 13, 15, 29, 32, 34); the first-aid chain (Ch 12, 15, 20); Ruth's box and the hidden network (Ch 16, 20, 34); the forge (Ch 14 to 23); Mrs. Sun, Latin and a language a year (Ch 12, 14, 28, 30); the NDA (Ch 18, 30); Bex's geometry (Ch 17, 21, 30); Priya's horses (Ch 4, 12, 23, 24, 36); the library without a card (Ch 1, 7, 10); Hearn and the hedge (Ch 13, 14, 21, 33); chemistry's change-one-thing method (Ch 12, 27, 32). Ch 30's interview and Ch 33's rules document are, structurally, the book reading its own middle back as evidence, and they only work if the reader saw the middle happen. A time skip would break them.

What the later book does not depend on is the volume. By my count about thirty subjects and activities in Ch 10 to 19 (dance, drawing, choir, the team, biology and the fish, textiles, cooking, geography, French, German, Japanese, Portuguese, physics, art history, epistemology, fencing, game theory, music, Russian, Arabic, comparative religion, logistics, algebra, calculus, rhetoric, anatomy, psychology, surgery, accounting, Hindi, topology) have no later dependency beyond a clause, and several are named once in Ch 30's list anyway. Another batch (Bex's repeats, Iyad's repeats, the retellings) is a repeated beat.

So: the middle can lose roughly 15 to 20 percent (about 9,000 to 12,000 words of about 60,000) by tiers 1 and 2, without touching any dependency. A bolder pass that compresses every catalog paragraph to a sentence and trims the technical set pieces by half could reach 25 to 30 percent (about 15,000 to 18,000 words) but would start to eat the very things the author wants the reader to watch (the wheel problem, the sound array, the forge arc, the hedge lesson) and would make Ch 14, 15, 17 and 19 less textured. I would not go past 25 percent.

The right shape of the cut is concentration, not removal. Each middle chapter now carries about a dozen threads at a paragraph each. The strongest chapters (14, 15, 17, 19) have one or two threads with scenes behind them and a catalog around them. If each chapter carried three to five threads, with the rest as a clause or a single sentence, the reader would feel the acquisition without reading the inventory. The readers who say "lists" are right about the texture; the author is right about the need. Both are served by turning paragraphs into clauses, not by deleting years.

Three further honest points:
- The rest of the book has the same disease after Ch 20: Ch 21 (representatives), Ch 23 (futures), Ch 24 (Nadia), Ch 28 (the office), Ch 30 (the recap), Ch 35 (logs), and the three-chapter detour of Ch 25, 26 and 27 away from Chloe. If the middle is trimmed and these are not, the complaint will move, not vanish. The later detours total about 20,000 words with Chloe offstage or silent.
- The strongest argument for the author's side is that the best scenes in the book (Ch 17's dinner, Ch 19's exam, Ch 29's report, Ch 31's professor, Ch 34's report) work because the reader has already lived what they refer to. Each is "the middle, read back".
- The pill thread of Ch 24 and Ch 28 (a white sinus tablet with no record) is never picked up in Ch 29 to 36. It is the one plant whose absence a reader will notice; either it needs a payoff or the two scenes can be cut (about 900 words).
- The time capsule of Ch 7 and 8 is never opened. It can stay as a self-contained scene.
