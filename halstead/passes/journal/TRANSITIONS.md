# Transitions read

Lens: how the book moves from scene to scene and chapter to chapter: joins, scene openings and endings, viewpoint shifts, and where the reader is told when, where and who.

## Chapter 01: Before

Join in: none, this is the opening. The italic dateline "September 2011 – February 2012" is a good, plain orientation, and the first line puts us in a kitchen with a named character at once.

Scene shape: This chapter is a run of short scenes strung over six months: the phone call, the worksheet and parking lot, the library, the dinner, the recess game, Peanut, then the conferences, doctor, Christmas, sled-dog book, and the sticky note. Most joins are made by month names ("In October, back inside", "In November", "In December", "In January"). That is clear, but the habit is heavy. Several scenes begin with a time stamp and then a summary line before the scene proper starts.

Weak joins:
- Scene 1 to the worksheet is a bare "The next morning". Fine. But the library scene is bolted on mid-paragraph: the long "book comes out of the desk" paragraph slides from a list of Chloe's reading habits into "At the library, Chloe stands at the circulation counter" with no break. The reader is at a school desk and then at a library counter in one sentence. A paragraph break or an earlier cue ("on Saturday") would help.
- The dinner scene ("At dinner in September") jumps back a month relative to the October recess and Peanut scenes in order, and the recess scene then opens with no time marker at all ("She takes the book out to recess"). The reader has to guess it is later than the dinner. Then Peanut says "In October, back inside", which is useful but the "back inside" is odd after a recess scene that began with the day being warm.
- The first section break (the underscore line) arrives after Peanut and the cafeteria, and the next scene opens with a loose line about "yesterday's spelling list chalked half erased" before the month. The scene's real start is "In November, Mrs. Aldana asks what clouds are made of". The chalk line is throat-clearing. Start on the question.
- "The exchange is over before anybody looks up. In December" is a summary hop that blurs two scenes into one. Then the Fridays paragraph steps back to November again. The month order in this section goes November, December, November, December, then the conference "in November". The reader loses the calendar.
- The conference scene begins "The papers Mrs. Aldana keeps go across a table on a Thursday evening in November" which is confusing: papers go across a table. It is also an odd place to learn it is conference night. Chloe is outside the door, a strong image, but the opening line makes the reader work.
- The car ride and doctor's office are joined by "The papers go with them to the doctor", a smooth object-based bridge. Good. But the doctor visit has no time marker; it could be days or weeks later.
- Christmas opens "Christmas is at her grandmother's", clear and quick.
- The January scene opens with "In January they read the book about a boy and a sled dog" which is clear.

Endings: The cafeteria sandwich crust ending of the Kayleigh scene lands well (silent, physical). The car-ride "the heater ticks the rest of the way home" is good. The Christmas car ride ending ("keeps her eyes shut for the rest of the drive") lands, and it is a strong repeat of the shut-eyes image. The dinner scene's ending, mom's mouth dropping and the dishes, is good and quiet. The recess scene's ending runs long and is mostly one very long sentence of staging after the point has landed. The January ending paragraph ("The only fix is getting better at sitting there... hold their breath") turns into explanation of what Chloe has concluded, which tells what the scenes already showed.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe throughout, with some drift into narrator summary ("The parking lot, the boxes, the question that had already happened without her: all of it is true") which sounds like an author comment, not Chloe.

Chapter ending: The sticky note, "MARCH 7, a time, and a name", is a clean hard-cut ending that pulls straight into chapter 2, whose title is the date. Strong.

What would make it worse: adding a bridge sentence that explains how months passed, or ending on a reflection about what the appointment will mean.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

Join in: Chapter 1 ends on the sticky note with MARCH 7. Chapter 2 opens on the date in its title and the dateline, then in the waiting room. This is a clean hard cut and the reader needs no bridge. The only snag is the first paragraph: it opens on "Her dad is at work" and recounts the morning before we are shown where Chloe is. The reader is in the past for four sentences before reaching the waiting room. Start in the waiting room and let the dad's absence arrive in one passing line.

Scene shape: Waiting room, testing room (very long), door-gap eavesdropping, car, "nothing changes" summary, end of year. The test scene is the bulk of the chapter and is built almost entirely from "Then... Next... After that..." joins between subtests. These work as a rhythm at first, but the stack of linking sentences ("Then the cards want nine blocks", "After the blocks he asks", "Next it's words", "Now he asks about things people do") runs long enough to read as a list. Some subtests could be cut or collapsed so the reader's patience is not spent before the door scene, which is the real payoff.

Weak joins:
- The lone line "The lid goes on the bin." and "He reaches the last pair, closes the book, and puts it away." are tiny bridges that exist only to tidy props. Cut or merge.
- The underscore break before "Then he talks to her mom, and Chloe waits outside" is fine, but the phrase "Then he talks" is a small summary where a direct cut to the door gap would be stronger.
- Ben's long speech about the composite score is the one place where a scene stops to explain. A ninth grader will not follow "composite that averages two very different pictures". Chloe overhears it, so the confusion is partly the point, but the passage is long enough that the reader checks out before Ben's real line ("the only lever the school has for her").
- The car scene opens "In the car her mom puts her hands on the wheel" with no marker for leaving the building, but it is clear from the context. Good.
- The last three short sections ("After the appointment, nothing changes", the June paragraph, the report card) are summary, each separated by an underscore break. They do the job of a time skip, but three breaks in a page and a half makes them feel like leftovers. Two of them could be one section.

Endings: The scene in the test room ends with "Finishing that page isn't what tells me how you did" which is good. The overhear scene ends on "holds still, in case moving is the thing that makes the door close", a strong physical ending. The car scene ends with ice cream on the seat, a deflating image that lands well. The "What she works out that month is bigger than the pencil trick" paragraph is the author explaining the lesson; the scenes before it already showed it, and it is the kind of ending that fizzles.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe. A few lines slip into narrator wisdom ("because that question is off limits to children").

Chapter ending: The report card under the strawberry magnet "where the sticky note used to be" is a lovely callback that closes the loop from chapter 1, and it is a quiet ending. It does not pull forward much, but chapter 3 is titled The Letter, so a new object is promised.

What would make it worse: adding a "she did not know then that" bridge to the school year, or letting Ben's explanation run longer.

## Chapter 03: The Letter

Join in: Chapter 2 ends in June with the report card and the strawberry magnet. Chapter 3 opens with the dateline "July 2012" and an envelope with her name on it. A clean hard cut; the month gap is handled by the dateline alone, which is enough. The opening three short lines (envelope, name, "Miss Chloe Kessler") are a strong, late-starting scene opening.

Scene shape: Mailbox, dinner reaction, phone calls (district, then the school), website with dad, mom's research, Monday teacher page, nine days of argument, library, packing and drive. Over nine pages the chapter is mostly adults talking, with Chloe on the stairs. The stair-listening device is a good consistent way to keep it in her viewpoint.

Weak joins:
- The timeline is the main problem. "It takes them nine days" arrives after the Monday scene, then mentions "the fourth day", "the eighth", "the sixth day" (out of order), then "the ninth night". The reader has to count. The earlier calls (Monday, Thursday, Saturday) and websites (Friday, Sunday, Monday) are also tangled with the nine days. Pick one clock and use it. A reader in 9th grade will lose track of what day it is.
- "Chloe asks once more on the Sunday" ends the dinner scene by skipping forward, then the next section starts "on Monday". Clear enough, but the two-step hop is fussy.
- Inside the phone-call section the narration jumps between the district call, the neighbours, and the call to the school number with only "Then" as the glue. The neighbour paragraph ("A few more houses...") is summary that stalls the tension between the two calls. Cut it to a line or move it after.
- The website section opens "Her dad looks up the website that night" ("that night" is unclear since the preceding section covers days; it must be Friday, which the sentence later says).
- The mom-searches paragraph and the Monday-morning laptop scene are joined by the laptop still being open, a nice object bridge.
- The librarian scene opens "On the Thursday before they go" with no previous mention of a departure date. The reader does not know how much time has passed since the nine days. Add one marker earlier or lead with the packing.
- The final section is a stack of summary sentences (drive, hotel, emergency number, dad's Friday). The scene dissolves into "they drive her the next day" rather than arriving. It reads as summary where a single arrival image would be stronger.

Endings: The ninth-night eavesdrop ending (crawling up the stairs in socks) is the best scene end here: small, physical, funny and sad. The phone-call section ends on Chloe's mistimed cartoon question, which lands as a light button. The library ending ("This one she can read again") is good. The chapter's last line, "the envelope, in the front pocket of her bag, with her name on it", is a strong object-based end that echoes the opening.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe, with the overheard adult scenes anchored by her position on the stairs. In the dinner scene and the parents' kitchen debate there is some drift: "Her mom's position is that..." and "Her dad's position is that..." are narrator summaries of adult argument that Chloe could not know. The final hotel paragraph (mom checks the nurse, front desk, emergency number) is also beyond what Chloe sees.

Chapter ending to next: The arrival is promised but not shown, so chapter 4 (Pluto) has to open at the school. That is a good pull.

What would make it worse: adding more calendar words to patch the nine days, or ending on an explanation of why Chloe wants to go.

## Chapter 04: Pluto

Join in: Chapter 3 ends with the envelope in the bag, heading for the school. Chapter 4 opens "The first day is awful." The same dateline month keeps the reader oriented, and the blunt opening line is a good, quick payoff after nine pages of delay. The place is not named for a paragraph or two (hallway, lot), but "a hundred-odd children" and the folder tie it to the letter. Fine.

Scene shape: Arrival and registration, orientation, room with Priya, the phone call and hotel night, the Pluto door, Sam and Ruth, dining hall, the fourth night phone, mom leaves, Kavi and the volcano, Mr. Doyle and Ruth. A lot of material; most joins are underscore breaks, and they mostly work.

Weak joins:
- Orientation is rendered as a list of rules with Chloe looking around. It is functional, but it sits at the top of the book's first big new setting and slows things. The beats that matter (the same-day-home promise, mom writing "same day" and underlining it) could come sooner with fewer laundry details. The laundry bag repeats three times; that is fine as a thread, but the first mention is dull.
- "But Chloe's own bag stays zipped" at the start of the room scene begins with "But", answering nothing. It reads as if a paragraph was cut. The Priya dialogue then begins with a line of action in the middle ("Pushing the drawer shut with her knee...").
- The Priya horse monologue is long and the narrator stops it with a summary line ("Priya tells Chloe about the horse she rides, the horse she is saving up for..."). The summary, then "Then Priya says goodnight, and inside a minute she is asleep", is a good hard cut. The summary is fine.
- The hotel scene ends on "the words blur into one long hum, still going" and then jumps with "In the morning her mom drives her back" to a quick note about doing it every night. That last sentence is a reasonable bridge but delivers the plot rule (she can do this nightly) in plain exposition.
- The Pluto door scene opens "The rooms are named after planets, which she works out on the second day". The place-and-time marker is good. The panic paragraph ("her ears do what they did in October") is a flashback inside a flashback to chapter 1's Peanut/Kayleigh moment and is vague for a reader who has half-forgotten it. It also appears mid-sentence without a signal.
- The sleepover section "That night Chloe calls the desk again and sleeps in the hotel again" jumps through a day of events in a single sentence. Fine, but then the next section opens in the dining hall with Owen with no "when". "On the third night" appears only in the second paragraph. The viewpoint of the first sentence (Priya noticing Owen) drifts away from Chloe.
- The Kavi scene opens "There's a fourth in Pluto who stays silent day after day" which is a note about a character before the scene. It works as a late-start, but the reader has not been told this is the same room at the same time as the dinners; "On the seventh day" is the first clock. And Kavi's name is only given as the dialogue tag at the end of his speech ("Kavi says"). Whose scene it is gets clear late. Name him earlier.
- The Mr. Doyle section opens "On the second Tuesday" with no indication of time earlier than the "seventh day". Calendar words are doing double duty (days of the stay and weekdays). Pick one.

Endings: Strong ones: "I know she did. I wrote it down" (mom leaving) is a very good scene end. Sam starting a margin volcano is a nice comic button. The last line, "You did it to yourself," Kavi says, is a crisp close, and a hard cut to what comes next. The Pluto door scene ends on "Chloe hasn't let go of her folder when she sits down", a good tiny note. Weaker: "But nothing comes, all day" then "That night it catches again" is a small fizzle with explanatory work; the chapter's lesson about holding back is stated twice.

Viewpoint: Chloe throughout, with small drifts (the Owen opening, the "Chloe has a very clear idea of what is supposed to happen" lines).

Chapter ending: Ends on dinner banter, not on a hook, but the new characters are now planted and chapter 5 is titled Behind.

What would make it worse: adding a recap of the same-day-home rule, or a summary of how many days she spent at the hotel.

## Chapter 05: Behind

Join in: Chapter 4 ends at dinner in about the second week ("You did it to yourself," Kavi says). Chapter 5 opens on a general statement about speed and goes back to "the first week". So the book steps backward in time without saying so. The reader is told "July 2012" again, which is the same as the last chapter, so nothing marks the rewind. Two small fixes: open on a specific moment (Tuesday of week one, a page finished) rather than the general statement, or note it is week one earlier. The opening sentence is a thesis, not a scene, and the first paragraph is summary before the reader is in a room.

Scene shape: Week one speed and writing prompt, bridge building and Tuesday test, week two fraction division, the pool, Owen gone, the moon at dinner in week three. These are mostly well cut with underscore breaks and clear markers ("On the Thursday", "On the Tuesday", "The second week is the week that gets her").

Weak joins:
- The writing-timer paragraph begins with a flashback ("a room with a small table... a man who held the timer") to chapter 2 inside a sentence about the present. It works as a quick echo, but the sentence is long and the reader has to hold two places at once.
- The Mrs. Aldana comparison in the Marisol paragraph ("which would have been the longest answer in Mrs. Aldana's room by half a page") is a third back-reference crammed into one sentence. Three flashbacks in the first two pages make the chapter start slowly.
- The bridge section runs "On the Thursday" to "Before they load anything" to "On the Tuesday" within three paragraphs, with the whole week of building collapsed into "Their bridge takes most of the afternoon". It is clear, but the Tuesday test could open the scene with the bucket and hanging weights, and the Thursday building could be shortened.
- The fraction section starts with a general statement ("The second week is the week that gets her") and then "They are dividing fractions". Late-ish. Start on "Why do you flip it."
- Within the fractions section the scene runs past its natural end: after Ruth's zero question the scene could end at "The bell goes before Ruth has finished arguing the point with him" or on "a question she asked first". Then it continues with a paragraph about her ears hot, the hallway reading to Sam, packets, and finally the night-time rectangle. The night discovery is the real peak. The summary paragraph "The packets here belong to whoever works in them" is explanation that delays it.
- The swimming section is a stub of two paragraphs between two heavy scenes. It earns its place only by the Ruth swim ending. It could be folded into the dinner scene or cut.
- The Owen scene opens with a strong image ("His door is standing open and the bed inside it is stripped"), a good late start. The scene then ends on dinner with Ruth, followed by an extra summary line ("More leave before the end of the month... by the fourth time Chloe reads the event off the corridor"). That last paragraph is a good fast-forward, but it follows the better ending (lining up roll pieces on the tray) and steps on it.
- The moon scene ("at dinner in the third week") has a confusing opening line: "That's why it's a liar" with no context for the reader, who is told afterward that Kavi said something about the moon. That is a clever cold open, but the "just after Kavi has said something" bit is clunky. A hard start on the argument followed by one line of setting would be tidier.

Endings: The 'Six.' and "Priya's light goes off" after the rectangle work is the best beat in the chapter, a one-word paragraph and a dark room. "She checks for the damage anyway, out of habit, and the sentence comes back clean" ends the chapter on a quiet, earned turn. The Owen dinner closing image (lining up the roll pieces) echoes chapter 1's crust strip nicely.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe, steady. The Priya light line is a small camera observation outside her thoughts but still inside her room.

Chapter ending to next: Ends with Chloe not worrying about what she said. It resolves instead of hooking, but chapter 6 is titled The List and this is a fine breath.

What would make it worse: adding a line that explains why the moon argument matters to her, or putting a date on every scene.

## Chapter 06: The List

Join in: Chapter 5 ends mid-stay (third week, a walk back across the grass). Chapter 6 opens "On the last Wednesday" in the parent hotel breakfast room with her dad. The dateline "August 2012" helps, and the jump past the rest of the month is clean. The opening is also a viewpoint step: Chloe is in the room, but the scene is a long parents' conversation, and she is a bystander. It is a good way to bring the parents' side to the reader without leaving her. The first paragraph is a little long on setting before the first line of dialogue.

Scene shape: Parents' breakfast, last night family dinner with Chloe's recap monologue, hallway talk outside Pluto, departure morning, and the car ride home. Five scenes, joined by underscore breaks, and all are clear on place and time.

Weak joins:
- The dinner recap is the main problem. It re-narrates the bridge, the pool and the fractions in detail that the reader has just seen in chapter 5. The "Then X. Then Y." list stretches for a page and a half and the narrator says it three or four times ("Then the robot arm", "Then chess", "Then karate", "Then the nerf guns", "Then the wood shop again"). The idea (a child pouring out a month) is good; the length is not. A second reading of the bridge and fractions is a repeat, not a join. Cut the repeats of material already dramatised and keep only things the reader has not seen (Gary the CPR dummy, Spanish, signing, the cold computer room). Making it worse: adding more items to the list.
- The breakfast scene's end ("by the time somebody's kid has come over... half the table has gone") is summary that fades out. The better end is the syrup line right after it; the fade just before is clutter.
- Between dinner and the hallway scene, the change is signalled with "After, they sit on the floor in the hall outside Pluto". Fine, but the time of night is unclear until the lights-off detail. Kavi's pajamas appear later than the setting; fine.
- The hallway scene is a long argument about the form with a lot of crosstalk, then Chloe's number-running paragraph, then "Chloe," Sam says, "obviously." The mid-scene stop to run the four-hours-each-way numbers is interior explanation at the point where the dialogue should keep moving. The reader already has the reasons from earlier chapters.
- The departure scene opens "Long before anybody comes to get her, she is awake" which is a nice small way to enter it, and the goodbye beats are fast and specific (hair tie, open hand). A good place where the hard cut works.
- The car section starts with a mismatch: a paragraph about the days-of-the-week Spanish, then "An hour into the drive" with her eyes shut. Chloe is faking sleep to listen again, which echoes chapter 1 and 3. The device is reused so often that the reader sees it coming.

Endings: The napkin in the shirt pocket is a strong end for the dinner scene. "So there's a day," she says, and the fist closed on the pajama leg is good. "Chloe keeps her eyes shut the rest of the way home" is a tidy end to the chapter and echoes chapter 1's Christmas drive. The hallway ending, on Ruth's "How hard can that be", is a good ironic button that points ahead.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe, with the parent scenes seen through her. The final car scene is overheard, but the narration says "Chloe keeps her eyes shut" and so stays in view.

Chapter ending to next: The list question (what is this place) lingers and chapter 7 is "The Same Room", so the pull is real, though a mild one.

What would make it worse: adding more month-recap items, or ending the car scene on a line that says what Chloe feels about her parents' conversation.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room

Join in: This is the worst chapter-to-chapter join so far. Chapter 6 ended in the back seat on the drive home, after goodbyes at the school where Sam says "see you at the thing in the fall" and Kavi shakes her hand. Chapter 7 opens back on the pickup loop and says Sam "left early" and Kavi "has gone" by the time she looks, with a girl she never spoke to telling her. The reader is thrown back in time and the two departure scenes contradict each other. If chapter 7 is meant to be the same morning from a different angle, say so with a marker. If it is meant to replace the goodbyes in 6, one of them has to go. As written the reader thinks they missed something. The first paragraph is also one huge sentence trying to track three moving people, and there are two blank lines after it, which may be a stray formatting break.

Scene shape: Eleven short sections: the loop and Ruth at the flagpole, the phone book (names), waiting for the phone, the car question, the library return, Ms. Vance's first day, the time capsule, the library card going in the drawer, the daily asking, mom on the phone with the school, and the classroom cry. Most are short and cut on a clear beat. The month markers carry the passage of time well ("In August", "On the tenth day", "second week", "end of September", "first week of October").

Weak joins:
- The phone-book scene is "In August" while the departure is also in August; fine. But it begins mid-flow ("Can you call Sam?") with Chloe "already talking before she has finished crossing the kitchen". That is a late start and works. The scene runs long because mom keeps asking for a last name four times. Three would do.
- The waiting-for-the-phone section covers ten days in three paragraphs. Good compression, and the closing line about checking the phone is on the hook is an excellent quiet bridge.
- The Sunday car question is a hard cut that works, but the response arrives without a scene: "we can talk about it tonight" and then the very next scene is the library on Tuesday. The reader never sees the talk. Either the talk happened off the page (then say what came of it) or it is a gap. The library visit is also about the book that "has to go back" after "five weeks", while the Sunday car ride is in the same window. The timeline is hard to hold.
- The library scene repeats Chloe's camp monologue (Gary, bridge corner, fractions) a third time in the book (chapter 6 dinner, chapter 5 scenes, now here). The librarian's reply is nice, but this is the third repeat and drags. Cut the recap to a few words.
- Ms. Vance's first-day scene and the math sheet scene are joined with no bridge in the second section ("The first math sheet arrives midmorning"), a minor hard cut; works.
- The time capsule scene opens with a prop ("The shoebox has foil taped all over it") and a time marker, a good late start. It ends on the sad "I hope you like fifth grade", a strong ending.
- The library card section opens with "Having taken the third library book to bed on the Monday", a clumsy participial opener that makes the reader piece together the week. It ends with the card in the drawer with the batteries, a good callback to the key in chapter 1.
- The "At breakfast she asks" paragraph is one sentence of well over a hundred words. As a device it shows repetition, but the sentence is hard going for a 9th grader. Break it by using several shorter beats in the same place (that is a form note; the structure itself is fine).
- The mom-on-the-phone section announces what it is about in its first sentence ("her mom starts on Chloe's own school...about what it has for a child who finished the third-grade work in October"), then shows it. The first sentence also contains an odd error: "in October" while the section is dated the last week of September. Cut the summary sentence and begin on "Coming down for water".
- The final scene ("In the first week of October...") has no connection to the call before it. The reader may infer the crying follows from the school conversation, but the chapter does not link them. That is probably right: the cry is unexplained, and the cut is stronger without a bridge.

Endings: The final line, "I don't know," Chloe says. "I don't..." She tries to stop, but she can't," is a good unresolved ending that pulls hard into chapter 8, The Asking. The Ruth flagpole promise gives a strong ending to the opening scene. "Ms. Vance says no in a way that has clearly been asked for before" is a good small cut-away.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe throughout. The Ms. Vance and mom's scenes are seen from her position.

What would make it worse: trying to patch the departure contradiction with a line of explanation inside the new paragraph, or adding a scene of the parent talk that the book skips on purpose.

## Chapter 08: The Asking

Join in: Chapter 7 ended on Chloe crying at her desk in the first week of October and saying "I don't know." Chapter 8 opens with Ms. Vance calling the house "on the Friday". That is a strong, sensible next step: the school reaction. Good pull. One small gap: the reader does not see the cry resolve and the dateline says October so the move is easy.

Scene shape: This chapter has far fewer scene-break lines than its length needs. The doctor's office, the car, the school phone calls, Mrs. Prahl's first class, the waiting, the fight, the Thursdays, the December talk, the bike, the grandmother's, the end of asking: all run together as one block of paragraphs, with most scene changes made by a topic sentence rather than a break. This is the chapter where the joins are least clear.

Weak joins:
- "In the car her mom holds the wheel, the engine off." arrives right after the appointment ends with the clock and floor tile. It is clear enough, but a break line would help. The doctor's office scene is strong, and it is better if the reader leaves it on "It was hard."
- "Her mother starts on the school in October, every week, on the phone first and then in the building itself" follows the car scene's final image (mom's forehead on the wheel) with no break and a time jump of weeks. The weak opening is a summary sentence. A line or two on what happens next, or a break, is needed. Also "October" is already in the dateline and the doctor visit was in October, so it is not clear whether this is the same month.
- "At the counter, with her back to the room..." is an unlinked fragment (what words in writing?). A reader cannot tell whose counter or what is being said. It reads like a cut sentence.
- "Then on a Tuesday she comes up and sits on the end of Chloe's bed with her coat on" is OK as a scene start, and the Prahl description is solid, but "It takes weeks. In the second week of November the school calls back" jumps over the main event (a year-early exception) in one sentence. This is a big plot beat told as summary.
- Mrs. Prahl's first class opens "There are nine kids and a cart with shelves", a good late start with no throat-clearing. But the reader has to wait to learn this is the first Thursday. Add the day marker.
- The long "Chloe finishes the packet that night ... but then it's Friday, and Saturday..." string is a clever way of showing waiting, and the listing of days works.
- The jacket fight is introduced as "The jacket is Friday, and any excuse would have done as well." Good, blunt. It ends strongly on "until her legs ache from standing behind the door" and the dinner paragraph that follows (bread, salt) is excellent as a cool-down.
- "So is the third, except that Mrs. Prahl puts them in pairs" is the most confusing join in the book so far. It follows a dinner paragraph and refers back to "The second Thursday goes like the first" from several paragraphs before, so the reader has no way to know "the third" means the third Thursday. Name the Thursday and the place. This one needs rewriting.
- "In December her mom comes up and sits down on the end of her bed" repeats the bed-edge sitting set-up used earlier in the chapter, and the "Nobody was mean" scene is the heart of the chapter. It is a very good scene, but the sentence "Her mom waits... that is all of it:" bundles an interior list into the dialogue; it breaks the scene's rhythm.
- The bike and Christmas paragraph opens on a very long sentence that has the bike in the garage, then Christmas morning. The Dana beat repeats chapter 1's Christmas lines nearly word for word ("Dana asks how school is, and Chloe says it's good"). This is deliberate, but there is no signal to the reader that it is a deliberate echo, and the "grandmother's" part is thin.
- "She stops asking in January" is a short, lovely hard cut. The scene after it explains the stopping ("It happens without her...") a little. The very last paragraph about the envelope in the sock drawer is the right end and the explanation before it can be trimmed.

Endings: "Her mother puts her forehead on the steering wheel and stays there" is a clean end (though a break would help). The jacket-fight door-standing and the bread-and-salt dinner is the best pair of beats in the chapter. The final envelope-under-socks ending lands and echoes chapter 3's envelope.

Viewpoint: Close on Chloe. The doctor-office scene is very tightly inside her; the Prahl scenes too. One paragraph ("She can't work out what's wrong with it, because it's faster than her class...") is long, argumentative interior summary.

What would make it worse: adding date stamps to every Thursday, or a bridge sentence that states Chloe has "given up".

## Chapter 09: February

Join in: Chapter 8 ends in late January with the envelope in the sock drawer and Chloe having stopped asking. Chapter 9 opens "February passes with the telephone silent". The join is smooth in time but the opening paragraph is one long block of summary (the sheet, the lunch tray, the plant worksheet, Dr. Ammons visits) before any scene. - The next section is a quick montage of each person reading the thread in a different place (Nadia at the register, Kavi at the board, Sam on a bench, Chloe at the counter). It is a good way to move between five people, though the device is the same as in chapters 24 and 32, and by now it is a habit. The best line is Nadia's sleeves ("They go up when something is going to cost her"). Chloe's entry ends the section in a plain statement of her view, which is a good beat.
- "Theo could have written cleaner rules than anyone else at that table" opens a new section with a retrospective statement, followed by his two chat lines and "So the job falls to Chloe." It is a clear hinge. The next paragraphs about building the entry and the exit and the five-week test are summary of the technical work. These are short. But the time order within the chapter is not clear: the test runs five weeks, Chloe finishes the rules, the sign-offs go "over the weeks that follow", and Theo signs "at the far end of the test". A reader cannot tell if the test is going on before, during or after the rules, or when the go-ahead happens. Add one marker such as "while the test runs".
- "Chloe turns twenty-one in August, two months after she adds the last page" is a flash-forward inside a chapter dated May to June. It reads as a throwaway line, and it also tells the reader when the last page is done. Cut, or fold into the dateline.
- The rules paragraphs ("The first page is the shortest to write and the surest...") are an explanation of her rule-writing in a mixture of summary and quotation-free paraphrase. They are clear but long, and the key idea (any one of them can stop it, no reason needed) is stated three times: once in the summary, once in the stop clause paragraph, and once in the argument. The Ruth and Nadia argument over the one-sentence reason is the best scene of the chapter's second half. "a sentence is a case. if youre building a case youre not stopping, youre asking" is a very sharp line. It ends well on "strike it".
- The sign-off sequence is a clean device: each person signs in one line, in a style that fits them (Eli "in", Nadia "fine. im in", Kavi "looks fine", Sam waiting for a number, Ruth "the numbers stand", Theo "still the stupidest thing any of us has ever done / in"). It takes only half a page and carries a lot of character. It is the best use of the chat in this chapter.
- The last paragraph, in Chloe's close view, says "She holds a clearance... and she is about to spend it on a file it meant to keep sealed from her too. What that costs her she works through once, on the drive home the week the document arrives, and the thought ends in about as long as it takes a light to change." The idea is right and the last image is good, but the passage is an explanation of her inner state placed after the strong sign-off list. End on Theo's "in".

Endings: Best: Ruth's "number of cameras" line; "strike it"; Theo's double line ("still the stupidest thing... in"). Weakest: the final explanation of the clearance.

Viewpoint: The chapter is shared among six people. The close third touches Theo first, then Nadia, Kavi, Sam, Chloe in turn, and Eli. Shifts are made by place and a clear first noun. It is the most diffuse chapter so far because it has no single person's eyes; the reader's centre of gravity is the chat.

Chapter ending to next: The clearance line gives a mild pull. The next chapter, The Files, answers it directly.

What would make it worse: adding a scene of Theo's change of mind with a long speech, or restating the stop clause a fourth time.

## Chapter 34: The Files

Join in: Chapter 33 ended on Chloe's thought about spending her clearance, with the group signed in. Chapter 34 opens "They open it in seven different places, each at a different hour, each of them alone with it when it happens," dated June 2026. This is a close, continuous join: "it" is the file, the title says so, and the reader is already waiting. It is the best direct chapter-to-chapter handoff in the back third. The opening sentence is a strong hook. The paragraph it begins, however, is a single block of about two hundred words that sets seven people in seven rooms, one clause each, including Theo's twenty minutes outside. A ninth grader will lose track of who is who. Keep it, but split into two or three short paragraphs, or reduce to the three people the chapter needs (Chloe, Ruth, Theo).

Scene shape: The seven places; the file's contents in summary (header, the details on each person, the standing line about contact, the oldest pages); the incident report on the Waffle House night; a chat in three tiny bursts; Ruth's confession; Chloe and Nadia on the same page; "how good are we"; Chloe's "were about to find out"; Theo goes back in.

Weak joins:
- The file-summary section runs about a page and a half in a calm, almost inventory voice. Some of it is lovely (Kavi's one-word margin note, "continuity"; Chloe's entry dated the week she signed her lease; the standing line rewritten for years "by a clerk who has stopped expecting the retyping to change anything"). Some of it reads as description of the file as an object: its paper, its scan grain, its thickness, the word changing from "students". For a ninth grader this is the slowest part of the chapter. Keep the personal items and cut the object description.
- Theo's note is a dropped thread: "mine has a name in it I already know" and Sam's "whose name". It is a good hook, but it sits in the middle of the file-summary and then disappears; Theo closes the laptop and goes outside, and the book never goes back to the name in this chapter. It is clearly meant as a promise; if so, a single later callback must pay it. If not, cut.
- The incident-report passage is the best writing in the chapter, and the joins inside it are clean: "And there's an incident report from four years back." then flat short paragraphs, each adding a fact (seven men, three firearms, twenty-two seconds, "a blank on what the students looked like", the footage unrecoverable, the observation unit's own recordings gone). The last detail, that the unit watching the school lost its own recordings of that night, is the real hit. This is the second time the book retells a scene the reader saw earlier from a hostile record (chapter 29 did it with the four a.m. drill), and the second telling is stronger because it is shorter and because it turns on Ruth's box from chapter 16. The reader gets a satisfying click. Do not add any explanation of how the box worked.
- The line about Chloe reading "blank" as a translation problem is a good small character beat, a bit long. The paragraph explaining the scan ("the text sitting slightly crooked on every page...") is a tell about what the author expects the reader to infer; cut.
- The three short chat bursts that follow ("thats us", Ruth's box, Kavi's "read that back") are each separated by a break line. The rhythm is nearly right, but the breaks are used as pauses for individual people to read, and they make the page jumpy. Merge the first two.
- The sequence "Sam's phone buzzes... a good twenty minutes before they're stood down" and Sam answering "inside a minute" earlier are not consistent. The chat's speed is blurred by the many stage-setting paragraphs.
- The paragraph "On the page about who is in contact with whom, Chloe and Nadia sit in the same list under the same date. Sam has been between them at every table since they were children and the file has no way of knowing it" is confusing and its point is unclear. Cut.
- The Ruth, Kavi, Nadia exchange ("i was thirteen" / "it wasnt even hard" / "and four years later the federal government wrote 'no known method'") is the heart of the chapter and works through repetition ("yes", "yes"). "how good are we" and "i have never once been at the top of a room in my life" is a strong turn, and Eli's line is the best single line of his character in the book. Keep it.
- The paragraph after it explains where each person is looking (Eli's tap, Ruth's ceiling, Chloe's empty box). It is a long piece of staging between two lines of chat. Trim to Eli's tap and Chloe typing.
- Chloe's "were about to find out" and "it wasnt supposed to be one" is the right ending. The final sentence, about Theo finishing his twenty minutes and going back in, closes the frame from the first paragraph. It is a quiet image and carries a faint danger. It would be stronger if the preceding chat line were allowed to stand last; the Theo line is a second ending that softens the hit. Choose one.

Endings: Best: "were about to find out" / "it wasnt supposed to be one"; "i have never once been at the top of a room in my life"; "yes", "yes". Weakest: the Theo frame line as a second ending.

Viewpoint: Spreads over all seven in the opening and then follows Chloe, Ruth and Kavi in turn through the chat. The shifts are by place, signalled well; the many place-setting lines slow the chat. It is the same "ensemble" mode used in chapters 32 and 33, and the reader is well trained to it by now.

Chapter ending to next: Strong. "Were about to find out" is a direct hand-off to the final two chapters (Nine Minutes, Seventy-Five).

What would make it worse: explaining how Ruth's box beat the cameras, or giving Theo's secret name a reveal here.

## Chapter 35: Nine Minutes

Join in: Chapter 34 ended on Chloe's "were about to find out" and Theo going back inside, with the group about to read the government's file and (they assumed) act on it. Chapter 35 opens "The financial worm goes quiet on a Thursday in June." So the promise at the end of 34 is answered by a different problem: the first worm, not the second, and not the file. A reader who expected an intrusion plot has to adjust. The new line is a good hook in itself, and the thread (someone is playing with their tool) is a fair escalation, but the join needs one sign that this is a new development and not the thing "we are about to find out". Note that the dateline begins in June, the same month as chapter 34, so the book moves almost without a gap. A plain opening line placing the first night in the days after the files were read would help.

Scene shape: Eli's discovery and Kavi's check; a short chat; a break; two more gaps and the group's reactions; a chat argument; a break; Ruth on page seven and a chat plan; a break; Chloe at midnight and the next day.

Weak joins:
- The opening paragraph is about two hundred words of setting (the second desk, the dark apartment, the terminal in a window, router resets on the far side of the planet) before the key event. The second paragraph then restates the key event ("A check-in doesn't arrive"), so the first paragraph's own account ("the check-in misses its window") appears twice. Start at the missed check-in and let the desk come in a clause.
- The three-log paragraph and the page-five paragraph are a sequence of Eli's checks. They are logical and calm, but each one is long. The clause of the rules ("an external party observing, recording, or interacting with the tool") is quoted and re-read, a good tie-back to chapter 33, but it takes a paragraph to make a simple point: by their own definition, nothing has happened. Cut to one reading of the clause.
- Kavi's paragraph is long and almost entirely interior (his watcher, his habit of distrusting a clean log). The images are good ("rereading his own handwriting, checking for a lie he'd have had to tell himself first"). The scene ends with Eli's first chat lines, a sharp short exchange. The two-line chat ("somebody stopped it and started it and cleaned up after themselves" / "if it was the government we would be in custody") is the true end of the section, and it is a good one.
- "It happens twice more in six weeks." is a clean hinge. The paragraph that follows is a roll-call of how each person is spending the summer (Eli, Ruth, Kavi, Theo, Sam), a device from earlier chapters. By now it is mechanical, and it delays the event. Keep one line per person at most.
- The second occurrence ("the second Tuesday of July") is told in a way that is hard to follow: Kavi flags it in two minutes, nine minutes again "to the second this time", he sends the two timestamps to Eli alone. The reader must hold three timestamps in mind. The point (the identical number twice means "measured") is clear and a good small idea. The chat argument that follows (Chloe: he is telling us he is there; Eli: no, he made a mistake) is the strongest piece of the chapter: two readings of the same fact, argued in a few lines. Eli's "hes very slightly wrong about how good we are" is a very good line.
- "By the third time the days are properly hot" is another time-hop in the same paragraph as the heat description. It reads as a stage direction. The detail that the gap "runs longer than it has before" is the real news and is buried in the middle of a sentence. Make it its own short paragraph.
- Ruth's section, "Ruth starts typing, stops, and starts again before she sends anything," is a clear scene opening. Page seven of the rules is a good concrete anchor, and the chat that follows, with "so he doesnt know we know" and Theo's closing line ("how many has he made that we didnt catch"), is the chapter's best forward-looking beat. It ends with a paragraph of small gestures (laptop, laundry, screen dimming) that dissolves the line's force. End on Theo's line.
- The last section is the weakest. It opens in the middle of the night ("It's still warm enough at midnight that Chloe has the window cracked"), with "Chloe reads that at one in the morning" (what is "that"?). The paragraph about her badge and the elevator is a stray aside. Then she tells Ruth to watch the thread, and the text ends with a vague sentence ("Her eyes close on the version of tonight that ends here, not on the one that keeps going somewhere she'd have to stay awake to follow") that tries to be ominous and instead is hard to parse. The last line, "texts Ruth once before lunch to ask if the night held", is a clear hook if the reader knows something is coming; it is the only clear line in the section. This is the book's "go to sleep and miss it" device, and it works only if the previous line is plain. Rewrite in words: make the night concrete and end on the text to Ruth.

Endings: Best: Eli's "hes very slightly wrong about how good we are"; Theo's "how many has he made that we didnt catch"; "texts Ruth once before lunch". Weakest: the sleeping sentence and the page-seven aftermath.

Viewpoint: Eli, Kavi, Ruth and Chloe in turn, plus the chat, each with a clear first noun and a place. The viewpoint is dispersed but readable. The point of view is the group's, so the reader sees every perspective on the same problem except the one that matters (the watcher's).

Chapter ending to next: A real if oddly written hook. The next title is Seventy-Five and the reader is ready for something to happen.

What would make it worse: explaining who is behind the nine minutes, or adding a chat line in which Chloe says she is worried.
