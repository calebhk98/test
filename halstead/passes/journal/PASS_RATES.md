# Pass rates and reactions

Lens: how many of the cohort succeed at each skill, how fast, and whether staff and outsiders react to those rates in a believable way.

## Chapter 01: Before

Pre-Halstead. Chloe, age six, is far ahead in an ordinary school. She sees the subtraction worksheet as "all thirty are just one problem", reads a chapter book ahead of the class, and has a pediatrician send her for an attention evaluation.
- Rate: one child, an outlier in a normal class. Nobody sees this as giftedness.
- Reaction check: this is the book's baseline for how real adults react to a very bright child, and it is believable. Teacher Mrs. Aldana reads it as inattention ("trouble focusing"), takes the book away, and the doctor finds "None of this is medical." The teacher does say she is "among the strongest readers she's had, and she said it twice", so the signal is noticed but pointed in the wrong direction.
- Useful for later: this sets up that ordinary adults under-react or misread. Halstead's staff will be measured against this.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

Chloe, age six, is tested by a psychologist (Dr. Prentice). Block design, similarities, digit span (eight forward, six backward), coding, matrices, vocabulary.
- Rate: one child. Top of the scale on reasoning and talking, ordinary on processing speed. "On a couple of them she answered every item the form has for a six-year-old."
- Plausibility: good. A real child with a spiky profile (high verbal and reasoning, average coding speed) is a believable result, and the average "says bright kid, but bright kid undersells the room".
- Reaction: Prentice is the book's one realistic expert reaction. He notices, refuses to give the number, and warns that "a bad number is the kind that gets a school to do something." That is a correct description of how schools respond. He also names the real system lever (gifted program starts in third grade).
- Outsider reaction to compare later: the school does nothing. "The rest of them put it in the folder." Report card says "meeting expectations in all areas."
- Note: this is the only place the book shows a measured percentile. Later chapters never return to numbers like this for the cohort, which is a gap (see whole book).

## Chapter 03: The Letter

Chloe is invited to a free four-week summer session. No pass rates yet. Reactions are parental and the district's.
- Staff reaction: the school phone line answers every question "so fast I ran out of things to ask her". That is the first sign of a staff trained to answer. The one soft spot is "their list is usually pretty good", which the mother catches as the only non-fact.
- Outsider reactions: district office has no record and warns of a scam. Mother does real due diligence (postmark, neighbours, web search finding nothing but the school's own site). Father notices the faculty page (Olympic swimmer, SEAL, two master's degrees each) and goes quiet. The parents' wariness is proportionate and believable.
- Slight weakness: a school with "over a hundred children" a summer, "every year since" four years ago, and no news, forum or parent mention at all is a stronger red flag than the family treats it. They go in nine days. The deciding factor is the child's flat mood, which is believable emotionally, but the absence of any independent trace is accepted a little fast.
- Number: "over a hundred" summer children, "one of the closer ones" at four hours. Later cohort is about ninety, so watch whether the summer pool of 100+ versus the final cohort gets explained (it is a selection filter, which is a good place for a pass-rate story).

## Chapter 04: Pluto

Summer session, about a hundred children. Spread of knowledge among six and seven year olds: Ruth corrects a teacher on mass versus weight, Kavi explains viscosity, Priya knows horse measurements, Owen is still on a worksheet and Sam is wrong about volcanoes.
- Rate: a roomful of unusually knowledgeable kids, but with visible spread. Sam is wrong, Owen is slow, Chloe is shy. That is a good sign: not everyone is a prodigy in every subject.
- Staff reaction: Mr. Doyle's "Mass, and thank you for catching that" is the model reaction. Brisk, warm, no fuss, no punishment. It fits staff told not to make a show, and it also fits a good teacher. Nothing here is mute or overblown. The staff are specifically kind about homesickness (same-day exit promise, phone, nurse), which is credible for a recruiter that wants parents to trust.
- Outsider reactions: parents are mostly anxious about safety, not about ability. Plausible for a first day.
- Spread: the summer pool is selected (identified by "networks"), so a high base level is believable. A six-year-old explaining viscosity is on the high end but a few such children in a hundred picked for it is fine.
- Watch for later: the session seems to have many children who are already at a high level. The question of what share were cut after the summer is not answered yet.
