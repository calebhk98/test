# Pass rates and reactions

Lens: how many of the cohort succeed at each skill, how fast, and whether staff and outsiders react to those rates in a believable way.

## Chapter 01: Before

Pre-Halstead. Chloe, age six, is far ahead in an ordinary school. She sees the subtraction worksheet as "all thirty are just one problem", reads a chapter book ahead of the class, and has a pediatrician send her for an attention evaluation.
- Rate: one child, an outlier in a normal class. Nobody sees this as giftedness.
- Reaction check: this is the book's baseline for how real adults react to a very bright child, and it is believable. Teacher Mrs. Aldana reads it as inattention ("trouble focusing"), takes the book away, and the doctor finds "None of this is medical." The teacher does say she is "among the strongest readers she's had, and she said it twice", so the signal is noticed but pointed in the wrong direction.
- Useful for later: this sets up that ordinary adults under-react or misread. Halstead's staff will be measured against this.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

Chloe, age six, is tested by a psychologist (Dr. Prentice). Block design, similarities, digit span (eight forward, six backward), coding, matrices, vocabulary.
- Rate: one child. Top of the scale on reasoning and talking, ordinary on processing speed. "On a couple of them she answered every item the form has for a six-year-old."
- Plausibility: good. A real child with a spiky profile (high verbal and reasoning, average coding speed) is a believable result, and the average "says bright kid, but bright kid undersells the room".
- Reaction: Prentice is the book's one realistic expert reaction. He notices, refuses to give the number, and warns that "a bad number is the kind that gets a school to do something." That is a correct description of how schools respond. He also names the real system lever (gifted program starts in third grade).
- Outsider reaction to compare later: the school does nothing. "The rest of them put it in the folder." Report card says "meeting expectations in all areas."
- Note: this is the only place the book shows a measured percentile. Later chapters never return to numbers like this for the cohort, which is a gap (see whole book).

## Chapter 03: The Letter

Chloe is invited to a free four-week summer session. No pass rates yet. Reactions are parental and the district's.
- Staff reaction: the school phone line answers every question "so fast I ran out of things to ask her". That is the first sign of a staff trained to answer. The one soft spot is "their list is usually pretty good", which the mother catches as the only non-fact.
- Outsider reactions: district office has no record and warns of a scam. Mother does real due diligence (postmark, neighbours, web search finding nothing but the school's own site). Father notices the faculty page (Olympic swimmer, SEAL, two master's degrees each) and goes quiet. The parents' wariness is proportionate and believable.
- Slight weakness: a school with "over a hundred children" a summer, "every year since" four years ago, and no news, forum or parent mention at all is a stronger red flag than the family treats it. They go in nine days. The deciding factor is the child's flat mood, which is believable emotionally, but the absence of any independent trace is accepted a little fast.
- Number: "over a hundred" summer children, "one of the closer ones" at four hours. Later cohort is about ninety, so watch whether the summer pool of 100+ versus the final cohort gets explained (it is a selection filter, which is a good place for a pass-rate story).

## Chapter 04: Pluto

Summer session, about a hundred children. Spread of knowledge among six and seven year olds: Ruth corrects a teacher on mass versus weight, Kavi explains viscosity, Priya knows horse measurements, Owen is still on a worksheet and Sam is wrong about volcanoes.
- Rate: a roomful of unusually knowledgeable kids, but with visible spread. Sam is wrong, Owen is slow, Chloe is shy. That is a good sign: not everyone is a prodigy in every subject.
- Staff reaction: Mr. Doyle's "Mass, and thank you for catching that" is the model reaction. Brisk, warm, no fuss, no punishment. It fits staff told not to make a show, and it also fits a good teacher. Nothing here is mute or overblown. The staff are specifically kind about homesickness (same-day exit promise, phone, nurse), which is credible for a recruiter that wants parents to trust.
- Outsider reactions: parents are mostly anxious about safety, not about ability. Plausible for a first day.
- Spread: the summer pool is selected (identified by "networks"), so a high base level is believable. A six-year-old explaining viscosity is on the high end but a few such children in a hundred picked for it is fine.
- Watch for later: the session seems to have many children who are already at a high level. The question of what share were cut after the summer is not answered yet.

## Chapter 05: Behind

Summer session weeks one to three. Bridge-building (gap of eighteen inches): breaks at 3 pounds for the first group, Chloe's group holds to 11, Ruth's to 19. Fractions: Chloe does eight problems right without the reason. Swimming: Chloe cannot swim at first, one length with a stop by the end of week two, Sam two lengths, Ruth a full length on a Thursday. Writing in fifteen minutes: Chloe six sentences, Ruth a page and a half, Kavi most of a page, Owen two sentences with one struck, Sam four.
- Rate and spread: this is the best-shaped spread in the book. Some bridges fail at three pounds, results range from 3 to 19, Owen cannot follow the fraction reason, and the "more leave before the end of the month" line implies attrition. Chloe, who is average among these, is "wrong by three pounds" and gets the math concept late at night.
- Staff reaction: Mr. Baptiste ("Good, that's the question"), Mr. Doyle and Deb all treat results as ordinary. Deb spending two days on bubbles shows a patient normal teacher. Baptiste's "a rule that survives every case you can throw at it is what a rule is" is a notch over a typical teacher's answer, but fine. No staff member shows surprise at a class that does fractions by flipping on week two.
- Gap: children are leaving in numbers ("by the fourth time") and the book gives no number of departures or any sign staff track pace. Owen leaving after the "I've lost the thread" lesson is the book's only visible failure case. The reason given is "wanting to be home", not ability, which understates the possibility that some children are simply outpaced. A realistic selective program would have some of those too.
- Note: swimming from non-swimmer to a length in two weeks is quick but plausible for a six-year-old with lessons every day.

## Chapter 06: The List

End of the summer. Chloe reports twenty-eight days of learning: CPR, bridge, swimming, fractions, Spanish, sign language, an invented-language puzzle, first aid, cooking, programming, chess, karate falls, nerf guns. Parents at the hotel compare notes.
- Rate: the book states no pass rates here. Everyone seems to be doing Spanish at lunch ("Everybody else at that table is doing Spanish as well"). Kavi gets the endings in the language puzzle, Ruth the plurals, Chloe is half behind. Sam does not get the CPR click. That is a decent small spread.
- Number check: Chloe says she "wrote fourteen and was wrong by three" for the bridge. Chapter 5 said her group held to eleven, so fourteen minus eleven is three. Consistent. Chapter 5 only said she wrote "a low number", which is a small softness, but fourteen of a bridge that held eleven is not very low, so not a real conflict.
- Staff reaction: staff are consistent with the hide-it brief. Nothing is said to parents about how the month compares to other children. The parent hotel gets "the list is a good list", with no figures.
- Outsider reaction, parents: the hotel conversation is excellent. They are suspicious, can not name what the school is, and the one mother's boast ("top of her reading group at home") is deflated by "Here it buys you a paper plate." That is a believable, proportionate reaction.
- Chloe's father's reaction ("Cool") followed by the car-speaker call ("they taught her CPR... I don't know what that is") is proportionate, if on the quiet side. Four weeks of CPR, Spanish and fractions reasoning in a six-year-old would register, and it does, privately.
- Selection: the children are told there is a "day where they look at you" in the fall. This is the first mention that the cohort is picked in a further step, which makes the later cohort of about ninety a filtered group from over a hundred.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room

Aftermath of the summer. Chloe back in second grade, bored again, and her mother asks the home school about acceleration.
- Rate: no cohort numbers. Chloe says she "finished the third-grade work in October" (this is her mother's phrase to the school, so it is a claim that the mother must have formed from Chloe's work or the camp). That is a mild claim for a Halstead-trained child.
- Outsider reaction, the home school: the most realistic reaction in the early book. "Third grade is the floor, whatever the testing comes out at." Testing only in spring, program the following fall. A real school would be this rigid. Ms. Vance is warm and ordinary and responds to the crying properly.
- Outsider reaction, the library: the librarian's "That's a real month, that is" is warm and mild. Fine for a librarian who has no way to judge the details.
- Staff reaction: Halstead's office is accommodating ("we sent the message out that same week") but keeps families anonymous. That fits a program that wants children kept inside their own group.
- Plausibility: the book here uses ordinary adults to show how a normal system under-reacts to unusual children. This sets a baseline that later makes any under-reaction by outsiders in later chapters consistent. The risk is later outsiders (college admissions, Army) reacting no more strongly than the second-grade teacher did.
- Small point: Chloe's loss of reading focus in this chapter is emotional, not a skill failure. Nothing to track for pass rates.

## Chapter 08: The Asking

Chloe in second grade, depressed, with a therapist, then a pull-out enrichment class (nine kids, Mrs. Prahl). Knights-and-knaves puzzle sheet of twelve problems.
- Rate: Chloe finishes the first puzzle before it is read out, and the packet of twelve "in under an hour". Dev solves the first about ninety seconds later. Hana is careful and slow but finishes at the same time as Chloe in pairs. So in a pull-out group of nine picked from a school of about 26 per class, at least three children solve puzzles of this type quickly. Plausible for a gifted pull-out.
- Staff reaction: Mrs. Prahl is the book's best example of an ordinary teacher meeting a fast child. She learns every name, makes "a real thing out of" Dev's board work, tells Chloe "Some children get there in October and some get there in May." She does not notice that Chloe is bored to the point of withholding work. This under-reaction is believable for a teacher who covers five buildings.
- Outsider reaction, the school: grants a one-year early start as an exception after weeks. Mild and bureaucratic. Believable.
- Outsider reaction, the therapist: asks about the summer program ("Did anything happen to her there?") in a way that sounds like a worried clinician and not like someone who thinks the program is remarkable. Believable.
- Parent reaction: mother's "I still don't have one good enough" reason to say no is emotionally real. Neither parent asks why a month-long free school would leave their child with a stronger measured response than a therapist.
- Spread: Chloe is described as the fastest in the pull-out. Compare later against Halstead where she is mid-pack. That contrast is the book's main point about selection and works.

## Chapter 09: February

Chloe begs, parents say yes, the school offers a free furnished house, and there is an April visit day.
- Rate: none stated. The admissions visit is observational ("No test arrives"). There is no number for how many summer children are invited back, and no sense of rejection. For a school that put over a hundred through the summer, the book never shows what share is asked to stay.
- Staff reaction: Mrs. Okonkwo handles questions with the practised line "I can't tell you who's enrolled here." The correction of Ruth's rumour ("We take people all year round") is a little quick, and it contradicts Ruth's claim, which could suggest staff want to dampen competitive talk about admissions. Plausible for a staff trained to hide stakes.
- Outsider reaction, parents: the mother's refusal of the house ("they are not getting the roof over her head as well") is a believable, shrewd reaction to a too-generous offer. The father reads contracts for a living and says nothing to counter. Believable.
- Missing: a real school offering free housing, free tuition, and rolling admission to a child after a day of play-based observation would have a stated reason for wanting her. The family never asks what Halstead saw in her that day. The father gets no feedback and the mother asks about the nurse. A little under-curious, but consistent with the book's theme of parents not understanding the place.

## Chapter 10: April

Chloe enrolls mid-year at seven. Third floor, new arrivals. Numbers: "of the ninety who were at camp, twenty-some came back that first fall, and a few more have drifted in a term at a time since." Seven subjects each, with a cap of ten.
- Rate: this is a key number. Only about 20 to 30 of the camp's ninety enrolled the first fall. That is a pass rate of roughly a quarter to a third for the first conversion, but it is a family choice, not an ability cut. The staff say so explicitly: people go home "because their family decides", "not for being bad at something."
- Number conflict: chapters 3 and 4 give "over a hundred" and "hundred-odd" for the summer. Chapter 10 says "ninety who were at camp". Minor; attrition ("more leave before the end of the month") could account for it, but the book should say whether this is the starting or finishing count. It also matters for the later cohort of about ninety, which this chapter implies starts around 20 and grows by trickle. See later chapters for how the cohort grows to ninety.
- Spread: Chloe is "behind in Spanish, mathematics, writing, and history, but has caught up in literature." Her wheel-mark problem takes days; Ruth solves it faster. The staff will not tell her which line of a stack of pages is wrong beyond the second. That is a believable portrait of real difficulty for a newcomer, and it is the first time the book shows a child struggling across the board.
- Staff reaction: the teachers are exactly in keeping with the hide-it instruction: no list, no grade, no praise, "tells you whether it is true and stops there." They never say anything about how well she is doing relative to anyone. Plausible and consistent, if a little perfect.
- Outsider reactions: parents are pleased and relieved. The Olympian swimming teacher is reported by "somebody's mom" rather than staff, a plausible leak. 
- Note: Owen "was keeping up" in Kavi's telling. So the book's Owen case is a deliberate non-ability departure. That undercuts any worry that the cohort contains a hidden drop-out rate by performance, but it also means the book never shows a child removed or leaving for ability.

## Chapter 11: Eight

Age eight. The nerf-dart interception game starts. Ranking sheet is posted at end of term with "ninety names". Chloe is ninetieth of ninety in drawing and in dance. Politics, French and logic added.
- Nerf rate: "By the end of the first month everybody in the year can do it at will." The game is dart against dart, where a dart knocked down before it crosses the net puts you out. "Both sides get good at the same rate and neither ever runs out of people." This is the cohort's first full-class mastery, and the book treats it as 100 percent success in about four weeks, at age eight.
  - Plausibility: low as stated. Shooting down a foam dart with a foam dart is hard, and a realistic spread would have a clear top third and some children never getting reliable. "Neither ever runs out of people" is the odd part: if everyone were equally good, rounds would still end. The line only works if everyone is near-equal.
  - Staff reaction: Coach Bell does not set it up expecting failure. He pre-empts doubt ("It isn't impossible... You'll all be bad at it until Thursday") and states that the task is the easiest thing in the building. That is a staff that wants children to believe it is easy, which fits the hide-it brief. The weakness is that Bell predicts mastery and gets it, then nobody reacts to a class succeeding in a month. Real coaches who see a whole year-group of eight-year-olds do this would at least comment to each other. Staff say nothing.
- Ranking spread: Chloe is last of ninety in drawing and dance and ranks are posted openly. This is the best spread evidence in the book so far: someone has to be last, and she is an otherwise strong student. It is also plausible that a drawing ranking exists. It does clash a little with the claim elsewhere that there are "no grades" and that staff do not tell children how they compare (here the sheet is on the wall).
  - Her fortieth hand drawing "looks like a hand" and Ruth says "Last term you were drawing mittens." Realistic slow skill growth.
- Choir: Chloe matches Amara about a third of the time. Another real failure, and she quits, which is believable.
- Staff reaction, Mrs. Okonkwo: flat "No" to dropping dance. Fits a staff that treats rank as irrelevant. Believable and a good detail.
- Number conflict: chapter 10 said only "twenty-some" of the camp came back that first fall and "a few more drifted in". Here the year sheet has "ninety names." The cohort grew from a quarter to the full ninety between April and the next autumn without comment. Either the summer group of a hundred all came, or the earlier number was only this floor's new arrivals.
- Outsider reactions: the parents see an eight-year-old who argues a hot dog is a sandwich and wins. The father admits to being beaten ("nobody's ever put the hinge to me"). Proportionate and funny. Nothing yet about pass rates.

## Chapter 12: Nine

Age nine. Course load is eight subjects (it rises by one each year). Mandarin, chemistry, statistics, biology, textiles, first aid under stress, riding, climbing.
- Mandarin: reading comes fast ("novels" by February, ten-page reports in Mandarin by March, at nine, in her first year of the language). Speech lags: "a stranger loses her by the second sentence." Spread: "half the room has" the tone problem, Ruth is far ahead. Plausibility: reading novels in Mandarin in about five months is far beyond real learners, even full-time. The speaking weakness is the realistic part and is exactly what a plausible curve would look like.
- Bridge: Chloe and Bex build one that carries nineteen pounds. This matches Ruth's summer score of nineteen. Fine, though a nine-year-old repeat of the same task after a year of schooling reaching only the same load as a seven-year-old triangle build is slightly flat.
- Climbing: no one makes the overhang in the year until January, when "a girl in the year below" does. Sam falls repeatedly, makes it May 11th. Believable and a good failure record: a hard task that most of ninety children do not conquer early. 
- Riding: Chloe can canter and jump a low fence by March but loses interest. Priya is better. Textiles: Chloe drafts two bad shirts. Chemistry: Chloe gets gray, not white. All believable partial failures.
- Staff reaction: Mrs. Sun, Vasquez and the biology man all treat results as routine. Vasquez's "which of the three" is a patient method teacher. The staff know and manage the pace of the cohort (one more subject each year, ages fixed). They refuse Kavi's request to take cryptography at ten, which shows the school controls pacing by age. Believable.
- Outsider reaction: her father reads a vendor proposal with her and says "I would have signed it on Tuesday." This is a realistic and proportionate reaction to a nine-year-old spotting an unfalsifiable deliverable. He does not question what the school teaches; this under-reaction is consistent with prior chapters.
- Spread: Iyad "never wrong" about everyone's numbers. Bex finishes sentences and takes credit. This is human texture, not a pass-rate point, but it shows rank and rivalry among the ninety.
- Flag: first aid drill under a shouting bystander at nine and a posted-ranking system point to staff testing under stress; no rates given.

## Chapter 13: Ten Pages

Age ten. Ten-page essays weekly (Hearn). 10v1 sparring begins in January, goal a minute. Paintball ball-on-ball interception begins in October. Defensive Watch drills begin, every first drill failed.
- 10v1 numbers: first day best in the room is nine seconds, Chloe four then six. "By March most of the year is in the single figures on a good day", Sam fourteen seconds in February (the year's record), Chloe eleven by June. Target is sixty seconds ("the whole fight").
  - Plausibility: good. Nobody comes close to the stated goal, times are short and show a real spread, one outlier (Sam) is named. The only odd note: the staff set the goal at sixty and the best at year end is fourteen. A real instructor might revise the goal, but a staff hiding how impressive these children are is a believable setter of a goal nobody hits.
  - Staff reaction: Kowalczyk is calm and says the number "is the shape of every long fight there is". She does not comment on ten-year-olds fighting off ten people for even four seconds. Neither does anyone mention injuries (a bloody nose is the only one).
- Paintball intercept: Chloe gets a ball-on-ball hit on her fourth go on a Monday, and by Thursday she and Ruth have nineteen. Kavi, Nadia and Priya have it within weeks and "by Christmas most of the year is past where they ever were with darts."
  - Plausibility: low. Hitting a paintball, which leaves the barrel at a fixed speed, with another paintball is extremely hard. The insight (watch the barrel) is realistic, but turning it into nineteen hits in a few days and most of a year-group by December is the sort of uniform success the book should be questioned on. The best part is the rate by individual: Ruth, Chloe, Bex, Kavi, Nadia, Priya each get it on different dates, with Kavi saying his "number goes from three to double figures inside an hour." That is a believable individual learning curve, but the whole-year success by Christmas is too fast and too even.
  - Staff reaction: Bell puts Bex's explanation on the board and "runs the rest of the hour off it". He does not react with surprise at children solving a problem he apparently set. A real coach would have to have seen this discovered before, which he has not hinted at; his silence about the first successful intercepts, and taking the credit sheet as already written, reads like a well-trained staff, but also like a staff who never expected it.
  - The credit mix-up: Bex gets the board, Chloe and Ruth get none. This is social, not about rates.
- Defensive Watch: first drill, "every name has an F". Failure is stated and graded against a standard no one can see. This is a realistic counterweight: a staff that fails all of a group on the first drill. Scored and posted, not hidden. The staff do not hide rank here; they hide only the meaning.
- Outsider reaction: father at Thanksgiving, ten-year-old arguing tariffs: "You've got most of an argument there." Proportionate, even a touch low, for a ten-year-old making a conditional argument about defence industry. He reacts with the quiet pleasure of a parent.
- Hearn's blank-note essay and Burke next: a staff member quietly escalating a child without telling her. A good teacher reaction. Note that a ten-page essay a week at ten is a very high output, but the book includes the weaker version (Chloe's essay is "comfortable", not right) so it does not read as uniform success.
- Spread: Chloe mediocre at every instrument but not the pitch-run (she keeps the block). Fine.

## Chapter 14: Sixty Degrees

Age eleven. Archery (butts at twenty to fifty meters, then arrow-on-arrow collisions at sixty degrees, lanes seventy meters apart). Latin, calculus, astronomy, rhetoric, art history, a 7,000-word essay (Chloe 28 pages, B). Whole-school Defensive Watch against staff.
- Arrow collision: Coach Bell says "shooting one down in the air is beyond everybody on this field. I have watched every one of you try it anyway; I have watched every one of you miss." He promises breakfast to the first hitter. Chloe has the first of hers in the last week of October, with Odile, so within about two months. "By March most of the year has managed a collision at least once", and Odile does it "three days out of five", twice in an hour in April. Chloe says she has done it "a dozen times, more than a dozen" by Christmas.
  - Plausibility: very low as written. Two arrows launched from lanes seventy meters apart meeting at the top of their arcs, timed by metronome, by eleven-year-olds, with a hit rate of most of a year-group within six months and one child doing it three days in five. Even the staff's own framing (the block is the real target, the collision is the "target" word) turns an extreme feat into a counting exercise. This is the single largest uniform-success claim so far and is the one that most needs either a lower rate (a handful of children, only by luck) or a visible staff reaction.
  - Staff reaction: Bell's line "the day somebody actually hits one I will buy the whole line breakfast" is the book's best set-up of a teacher expecting failure. The payoff is missing: when Odile and Chloe do it the line is "shouting", "somebody at the far end is banging on the equipment box lid with a bow stave", but no adult is shown responding. No breakfast, no call to anyone, no change in the program. A real coach who said that line would be in the grass. This is a slip by omission and the one best place to add a staff reaction, which could also be a hide-it slip (he says the wrong thing, then covers).
  - Wider context: Bell also frames the arrow as "beyond everybody", which fits a staff wanting children to believe it is hard in the way a normal sport is hard, but it also tells the reader the staff expects not to be beaten.
- Spread: Ruth's release is "late, always late", Chloe's early. The ranking among children is real: Odile is a clear outlier. Good as a spread signal; poor as a rate.
- Whole-school Watch: about ninety eleven-year-olds, no adults in the building left by 3:42, with Hearn holding out the longest. The board grades students a B and lists failures, including "at no point... did anybody establish how many adults were in the building." Plausibility: lower as stated (thirty-odd adults beaten by children, in a game the adults agree to lose to), but this is a drill where staff are sparring partners. The graded B and failure list is a good staff reaction: no gushing, a cold rubric, no mention that children took a building from adults.
- Essay: Hearn gives her a B on a 28-page essay with a note that finds the exact hedges she put in on purpose. Realistic, strong staff reaction (no inflation).
- Outsider reaction: her mother: "I think that's a big subject for a school essay... A B's a B." Her grandmother, on arrows hitting arrows: "once in an whole year is hardly a lot." Both are very muted. The grandmother does not understand, and Chloe cannot explain; that is believable. The parents ask nothing about the program at all about an eleven-year-old writing 28 pages on strategic bombing and doing daily archery. This starts to feel like an under-reaction by the family: a mother would not just wash dishes and say "A B's a B."
- Pass-rate inconsistency: Bell says "every one of you miss[es]" and the ninety children do at first, but the book never gives the first-day rate or whether any hit in October besides Chloe/Odile. The "by March most" figure is vague.

## Chapter 15: Twelve

Age twelve. Forge, anatomy, physics, psychology. An unannounced 4 a.m. Watch: four adult men are cracked on comms (AES-256 broken in "four seconds of compute"), lured, sedated with injections and tied up by four twelve-year-olds. 10v1 at Christmas: twenty-four seconds, "thirtieth out of ninety." Blind bread test: five of eight correct, scored as chance.
- 10v1 spread: a figure of 24 seconds sits at rank 30 of 90. That means the top third is at 24 seconds or more, the top of the year is well above, and the bottom third is much below. Good spread. It also implies the year-mid at ten was around 9 seconds and rose steeply. Plausible progress curve for a controlled drill, but 24 seconds against ten attackers of any skill level is a very high figure for twelve-year-olds.
- Watch result: "Response seven minutes fifty against a standard of six." Chloe C, Sam C, Ruth B minus, Kavi B. Staff reaction: Sinclair arrives, asks "Who's monitoring?", takes the respiration numbers, and says "Grading's in the morning, go to bed. Now." No comment on four adults sedated and tied by children, and no medical check beyond Ruth's pulse counts. 
  - Plausibility: staff reaction fits the hide-it brief and the school's idea that the drill is routine. As a real adult reaction it is too flat. A grown man who sees four tied-up, sedated adults and four children reading numbers off an arm would at least react physically (relief, a quick check of the men, a lift of the eyebrow). That Sinclair says nothing about the children's performance supports the intended theme, but it is a place where the book trades realism for the point.
  - Scoring detail: failing children for taking "seven minutes fifty" to subdue four armed intruders while grading a C is a believable harsh-rubric staff. Good.
  - The attackers: "They're actors." The actors accept being drugged (the school's kit has "the traceable one" and "the untraceable one"), tied, and interrogated by children without breaking character. A real contractor would balk at being sedated by a twelve-year-old. This is where outsider-reaction logic is weakest in the chapter. If they are actually real or hired intruders, their reaction is also muted: one man's "I don't know" is the only break.
- Chloe's "I'm bad at it, just like drawing and dance" repeats her measured weakness: acting. A good mark against uniform success.
- Spread elsewhere: Chloe's second hook scroll is wrong, Pruitt tells her "your heat's wrong." Sam says "Looks fine to me." Kavi's AES cracking is a stand-out skill, not shared by Chloe. The forge teacher does not praise; he says "You lost the curve on the second turn." Good.
- Bread blind test: five correct of eight, with Sam scoring it as "sixty-two and a half" and Ruth correcting "what a coin does". It shows the children are as bad at distinguishing as anyone, not superhuman. Believable.
- Outsider reaction, parents: mother's reaction to a gas oven lit at midnight is proportionate and funny and, notably, focused on the wrong event (she does not know about the 4 a.m. drill). The father hears "I'm twelve and I've been signed off on that oven for three years" and laughs. Parents never learn of the drugs and tied men. Plausible, but the book keeps parents away from any real mastery, which means the outsider-reaction test is never run on the parents for these skills.
- Dinner test: "Because you can come home." The mother repeats the exit offer. Realistic.

## Chapter 16: Thirteen

Age thirteen. Students teach the year below. Real analysis, differential equations, German, a materials lab, cryptography (breaking ciphers, live parking-system cipher), epistemology, law (Amberg: "at sixteen you'll take the bar... every citizen in this room ought to pass it"). A covert network is built.
- Marek fails: he does the mathematics but will not hand in a sheet, and "Marek fails the course, because a blank sheet leaves the mark scheme exactly one option." This is the cleanest real failure in the book so far, and the school's response is minimal (he is at breakfast in January, "expulsion is no part of it"). It is a good realism point that a child of this level can fail for non-ability reasons. It is also a mild slip: a genius being failed for a blank column and nobody in the staff reaching out is not what a real school with a 90-person cohort would do, but it fits the hide-it brief that rules are mechanical.
- Pass-rate claims: Amberg tells thirteen-year-olds that at sixteen "you'll take the bar... every citizen in this room ought to pass it." This is the first statement that the entire cohort is expected to pass the bar. Later chapters will test that.
- Math spread: of Chloe's seven students: Ivy and Tomas want answers, Beatriz arrives knowing it, one cries, two just work. A good spread. 
- Fast learners: Eli does the November cipher set in an evening, where the rest had a week. Beatriz arrives knowing most of the material. Marek outpaces Chloe's lesson plan. These are named outliers. Chloe "has" German in November; game theory in a fortnight, but is bad at fencing (an extra skill where she is weak). Good detail.
- Staff reaction, Sanders: after nineteen days she reconstructs a hidden recalibration in a paper's instrument logs. He "reads her five pages standing up, puts them in a folder, and says thank you." For a thirteen-year-old catching a probable data problem in a published paper, this is a muted reaction. It is consistent with a staff told to hide impressiveness; it reads as slightly too flat if the paper is real research. A postdoc would react or alert someone.
- Staff reaction, Amberg: says he "could take apart" the covert network "in an afternoon, but not one of them asks him to." So the staff either know or the students believe so. No reaction. Fits.
- Outsider reactions: father's "this stays between the two of us" about a four-millimeter riser is warm. Mother "Great improvement on the old lot." Parents again see nothing of the skills. No actual number appears from outside.
- Number: seven names in her class of twelves; dinner "a dozen". No count conflicts.

## Chapter 17: Fourteen

Age fourteen. Marksmanship replaces archery (rifle prone at fifty meters, then a hundred meters in crosswind, swinging target, then clay trap). 10v1: Chloe holds fifty seconds, average forty-five, "the longest anyone's held all week." Engineering project: locate a sound on a field within a hand's width. Chloe teaches the year below an error. Japanese, abstract algebra, logistics.
- 10v1 progression: age ten best nine seconds; age twelve, Chloe twenty-four and rank thirtieth; age fourteen, Chloe fifty and the best that week, average forty-five. Rank thirtieth at 24 s and best-of-week at 50 s in two years means the top end moved from about 14 to over 50. Internally consistent, but the speed of progress is high. The notable plausibility-preserver is that nobody reaches sixty. The target is still unmet. The "average forty-five" for a child who was thirtieth of ninety two years ago suggests a rise in the cohort average to about 40-plus seconds, which means the bottom of the year is still holding around thirty. For ten adult attackers that is extraordinary.
- Spread in marksmanship: Ruth puts "three rounds through one hole" at fifty meters, bored; Odile burns her hand; Chloe fumbles the bolt release for weeks. Aymar corrects Voss's stopwatch card. Voss's reaction: "Firing point four, you're up", then a new card appears. This is a good, small staff slip: Voss silently accepts a fourteen-year-old's correction of an official card and does not comment. Consistent with a staff told not to praise. 
- Staff reaction to skill growth: Kavi's line, "Six weeks. It was that on the nerf and that on the paint", reads as students noticing that everything is gated by six-week boredom, which is a believable sign that staff adjust difficulty up as soon as a skill is mastered. It helps explain uniform success: staff keep raising the bar. But the book never shows a child who stays stuck on rifle fundamentals.
- Engineering: result "a hundred and nineteen meters up the field, thirty across... a hand's width and a half from where Chloe said it was." Miss by 50 percent. Grade B. Failures listed. Good realism: the group fails the spec narrowly and is scored, not praised.
- Her teaching error: all seven of the twelves she taught got the same ordering wrong. Chloe admits it. Believable and humbling.
- Sanders adds her name to the acknowledgments "at the end of a list of five". Mild reaction again. A fourteen-year-old gets acknowledged on a paper "kept inside the department." Under-recognised but plausible.
- Outsider reaction: mother learns at the table that Chloe has been shooting rifles since September. Her reaction is the strongest outsider reaction so far and is proportionate: "You're shooting rifles?" and "Why is this the first I'm hearing about it?" Then the family covers it with pie. This is the best-written parent reaction in the book, and it shows what a parent would do. Notably she asks about safety (a man behind the line, drops, turning round), not about whether it is normal for fourteen-year-olds to do this at a school with a hundred-meter crosswind range. Realistic: a mother thinks about risk first.
- The father "knew from the phone in October" and thought it was fine. Believable under-reaction from the less anxious parent.

## Chapter 18: Fifteen

Age fifteen. The bullet-on-bullet turret lane, 10v1 at a minute, a money exercise (Hark), surgery, a research NDA, learning to drive. This is the book's richest chapter for rates.
- Clay baseline: "The whole year is breaking clays at between ninety and a hundred", presumably percent, so no spread at the bottom, effectively a ceiling. 
- Bullet on bullet: "For weeks every card in the year reads zero." Odile lands the first on the eleventh of November, Chloe days later. Chloe is on twenty by late November and sixty by the last week of January. Then plateau: Chloe 61, 59, 60 in February. Voss: "Point six. Six in ten, near enough, and that is where you are and where the other ten are." Chloe's card study: "eleven people who have stopped moving" in a narrow band. Sam is on forty. Ruth is "on ninety-one" and "ninety-something". 
  - Plausibility: very low as a rate. Hitting a bullet in flight at sixty meters with a rifle round, across its path, at sixty percent, is far beyond human ability; ninety-one percent for a fifteen-year-old is stranger still. The book is clearly intentional about this being impossible-seeming, but the pass-rate lens still has to note it: all ninety students hit a moving bullet at least some of the time within about six weeks of the first success, and the lowest named rate is forty percent. Nobody is shown at zero after February; "ninety of them at it" starts at zero and ends with a real spread (40, 60, 91), but the floor is high. A realistic range would have a third of the year still at or near zero.
  - The one real realism feature is the plateau: Chloe's curve stalls at about sixty and eleven others stall in "a single narrow band." This is a very good human detail. Many others also may be at 40 or 91, so the spread is acknowledged.
  - Timeline: first hit in about six weeks (October to Nov 11), and Chloe's twenty and sixty within three months. That matches the book's own "six weeks" pattern for every skill. Real learning curves for extremely hard skills would be slower and lumpier.
- Staff reaction, Voss: "It's a real thing, and you're far from the first person to find it", then walks her through nothing. He also says he has delivered his speech to "every class for six years running", implying previous classes also succeeded. His response to the plateau is a deliberate non-answer ("Put another two hundred rounds behind it"). This is a believable teacher who knows the plateau and withholds the solution. It fits the hide-it brief (he gives no sign this is remarkable), but a real instructor of this skill would have a lot to say about a plateau with eleven students. That is a slight slip toward over-silent.
- 10v1: Chloe's average is forty-one against forty-five the year before. Odile goes into the middle on a Thursday in March and holds a minute and seven seconds, with thirty people watching: this is the first full minute on record in the book, and the room "makes a noise she can feel through the floor." That is a believable social reaction among peers. There is no staff reaction recorded to the first full minute. Kowalczyk's earlier line ("you're fighting people who have been doing this for years... It'll happen to you again at eighteen") is a good, plain staff reaction.
- Money exercise: Rustem ends at $940 from $200, Chloe $217, Priya down $80. A real spread, including losses. Hark's line "you are up seventeen dollars... barely beating inflation" is a believable cold teacher reaction.
- Surgery: first suture takes "twice as long as the boy at the next table", the instructor writes "steady" and the time. A weak result noted.
- Staff and the federal NDA: Dr. Sandoval says there is an NDA "on that project. Four people have signed it" and refuses to put it in front of a fifteen-year-old because "it would fail in court." This is a very believable adult reaction: careful, honest, asking a favor. It suggests the school's research is being used by a federal agency, which is the single most realistic "outsider" element so far (government interest), though it is handled as a hushed request, not a recruitment. It is also a hint that the school has government ties it keeps from the children.
- Outsider reaction: father teaches her to drive: "I bet they teach driving at school next year." Warm and slightly flat. Grandmother sees the burn scars and a self-set finger and says nothing about them. Muted by design. A grandmother seeing a self-set knuckle "in a corridor at midnight" would ask more, but the book plays it as the family choosing not to ask.
- Number conflict: the range has "ninety" students at it, the same as the cohort in Chapter 11. Consistent.

## Chapter 19: Sixteen

Age sixteen. The bar exam, taken over two days in April in the school's own examination room, marked by teachers. Driving emergencies. Negotiation board.
- Bar result: "Fifty-two in the year pass, but thirty-nine do not." That is 91 candidates, a 57 percent first-time pass rate. Chloe fails by four points out of two hundred, "the closest miss on that board" per Bex; Kavi passes by six. Retake in October: Chloe passes by twenty-two. Nobody gives the retake rate for the other 38.
  - Plausibility: the number is the most believable cohort result in the book. Real first-time bar pass rates run around 60 to 80 percent for adult law graduates; for sixteen-year-olds it should be near zero, so the book is claiming the cohort is roughly as good as law graduates after three years of law. As a spread it is excellent: a clear failure group (43 percent), a named near miss, a reason for failing that is a skill gap (not writing out reasoning, not knowledge). The reason is realistic and nobody is above it.
  - Weak spots: (1) the paper is set and marked by the school's own teachers ("the teachers who wrote the paper and will mark it, proctoring both days themselves"), so the cohort is passing its own teachers' exam, not an outside one. The text says "the examination this country uses", but there is no outside body shown. A real bar has an outside licensing board with identity checks and age rules; no outsider reacts at all to sixteen-year-olds sitting or passing it. (2) The retake outcome for the 39 who failed is never given. If most passed, the cohort cleared the bar at close to 100 percent within six months, which would go from "believable" to "uniform".
  - Staff reaction: Amberg, who set up the scheme, is exact and unsentimental about her marks. Bex's reassurance is social. There is no staff reaction to the headline (52 of 91). If the staff had hoped for fewer failures or more, they say nothing. A real staff of a program like this would have its own view and would be publicly anxious or proud of that number. The staff's silence is consistent with the hide-it brief but is the largest missed staff reaction in the book so far.
- Spread: Sam writes four pages on question three, Chloe fourteen, Chloe's best skill (knowing) sits next to a weak one (writing the steps). This is a realistic failure mode for a quick student.
- Range: Sam "on forty percent... since he started", Ruth "on ninety-one". These match Chapter 18. Consistent. The best is 91, the worst named is 40.
- Driving: Chloe overshoots a stop mark by thirty meters, with an ignition killed at sixty. A real failure documented. Good.
- Negotiation board: Priya "lost it on purpose" against Nadia. A hint that some children throw tests, which is believable.
- Outsider reactions: mother and father on the steps ask about her friends and not about the bar. They know she sat "the bar" only if she told them; not shown. No outside observer of any result appears in this chapter.

## Chapter 20: The Parking Lot

Age sixteen, June. Four students sneak out (cameras looped, a badge duplicated, a recorder-jamming box) to a diner, then meet seven adult muggers, three armed. Sam disarms and drops all seven in "twenty-two seconds" while three others watch from a loading dock roof.
- Combat result: seven adult men, three with guns, beaten by one sixteen-year-old in twenty-two seconds with no injury to him. Sam says "that was nothing", and Chloe explains why the men were bad (bunched, locked elbows, no skills). Ruth: "They fought like ten-year-olds." 
  - Plausibility: the book works hard to make this believable by showing the men as incompetent, and the explanation is a good one (muggers who had a skill would use it elsewhere). Still, one unarmed teenager clearing three drawn guns and four more men in twenty-two seconds is a very high success rate for a single fight. Compare with the school's own numbers: Sam is the year's 10v1 record-holder at eleven (14 seconds) and holds forty percent on the range (below the others). He is the strongest fighter, not the strongest shooter, so a fight where he wins is consistent. The rest of the cohort has not been shown to be this good, so this does not break the spread rule.
  - The conflict with ch 18: the best 10v1 anyone has held is a minute and seven seconds in a controlled drill (Odile). Here Sam finishes seven in twenty-two. Not inconsistent because it is a different test with unskilled opponents, and the book explains why. Good.
- Staff reaction: none. The school is unaware. This is the point of the chapter, and it is the cleanest example of the cohort acting competently outside school and finding it dull. "That was nothing" is the book's best summary of the issue: the students do not realise their skills are unusual because every comparison they have is to each other.
- Outsider reactions: the muggers are unconscious and never react. The waitress is warm and ordinary. The students decide not to report: "somebody robbed them of a robbery." The chat gets sixty replies and an argument about whether it counts. There is no outside reaction at all (police, hospital, press) to seven men with a dislocated shoulder, concussion and a bitten lip. In the real world seven injured men might self-treat or ignore it, so this is acceptable as a one-off, but it is a place where the book avoids testing an outsider reaction.
- First-aid skill: Ruth checks airway, breathing, concussion; Nadia reduces a dislocated shoulder "with one motion"; Chloe checks ribs. The group handles seven casualties alone, calmly. Believable as the cohort's pass rate for first aid, since earlier chapters showed first-aid drills under stress from nine.
- Spread: Nadia is better at the cart stunt than Sam; Sam cannot find a stronger opponent. The jokes about the waitress "taking him apart" are an implicit check on pride, and the book is aware of the overconfidence risk.
- Number note: "They are the first to sneak out since the school opened" is a claim that no one has done this in about fourteen years, among ninety teenagers a year. That is a small plausibility problem on its own: first-time sneak-outs in a school of this size would be common. It implies either strong control or cohort-wide compliance.

## Chapter 21: The Applications

Age seventeen. College applications. The biggest set of outsider reactions in the book.
- College results: Chloe, "squarely in the middle", applied to fourteen and is admitted to twelve ("She has twelve"). Odile "gets in everywhere" (she never opened the letters). Ruth "gets everything she asked for". Kavi gets MIT, Caltech and Chicago. Sam "all but one". Chloe's own line: "Ninety-one out of ninety-one bought something." So the book claims every one of the 91 got at least one acceptance, and the median student got 86 percent of her list, including Ivies. Nadia does not apply and instead sends 190 job applications for 12 replies.
  - Plausibility: the pattern is internally consistent and well built (a middle student with 12 of 14, a top with "everything", Sam one short). But a median student admitted to Ivies at an 86 percent rate is far above what the stated admit rates (three percent) would give even a very strong applicant, and the cohort is applying as a bloc. The book acknowledges this via the "lying" statistic joke. The test it passes: a believable reason is given for why it works (admissions officers have no model for the transcript). A realistic book would still show a few children not getting into their top-tier choices and at least one student with real disappointment. Only Sam's "all but the twelfth" and Chloe's two rejections give that, and both are mild.
- Outsider reactions, in order of the book:
  1. Registrar and the first caller: transcript so full that admissions thinks it is "a formatting error"; the caller hangs up first. Excellent, believable, proportionate.
  2. Review committee: assumes ghostwriting ("a mill or a very good teacher with a template"), then "transfer-cohort theory" until someone checks birth years. Very believable.
  3. The Penn officer: flags the files after reading a sentence about a "self-defense assessment involving a room full of people", then more than thirty institutions open files by January. Believable and appropriately escalating.
  4. The paper chair: five accept an escorted, bagless, pencil-only reading; the Caltech man spends all day and asks to speak to a sixteen-year-old third author. Strong and believable. The refusal ("No") is a good staff slip-proof reaction.
  5. Campus visitors: Stanford rep follows "about a third" of a crypto class; Yale rep asks Hearn whether ten pages a week is unusual and gets "It's what the material wants"; MIT rep watches a shortwave receiver built from a bag of parts. These reactions are the most muted; they are noticed but no rep says what they actually think. This is the right area to strengthen. A Yale writing professor told ten pages a week at seventeen should have an obvious reaction beyond a polite follow-up.
  6. Duke rep: three words, "Sport", "Athletics", "Recreation", for the sports building. Funny, and shows staff are coached. 
- Staff reaction: school says yes to every representative, gives desks and meals, but refuses the fighting hour and the range ("an identical answer every time"). That is how a staff hiding impressiveness would act, and it is clearly a decision made at a level above the teachers. The staff's calm in the face of more than thirty institutions opening files on the school is the area to check: the school has no visible reaction to the opening of those files, which may be justified by the school having planned for this.
- Another conflict: the school lets outsiders read the research papers only with an escort, in a room with no bag; "it will not send a copy to anyone". That is a plausibly federal-sensitive stance and matches the NDA in ch 18.
- Spread: Nadia's job search is the best realism device in the chapter. The same education that gets Ruth into MIT gets Nadia 12 replies from 190 applications, "eight of them written by software." She is never given a reason to want college. A believable counterweight: not everyone is a college candidate, and employers have no way to read the transcript. But the cohort rate for choosing college is not stated; one in 91 is plausibly not the only non-applicant.
- Another measure: "Sam: Fighting... first in my year... There's a board, right outside the gym." There is a ranked fighting board. Odile is second "close enough it goes her way plenty of the time." The board contradicts the idea that staff hide ranking.
- Outsider reactions, parents: Chloe's mother only asks whether she is okay and whether "she got it right" about Bex. Ruth's parents "talk over each other to ask the next question first". Parent reactions are fine and rather understated; the mother does not react to 12 of 14 at all in this chapter. In the same call the mother is not told the result, which is why.
- Number note: "identical ninety-one" matches the 91 on the bar list (52 plus 39). Consistent.

## Chapter 22: The Offer

Age seventeen, April. Exit interviews with Amberg for all 91. The school offers research jobs: $135,000, housing, car, medical, degree paid, "the post stays open." Thirteen accept. Chloe and Nadia decline, Kavi accepts.
- Rates: "All ninety-one names are posted in the end, and thirteen of them turn into yeses" (14 percent). The text then says "The eighty saying no", but 91 minus 13 is 78. Small inconsistency in a headline number.
  - A second number conflict: on the Thursday of the first week, Amberg tells Nadia "Eighty people have sat in that chair this month." At about a dozen a day and a Thursday in week one, only 40 to 50 had been interviewed. Either the count is loose or the interview pace is not what chapter says.
  - Plausibility of 14 percent: believable and a useful middle figure. An employer offering this package to every graduate and landing 14 percent against a competing offer set that includes MIT, Caltech and the Ivies is realistic. It also shows the school is not a monopoly on these children's futures.
- Staff reaction: Amberg's recitation of the same four points to every student is a good "scripted, no favors" detail. He pauses the pen once. "Four of them told me they were starting something... Three of them will be doing something else by Christmas." This is a believable human staff reaction; he has data on how the students behave and gives it without emotion. A school that spent eleven years on each child and offers a $135k job to all 91 shows it wants a share of the output, which is a strong, quiet staff reaction (an institutional one) and a believable one. It signals the school's outcome pressure.
- Outsider reaction, parents: the best outsider reaction in the book. The mother: "A hundred and thirty five thousand dollars... That is more than this house cost us." The father: "That's a mistake, however it's dressed up." They also work through cost of Georgetown, a loan against the house. It is proportionate, practical, and emotionally right: parents see a lifetime of financial risk, not an educational achievement. It also exposes that the parents have never seen or been told how the cohort compares with other 17-year-olds. They do not ask what makes the school offer this much.
- Believability gap: a 17-year-old offered this much by a private school with no named employer or funding body would draw a question from any parent about who is paying. The parents' reaction has no "who is this" question. Given ch 3's "who is paying for it?", it is odd that they never revisit it here.
- Spread among exit choices: Nadia declines because jobs and colleges differ for her; Kavi accepts; Chloe declines, "I'd rather do the ordinary thing and owe you for it." The split shows no uniform response. Good.
- Odd line: "Eleven years" matches Chloe's duration (age 6-7 to 17-18). Consistent.
- Sam is "doing the thing with the Army, the officer program." Held for ch later.

## Chapter 23: The First One

Age seventeen going on eighteen. First graduation, 91 graduates, about eight hundred children watching. Outcomes: Sam to the Army, Ruth to MIT, Kavi stays on staff, Priya to South America, Theo to a federal analyst job, Nadia's start-up with nine employers out of 61 asked and one hire, Chloe to Georgetown with the Foreign Service as a goal.
- Headline numbers: 91 graduate, first graduation ever. "Eight hundred children who are not graduating" implies a school of about 890. At about 90 a year that is nine cohorts, which fits. 
  - Possible conflict: Voss said in ch 18 he had given his bullet speech "to every class for six years running", and Pruitt, Amberg and the woman "who signed nine years ago straight out of a program not unlike yours" all imply earlier cohorts. But ch 16 says Chloe's cohort is "the oldest in the building" and this is "the first graduation Halstead has ever held". Either earlier classes did not graduate, did not reach marksmanship, or the "every class" line refers to younger classes being taught the speech in sequence. The cohort's results then lose their comparison class: there is no earlier group whose rates the staff can be calibrated on. This affects the reading of staff reactions: they have no precedent for what a full cohort should achieve, so their muted reactions are less explicable as experience and more as policy.
- Staff reaction: the head of school's voice breaks on "accomplishment" and the applause track lands "a half-beat late", which gets a laugh. This is a lovely, believable human slip in a staff otherwise trained to hide significance. It is also the only point where a staff leader shows emotion over the cohort's success. 
- Outsider reactions: the book's family and friends react to graduation as to any graduation. Chloe's mother cries "from the K's onward". Chloe's grandmother asks "What are you doing next?" and says "Well, that's something" to Sam (Army), Kavi (staying on), and Chloe (Georgetown), in the same words. Cousin Dana: "That's good." Parents never see or hear what the cohort did, so they see only a normal graduation. Believable and consistent: they do not know what an extraordinary number looks like. A father who studies the stage's bracing and a stranger who turns out to be Kavi's father, a physics professor, share the same calculation, which is a subtle clue that outsiders could notice the same thing.
  - Missing: no outsider from government, press, or the colleges attends the first graduation. That is plausible only if the school has kept the lid on it. After the spring's thirty-plus open files at colleges, a graduation without a single visiting college rep or journalist is a stretch.
- Rates of outcomes: 13 of 91 stay with the school (Kavi included). Of the rest: Army (Sam), MIT (Ruth), Georgetown (Chloe), federal government (Theo), start-up (Nadia), travel (Priya). The spread is varied and not all conventional. Good.
- Nadia's start-up: she asks sixty-one employers, nine agree, one hire in the first month "who already had the job." A real failure, and her father's skeptical reaction ("This is what people say when nobody will hire them") is a proportionate and well-observed outsider reaction. A believable lower rate of success than the cohort average, so it helps break uniformity.
- Sam at the recruiter: Sam reads the pay-grade form twice and asks why the PT score is capped. The recruiter shows no awareness of the cohort. Notable: no recruiter reaction to a graduate of a school of this profile. The recruiter shows "no reason for the cap" but nothing else. Outsider reaction is muted.
- Theo: "answered every question completely" through "round after round" of federal interviews. Another cohort member succeeds at everything outsiders test. 
- Nothing here contradicts the 91 total.

## Chapter 24: The Chat

September after graduation. Group chat of former classmates in new settings: Eli, Ruth (MIT), Sam (basic training), Kavi (staff), Nadia (start-up), Theo (agency), Priya (South America), Chloe (Georgetown).
- Outsider reactions to results, this chapter is full of them and they are well judged:
  1. Eli's boss jokes that breaking into the company's systems earns a million dollars. Eli finds five breaches in an afternoon, reports them, and gets "a meeting with a lawyer." Very believable. A security team that thought it impossible would react with fear and procedure, not a bounty. The speed (five in an afternoon) is the part that is high; the reaction is realistic.
  2. Theo's supervisor "keeps sending my work back and asking me to show the reasoning." She reads fast skipped steps as sloppy, not as brilliance. Theo says "do they also want me to write out how to take a derivative." Believable and funny. This is exactly how a real supervisor reads work from someone far ahead: as missing steps. A sharp, proportionate outsider reaction.
  3. Sam: ordinary basic training, trust falls, "twenty other guys." No outsider reaction recorded; the Army does not treat him as special so far.
  4. Ruth's MIT performance review: "mine says something almost identical" to another screenshot of compliments. Kavi's reading: "you tell people theyre exceptional early so they work harder and dont leave. its in the same chapter as the acceptance rate thing... not fake. not informative." This is the book's own meta-commentary on how institutions respond to success: praise as retention. Good, and it deliberately explains why outsiders' reactions are weaker than they should be.
  5. The bank manager gives Nadia a hard time over a $20,000 balance but ends up granting two-day holds after 22 minutes, and waives a fee for a year. A believable reaction to a young person arguing with the numbers. Not an extraordinary reaction.
  6. The man asking Nadia when she will "go do something with herself": a very believable muted outsider reaction.
- Pass-rate evidence, Nadia's interviews: she interviews 41 people in July for two jobs, with tests. Of them, "thirty seven said it doesnt break" when asked where their work breaks. 90 percent failed the one question she cares about. This is the book's first clear measure of how the outside population compares to the cohort. It supports the claim that the cohort stands out. Believable as a hiring screen of ordinary applicants.
- Nadia's start-up numbers: 212 people since June, six hires. Nine employers who agreed to pay. 19,000 openings listed. "I asked sixty one to get nine." These are low results and plausible for a young start-up, which keeps the cohort from looking uniformly successful.
- Staff behavior: Chloe's sinus medication, a small white pill taken twice a day for two weeks, is not in any pharmacy listing; the school's reply is "internal" for both the process and the formulation. A believable institutional stonewall and the first sign that the school has done something to the children that the staff want hidden. For the pass-rate lens it raises the possibility that results were assisted. It also shows staff protecting information. The school also refuses to sell laptops to non-students or non-staff, a smaller sign of the same pattern.
- Kavi's status: salary "enough"; a staff member "still calls me by my year." Staff treat him as staff in practice. Believable.
- No parent or teacher appears in this chapter.

## Chapter 25: Forty Targets

Age eighteen. Sam in Army basic training. Fitness test: 600 of 600, "the top of the scale." Rifle: first live groups all inside the diamond; qualification forty hits of forty. He tells the company commander he hits bullets at forty percent. The commander escalates.
- Results as the Army sees them: 600/600 on a six-event test where "sixty required to pass on each"; 40/40 on qualification, "the first I have signed for in two cycles"; first-morning groups tighter than a platoon of peers who "would cover a dinner plate." Sam notices he is simply better than everyone and spends weeks looking for the catch ("this is a screen").
  - Plausibility: good as a depiction of what a very strong, very well-trained recruit would do. Sam is also the book's weakest named shooter on the interception drill and the strongest fighter, so using him is a conservative choice and shows how far a below-median interceptor outperforms real soldiers. Believable, and a useful contrast with the school's own numbers.
- Staff (outsider) reactions in order of belief:
  1. The fitness grader adds the column twice "with the pen held clear of the paper", says "You have done this before somewhere" and "Whatever you were about to ask me next, the answer to it is no." Excellent.
  2. The mess-table men: "Nobody maxes that test on a cold morning but then sits here telling you what he's bad at." One stops believing him. Excellent.
  3. Drill Sergeant Ruiz: "Again." He reads Sam as a recruit to be corrected; he reacts to the performance as a problem, not a gift. Good.
  4. The company commander: disbelief at the bullet claim ("nobody shoots a bullet out of the air... not anywhere I have been in sixteen years"), then he calls the number on the file twice. Three-part escalation: memo for record, counterintelligence, battalion S-2 check, plus a mental-health referral. This is the most realistic government-style reaction in the book. A real Army would treat a soldier who says this as either a liar or a security issue. The referral is a sharp realistic touch.
  5. The woman "well above the captain" who draws out "a folder far older than the captain's commission." Believable, but it moves from realism to plot by signalling that the Army already has a file on Halstead. It shows institutional knowledge, not surprise, and that is the right level.
  - Too muted: nobody asks Sam about fighting, languages, or any of the dozen other skills; the Army fixes on one claim. This is believable because Sam chose the one claim.
- Sam's slip: he volunteers "forty percent" and "Halstead", then reaches for "boarding school... small... you wouldn't have heard of it." The school-coached line is repeated; it fails. This is an excellent example of the hide-it brief failing at the edge of the school.
- Sam's self-assessment: "At school his was the number that never moved." He holds the cohort's relative scale internally: Ruth 91, Kavi better at games, Odile once put him down. The cohort has no outside scale, so Sam's repeated search for "the catch" is believable.
- Number notes: bay of about sixty men. Twenty-two weeks of training. Consistent with a real Army basic course.
- Consistency check: Sam's 40 percent, Ruth 91 percent, Odile "second in the year" in fighting and "first" for Sam. All consistent with earlier chapters.

## Chapter 26: The Exercise

February. Sam on a four-night Army field exercise (Winter Anvil) with laser harnesses. Two engagements won at a fence, a violation of the no-hand-to-hand rule in a culvert, a win against a man who lay still for seven hours, and a stern after-action review.
- Results: Sam's tally at the review is "a two, a four and a one", which he calls "a lot of walking for very little." The major reads out others' results: one soldier "went the whole exercise untouched", another took a wrong bearing for three hours. So Sam is good but not superhuman in a field setting. He loses the culvert by breaking the rules and learns his mistake (watching the clock instead of the pipe). A man lies motionless "once in seven hours" and nearly beats him. This is the right tone: he is better than the field but not a movie soldier. Plausibility: high. 
- Staff (Army) reactions: very proportionate. Sergeant Ives treats the culvert as a rule violation ("Say the contact rule"). The major shows no awe about the interception boasting that does not appear here; he marks Sam for patience ("I have watched two hundred of you fail that test") and for rules. He answers the school question with silence ("Which school, he leaves alone"). The major leaves the school question unasked after Sam says "School, sir." This is an interesting under-reaction after the captain's strong reaction in ch 25. It is believable if the memo already went above his head and he has been told to stay out of it. The text supports this ("It goes upward anyway").
- Sam's request to make the next one harder, "Was that it, sir, or is that the practice one": the response is institutional: "Difficulty is set above you." The platoon's reaction is hostile ("ask the rest of us first and we will tell you no"). A very believable peer reaction to a standout asking for more. 
- Spread: Sam's group is clearly the best on this exercise by Army yardsticks, but there is no table. The text offers one soldier who went untouched and one who lost three hours. No pass rates given.
- Friends' reactions in the chat: they all say they would have asked the same. Nadia challenges the timing. This shows the cohort's shared assumption that every test is a screen. For my lens: outsiders see a keen, slightly arrogant recruit; the cohort sees a normal question. The gap in expectations is the real source of friction and is reasonable.
- Nothing here contradicts earlier numbers.

## Chapter 27: Nadia

May of the first year out. Nadia's start-up has five staff. A scam run through her site is traced to four men above a tire shop. She confronts them alone with a bluff. Her skills tests have a pass rate under a third even after she makes them easier. She interviews 31 candidates and keeps only those who can answer the second question.
- Outside-world pass rates, this is the book's cleanest quantification of how ordinary adults fare on cohort-style tests:
  - "The first thing is that almost everyone fails." The auto-generated skills tests are made easier twice, down to "a version she'd have handed the thirteens at Halstead", and the pass rate is "still under a third." She finishes the Python set "in minutes."
  - Interviews: 31 candidates; "most of them run out of road" on the edge-of-the-system question. She keeps those who answer it.
  - Earlier (ch 24) 37 of 41 candidates said the thing they built "doesn't break." So the sample is consistent at about 90 percent failing the key question.
  - Plausibility: believable as an experience of a very sharp young founder with a harsh screen; many real managers say they cannot find candidates who can reason about failure modes. It is also plausible that Nadia's skewed baseline (ninety-one peers) makes the outside world look slow. The book allows the reader to draw both conclusions, which is the right balance. Nadia herself says, "i cant find anyone who can finish a thought", and Ruth suggests it is a hiring pool problem.
- Hired staff show more spread: Tomas rewrites the back-off and leaves the ceiling untouched ("I don't go through a thing changing whatever already works"), Bev closes sales with a made-up 30-day clause, Manny finds a bug and comes with the fix. Good: the employees she hires are each good at one thing and not all at the same level. Believable small-team hiring.
- Outsider reaction, the criminals: four men react with proportionate fear and menace. They photograph her and her plate, read her registration aloud, shut the door, and talk about the bay locking at five. They ultimately let her go after she gives a credible damaging threat (the Secretary of State letter). This is a good, believable range: they do not melt, they do not kill her. Ruth and Eli's response in the chat ("a dead mans switch only pays off if youre already dead") is the right outside check on the bluff and the best reaction to a cohort member's overreach. A tight, realistic piece.
- Business outcomes: Nadia reports hires "between the 5th and the 20th", two employers slipped in April, she covered payroll from her own account. A realistic failure margin.
- Staff reaction: none; this chapter has no staff.
- Parent reaction: her mother asks where she was. "Out at a tire place" is enough. Believable and consistent with the book's pattern that parents do not probe.

## Chapter 28: Nineteen

Age nineteen. Chloe works at a translation firm of about fifty while taking a heavy college load, learns Polish, and runs a blog. Group chat compares the cohort with national statistics.
- Workplace reaction (the most extended "ordinary people meet cohort member" scene): Chloe finishes her work in three hours a day, catches a decimal error that would have had a warehouse load ten times a machine's rating, and has Mandarin and Russian routed straight to her. A coordinator upstairs emails her directly. Deb, her desk neighbour, never asks how work got done overnight and never treats her as exceptional. Plausibility: high. Real offices adapt quietly to a fast worker and do not call it genius; they route work to her and stop checking. The social failures (she answers "how was your weekend?" in fifty seconds, volunteers for filing) are a good, realistic counterweight: being very capable does not make her read a room.
- Spread: Chloe is slower with an unskilled teacher ("Polish... the instructor reads down the page"). She loses a highlighter twice. She is capable and visibly imperfect. 
- College: she takes a political history course "in an afternoon rather than the fortnight the course gives her" and then 21 credits plus an evening seminar. The registrar's "guideline" that "a heavy load waits" is a good under-reaction: no one thinks she is unusual, just that she is overreaching. Believable.
- Blog: she writes 2 to 3 pieces a week of up to 6,000 words each at night. A man emails to ask about her "research team" because "he genuinely can't work out the arithmetic." Two more emails like it follow, from unnamed writers who never say who they write for. This is a strong outsider reaction pattern: curious, disbelieving, quiet, slightly institutional (the empty column "who anybody writes for" suggests agencies). It is believable and ominous, and it is the book's best hint that someone is watching outputs.
- Peer comparison: the chat's national statistics (25 percent of Americans speak a second language, 21 percent read below sixth grade, about a third can do 3/4 minus 1/2) are used to calibrate how unusual the cohort is. The cohort guesses 70 to 90 percent for the second-language question and are off by about 60 points. This is a clear, believable demonstration that the cohort's baseline is skewed. Slight inconsistency: Eli says Chloe "could've done that at four" and she replies "I was still stuck on why you cant divide by zero at four", which clashes with the timeline (at six she was on subtraction, at seven learning fractions). A joke, but the age slips.
- Sam's reading test in basic: he finishes "in a quarter of the time they gave us", the proctor pulls his sheet and makes him redo it on Thursday, and he gets the same score; "they filed both." A believable, small institutional reaction (distrust fast results, retest, file both).
- Chloe's sinus pills: she gives a coworker a white tablet she has "taken since I was seven"; he wants to buy them; "it is not anywhere." Again the school medication. For the pass-rate lens: this suggests cohort performance was chemically supported, which would change how to read all success rates and would make staff concealment more serious. The book keeps it as a hook only.
- Ruth goes quiet in April, six months to the next chapter. Not a pass-rate point.

## Chapter 29: The File

October 2025. Theo, now a federal analyst, inherits a box of old files and finds a government file on Halstead going back to 2013, with a memo on an infiltrated teacher and a report on the 2017 night "Watch" showing the four intruders were real operators sedated by four twelve-year-olds.
- Government reaction, over twelve years: eleven sets of initials, "state actor" revised to "probably corporate" with the same caveat that "the funding cannot be traced." One undercover teacher "identified in less than a day" and escorted out. One probe by four operators over the fence, ending in sedation and tying-up by four children. "An attack on a school in the United States would not be accepted", said an adult after, "There is no reply on file."
  - Plausibility: the passive, continuous, under-resourced attention is believable for a mid-level watching file with a changing cast of owners. The thin paper stock and different budgets are a nice detail. The weaker part is the reaction to the 2017 incident. If an operations team of four was beaten and sedated by children on a campus, an agency would almost certainly escalate: warrants, reporters, or at minimum a risk assessment of a school producing combat-capable twelve-year-olds. Instead the file shows a bland continuation. The text supports this partly by saying the funding cannot be traced and that the school itself has shown it will not be pushed (the adult tells them they are "free to leave" and gets no reply). Still, a stronger agency reaction seems more likely than twelve years of observation.
  - Also: the agency apparently knows enough to see that four operators were sedated by children, and yet one of those children's classmates (Theo) is later hired by the same agency without any flag mentioned. Either someone should have flagged him, or the book should explain.
- Staff (school) reaction: the school expelled the infiltrator within a day and a half, stating it does not permit "unidentified adults around children." This is a sharp, believable institutional reaction. It shows the school monitors staff at a high level. It is consistent with the school's earlier hide-it behavior and with the drills.
- Retrospective check on ch 15: the operators there were presented as "actors" and "maintenance crew." The file says they were real operators with a sedative dose recorded to the milligram. The text in ch 15 leaves open whether the school staged them; Chloe's analysis had both readings. The new text resolves it towards real intruders, and also that the school set the drill to follow, or that the drill was a response. The 7:50 response time matches.
- Theo, age twelve, recalls the morning after "a drill overnight... the whole year stood out on the grass", and "thought it harsh at the time" about the grade. The cohort's lack of surprise at its own performance is consistent with the pattern: the children have no outside reference and think it was routine. Good.
- Numbers: eleven sets of initials across twelve years; the file goes back to 2013, the same year as Chloe's enrollment. The school itself is older (open four years by 2012). Fine.
- Supervisor: reviews Theo's cable summaries with three questions each time. At work, no outsider treats Theo's pairing of Pashto and Dari as extraordinary: "his supervisor takes them all seriously, no follow-up questions." A believable mid-level reaction; nobody treats a young analyst as a prodigy.

## Chapter 30: Cleared

Age twenty. Chloe's blog grows. She takes the Foreign Service exam and passes. A federal background investigator, Whitaker, interviews her twice. She is cleared and starts a trainee job in January.
- Foreign Service result: no score is given. The text says she "reads it as having sat an exam" and later gets cleared and starts as a trainee, so she cleared the written exam, the narrative and oral stages in a few months with no failure and no suspense. In reality the written test is passed by a minority and the oral assessment by a minority of those. The book gives no rate and Chloe never fails a stage. The one miss that did occur (the bar by four points) is the only such failure in her adult record. Slightly uniform, but it is one candidate, not the cohort.
- Outsider reactions, Whitaker, ranked by believability:
  1. He tells her what the file is for and what stays sealed, direct and plain. Believable.
  2. He tests her languages by asking her to "say something", writes "the fact that she said it" and ignores the content. A real investigator would probably not be able to judge the content, so this is believable, though it is odd that he cannot even confirm competence in one of thirteen languages. 
  3. On the bar at sixteen: "Whitaker's pen stops for about the length of a word, then starts again. 'Sat where?'... 'Sixteen,' Whitaker says, and writes it down, and asks her nothing else about it." This is the one place where a trained outsider's reaction is too muted. A federal investigator told that a sixteen-year-old sat and passed "the bar" at a boarding school, proctored by the school's own teachers, would ask who certified it, whether any state recognises it, and whether Chloe holds a licence. Having it "written down" without a follow-up is a gap. This is the same gap as ch 19: no external body has ever validated the bar result.
  4. He asks about the school's principal donor and she says "I have no idea." He writes the reasoning down. Believable. The agency does not know the donor either.
  5. He calls her a thorough, reliable answerer; there is no wow. Believable and consistent.
- Whitaker also asks whether she'd disclose the internal paper if he said it was necessary, and she says she would want to talk to her first. He writes that down. A good, measured reaction. This is another sign that the federal side of her work (ch 18) is probably linked to the same agency.
- Another reaction signal: a third of blog inquirers end the conversation when she says it is just her; a dozen rows, none willing to say whom they write for. This is the same pattern as ch 28 and fits an intelligence-style collection of interest rather than normal editorial interest, with one real editor at a newspaper among them. Believable and ominous, and it adds up.
- Her mother-call message and the clearance: she is sworn in on her birthday in August; the age rule is consistent with ch 23.
- Languages listed: Spanish, French, Mandarin, Russian, Latin, Arabic, German, Japanese, Hindi, Korean, Swahili, plus Polish and Hebrew: thirteen. Fits the one-per-year pattern (some years had more). Fine.

## Chapter 31: Ruth

October 2023 to November 2025. Ruth at MIT thinks she has been wrongly placed in a remedial track because first-year material is what she did at eleven. She works out that the problem is not the track. She posts the realisation to the chat and asks how the cohort was chosen.
- Outsider reactions at MIT, in order of believability:
  1. The study group laughs, then stops when she says it seriously ("when they think the course will get past fifth-grade concepts"). A very believable social reaction.
  2. The preceptor: "he hasn't graded enough of these to know yet." Believable: he is a junior grader and has no baseline.
  3. Devansh: "Are you ever stuck on anything?" "No." Believable and slightly eerie.
  4. The professor: "That took you five passes... I have graduate students in their third year who would not have got me there at all. Most of them would have told me the step was obvious." A good, sober reaction that makes the point about not writing steps down (compare Chloe's bar). It is one of the few places where an outsider names the gap precisely.
  5. The registrar and the department office: they treat her complaint as a placement dispute and hand her a pamphlet. The woman says "there is not another track... not in all the years I have worked in this office", and has "written this exact letter more times than I could count." This is too muted and slightly false to real MIT practice. MIT allows advanced standing exams, credit for prior work and graduate-course enrollment. A real student who produces highlighted syllabi and a diagnostic would be sent to a conversation with a faculty adviser within days, not a pamphlet. It also fits poorly with the idea that MIT's own admissions saw her as exceptional. The book's point (that no one can see her) is served, but the reaction is less believable than the others.
- Pass-rate evidence: Ruth, who is "on ninety-one" at bullet interception and the top of the year, "finished in the bottom third" of an advanced Portuguese class of native speakers. A believable and useful limit on her ability: even the best of the cohort are only average when the outsider group is selected on the skill. Nothing else in her first year is below perfect.
- Ruth's central claim, in the chat: "put any kid off my street in that building at seven and you get this. thats the part nobody will say out loud." This is the book's own hypothesis that the pass rate is a result of the environment rather than selection. For the lens, it is important. It makes the near-uniform success across 91 children sound less like a statistical miracle and more like a design. It also exposes a conflict: ch 3 says the list is built by "networks" that "identify children", ch 6 says "the list is a good list", and ch 10 says only some summer children stay. Ruth reads these as selection claims with nothing behind them, but the book does not say whether the summer attrition screened for something. It has an unresolved tension between "anyone would do" and "ninety were chosen".
- Cohort-level reaction: Kavi: "how long have you had this", "why didnt you say" is an understated, believable friend response. Sam and Chloe push back lightly. Cohort members each had a story of an outsider failing to match them and each "decided it means something about the other person." Ruth names the pattern across the cohort: Nadia's hiring, Sam's "real assessment". This is a good tie-in of the book's earlier outsider-reaction scenes.
- Nothing in this chapter changes the 91 or 91 percent figures.

## Chapter 32: The Money

November 2025 to February 2026. Five alumni (Eli, Kavi, Nadia, Ruth, Theo) build a slow "reconciliation job" that reads financial records for sixteen weeks, then Ruth proves from the gaps and timing that twenty years of decisions come from one man. The government's file, Theo says, has been wrong twice.
- Rates: no numbers except Ruth's own "99.99" confidence. Success is total: the worm runs undetected, the watcher notices a change in week two, the pattern emerges within two months, and the proof "holds" when Eli runs it against his own logs. Nobody fails at any step. Nadia's one correction (a timestamp from the wrong time zone) is the only error.
  - Plausibility: low as a group result. Five people, working after hours in separate jobs, build and deploy an undetectable intrusion tool against real financial records and, in two months, outperform whatever a federal agency has done in twelve years on the same question. The mechanism (arguing from presence, a man's daily band and preference for the worse instrument) is clever and plausible as an analytic move, but the clean success across all five steps and in all five people is the largest "everyone succeeds at too much" moment in the second half of the book. A believable version would have one or two false starts that cost weeks, or at least a result with real doubt.
- Outsider reactions: none in this chapter. The only references are the government (the file, wrong twice) and Eli's employer (he builds the tool after shift on company hardware, "a desk under a window that looks onto a parking structure"). There is no discussion of the legal exposure of running a worm against real accounts, beyond Nadia's fear. A real group of young professionals who hold clearances (Theo, soon Chloe) would be more anxious about this than the text allows; Theo only says "nobody repeats any of this." Believable fear, but the actual illegality gets little weight.
- Staff reaction: none.
- Cohort-internal reactions: Ruth is the proof-maker; Eli "needed it to not be true for an hour." These are good, human reactions. Nadia's "if this comes back to my company i will end all of you" is a strong realistic reaction from the person with the most to lose.
- Government claims through Theo: "they put a man on staff when we were eleven and we spotted him in a day." Earlier in ch 29 Theo is twelve for the 2017 incident and recalls the infiltrator only as a "new teacher for about a day and a half." Age mismatch between "eleven" and the file's dating is small; the infiltrator date is not given in ch 29.
- Check with ch 29: Theo reveals the file in the chat, contradicting his own "nobody repeats any of this", but he says it. A believable human lapse.
- Chloe's reaction: "theo that was a drill", then "theo which four." Consistent: she does not know until now that the intruders were real.

## Chapter 33: The Other One

May to June 2026. The group plans a second intrusion, this time into the government unit that keeps the Halstead file. Chloe writes the rules; all seven sign.
- Rates: no outcome numbers. The only data are a five-week test with "clean" reports twice a week, and a unanimous sign-off by seven people (Eli, Kavi, Nadia, Sam, Ruth, Chloe, Theo). Ruth gives legal numbers (five years, ten with a financial angle) and presses Eli for "the actual number of cameras youve counted". He does not answer. This is a good, small realism beat: the person who sounds most sure has no count.
- Believability of uniform agreement: seven young adults with five government- or defense-connected careers among them agree in a few weeks, each in a line, to a federal crime. Theo says it is "the stupidest thing any of us has ever done" and signs anyway. A real group of this kind would have at least one person walk away, particularly the two with clearances and the one on active duty. That nobody leaves is another instance of the cohort being too uniform, though this time in choice rather than skill. The stop clause (anyone can end it, no reason) is a plausible attempt to deal with that risk.
- Outsider reaction: none. The legal community and the agency appear only as risks. Nadia's line "two of you carry it if it goes wrong, not seven" is the only concern about outcomes for non-participants.
- Staff reaction: none.
- A small timeline note: "Chloe turns twenty-one in August, two months after she adds the last page." The chapter is dated May to June 2026, so August is still ahead of the narrative, and the line reads from a later vantage. Consistent with ch 30's August swearing-in.
- A skill note: "All of them know the law cold. They had sat the same examination." This repeats the claim that every one of them sat and passed the bar. In ch 19, 39 of 91 failed the first time and the retake results are not given. Eli, Nadia, Ruth and Theo are the only ones whose bar status is unstated; Chloe and Kavi's are known (Kavi passed first, Chloe on retake). Saying "sat" rather than "passed" is accurate.
