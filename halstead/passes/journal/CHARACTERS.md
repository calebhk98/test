# Character behaviour read

Lens: whether each character acts like themselves, with believable knowledge, motives and proportion, and whether children and adults act their ages.

## Chapter 01: Before
- Chloe (6): finishes work at once, then idles; argues the library rule; retells books in detail; self-censors after Kayleigh says "You talk weird"; shrinks answers to one word. Believable, and the masking mechanism is earned.
- Mom: phones school angrily ("What is anyone in that building doing about it?"), then sits quiet in the car. Loving, anxious, action-oriented. Believable.
- Dad: quiet, watches his plate, "She was quiet tonight." Believable; thin so far.
- Mrs. Aldana: takes book at ch 9 vs ch 4 incident, returns it silently. Mildly harsh but plausible for a first-grade teacher.
- Doctor: quick, sensible referral. Fine.
- Questionable: a six-year-old who analyses "at a time" rules and the locker-key symbolism is very high-end, but the book frames her as unusual. Chloe deciding at six that "the only fix is getting better at sitting there" is a little adult in phrasing but the logic is child logic.

## Chapter 02: March 7th
- Dr. Prentice (Ben): discusses scores with Mom while the door is "open a few inches" and Chloe sits right outside. Questionable professionally, but the book uses it deliberately (Chloe overhears "percentile"). Minor. His blunt "the rest of them put it in the folder" and the pointer to the third-grade program are believable.
- Mom: tells Chloe "you're all good" and refuses to give a number, after having fought the school in ch 1. Mild under-reaction: she has been told the school will only use a report as paperwork, yet nothing is pushed here. Explained somewhat by "nothing changes" afterward, but mom's drive in ch 1 versus passivity here is a watch item.
- Dad: absent from the evaluation ("tried to move two things and managed neither"). Plausible, sets up distance.
- Chloe: knows she's being tested, strategic about neatness, refuses to hear the result. Believable; "that question is off limits to children" is a slightly adult gloss.
- Chloe's conclusion "finish first, sit still, keep your answers short" is a believable masking rule for a 6-year-old.
- Mrs. Aldana asking about home in April: nice, in character.

## Chapter 03: The Letter
- Parents (Meg and Dad): Mom investigates hard (district office, neighbours, postmark, calling the school with exact safety questions), which suits ch 1. Her suspicion is proportionate. Then, after nine days, they send a 6-year-old to a four-week residential school that has no outside footprint at all ("not one person anywhere who has been there to say a word"). The tipping reason is Dad's "She's been flat since Christmas." Emotionally believable, but the risk calculation is thin: Mom's own research found the school unverifiable and the only thing that changed is the kid's mood. Mitigated by the open-visiting hotel and Mom staying for days. Mild concern, not a flaw.
- Mom: "I have no memory of applying" and the postmark detail show her sharp. Calling the emergency number to check it rings: in character.
- Dad: stops doing the train-station voice when he reads the teacher page, rereads it, then takes the laptop away. Reads as him being impressed and uneasy; he later argues she should go. Believable.
- Chloe: "It says Miss." Good child logic. The library hold bargaining repeats ch 1 and is consistent.
- Parents' loud arguing within earshot while believing she is in bed: fine for the genre.

## Chapter 04: Pluto
- Chloe: calls Mom the first evening, sleeps at the hotel, then weans herself ("if I call her she'll only ask me if I'm all right again"). Believable and well paced; the physical flashback to October is a fair detail. Staying silent for six days matches ch 1.
- Mom: comes at once, sleeps in the second bed, underlines "same day" twice, stays extra days, repeats instructions already given. Proportionate and in character.
- School staff: promise same-day pickup, no pressure; consistent with the open-visiting letter.
- Priya, Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Owen introduced with distinct habits: Priya monologues about horses, Sam jokes, Ruth corrects, Kavi corrects Sam on volcanoes. Ruth's "I said that on Friday" and her old-school admission "I say it and then I find out" are believable for a 7-8 year old.
- Kavi using "viscosity" at age 7: within the book's premise.
- Mr. Doyle thanking Ruth for the correction: sets the school's tone as plausible, if a little too perfect.

## Chapter 05: Behind
- Chloe: relieved by speed, then stung by Mr. Baptiste's "why do you flip it" exchange; her private breakthrough with the eighths is a satisfying, consistent detail (she needs the why, not the answer). Defers to Sam on the bridge ("Okay," holds the corner) despite thinking Ruth right: consistent with her people-pleasing silence, and costs the group.
- Sam: dismisses Ruth ("Ruth doesn't know. She just says things.") and his bridge fails; later shrugs about Owen. Believable seven-year-old pride; not yet disproportionate.
- Ruth: pushes unwanted correction, then "I told you" after the test; tells Chloe why Owen left, bluntly. In character.
- Owen: quiet, loses the thread, cries in the stairwell, goes home. Believable, and the school's immediate release matches the promise in ch 4.
- Mr. Baptiste: asks "why", handles divide-by-zero well. Fine. Mr. Doyle and teachers are idealised but consistent.
- Chloe's interrogation of Ruth about whether anyone went in after Owen is a little cross-examining for a seven-year-old but matches her debating habit.

## Chapter 06: The List
- Chloe: talks non-stop to Dad for twenty minutes (a flood after months of silence), explains why the fractions rule works. Consistent with ch 1's retelling habit and the relief of ch 5. Her plan to "talk their parents into it" is child logic. The line "the rightness belongs to him and not to you" is very sophisticated for a seven-year-old, but she has been shown to think this way.
- Dad: says "Cool" and looks away; folds the napkin into his pocket; afterward tells Mom about CPR, Spanish and fractions. Believable: stunned and proud, not sure what to do. Good use of a quiet man.
- Mom (by phone): "what's the name for that" is the first time anyone voices the giftedness. Realistic.
- Other parents at breakfast: all admit they suspected a scam and came anyway, and nobody knows what the school is. This quietly makes the shared parental behaviour plausible. A group of parents each leaving a seven-year-old with an unexplained organisation for a month is the book's main credibility load; it is acknowledged on-page ("I still don't know what it is").
- Ruth/Sam/Kavi: Ruth promises weekly letters, Sam predicts she will quit ("by about the fourth letter"). Good seed. Ruth hearing half a call through a door is in character.
- Chloe's flashback to Mom's hand on forehead and "be careful" shows she is carrying parents' worry. Believable.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room
- Chloe: realises she has no last names for anyone; waits by the phone; abandons library books; masks again after Kayleigh and Bryce mock her; counts days without a yes; breaks down in class. A coherent decline, and the library detail ("renew them please") shows a believable withdrawal. Strongest chapter for her consistency.
- Mom: "Who's Sam, honey? Give me the whole thing" is slightly off, since she says in the same scene that Chloe said "Kavi a hundred times in that car" and knew about the bridge; she would know the name Sam. Minor, and it may be read as drawing Chloe out. Her cleverest move is asking the school to pass on the number to the other families, which is practical. Her stalling on the Halstead question for a whole summer ("We're thinking about it") while watching Chloe count days is a mild under-reaction, but she is simultaneously phoning Chloe's own school about acceleration, which shows she is working it.
- Ms. Vance: crouches in front of the class, asks "Did somebody say something to you?" Proportionate and kind. Her time-capsule exercise is natural.
- Kayleigh and Bryce: "You were weird before, but now you're weirder" is believable second-grader bluntness.
- Ruth: demands a promise at the flagpole. In character (blames "whose fault" if only some get in).
- Sam and Kavi leave without a goodbye; Chloe can't find them. Plausible, school logistics.

## Chapter 08: The Asking
- Mom: takes the afternoon off, brings a prepared list to Dr. Ammons, fights the school for the Thursday program, admits "every night since the letter came" she looks for a reason to say no. Believable, and the confession explains her stalling. Still, she is stalling on the one thing her depressed daughter responds to, for months. It is the book's first sustained parent under-reaction in the child's favour (she is over-cautious, not neglectful). The reason is visible, so I rate it believable but at the edge.
- Mom telling a seven-year-old plainly that the therapist said "you're depressed" is a bit blunt but in keeping with her honesty.
- Dad: "it isn't only up to him" after earlier arguing for Halstead (ch 3). Slight reversal, explained by parents' agreement to present a united front; consistent with his conflict-avoiding pattern.
- Chloe: screams "you hate me", then is perfectly polite at dinner. Realistic child pattern. Her choice to hide the fast packet ("I'm still working on it") and to not say "this is the wrong thing" to Mrs. Prahl is consistent with her learned masking.
- Dr. Ammons: asks sharp, protective questions about the program ("Did anything happen to her there?"). Reasonable.
- Mrs. Prahl: warm but unable to deliver challenge. Fine. Her "Both of those are the same speed as far as I'm concerned" is a bit pat.
- Chloe tells Mom "Nobody was mean" and then defends Bryce ("That isn't bullying. It's only true."). Strong, believable.
- Mom's reply "You'll make friends this year" misses the point; realistic.

## Chapter 09: February
- Chloe (7): collapses on the floor offering her bike, birthday, Christmas; then, after yes, tries to return the bike as the "deal". A strong, believable and painful child-logic scene. The second-half bike return is arguably the best bit of age-true behaviour in the book.
- Mom and Dad kitchen argument: Mom has been "the only one saying anything" since August; Dad has avoided saying it aloud. Consistent with his doorway-standing in ch 8. His breaking the standoff by sending the form "days ago and kept to himself" is out of his pattern but earned by the collapse.
- Dad: filled in and sent the Halstead form without Mom saying yes. It is not clear on the page whether Mom knew. The scene has them present it as joint ("We're saying yes"). If she did not know, her acceptance is fast; if she did, the argument above seems too raw. Mild ambiguity. Check ch 10 to see if it resolves.
- Mom: refuses the house offer because "the lease ends the day we take her out". A smart, protective, in-character call. Contrast: Dad ("you read contracts for a living") is less suspicious than Mom; fits earlier.
- Mom sitting alone in the car after the visit: right in character, grief for the move.
- Mrs. Okonkwo: dodges "who's enrolled", corrects Ruth's rumour, says anytime enrollment. Plausible; but she says Ruth's claim of an autumn-only evaluation day "was silly" when the parents in ch 6 were told of a fall day. Not an inconsistency for the characters, a reminder that Ruth reports half-heard things.
- Chloe's accurate wait for "the test" that never comes: consistent with ch 2.

## Chapter 10: April
- Chloe: arrives, collects last names (answering ch 7's loss), fails at first in every subject and for the first time is genuinely behind, hates it for a month, then catches up. Believable growth; her habit of interrogating staff ("Does anybody actually get sent home?") is consistent. Her asking Ruth and Kavi about Owen repeatedly, so that Kavi says "You already have all of it", is a good small sign she is anxious about being sent home.
- Mom: the goodbye is skipped, and Mom's phone voice ("Are you eating?" and "actually happy, not just doing fine?") is in character. No complaint.
- Ruth: apologises for never writing (she lacked an address), which clears the ch 6 letter promise as circumstance rather than flake. Takes Chloe's tray and bag; protective. In character.
- Kavi: "he knew and he forgot, and that's a decision about how much it mattered" is a sharp, believable bit of Kavi pedantry.
- Sam: runs the length of the hall; later "talking to somebody else's grandmother". Consistent socially fluid, no depth yet.
- Fen: rock collection by size; steady, believable quirk.
- Teachers (maths man taps line two, literature teacher with no list): consistent with school premise; the teachers are uniformly wise, which flattens them.
- Dad: absent in this chapter.

## Chapter 11: Eight
- Chloe (8): home for the summer talking, singing, arguing about the hot dog; tests the curfew with a pre-built case. She argues like an adult lawyer; the book has earned this. She then eats half her dinner after Grandma says she has put on weight: a quiet, believable body-image hint that is never followed up in this chapter.
- Mom: Mom's "she has never fought me... she always stops the moment she knows" is an acute read of her child and fits ch 8's insight. Firm on curfew; good. Her line "I'm not having this conversation again in a hospital" is a slightly odd threat but harmless.
- Dad: lets Chloe win the hot-dog argument by "technicality", and Mom says "You have never held a line with her in your life." Matches his earlier conflict-avoidance, but also shows he enjoys her.
- Mrs. Okonkwo: flatly refuses to let Chloe drop dance or change teams, and says three children asked about the same team that week. Consistent with the school's rules. Chloe in the office is a decent child-lawyer bit.
- Chloe quits choir, keeps singing in the shower; reads the "ninetieth of ninety" sheet. Believable and small.
- Chloe also keeps a count of passes teammates never send her (a habit from ch 1, counting words she could not hear). Consistent.
- Sam, Kavi and Ruth: apples at a drainpipe, the bracket rule. Kavi's stricter logic and Ruth's competitiveness are in character.

## Chapter 12: Nine
- Chloe (9): does not tell the teacher that the bridge joints were hers when Bex says "we" then "I", and stops saying "we" for the year. Quits riding without telling Priya, who finds out from Anne. Avoids Iyad after he repeats a remark about her accent. All three are one pattern: she absorbs a small slight, says nothing, and withdraws. Believable and well drawn; it is a real flaw with a cost (Priya's hurt, the stolen credit).
- Ruth: "Then she should have said it while he was writing." Straight talk, in character.
- Kavi: lists three grievances about the elective form, then wants cryptography. His appeal to the head with a letter is slightly adult but fits his pedantry. Believable.
- Sam: months of failure on the overhang, publicly announces the day he will do it, and does. Believable, and it shows his pattern: loud commitment, public stakes. Good.
- Bex Alcantar: finishes people's sentences and claims joint work. Plausible, if a bit schematic as a foil.
- Iyad Mansour: recites everyone's numbers at dinner unasked. Very socially oblivious; plausible for a certain child, one note.
- Priya: confronts Chloe kindly about riding. Good, believable friend.
- Mom: teaches cribbage, sleeps-in jokes dropped after day five. Gentle, realistic parenting.
- Dad: reviews a vendor contract with Chloe; Chloe's observation that "that's not actually a promise" is more adult than a nine-year-old but fits her established precocity. Dad's response (reading it again, accepting) is plausible and loving.

## Chapter 13: Ten Pages
- Chloe (9-10): works out the paintball method with Ruth; Bex overhears and presents it to Bell as hers; Chloe, with her ears hot, again does not claim it, and by the sink lets Ruth's impression distract her. Second time the same pattern as ch 12. It is consistent, and the chapter shows a cost (she stops saying anything about the sheet), but a reader may want her to push once by now. She does tell Bell afterward, in the doorway, and is told "the sheet is already written". So she does try once; the under-reaction is in the follow-up. Believable.
- Bex: takes credit a second time. A believable second occurrence if Bex is a type, but her behaviour is a bit repetitive; she never has an inner reason on the page. Risk of a one-note rival.
- Bell: puts Bex's name on the board and says the sheet is written. The school's mechanism of recording "who said it" is consistent with Hearn in ch 13 and Bell in ch 12-13. Fine, but a teacher who knows Chloe and Ruth had worked it out first never checks. Mild.
- Iyad: turns Chloe's remark "Nobody scores the drill" into a joke about her, laugh at her expense. Characteristic. Chloe leaves; again withdraws.
- Ruth: "Six is all of us, Chloe." Supportive and sound.
- Kavi: has the pump count first and walks out in a huff when others get it; sulky about not being first. In character.
- Dad: at Thanksgiving, treats her argument as real; "No, hold on... You've got most of an argument." Warm, in character. Chloe's "I know it's thin" is believable honesty.
- Grandmother: "Your mother did exactly that, floor, back against the bed". Nice continuity with ch 9 (mom on the floor).
- Defensive Watch: ten-year-olds are tackled by adults in drills, and the response is simply scored. Never questioned by Chloe or any child here as odd; normalisation of a setting that parents at the school's start worried about. Watch for parental reaction later.

## Chapter 14: Sixty Degrees
- Chloe (10-11): third time Bex pre-empts her (astronomy, the age-of-universe numbers), but this time Chloe goes straight to Kavi and gives him both numbers before Bex is out the door. A small, earned change. She vomits over the bombing essay and then writes 28 pages and hedges; Hearn's note about hedging lands. Believable perfectionist behaviour. Her telling Mom only "That it was wrong. I got a B" shows she edits what she tells parents, which is consistent with her lifelong habit (ch 1 "It's good").
- Bex: again re-presents others' ideas (Chloe's, Sam's) first and loudest. Third repeat. Believable as a trait but now predictable; the book gives her no private motive yet.
- Ruth: stronger in command (east stairwell), and teases Chloe about Latin. Believable.
- Sam: goes over the bannister onto the teachers, is "marked out" in 90 seconds and tells the story all year. In character (gesture first, joy in story).
- Nadia (new): takes a floor and splits it; students obey. Early sign of a leader.
- Hearn: leaves the library door as the last holdout and politely asks about the Burke. Delightful, consistent.
- Mom: "I think that's a big subject for a school essay" then "A B's a B". Under-reaction: her daughter has written 28 pages on a bombing campaign for school and she barely engages. Consistent with her reduced view of Chloe's school life in this stretch, but compared with ch 1-9 she is oddly incurious. Dad: asks about archery "every day like you said". Neither parent shows concern about archery lines with arrows crossing, or about Watch. Chloe is not telling them details, so it is defensible.
- Grandmother: "once in an whole year is hardly a lot, is it" is a believable older-relative misread of what Chloe achieved.
- Coach Bell: puts the metronome on, promises breakfast if anyone hits an arrow with an arrow; sets the standard the children later meet. Fine.

## Chapter 15: Twelve
- Chloe (12): volunteers as bait at the 4 a.m. unannounced intrusion; plan is reasoned ("they'll spend at least a few seconds deciding what to do with me"). Acts calm, then her legs give. Believable for her trained, calculating character, though the self-assessment "I look smaller, it's psychology" is a small wink. She posts a note to the duty officer about the sensors. In pattern: she now writes down the point the sheet omits. Afterward she suspects the men may not be actors ("four real people... handed the wrong list") but does not act on it; consistent with the book's later turn.
- Ruth, Kavi, Sam: sedate four adult men, monitor respirations, and write the numbers on an arm. Ruth worries about their work physicals and says "It's rude besides." Four twelve-year-olds injecting unknown adults is the least believable scene on pure realism (children, not staff, using a sedative on grown men on a guess that they are actors), but it is within the school's own rules. Ruth's choice of the fast, traceable drug after complaining is credibly argued. Sam's "That's not very twelve" is the one honest reaction; the rest are calm to the point of eeriness.
- Kavi: cracks the handset in four seconds; his indifference to the bread test because the oven's heat curve is more interesting is perfectly in character.
- Mrs. Adeyemi: finds children in the kitchen at midnight with a gas oven lit, writes names, says "Go to bed." A tiny under-reaction compared with the letter home later. The next day the school sends the parents a letter about it; the gap between her calm and the letter is a mild inconsistency in institutional response but works as the school's formal handling.
- Mom: furious about a gas oven ("a flame going while the room is empty") while knowing nothing of the intruders or drugs. This is the most striking proportion mismatch in the book: proportionate to what she knows. Believable and the irony is intended. She again offers "you can come home", consistent with ch 4.
- Dad: tries not to laugh; asks about psychology; "Huh." Fine.
- Chloe's omission to parents (she tells them only "the sixty degrees" and then gives up) fits her long habit.
- Bell removes the metronome; Odile and Chloe argue over who counts. Plausible.
- Pruitt: terse forge teacher; "Your heat's wrong". Good.

## Chapter 16: Thirteen
- Chloe (13): as student teacher she asks the office how a blank sheet is scored, learns it must be a fail, and records the fail for Marek. She never tries the obvious workaround (writing down his spoken answers herself, or asking a staff member to accept his pencil work), and she does not ask him again. In character as a rule-bound child who hates being the one who decides, and the book is clear that she is "careful for a long time" afterward. Still, it is a thin effort for the one person who could save him, and the consequences for Marek are left open. Rated: mostly believable, one of the weaker choices for a clever character.
- Marek: refuses on principle to hand in anything, thinks in tangents, apologises constantly. His "A mark on a sheet stays exactly as true" is consistent and the failure is a believable consequence of his own stance. Very consistent.
- Chloe at dinner: holds the napkin answer back until Nadia calls it; Nadia says "You always go quiet like this" and Iyad retells it as an hour. This is Chloe's lifelong habit (ch 1 held answers) now reading as a flaw to peers. Strong. She avoids Nadia's side of the table afterwards, repeating the withdrawal pattern from ch 12 and 13.
- Nadia: states things flatly "on principle", half of it lands as an accusation. Consistent, believable.
- Iyad: again narrates Chloe's moves publicly, again slightly wrong. Repeated pattern; shows his compulsion.
- Bex: again takes the plan ("the workshop first"). Chloe simply goes and does the job; a quiet change.
- Ruth: builds an eight-method camera tool and the cover traffic. Ruth, Kavi and Chloe secretly hide their network from the school at 13. The group's wish to avoid the school's reading of their messages is established. A consistent step in distrust.
- Dad: "this stays between the two of us" over a riser that is 4 mm proud. Contrasts with Chloe's perfectionism, and is a sweet moment. Mom: "Great improvement." Believable, low-key.
- Amberg: law lecture promising the bar at sixteen; the institution again pushes children into adult roles without comment.

## Chapter 17: Fourteen
- Chloe (14): teaches the year below a wrong ordering, tells them straight ("what I taught you was wrong"), then confronts a girl at the sinks to trace the rumour to the far table. This is new: she presses an informant, and tells Ruth Bex's story herself "ahead of it going round". Real change, earned across chs 12-16. Moves Ruth's box without asking and is told off ("leave mine alone"), which is a small unforced error that fits her habit of fixing things silently.
- Chloe with Mom at the winter dinner: she has not told her parents about rifles since September. "And Chloe has nothing at all." Her omission is in character (she edits her parents out; ch 1, 14). Believable.
- Mom: learns her 14-year-old has been shooting rifles for months, says "Why is this the first I'm hearing about it?", fights quietly in the kitchen, asks three practical questions on Sunday, and then phone calls resume at twice a week. This is the book's largest under-reaction. Mom, who in ch 15 would not let a 12-year-old near a gas oven at midnight, accepts live rifles after one evening; she never raises withdrawal, which she offered in ch 15. Partly explained by "That is a hold" (she knows leaving costs the house/lease) but the book does not say that, and a reader will find the quick return to normal weak.
- Dad: admits Chloe "said something on the phone in October" and "I thought you knew." Out of the two parents he has long been the passive one, but a father who knows his 14-year-old is using rifles and does not mention it to his wife for two months strains belief. Characteristic avoidance, but not enough to cover the weight of it.
- Iyad: spreads the "taught backwards" story at the long table when Chloe is absent. Consistent; still gossip-as-compulsion. The girl at the sinks, who will not name him, lets Chloe infer the source. Good.
- Bex: again narrates Chloe's event before she can. Fourth time; Chloe's reaction (tell Ruth first) is a response, not just endurance.
- Ruth: "I need to know that, and now I can't. You've made that box different." Fair, in character. Her remark about Aymar's stopwatch (calls someone with her last name "mine") is unclear on the page.
- Kavi: sits on a known error for a fortnight, hoping it will fall; Ruth: "That's a hope with a schedule on it." Consistent with his sulking at ch 13 and his private working.
- Sam: asks a girl out vaguely ("I'd think about it and get back to you"); Ruth criticises. In character; no depth.
- Sanders: puts Chloe at the end of a list of five on a paper and tells her in passing. Mild, plausible adult behaviour.

## Chapter 18: Fifteen
- Chloe (15): hits a plateau at sixty on the range, cannot let go, "loading again before the card is marked"; skips dinner thinking; collects every card in the year to prove a pattern; then calls her own plateau a pattern of eleven people. Good perfectionist behaviour. Her handling of Iyad's Aurel rumour is her most direct confrontation yet ("Say who told you before that, or say there wasn't anybody"), then she stops using the language-block stairs; a believable mix of nerve and avoidance. She sets her own broken finger in a corridor at midnight and never tells staff; in-world plausible, a sign of how much she hides.
- Chloe and Sandoval: negotiates for her name on the next agreement. A 15-year-old asking for credit is in character (credit theft, ch 12-17), and a growth step. She does the opposite of ch 12: asks. The adult's candour that she would not put a federal agreement before a minor is a bit stagey, but Sandoval's request framed as "not an instruction" is believable.
- Voss: gives the stock speech, then answers Chloe's data with "Some things you have to work out on your own." A teacher refusing to explain an observed plateau after Chloe brings every card is in character for the school but is a mild lapse in a coach who wants her to improve.
- Kowalczyk: "It's the only one there is, you just have to learn faster than they do." Same school-wide non-answer.
- Hark: the money exercise; Chloe lends cautiously and finishes with seventeen dollars. A consistent, risk-averse result. Hark's rebuke "You bought a piece of somebody else's ceiling" is sharp teaching, plausible.
- Iyad: runs a ledger of everybody's results and then pairs her with Aurel. Gossip is now a consistent habit; Rustem's rebuke of him is a nice minor turn.
- Rustem: buys dead motors, argues for twenty minutes about the rate, loses; he is the winner of the money exercise. Believable.
- Sam: "I'm on forty. I sleep fine on it." Content with middle. Nice contrast to Chloe; consistent.
- Priya's phone and Kavi's fix: school full of children who treat their own chat as a security problem; Ruth immediately calls "held" the wrong sentence. Sensible and consistent with ch 16 secrecy.
- Dad: teaches her to drive and refuses to be excused ("that one's mine, other side"). Warm and in character. His coffee still full at the far end of the reservoir is a good detail.
- Grandmother: reads the burns and the offset knuckle, hears "I'm not telling you which corridor", and changes the subject. A grown woman learning her 15-year-old granddaughter set her own finger and not pressing is an under-reaction, but fits an older relative who does not want to know.

## Chapter 19: Sixteen
- Chloe (16): fails the bar by four points because she wrote only the answer and not the four marks of reasoning; the school's mark scheme punishes the same economy that kept her safe in first grade (ch 1-2). Her refusal in Amberg's office ("Writing it out afterwards is just me proving I can hold a pen") echoes Marek's "receipt" refusal in ch 16, which she punished. She is now on the wrong side of her own rule and needs Amberg to point it out. This is a strong irony and a believable blind spot; she then writes the paper out and passes by 22. Good arc beat.
- Chloe does not answer Bex in the hall except "I can do the fraction myself" at her back. Underplays; consistent.
- Bex: "A fiftieth... it's the closest miss on that board", said loudly over everyone. Clumsy, public, and "kind". The reader can now see she is trying to comfort. A good turn that complicates Bex; the repeated credit-taking (chs 12-17) and this reassurance are both loud, which makes her coherent as someone who cannot speak quietly.
- Kavi: "I passed it by six." and then apologises. In character: awkward, then marks her practice paper honestly.
- Amberg: reads out each mark and lays her paper next to the scheme. A strict but supportive teacher; plausible.
- Marek: returns the practice stack unread, with his own improved version of the shed question. In character.
- Mom: "That's not the girl who left here for that school in the first place." First time she says aloud what she has worried about since ch 11. Believable, mildly late.
- Chloe asks Dad whether they wanted her to stay home; he stalls. Strong, honest, in character for him (ch 8-9 slow responses).
- Ruth: turns up to the range every Saturday although bored; Chloe's account is generous and probably accurate. Consistent with her loyalty (ch 1-18).
- Priya and Nadia's negotiation: Chloe believes Priya lost on purpose. A guess, but not verified.

## Chapter 20: The Parking Lot
- Ruth: in ch 15 she insisted on counting respirations every two minutes for four sedated men and fretted about their work physicals. Here she checks a concussed man, says "He's fine", and the four leave seven injured adults (one with a concussion, one with a dislocated shoulder Nadia resets, one with a bitten lip) on the ground, unreported, and walk home. Ruth's only objection is "Should we report them to the police?", and she folds at once to "That's home, and not coming back." The reasoning (they would be expelled) is in character for children, but it is out of character for the careful Ruth who sat with intruders for 15 minutes. Moderately believable; the main inconsistency in her.
- Sam: asks "Let me have this one", disarms three armed men in 22 seconds, then is upset it was too easy. Fits his overhang drive (ch 12) and his need for stakes. The complaint "Why was it nothing?" is a fine, true character beat. The fight itself is implausible physically but the characters' behaviour is coherent.
- Chloe: reads the scene as three exits and a bunched cluster; supplies the explanation of why the muggers were unskilled. But her record is "sixty-fourth out of ninety-one, bottom third four years running" and she is shown holding off or evading seventy-five trained men, then keeping two captives in a room over a feed merchant's. The book is aware of this and makes it the joke and the thesis (the attackers were "thirteen year olds"). The silence for seven months, while being hunted, is consistent with her offline habit and her wish for "something that belongs to me" (ch 23). Believable as a person; the physical feat is the stretch.
- Ruth: tells Priya she is "top ten" of "people", is corrected ("top sixty on a generous day"), and then works out a list of places any of them sleeps, noticing she wrote her own address last. This is a good ongoing flaw: Ruth still cannot see how far from ordinary the others are, and the self-protection is shown in the writing order. Strong.
- Chloe: types a paragraph, deletes it, sends one line (unseen), then goes to work; reads the intake form number Theo supplied and runs a search under a different name. A government employee who has just learned that two agents are held captive by a friend and who stays at her desk is plausible: she compartments exactly like the rule she wrote. Consistent.
- Eli: the kettle is left to boil dry while he reads; later sends bolts, braces and a second exit to a woman who "had already done" most. Characteristic usefulness, and in line with his need for lists.
- Theo: sends an intake form number, an act that could expose him; understated and consistent with his careful, quiet pattern now that the stakes are physical.
- Sam: offers leave and a cheaper flight on the next day, twice. Loyal, repetitive in offering, true.
- Kavi: takes six hours to answer, sends software, then explains it unprompted; his question "what is on the yard gate" is exactly him.
- Nadia: offers company in the yard; receives a photo of a hinge. Her quiet help fits.

## Whole book

### Chloe Kessler
- Want: to be inside a room where she is not the slowest to be understood or the fastest to be punished; at six, a place where "Nobody was mean" (ch 8). From adolescence, to be certain of her own work and not owe anyone. At the end, to belong to something that is not the school and still be known by it.
- Fear and self-belief: that wanting more makes her "weird" (Kayleigh, ch 1; Bryce, ch 7), so she trims; that her answers are hers alone and are safest short. She believes she has to work out the why herself ("the rightness belongs to him", ch 6).
- Arc: the best-built in the book. Masking at six, collapse at seven (ch 7, 9), failure and growth at Halstead (ch 10), a repeated pattern of absorbing small slights and withdrawing (Bex ch 12-17, Iyad ch 13, 18, Priya's riding ch 12), then slow change: telling Kavi first (ch 14), telling Ruth the round herself (ch 17), confronting Iyad (ch 18), asking Sandoval for her name (ch 18), confronting Bex (ch 21), saying "the geometry's mine" to Whitaker (ch 30). The bar failure (ch 19) is the book's neatest irony: her old economy of words fails her, and she resists Amberg exactly as Marek resisted her.
- Most believable moments: the bike and birthday offer and then wheeling the bike back (ch 9); the library renewal (ch 7); the 10-page cutting down in the college essay (ch 21); the "fifty seconds" shrunk to a sentence at work (ch 28); the unsent paragraph on the bus (ch 36); turning down the job for "the same thing with a different door on it" (ch 22).
- Least believable moments: handing a colleague an unidentified tablet she has just failed to identify (ch 28); signing a federal break-in with a thought that lasts "as long as it takes a light to change" (ch 33); not asking whether the offer stays open (ch 22, which she admits); the blank-sheet failure of Marek with no workaround (ch 16); "theo that was a drill" then "theo which four" (ch 32), when she was one of the four.
- Inconsistencies: ch 21 says she told Marek "it wouldn't count against him either way," which contradicts the office's answer in ch 16 that a blank column scores nothing. Ch 28 says she has taken the pill "since I was seven", while ch 24 treats it as last winter's. Ch 23 introduces the Foreign Service goal only after "I don't know what I'd be anywhere else" (ch 22), so the career arrives late and conveniently. Ch 30 has her knowing the house-offer reasoning she was not present for in ch 9.

### Meg (Chloe's mother)
- Want: her daughter safe, whole and close; to know "what the next step is" (ch 1) and whether she can pull her out any day (ch 3).
- Arc: strongest in chs 1-9 as investigator and advocate (district office, postmark, phone calls, Dr. Ammons' list, "the lease ends the day we take her out"). She then recedes. By ch 17 she is shocked about rifles but resumes calls within weeks; by ch 22 she is giving practical advice about loans. She is consistent in love and in worry about the wrong things (the gas oven, the curfew).
- Most believable: ch 9, sitting in the car after the visit; ch 11, "she has never fought me... she always stops the moment she knows"; ch 22, "a fact and a fault."
- Least believable: ch 17, learning her 14-year-old has fired live rifles for four months and then asking three questions and carrying on; she had offered to collect Chloe the Monday after a gas oven letter (ch 15).
- Inconsistency: ch 3-8 mother who investigates everything accepts "a school ... that gets over a hundred" without any check on what the school did with its graduates, but later chapters admit she cannot know.

### Dave (Chloe's father)
- Want: peace in the house and Chloe happy; to be the one who says yes when Meg cannot.
- Arc: passive, doorway-standing, in ch 8-9 ("with a dishcloth in my hand"); then acts (sends the form, pushes the house); then plays, teaches (hot dog, driving, steps); then in ch 22 turns to money as his language of worry.
- Most believable: ch 6 "Cool" and the napkin in his pocket; ch 18 the driving lessons; ch 16 the 4 mm riser.
- Least believable: ch 17, knowing about the rifles from October and saying nothing ("I thought you knew") to a wife who has asked every question about safety since ch 3.
- Possible inconsistency: ch 9 has him sending the Halstead form "days ago and kept to himself". The text does not say whether Meg knew. If she did not, her instant "We're saying yes" is fast.

### Ruth Aymar
- Want: to be right, to be told when she is not, and to belong in a room where saying so is allowed.
- Arc: loud, corrective, loyal (ch 4-21); steady behind Chloe on the range every Saturday (ch 18-19); sudden collapse in her second MIT year into a silent year (ch 31); returns by finding a proof, one man (ch 32), and then sees how far outside ordinary she and the others are (ch 34, 36).
- Most believable: ch 12 ("she should have said it while he was writing"); ch 31, writing her theory on a notebook cover and sitting on a wall until the light changed twice; ch 36, writing the address list from the bottom and putting her own last.
- Least believable: ch 20, calling "He's fine" on a concussed man and leaving seven injured adults without a call, after sitting with four sedated men and counting respirations every two minutes in ch 15. Also ch 36 "top ten percent of people alive" as an estimate of Priya, which is Ruth's blind spot and so, not a flaw.
- Inconsistencies: a year of not saying anything is the opposite of "I say it and then I find out" (ch 4), but ch 31 gives the reason. Ch 17's "He's got my last name... So he's mine" about a stopwatch owner named Aymar is unclear: it reads as if another student shares her surname, which nothing else supports.

### Sam Marsh
- Want: a hard thing to climb, and the next one after it; to be told when the real one starts.
- Arc: none by design. He is cheerful, social, hard to beat, content with forty percent (ch 18), and puzzled that the Army is easy (ch 25-26). The book uses his sameness to measure the world, which is good use.
- Most believable: the overhang (ch 12), "Why was it nothing?" after seven armed men (ch 20), "was that it sir or is that the practice one" (ch 26), repeating his offer of leave (ch 36).
- Least believable: telling the captain the bullet-shooting story outright (ch 25), which the book explains as naivety; the culvert fight (ch 26) is in character and he owns it.
- Inconsistency: ch 22 says he is doing "the officer program" in the Army, but ch 23 has him signing at a recruiting desk and ch 25 makes him "Private Marsh" in basic.

### Kavi
- Want: the problem nobody has the answer to, and to stay with it ("what I want to do is happening in one building and I'm standing in it", ch 22).
- Arc: steady. Child pedant (ch 5), sulks when someone else gets the answer first (ch 13), sits on a known error a fortnight (ch 17), turns down the fence outing to finish something (ch 20), stays at the school, builds the watcher (ch 32).
- Most believable: the stopwatch of tone in "That's a hope with a schedule on it" (Ruth to him, ch 17); the exit interview exchange in the network room; ch 34 going still at the sentence about collection failing.
- Least believable: little. He is the most consistent character in the book.
- Inconsistency: none found. Thin on feeling.

### Nadia
- Want: to be called for a price; a counter where somebody answers the phone; to owe nobody.
- Arc: from flat-spoken accuser at the table (ch 16, 20) to someone whose refusal of the school job and college looks like foolishness and is not (ch 21-24). She is the book's best-sustained secondary arc: 201 applications, the site, the hires, the interviews, the sentence "I cant find anyone who can finish a thought" (ch 27).
- Most believable: ch 24 with the bank manager, fee waived for a year; ch 21, "Then the form isn't reading the person"; ch 22, "A desk with nobody calling it isn't a job".
- Least believable: ch 27, going alone to four men over a tire shop with a bluff and a self-sending file, and the men letting her go. Eli's reply names the flaw. Everything about her risk model elsewhere is careful.
- Inconsistency: ch 32 lets Eli hold a key to her parents' shop's books on a test, against her habit of checking every ledger line herself.

### Bex Alcantar
- Want: to be heard first and to be the author of the story that gets told.
- Arc: a repeating pattern (ch 12, 13, 14, 17, 21), a clumsy comfort (ch 19, "A fiftieth, Chloe"), a flat defence of her habit (ch 21, "And I said it out loud, with somebody writing it down"), and a long, fair reference interview (ch 30). The book finally lets her be a person, though never a close one.
- Most believable: ch 19 the loud kindness; ch 21 the unrepentant reply.
- Least believable: ch 21, where she repeats Chloe's geometry in detail; without a clear way she knew the April drawing.
- Inconsistency: ch 22 has Ruth tell Sam she never gave a girl credit; Bex's behaviour with Chloe (a girl) fits, but Marek (a boy) also loses credit to her in ch 19 only slightly. No contradiction.

### Iyad Mansour
- Want: to keep the table's ledger of everyone's numbers.
- Arc: none. Retells everything at the volume of the far end (ch 12, 13, 17, 18, 22), always slightly wrong or cruel.
- Believable as a type; becomes a device. Rustem's rebuke in ch 18 is the only complication. Not inconsistent, only repetitive.

### Eli
- Want: to get in, and not be at the top of anything.
- Arc: from stray cipher-maker (ch 16) to breaking his employer on a joke (ch 24) to the book's casual criminal (ch 32-33) to a moment of honest vulnerability ("i have never once been at the top of a room", ch 34).
- Most believable: ch 34's confession; ch 35's overreading of the noticed clause (self-serving, human).
- Least believable: ch 24, five break-ins on his boss's joke "but hes my boss so"; ch 33 "everyone speeds" as if a federal statute were a traffic fine.
- Inconsistency: ch 16 has him patient and deliberate; ch 24 and 33 have him reckless. Not impossible, but the change is not shown.

### Theo
- Want: to be a careful reader of things and to keep his friends out of harm.
- Arc: a shy analyst (ch 24, 29) who tells the chat what he should not (ch 29, 32), refuses and then joins the break-in (ch 33), and reads a name in his own file that he will not share (ch 34).
- Most believable: his grandmother call about the dog (ch 29); his "still the stupidest thing" (ch 33).
- Least believable: the leaks. A man who holds his phone low "the way he'd hold a document above his clearance" (ch 24) posts the file to a chat from his desk within days.
- Inconsistency: ch 33 says he cannot write the rules because his name cannot appear anywhere on them, yet he has already put the classified facts in the chat.

### Priya
- Want: a year with nothing already decided, and a mare in foal.
- Arc: consistent from ch 4 (talks about horses), to ch 12 (hurt by Chloe's silence), to ch 19 (lost to Nadia on purpose), to South America (ch 23), to being hunted (ch 36). She is warm, practical, and independent.
- Most believable: ch 12, her kind confrontation at dinner; ch 36's feeding schedule.
- Least believable: ch 36, the physical story of seventy-five men and two captives from a 64th-of-91 student; the text notes the point.
- Inconsistency: none beyond that.

### Marek
- Want: to do the real work and never hand in a page.
- Arc: none; he is exactly the same from ch 15 to ch 23, which is correct. He is the book's one honest rule-breaker and Chloe's mirror.
- Believable throughout.

### The adults
- Mrs. Aldana, Ms. Vance, Dr. Ammons, Deb, Whitaker, the Army captain and the major, Okoro, and Hearn: grounded and distinct. Deb and the captain are the best "outsiders" (her son's batting average; the captain's calls and memos).
- The Halstead teachers (Doyle, Baptiste, Hearn, Bell, Voss, Kowalczyk, Amberg, Okonkwo): the same wise, spare, withholding voice; "Some things you have to work out on your own" (Voss, ch 18), "the sheet is already written" (Bell, ch 13). This is intended, but they flatten into one person.
- Parents of other children: all suspect a scam and come anyway (ch 6). It is a small crowd scene, believable and funny.

## The ten least believable actions, ranked

1. Ch 27: Nadia, eighteen, goes alone to a room of four criminals, bluffs a self-sending file, and they let her go after photographing her plate. Eli's "a dead mans switch only pays off if youre already dead" is the right reading. The criminals' behaviour is the larger problem.
2. Ch 17: Meg and Dave on the rifles. A father who has known since October and a mother who then restores the phone schedule within weeks.
3. Ch 28: Chloe giving a coworker a white tablet she has just failed to identify, "never once failed".
4. Ch 33: Chloe's near-instant yes to a federal break-in, the book's largest reversal of her rule-following character.
5. Ch 29 and 32: Theo's leaks to the chat, the second time after he has already told himself he shouldn't.
6. Ch 20: Ruth, medically careful in ch 15, folds in seconds on leaving seven wounded men in a car park.
7. Ch 35: no one invokes the stop clause after three unexplained interferences, including Chloe, who read the pattern correctly ("hes telling us hes there").
8. Ch 22: Chloe turns down the offer without asking if it stays open; she calls it a miss herself.
9. Ch 24 and 33: Eli's break-ins on thin grounds ("but hes my boss so"; "everyone speeds").
10. Ch 16: Chloe fails Marek without trying to transcribe his spoken answers or ask a teacher.

Honourable mentions: ch 3 (parents send a six-year-old to a school nobody can verify after nine days); ch 15 (four twelve-year-olds sedate four adults and calmly argue about scoring); ch 21 (Bex's knowledge of the April drawing); ch 25-26 (Sam telling a captain the bullet story, with the school's silence in ch 21 on the record).

## Things that looked wrong and are explained later
- Ruth's six-month silence (ch 28) and the group's lack of alarm: explained by ch 31, where the chat's joke-making habit is the reason she cannot speak.
- Mom's long refusal to say yes to Halstead (ch 7-9): explained by her own line, "every morning I still don't have one good enough" (ch 8), and the house lease (ch 9).
- Chloe's withdrawal at the table in ch 16 and her later credit to herself in ch 30: explained by the Bex thread and her growing willingness to claim.
- Chloe's hesitation about the "Foreign Service" career: late but connected to her languages (ch 12-19).

## Characters who work best and worst
- Work best: Chloe (consistent and earned change), Sam (a constant, used well), Ruth (loud, then silent, then restored with cause), Nadia (precise, costed, human), Kavi (consistent), Marek (does not change, correctly), Dave in his small scenes, Meg in chs 1-9, Deb, the Army captain and Okoro.
- Work worst: Theo and Eli (plot instruments in chs 24-35 whose carefulness and recklessness are written to the scene), Iyad (one function), Bex (until ch 19, a repeating device), the Halstead staff as a single wise voice, and Meg and Dave after ch 17, who become reactive to what the plot tells them.
