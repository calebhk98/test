# Reading journal: story and logic

Lens: a story editor's read for continuity, plausibility, character, setups and payoffs, and structure, one chapter at a time.

## Chapter 01: Before

Opens in autumn 2011 with Chloe, six, in first grade. Her mom is on the phone with the school complaining that the folder of notes on Chloe is not leading anywhere. The chapter then shows the problem from Chloe's side: she finishes work instantly, gets bored, drifts, and is told she has trouble focusing. Mrs. Aldana says she is among the strongest readers she has had, but also that she "can do the work when you want to". The doctor says nothing is medical and recommends an attention evaluation. The appointment is set for March 7.

The chapter is built from small scenes that each do one job: the library rule argument, the dinner retelling of the train-station book, the tennis ball game, Peanut and Icarus, "You talk weird", the one-word answers in November and December. Together they show a bright kid learning to shrink herself. That is the real engine of the chapter, and it works. The dinner scene (the locker key) shows her mind at full size, so the later shrinking costs something. I like that her mom is both the advocate and the one who half-listens.

Plausibility: a first grader reading chapter books, arguing library logic, and making the "ocean going somewhere else" cloud line is on the edge but the chapter earns it by making her plainly unusual. A first grader who decides on her own to "get good at sitting there" and manages it by February is believable and a bit sad.

Character: Chloe wants to be left alone to read, and then wants not to be noticed after Kayleigh's comment. She makes a real choice (mask herself) rather than just being acted on. The dad is quiet and nearly absent; his "She was quiet tonight" is the only hint he notices. The mom's mouth drop after the "same page" joke suggests she is more worried than she says.

Setups: the evaluation on March 7 (obviously the next chapter), Kayleigh Burns as the first social judge, the parking lot coffee cup (the lost moment while attention drifts, and the "question that had already happened without her"), the tiny habit of giving the short answer. Peanut the class pet. The grandmother's house at Christmas, cousin Dana (nineteen).

Small flag: the chapter header says September 2011 to February 2012, but the dinner scene "in September" and the conference "Thursday evening in November" are all consistent. The doctor visit comes after the November conference, so it is likely December or later; it is not dated. Not a contradiction.

Prediction: the evaluation either gets her labelled ADHD or "gifted", and the label will be wrong or incomplete in a way that matters. I expect the book to be about how she is read by adults.

Facts so far:
- Sept 2011: Chloe is six, first grade, teacher Mrs. Aldana (the kindergarten teacher started the folder).
- Parents: mom and dad (unnamed so far). Cousin Dana is nineteen. Grandmother hosts Christmas.
- Classmates: Kayleigh Burns, Maddie, Bryce Dolan, Aiden R. Class pet: Peanut.
- Mom says the doctor said "None of this is medical."
- Evaluation appointment: March 7 (2012), on a sticky note.
- Library card limit: four books at a time.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

The evaluation. Dr. Prentice (Ben) runs a long battery of subtests, and Chloe overhears through the door that her reasoning and verbal scores are off the top of the chart for a six-year-old, but her processing speed (the coding page) is only ordinary. He tells her mom that a good composite will not move the school, that the gifted program starts in third grade, and that the school will probably just put a report in the folder. Chloe asks what a percentile is, her mom dodges, and ice cream on a Wednesday. Afterward nothing changes; Chloe concludes that finishing first, sitting still and keeping answers short make the room leave her alone. By June she is quiet, eats alone, and returns her library book.

This is the real thesis of the book so far, and the chapter earns it. What I like: the test is shown from inside a six-year-old's head, and the details of the subtests are convincing without being explained. The "I lost the middle of that one" and the coding page, where she is bad at "the easiest thing on the table", is what makes the profile feel real. Ben is a good adult: honest, careful, not a magic helper. His speech to the mom is the one place that sounds a bit too much like a plot summary (a composite that averages two pictures), but it is plausible for a psychologist explaining a report.

Continuity check: March 7, 2012 was a Wednesday, and the chapter says "unheard of on a Wednesday". Good. "Nothing changes" matches ch1's setup. Class has twenty-eight kids. The coffee cup story is repeated to Ben and agrees with ch1.

Plausibility: the mom says "He didn't give me a number" but Ben said the number aloud through the door. That reads as the mom withholding, which is fine and in character. The school doing nothing for two years is believable. One odd thing: Ben says "she answered every item the form has for a six-year-old", i.e. she topped out the test. It would be unusual for a psychologist to leave a six-year-old with a "good" summary and no recommendation for testing or acceleration beyond the school; but he does say walk the report in. Acceptable.

Character: Chloe's decision at the end of the month (be quiet, finish first, be left alone) is now a stated philosophy, which sets up what has to break later. She is also learning that "good" questions are off limits for children. Her mom is protective but dodging. The dad is absent and apologizing; this is a quiet thread (he "tried to move two things and managed neither").

Structure: ch1 and ch2 together cover Sept 2011 to June 2012. The mom's phone-call fierceness in ch1 does not return here; she accepts "a report that goes in a folder" without a visible fight. I want to see if the mom actually takes the report to the school, since Mrs. Aldana appears not to have changed anything.

Prediction: the report goes in the folder and the label "bright but fine" follows Chloe for years. The "gifted program in third grade" is a payoff to watch.

Facts so far:
- March 7, 2012 was a Wednesday; evaluation done by Dr. Prentice, who goes by Ben.
- Class of twenty-eight.
- Gifted program starts in third grade.
- Chloe's mom promises nothing about a number: "He didn't give me a number".
- Report card in June says Chloe "is a pleasure to have in class".
- Dad missed the appointment because of work.

## Chapter 03: The Letter

July 2012. A typed letter addressed to "Miss Chloe Kessler" invites her to a free four-week summer session at a school called Halstead, with transport, room and board, and a free hotel for families. Her parents (mom is Meg, dad unnamed) are alarmed. The district says no such school exists and warns about scams, with a postmark from a city four states away and dated eleven days before arrival. The phone number is answered fast and with every answer ready, over a hundred children from all over, and "their list is usually pretty good." Dad finds a polished site with Olympian and SEAL teachers. Mom finds no outside trace of the school at all. After nine days of arguing they drive her four hours and mom stays at the hotel a few days. Dad will come for her on the last Friday.

This is the inciting incident and the chapter is good at making it feel both too good and too creepy. The parents' reactions are the best part: mom's insistence that "there's always a reason", dad's silent rereading of the teacher page, the overheard "She's been flat since Christmas". The real reason they say yes is Chloe herself, not the school, and that is the right reason. Chloe's own choice is also clear: "It's got my name on it. It says Miss." That is her want, to be addressed as a person.

Plausibility is where the chapter leans hardest. The parents hear the district say people do this, see a postmark from nowhere, find zero independent trace of a school with over a hundred children a year for four years, and still send a six-year-old, then leave her. The chapter tries to handle this with the emergency-number test calls and the hotel, but I still read the yes as the plot needing it. The best fix would be one concrete thing that tips them (a known person, a reference, the school having been in touch with Prentice's office), not more reassurance. I also suspect the letter's source is the evaluation in ch2: the name on a list the same spring. The book hasn't said so, but "usually pretty good" and "networks" point at it, and nobody has asked Dr. Prentice.

Small continuity points to track. Chronology of the nine days is jumbled: "on the eighth ... her mom says the words a building I have yet to see", then "On the sixth day her mom also comes into Chloe's room", then "the ninth night". Reads as sloppy ordering, not a contradiction. "Four hours is one of the closer ones" but the postmark is "four states off"; the school is four hours away, so the postmark city is not the school's city, which might be a clue or an error. "Ellie Peterson is in the pull-out group" sits oddly beside ch2's "gifted program starts in third grade"; maybe Ellie is older, so only a watch item. The school "has been open four years", yet the first sentence of the letter and the phone call imply a long routine ("run it every year").

Character: Mom as suspicious logician and Dad as gentle yielding man both feel consistent with ch1. Dad's quiet time on the teacher page is a setup I want paid off. The "train station voice" joke ties to ch1's train station book.

Prediction: Halstead is a legitimate but unusual school that recruits kids through test data. The cost will be that Chloe finds out how closely she was watched.

Facts so far:
- Chloe's surname is Kessler; mom is Meg.
- Letter arrives July 2012, session is four weeks starting the second week of July.
- Postmark: a city four states away, dated eleven days before it arrived.
- School has been open four years, over a hundred children this summer, drive is four hours.
- District says no school by that name works with them.
- Dad takes the last Friday off to collect her.
- The library lets Chloe hold one book outside the four-book limit.

## Chapter 04: Pluto

First week at Halstead, July 2012. Chaotic arrival with over a hundred kids and parents. Orientation stresses that any child can go home same day with no argument (mom writes "same day" and underlines it twice). Chloe's roommate is Priya, a horse talker. The first night Chloe phones her mom and sleeps at the hotel, and again the second night; the fourth night she dials all but the last digit and stops, then her mom stays a few more days and leaves on a Sunday. Chloe is placed in the Pluto room (rooms are named after planets) with Sam (seven and a half, jokes), Ruth (corrects everyone), Kavi (quiet, corrects Sam's volcano claim with viscosity), and Owen (copies off the board, in the dining hall and again in class). Mr. Doyle is the teacher; he accepts Ruth's correction (mass, not weight) without punishing her. Chloe keeps silent for six days, then watches Ruth get away with correcting.

This chapter is the emotional hinge of the opening. The homesickness is done carefully, and I believe it: Chloe does not collapse, she negotiates with herself, and the mom's behavior (writing "same day" down, asking how far to leave the door open) sketches a parent who is afraid but trying not to show it. The turn comes through small events, not speeches: the "room-shaped object" laugh, Ruth being corrected-not-punished, Kavi correcting Sam. The key for Chloe is that she has been punished for exactly what Ruth does, and watching it go unpunished starts to unlock her.

Plausibility: a six-year-old being able to leave every night to the hotel is generous but it is how the school sells itself, and the mom has a visible role in the plot. The "same day, no argument" promise is a setup: if the school ever breaks it, that is a big moment. Chloe's reaction to Pluto (the dorm of "the one that isn't a planet") is nicely in character and lets the book hit a larger theme (rules changed, nothing changed). I like that the first laugh is not a lesson.

Continuity: Pluto became a dwarf planet in 2006, Chloe was about one, so "Since before I can remember" is correct. Mom says Pluto "was a planet when I was at school", also fine. "Lights out is earlier for the sixes than for the sevens" and "until August": the age range seems to be six and seven only so far. Ch3 said mom is back at work on the Monday and she stays "the first few days", and here she stays beyond the fourth night and leaves on a Sunday. That roughly fits if she arrived near the previous Monday, but it is a little vague whether she left before Monday of week two. Not a clear contradiction.

Character: Chloe is still suppressing, and she is also noticing. She states plainly in this chapter that she "keeps quiet in class now". Sam is a likable comic, Ruth a corrector, Kavi a quiet expert, Priya a talker who does not need an audience. Owen is only a name so far, but he does not read as part of the Pluto group yet, since he is "a few tables over" in the dining hall; later he is clearly in the room. That is fine.

Setups: the "same day" promise, the free phone, Doyle's fairness, the nurse always present, the free hotel for parents. The school's friendliness is suspiciously perfect; I keep waiting for the cost.

Facts so far:
- Rooms are named after planets; Chloe's classroom is Pluto, at the end of the hall past the fountain.
- Roommate: Priya. Pluto classmates: Sam (seven and a half), Ruth, Kavi, Owen. Teacher: Mr. Doyle.
- Chloe stays six days silent, speaks on the seventh day period.
- Mom leaves on a Sunday; Dad will return on the last Friday.
- Lights out is earlier for sixes until August.

## Chapter 05: Behind

Weeks one to three. Everything moves at the speed of the fastest kid, which is Ruth, and Chloe finds that she likes it. Three episodes: the bridge project (Ruth says triangles, Sam builds flat and wide, Chloe half-sees the diagonal but goes along with Sam; their bridge holds eleven pounds and folds sideways, Ruth's snaps at nineteen); the fractions lesson with Mr. Baptiste, where Chloe gets every answer right but not the reason, and works it out alone that night with drawn rectangles; and the first departure, Owen, who "wasn't having fun" and was crying in the stairwell. More kids leave through the month. At the end of week three Chloe argues openly about the moon being "a liar" and realises she has stopped checking every sentence, "the check she has run on every sentence since November".

This is the first chapter where the book shows Chloe being challenged rather than rescued. The best beat is the fractions night: she is furious at getting right answers without understanding, and then actually does the work alone. It shows who she is: someone who needs the reason, not the grade. The second best is the bridge, where she sees the diagonal and lets Sam's confidence win. She is wrong by choice, and then she writes the right reason in her own analysis. That is character, and it sets up a pattern I expect to recur: Chloe sees something and defers to a friend.

The Owen thread is the main quiet worry. A kid cries on the stairs, his mom comes the next day, and nobody asks him why. Chloe asks the right questions at dinner ("How long was it between him saying it and his mom being at the door?") and gets nothing from Ruth. Then "by the fourth time Chloe reads the event off the corridor". I flag this as a loaded setup. Four early departures in a month out of over a hundred is plausible for homesick six-year-olds, but the way the book keeps returning to the empty rooms makes me think the school is hiding something about who leaves and why.

Continuity and logic. Ch3 has dad saying Chloe "is still learning" to swim; ch5 says "Chloe arrived unable to swim". These can both be true but the second is stronger than the first. Owen is "in her hall" and also had "his door", while Priya is Chloe's roommate in what looks like a girls' room; I can't tell whether the dorms are co-ed. Not a contradiction yet. Timeline of week two is a little tangled: the bridge is loaded "on the Tuesday", Owen leaves "yesterday" on Tuesday and Chloe says "We broke the bridge yesterday" on Wednesday, so that holds. The break between this chapter's "the second week" math lesson and the bridge test on the same week's Tuesday is overlapping but not contradictory.

Plausibility: kids here are very bright but not impossibly so; Chloe's rectangles-of-eighths insight is a real insight, not a trick, which helps. Mr. Baptiste is a genuine teacher, not a function.

Character: Sam is jokey and defends his own way, Ruth is right and unkind, Kavi is a smart quiet kid who wants to be accurate, and Owen has not been shown enough to count as more than a warning. Chloe is growing fast, and the line about no longer checking her sentences is a clean payoff of ch1.

Prediction: the school will use Chloe's willingness to defer as a thing to correct, and the dropouts will turn out to matter later.

Facts so far:
- Teachers: Mr. Doyle (science/general) and Mr. Baptiste (math); swim instructor is Deb.
- Bridge: gap eighteen inches; Chloe's group held to eleven pounds, Ruth's to nineteen.
- Owen left on Tuesday of week two; more kids leave before the month ends, four by the time Chloe counts.
- Marisol is another classmate.
- Chloe stopped self-censoring around week three.

## Chapter 06: The List

End of the session, early August 2012. Parents at the hotel breakfast compare notes: none of them was told where the list comes from, nobody has seen the bus, and all of them were first afraid of a scam. On the last night Chloe tells her dad everything she did in twenty-seven days (CPR on a dummy called Gary, the bridge, fractions on a napkin, Spanish, sign language, a made-up-language puzzle, wood shop, cooking, computers and a cold server room, karate, chess). Afterward the four friends sit in the hall and Ruth says they should all "get in" for the whole year; there is a form and a separate day in the fall when "different people" look at them. Ruth promises a weekly letter. Chloe does the math of the four-hour drive and decides she must persuade her parents. On the drive home her dad, thinking she is asleep, tells her mom (Meg) it was all of that in twenty-eight days, and neither of them can name what it is.

What works: the dad's face while Chloe talks, and the napkin he folds into his shirt pocket, say more than any speech. The father-and-mother call at the end is quietly effective, and Chloe faking sleep with "her breathing slow and even, on purpose" is a deliberate mirror of ch1's drive home, now reversed: then she hid from a worried conversation, now she is listening to a wondering one. Chloe's want has become clear and active: she wants to go back and she will work on her parents.

What I question: the chapter retells the bridge and the fractions in almost the same wording as ch5, which the reader has just seen. The list-of-everything is the point (title, her fullness), but the bridge and fractions repeat is long and the dad's non-reaction is strong enough without it. I would cut the repeated detail and keep the list of new things. Also a six-year-old doing CPR, karate, wood shop, Spanish, sign language, a computer lab and chess in four weeks is a lot, but the book frames it as a school built for this, so it is acceptable.

Continuity: dad is Dave, mom is Meg (named in the car). A woman at breakfast says "I told Dave I was keeping her home from a building I had yet to see", and the phrase "a building I have yet to see" is exactly what Meg said in ch3. Either a deliberate echo or a repeated line; there is also a second Dave, which is a confusion risk. Days counted: "twenty-seven" the night before, "twenty-eight" total, matches the four-week session and a Friday pickup. Kavi's parents asked about the year "on the third day". Chloe's prediction in the bridge, "I wrote fourteen and was wrong by three", matches ch5's eleven-pound result. A girl's dad drove twelve hours; the earlier note about four hours being "one of the closer ones" holds.

New information that matters: Halstead is also a full-year school, with a form and an in-person evaluation day in the fall, "different people doing the looking". That is new compared with ch3, where it was presented only as a summer session, and the parents were never told about a year. It makes the summer a trial run, which reads a little like a recruitment funnel. Another quiet note: nobody has seen the bus; the hotel parents say it is "a rumour". A free bus nobody uses is a small thread.

Prediction: the fall day is a selection filter, one of the four will not get in, and I suspect Chloe or Sam. Ruth's letters will fade or become important.

Facts so far:
- Dad is Dave, mom is Meg. Chloe's session is 28 days ("twenty-seven" counted the last night).
- Chloe wrote "fourteen" as her bridge prediction.
- Friends: Sam, Ruth, Kavi (and Priya her roommate). Instructors mentioned: Mr. Baptiste (math), Deb (swim).
- Halstead also runs a school year; admission needs a form and a day in the fall.
- Ruth promises weekly letters starting Monday.
- Another girl's father drove twelve hours.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room

August to early October 2012. Chloe goes home and tries to reach Sam, Ruth, Kavi and Priya, and finds she never learned a single last name: first names only were used at camp. Her mom phones the school and asks it to pass on the Kessler number, and the school says it did, "to all three numbers". Nobody calls. Chloe asks her parents repeatedly if she can go to Halstead for the year; at home they keep saying they are thinking. Second grade starts with Ms. Vance, the same classmates from first grade (Bryce, Kayleigh, Aiden), and work she did last year. A time capsule exercise gets Chloe mocked again ("Why do you talk like that?"), so she writes something safe. She stops borrowing library books. Her mom asks her school about acceleration and is told third grade is the floor and testing is in spring only. In early October Chloe cries at her desk and cannot say why.

This is the book's "return to ordinary" chapter, and it is where Chloe's cost becomes concrete: she has seen what a fitting room is like and cannot unsee it. The scene where she cannot produce a single last name is the best in the chapter, because it quietly makes the school look strange (first names only for a month) and makes Chloe's loss total. She had friends and no way to reach them. It also gives her mom a good beat: the hand on the head and "the last names can go."

Contradiction with ch6: the camp goodbye. In ch6 the departure scene has Sam saying "see you at the thing in the fall" and Kavi holding out his hand palm up to Chloe, then Priya hugging her. Here, ch7 opens with the departure again and says "Sam left early" and that Kavi was "over by the double doors" and "by the time they pass he has gone", with only Ruth reaching her at the flagpole. These cannot both be the same morning. One of the two scenes should be changed, and the book should decide whether Chloe got her goodbyes.

Other things to check. The school says it passed the message to "all three numbers", but her mom named four children (Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Priya). Her mom asks "Who's Sam, honey?" yet a few lines later says "you said Kavi a hundred times in that car", so what mom knows is uneven. Chloe says "Mrs. Prahl kept ours on the windowsill for a week", a teacher not named in ch4 or ch5 (Doyle, Baptiste); fine if there are more staff. Ms. Vance's capsule opens in fifth grade, "three years older", which matches second grade. Her mom on the phone says Chloe "finished the third-grade work in October" while it is the last week of September, a small tense slip. The time chain for the year-round program (spring test, fall start, "over a year from now") is a little loose but acceptable. Ch2 said third grade is the gifted start; ch7 agrees.

Character: Chloe now actively wants something and keeps asking, tracking the days with marks. Her mom is stuck between wanting to protect her and wanting to help, and has started on the school side without telling Chloe. Dad hardly speaks. The crying scene at the end lands because it is unexplained, but I want the reason to be given in the next chapter, since it comes from the waiting.

Prediction: the next chapter or two will have the parents decide, and the form and fall "day" become the next obstacle. The missing friends will turn up through a letter from Ruth.

Facts so far:
- No last names for any camp child; no contact details exist.
- Second grade teacher: Ms. Vance. Time capsule: opened in fifth grade.
- Local school testing window is spring only, third grade is the floor.
- Chloe's library card put away; she stops taking books out.
- Chloe begins marking days without a yes on a spelling worksheet.

## Chapter 08: The Asking

October 2012 to January 2013. Ms. Vance calls Chloe's mom after the crying. A therapist, Dr. Ammons, sees Chloe once, tells her mom the problem is depression, and notices that every question about the summer program produces the same answer: "The same" adults. Chloe's only answer to what she liked is "It was hard." Her mom pushes the local school and gets a weekly Thursday enrichment hour with Mrs. Prahl (a cart, nine kids, puzzles, one of whom is Dev, another Hana). The school allows her in a year early. Chloe finishes everything in minutes and waits for "the turn", the point where it gets hard, and it never comes. She keeps asking to go to Halstead, her parents keep saying "thinking about it", and she blows up at her mom ("I hate you"). In December she tells her mom what was different at camp: "Nobody was mean." She gets a bike for Christmas. By late January she has stopped asking, and the letter is under her socks.

This is the emotional low of the first act and it is well made. The "nobody was mean" line is the best sentence in the book so far because it is small and complete. The blow-up scene is believable and does not resolve neatly; the quiet dinner afterward is true to how families behave. Chloe has agency in how she asks and then in how she stops: stopping is the sad choice, and she makes it without deciding to.

The weak spot is the mother's motive. She spent July investigating the school and was the one most afraid of it, but by October she says "I still don't have one good enough" reason to say no, and still does not call. If the book wants her inaction to be fear, it needs one concrete thing she is afraid of (cost, distance, Chloe leaving at seven, not knowing who is behind it). As written, the plot needs the parents to stall so Chloe can wait, and the mother's explanation is "I thought about it a lot", which is not an answer. Dave is also oddly passive: he says yes but never pushes.

Continuity flags. In ch7, Chloe tells the librarian "Mrs. Prahl kept ours on the windowsill for a week", about the camp bridge, in the autumn. Mrs. Prahl in ch8 is the local enrichment teacher Chloe first meets in November. Either the ch7 line is a leftover or it hints that the enrichment teacher has a connection to Halstead; I would treat it as a contradiction unless the book says otherwise. Mom tells Ms. Vance Chloe has been like this "since August" but tells Dr. Ammons "September". Dr. Ammons said she would see Chloe again, and we never see it; the depression thread is dropped after one visit, which is fine if it stays dropped. The mom's exception "a year early" is for the local program, which matches ch7's "third grade is the floor". The class of twenty-six differs from Mrs. Aldana's twenty-eight but is a different class.

Setups: the halt is not Halstead's, it is the parents'. No one has said whether Halstead is still waiting for an answer, or whether the fall "day" already passed. The letter in the sock drawer is a good object. Dana repeats the ch1 Christmas exchange, a deliberate mirror showing Chloe is back where she started.

Prediction: something will force the Halstead question back open from outside (a letter from Ruth, another contact from the school), because Chloe has stopped pushing.

Facts so far:
- Therapist: Dr. Ammons; one visit shown. Enrichment teacher: Mrs. Prahl, Thursdays, nine kids (Dev, Hana named).
- Mom's phrase: "Every night since the letter came."
- Chloe's local class has twenty-six kids this year.
- Chloe is called depressed by Dr. Ammons, October 2012.
- Letter stored in the sock drawer. Bike for Christmas 2012.
- Chloe stopped asking about Halstead by late January 2013.

## Chapter 09: February

February to April 2013. Chloe's second grade is a loop of finishing early and waiting. One afternoon her mom finds her on the floor between bed and wall, begging "please just let me go to that school" and offering her birthday, Christmas and the bike. That night her parents argue through the floor: her mom (Meg) says "What kind of family sends a seven-year-old away to live in a building four hours from her mother?" and dad (Dave) says he has wanted yes since the car ride home in August. On March 14 they tell Chloe yes: dad already sent in a form, and in April they will drive to a "day" when Halstead looks at her. She immediately tries to return the bike as the deal's other half, and her dad refuses. In April, with Chloe at the Petersons', her dad shows mom a letter offering a furnished house near campus, paid as long as Chloe is enrolled. Her mom refuses, because a house that ends when Chloe leaves is a lock. They go on the Friday: a tour with Mrs. Okonkwo, who dodges whether Sam is enrolled and says the "fall evaluation day" Ruth mentioned is nonsense, since "We take people all year round." A man with a graying beard sits with Chloe and asks what she would do with a day of her own. Mom is still sitting alone in the car at the end.

This is the strongest chapter so far. The floor scene is hard to read and it earns it, partly because the offers are so childlike and exact. The bike-return scene shows Chloe's core trait: if there was a deal, it was a deal. That is a great character beat and it connects to her fairness arguments in ch1. The parents' argument is real and gives Meg a concrete reason at last (the house is a hold on the family), which helps repair my ch8 complaint that her motive was vague, though it explains the house refusal rather than the months of stalling. Dave is shown as a man who reads contracts for a living and knows the house deal is a hold, but wants it anyway; that is good.

New facts that change the picture. Halstead is a boarding school for seven-year-olds, with a "ten weeks a year" at home figure that dad uses (where ten weeks comes from is not explained). The school will pay for a house to keep the family close, with a lease that ends when Chloe leaves. A generous offer with a leash is a good ominous beat.

Things I flag. First, the beard: in ch2 Dr. Prentice is "a man with a beard" who sits "on a chair no bigger than hers"; here "a man whose beard is going gray sits down on a chair the same size as hers". Chloe, who remembers everything, does not recognize him, and the text does not say it is him. If it is Prentice, then the "how did they get her name" answer is nearly in the room and the book is hiding it from Chloe; if it is not, the repeated detail is accidental. Second, Mrs. Okonkwo says "We take people all year round... Somebody said something to her in a corridor", while ch6 had Ruth (and Sam, who said "see you at the thing in the fall") convinced there is a fall-only day after "my mom asked". Either the school is lying or staff are inconsistent; I would like one answer. Third, Mrs. Okonkwo refuses to say whether Sam is enrolled, which agrees with the strange first-names-only policy. Fourth, dad "sent off days ago and kept to himself" a form, which is a significant act of will from a man who says little; it is not shown, only reported.

Age check: Chloe is seven in February 2013 (mom says "she's seven years old"). That fits a birthday in or just before August 2012 given she was six at camp in July and six in September 2011. Dr. Ammons is still being seen ("a handful of visits"), so the thread from ch8 is not dropped.

Prediction: she gets in, her mom leaves her there, and the contract house comes back as a trap. Also the graying-beard man is the key to the list.

Facts so far:
- Chloe is seven in Feb 2013, birthday likely August.
- March 14, 2013: parents say yes. April 2013: the visit day, on a Friday. Staff: Mrs. Okonkwo.
- Halstead takes students in any month; it offers a furnished house near campus conditional on enrollment.

## Chapter 10: April

April to June 2013. Chloe arrives at Halstead for the spring term and is met by Sam and Ruth (Ruth's last name is Aymar). Third floor is for new students; her roommate is Fen, from Michigan, who keeps a rock collection in size order. Priya arrived in January and lives on second floor. This is a successful payoff chapter. The "no card, no limit" library answers the book's first scene in ch1 (four at a time) and ch3 (the bank of sixteen), and the line "if you lose one then come and say you lost it" is a good adult response. The wheel problem and Ruth wanting to see "the shape of it" is a clear sign that Ruth is a real intellectual friend, not just a corrector. Problems and flags. The biggest is structural: the chapter jumps from the April visit in ch9 straight to Chloe arriving at Halstead, and the actual decision, the acceptance, and especially the goodbye to her parents are skipped. Contradictions. Ch3 and ch4 say "over a hundred" and "hundred-odd" children at camp, and ch10 says "of the ninety who were at camp", which is a number mismatch. Character: Chloe is now doing what she wanted, and shows hunger rather than anxiety. She also protects her mother, giving a funny answer about Fen. Ruth's earlier fear that "it's only some of us" is over; the four friends are together again except Priya on second floor.

## Chapter 13: Ten Pages

September 2015 to April 2016. Chloe is ten. Mr. Hearn assigns a ten-page essay every week, modelled on Hamilton's rate, and marks whether she can argue a side she does not hold; his December essay comes back with no note at all. Self defense is an hour a day: 10v1 sparring (best in the room is nine seconds at first, Sam gets fourteen). Paintball: Chloe works out that you watch the barrel and feet and move before the shot, then Bex tells Bell the same idea first, and Bell puts it on the board under Bex's name; Chloe says nothing again. At age ten everyone starts Defensive Watch: a posted scoring sheet (they do not enter a building, they do not learn anything about you, you take their comms and equipment, you establish what they came for, minimum personnel). Drills use adults who come over the fence or walk in the front door in ordinary coats. In her first drill a boy called Wes answers a question about how many of them there are, and the north stairwell is uncovered; Iyad jokes that "Kessler was standing in it." Thanksgiving with her father is a warm argument about tariffs. By spring she no longer minds Hearn's ten pages.

This is the chapter where the book changes gear, and it works. Defensive Watch is the ominous thread that was a stray corkboard notice in ch10 and a stray word in ch5 ("Defensive" nowhere before ch10). The sheet itself is well done: it reads as a real standard written by adults, not for children, and the phrase "They do not learn anything about you. Not your numbers, not your capability, not your positions" tells us the school expects real adversaries who want information on the children. That reframes the whole "list", the free school and the house with a lease. The reader now wants to know whether the parents know any of this, and the book has not said. A school that sparring-trains ten-year-olds ten against one, uses paintball and stages adult intrusions is something Meg would have noticed in a phone call, and she has never mentioned it; the book needs to say what the parents are told, or why Chloe tells her mother nothing.

The Bex pattern repeats almost exactly from ch12: Chloe has the idea, Bex states it first and louder, the teacher says "the sheet is already written", Ruth deflects, Chloe laughs it off. Once is a character beat, twice is a pattern, and I think the book wants it as a pattern, since Chloe's flaw (not claiming) is deliberately built. But a third round would be repetitive. Ruth is also repeating the role of friend who deflects, which is a little flattering to Chloe's silence.

Logic checks. Hamilton's list of Federalist papers (1, 6 to 9, 11 to 13, 15 to 17, 21 to 36, 59 to 61, 65 to 85) adds to fifty-one, as stated. Federalist 10 is Madison, and Hearn's flat "That's Madison" is correct. Chloe is ten in September 2015 and "Watch, which everybody starts at ten" fits. The mat room has "ninety children", matching ch11's ninety in a year, though ch10's camp-to-fall numbers still do not fit. The sequence in the Watch section is out of order ("In the second week of September" after November), but the chapter is organized by topic, not date. Grandmother says "Your mother did exactly that", so she is Meg's mother, consistent with the walnut cribbage board from ch12.

Setups: Nadia (first appearance here, and ch27 is titled "Nadia"), Wes, Theo, Kowalczyk, Bell. The standard "is posted weekly". The unexplained claim that Sam's sheet is different (ch12) is still open.

Prediction: a real adult intrusion, not a drill, will come, and the scoring will turn out to be about the children's own capabilities being hidden from someone.

## Chapter 14: Sixty Degrees

September 2016 to April 2017. Chloe is eleven. Archery is taught as a counting problem: two firing lines seventy meters apart converge at sixty degrees, and the real "target" is an arrow fired from the other line, meeting yours in the air. Bell says no one can do it, and Chloe and Odile do, at the end of October. A string of study scenes: Latin (Ruth says it is cheating), astronomy (two measurements of the age of the universe disagree), art history (a painting's price does not track the picture, and the teacher says "a good question for a different course"). Bex repeats the pattern a third time: she takes Chloe's astronomy point and even Sam's ship-fuel correction and presents them to the teacher first. This time Chloe gives Kavi the numbers herself, out loud, before Bex does. The November essay on whether area bombing was right gets a B: Hearn marks her for hedging at two places she had already answered. At Christmas her mom does not want to hear about it. In April a whole-school Watch has staff declared hostile; Ruth runs one stairwell, Nadia the third floor, Sam is marked out early, Mr. Hearn is the last teacher taken. The board lists failures, and Chloe fixes on "at no point did anybody establish how many adults were in the building."

The big thing here is that the school is teaching interception of projectiles by projectiles, and the next chapter or two should say why. The archery set-up (an arrow coming the other way at sixty meters a second, "end-on... a point that gets bigger") is not a sport. Combined with ch13's Watch rules, it is plainly training for defending against something real. The scoring on the Watch, and Chloe's guilt over counting, build the idea that her habit of noticing what nobody counted is now a serious flaw to her. It also shows she is judged on things the school never tells the students: a hidden standard "posted weekly".

What works: Chloe accepts Sam's challenge ("go and do it properly"), builds the opposing argument for the area-bombing essay, and later takes the B and agrees with Ruth that Hearn is right. That is real growth, not a reward. The art-history exchange is thematically sharp (value measured by something other than the thing), and echoes the hotel breakfast in ch6 ("a paper plate and a seat"). The grandmother's "once in an whole year is hardly a lot" is a good misunderstanding, because she hears "hit the target" as the block. It also shows Chloe has no way to explain her school to her family. I still want to know what her parents are told about hostile staff and paintball, and the book keeps not saying.

Concerns. Bex's pattern is now three occurrences (ch12, ch13, ch14) and there is a risk of it becoming a tic. The difference this time (Chloe tells Kavi first) is the only reason it still earns space. The physics of the archery set-up is a thin point: the arrows travel about seventy meters in a second and a half at sixty meters per second, which does not quite fit, but it is close enough not to break anything. Bell says he will buy the line breakfast the day anyone hits one; no one collects. April 11, 2017 was a Tuesday, but the text says the Watch "is posted on the board on the Monday", which is fine.

Setups: Sinclair's voice on the intercom, Nadia running the third floor (she is clearly being built up), the forge past the kitchens on Thursdays, Ruth's Arabic and Chloe's Latin (languages that matter for reading documents, not speaking). Odile's brother "has been out here a few times", so family visits do happen.

## Chapter 15: Twelve

September 2017 to January 2018. Chloe is twelve. She spends time at the forge under Pruitt, learning to spot flaws (a bad scroll that Sam cannot see). Bell removes the metronome, so Chloe and Odile must count aloud. The friends test whether Wednesday bread is better than other days' bread, and Mrs. Adeyemi catches them at the oven at midnight. Kavi cracks their weak encryption in seconds, Chloe walks into their path in pajamas as bait, and the four friends sedate and tie the four men; Ruth monitors breathing; one man tells them "server room" without speaking. The men are probably actors (poor costumes, wrong bolt cutters), but Chloe privately cannot decide whether a school would get it wrong "in that direction" or four real people holding the wrong list would. Their grades: C for Chloe and Sam, B minus for Ruth, B for Kavi. The sheet counts the men as inside before contact and ignores the sensors; Chloe posts a note to the duty officer about it. She sits thirtieth of ninety in 10v1 with twenty-four seconds.

This is the chapter where the Watch becomes a plot instead of a hint, and it is also a good chapter, with the best dramatic scene of the book so far: the four friends do something real and slightly wrong in a way they will have to live with. Sam's missed vein, Ruth's insistence on respiration counts, Kavi's disgust at the lazy encryption and Chloe's bait are all in character. The final ambiguity (a drill or something real) is exactly what the book needs the reader to keep carrying. I also like that Chloe chooses to file a note about the sensors, which is her first active use of the "say it" lesson that failed her with Bex.

The plausibility edge is large. Twelve-year-olds inject unconscious adults with a sedative and an antidote, and the school sends a letter to parents about an oven and not about this. The book needs to settle, soon, what the parents know. The mother's offer to bring her home is the right instinct and she has no idea what she is offering to rescue her from. Chloe's choice not to tell is hers: she begins to explain psychology and the release and the count and gives up ("You mostly can't"). That is a real pattern: Chloe has more and more that cannot be explained to home, so home gets a thinner Chloe.

Logic slips. In the bread test, five of eight right is chance, and Ruth says so ("Come back to me when you get them all"); but at Thanksgiving Chloe tells her parents "It's the same bread. Ruth was right." The test did not show that. It reads as Chloe smoothing it for her parents, but the book does not mark it. "Three years" on the oven fits her second-year cooking in 2013-14. Ch13 said unposted drills come every few weeks; ch15 says "this is the first one nobody posted beforehand" and that she had pulled a night watch before, which probably means the first unposted night one, so I treat it as fine. The grandparent-versus-parents knowledge question is still open.

Names: Pruitt (forge), Marek, Mrs. Adeyemi (kitchen/night staff), Sinclair (arrives with a trolley, so he treats the men as bodies to collect). New fact: Ruth's father "sells insurance."

## Chapter 16: Thirteen

September 2018 to July 2019. Chloe is thirteen. Thirteens teach the year below, and Chloe takes seven twelve-year-olds in mathematics. One of them, Marek, solves everything but will not hand in a sheet, because "a receipt is a different object from work". Chloe checks the rules with the mathematics office, records his blank sheets, and Marek fails the course; she writes the mark herself. Her own mathematics becomes proof-based, her materials lab hands her logs that disagree with a paper (she finds the instrument was recalibrated and the later results were left out; the postdoc, Sanders, says thank you and files it), cryptography has her break other people's locks, and a law class with Mr. Amberg says the students will take the bar at sixteen. Ruth builds a shoebox that finds network video recorders and writes noise into their files. The Marek story is the most interesting thing here. Chloe checks the rule, finds it allows only one outcome, and writes the fail, and the text says what a fail costs him "stays outside her reach for years, and she is careful for a long time about how she asks". That sentence is a promise that a later chapter will tell us. If it never does, the thread is wasted. Her action is in character (the rule is the rule, as with the bike deal in ch9), but the book is also criticizing it, which makes Chloe a more complicated protagonist. The father's riser line at the end is a neat thematic rhyme: he is the man who hides a thing "between the two of us" (as with the form in ch9), and Chloe cannot live with a known flaw.

The covert network is a major new thread and a good one. A school that logs "who talked to whom and when" is a school that is watching its students, and the table has built a hidden channel against it, without so far saying why (the stated reason is to avoid Saturday tomato counts and cover for sneaking out). Ruth's recorder box in the drawer "stays there", so I expect it to be used. Both together recast the school as an institution the students are already treating as an adversary in small ways.

Logic and continuity. The thirteens are said to be "the oldest in the building", yet Mr. Amberg says they will take the bar at sixteen and "in two years most of you will be driving". If the school was already open four years in July 2012 (ch3), then there should be students older than thirteen in 2018, and the school's age range needs explaining. Either older students were removed, or the school is smaller in time than ch3 says. This could be a deliberate mystery (where do students go?), and I mark it as one to watch. The bar exam line is loose: a bar exam needs a law degree, so the statement is a school claim, not a fact. Marek's timeline is muddled: he fails at year end, but the passage says "In January he is at breakfast as he was in December", which seems to refer to a December interim failure. Chloe's pattern with Bex has changed: she still loses the credit but she acts anyway. Iyad's retelling of the napkin scene grows from twenty minutes to "most of an hour", which is a clever illustration of how stories change.

## Chapter 17: Fourteen

September 2019 to April 2020. Chloe is fourteen. Marksmanship replaces archery: rifles at fifty meters, then moving clays and swinging targets (Kavi predicts a version that fixes the "hang" and shared count of the arrow drill). Chloe holds fifty seconds in the 10v1, and Bex retells it before she can. Chloe discovers she taught her seven thirteens a term of a topic backwards, owns up to them, and Iyad spreads it around the school; she traces the source to the far table and says nothing. Engineering and design: the table is assigned a project to locate a staff-made sound on the long field to within a hand's width. They try wireless, Kavi hides a known timing error for a fortnight, they lay 160 meters of wire, and the final result is a hand and a half off, a B. A one-month no-libraries project (Chloe writes a text renderer, Ruth synthesizes sound). Sam is six foot two and has a short-lived girlfriend. At winter break Chloe mentions rifles at the table, and her mother did not know. Her father says "She said something on the phone in October" and "I thought you knew. I thought it was fine." Mom asks whether the man behind the line is armed and lets it go.

The ending scene is the best thing in the chapter and perhaps the most important character beat since ch9. Chloe has been making her school thinner at home since ch15, and the break shows what that costs: she does not even have a way to begin ("Which one was she supposed to have opened with? Why is it the rifle?"). The father is exactly as set up: Dave knows and does not tell, as with the form in ch9 and the riser in ch16. The mother's "That's a hold" echoes her own phrase about the house in ch9. That is a lovely deliberate rhyme: the unspoken thing is a lock on the family.

What I question is the mother's follow-through. Meg has been the book's most alert parent, offered to bring Chloe home in ch15, and now learns that her fourteen-year-old shoots live rifles. She asks three questions and lets it go, and the calls resume on schedule. Even given her fear of pushing Chloe away, a mother who once wrote "same day" twice on a map would at least phone the school, and nothing in the text says she does. The book may want that restraint, but it needs one line to say why.

Continuity flags. Ruth says of Sam "He's the only person who's ever got over that wall", but ch12 says "a girl in the year below gets over it in one go" in January 2015, months before Sam did. That is a plain contradiction. The scene with "Aymar" is confusing: the range officer calls a student Aymar, and Ruth says "He's got my last name... So he's mine", so there is a second Aymar, a boy, not Ruth; it reads like Ruth until the second paragraph. The chapter spans September 2019 to April 2020 and never mentions the pandemic, with in-person field tests, "exams", and weekly calls resuming "by April"; if the book follows real dates (the book uses a real Wednesday in ch2 and a real Monday in ch12), the silence is a flaw or a deliberate sealed-school choice, and I want to see which. The physics in the sound-locating project is right (two milliseconds of air is about two thirds of a meter, half a degree is about a third of a meter per second).

Theme and prediction: the school's tasks are drifting toward defending against projectiles, locating gunfire-like sounds, and responding to people entering. Kavi's "it escalates every year" matches ch15 "Escalation". The mother now knows about rifles and must know more next time.
