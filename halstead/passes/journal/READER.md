# Reading journal: the target reader

Lens: a smart, ordinary 14-15 year old ninth grader who picked this up with no obligation to finish it.

## Chapter 01: Before

Okay. Start of the book and I kind of like Chloe already, but I also had a moment where I almost drifted off, and I think that is a bit ironic given what the chapter is about.

What worked: the library argument ("What if I brought two of them back and got two more the same day?") is funny and the librarian going "Four" is great. The train station book she explains at dinner is also good, and the part where her mom stops listening and Chloe keeps going anyway made me wince in a good way. I know that feeling, being excited about something and watching an adult's eyes go somewhere else. The coffee cup on the car roof is a nice little thing too.

What really got me was the "You talk weird" moment and then her sitting on her hands and shrinking her answers to one word ("Water"). That is the part where I actually felt something. Nobody told me she was sad, I just saw it. Also the kid who takes her book away for reading ahead in reading class. That is so unfair and I was annoyed for her.

Where I skimmed: the first page. Her mom on the phone with the school is a lot of talking and I did not know who she was talking to or what folder or what "next step" meant until later. Also the middle section, the doctor visit, is long and kind of flat. I got that she is going to get tested for attention stuff, I did not need all the sticker and poster stuff. I had to re-read a few sentences because they are built weird, like "Chloe is about an inch out of her seat, watching the trunk shut and the car pull away with the coffee cup still balanced on the roof, right up until it tips". That is one long sentence for one small thing.

Words I would not know: "circulation counter" (I guessed), "conferences" (parent-teacher, I figured it out), "evaluated for attention" (I get it, but a younger kid might not). The Icarus joke only works if you know the myth, but the text explains it ("flew too close to the sun") so I am fine.

Weirdly, the date line "September 2011 – February 2012" threw me a little. Why does it matter that it was 2011? I assume it will come up.

Do I care about Chloe? Yes, more than I expected. Her parents seem okay but not really hearing her, and the dad barely says anything. I do not like that she decides the problem is her and she just needs to "get better at sitting there."

Favorite moment: the library "Four." Weakest: the doctor scene.

Prediction: she gets diagnosed with ADHD, or they find out she is gifted, or both. I want someone to finally tell her she is not weird.

## Chapter 02: March 7th

This one is mostly the testing day, and honestly it dragged in the middle. But the ending hit hard.

What worked: the waiting room. The empty fish tank, the NO CELL PHONES sign with a picture for "people who can't read," and Chloe reading the Spanish side of the poster by accident. Funny and it made her feel real. I also liked the answers she gives ("They're the covers for the ends of you" for boot and glove). Her "an hour isn't really a thing out there" answer made me think, okay, this kid is actually smart, not just "reads a lot."

Where I skimmed: the testing itself. Block after block, number strings, symbol sheets. It goes on for pages and each part sounds like the last. I did not need all of it to get that she is fast at some things and slow at the pencil one. Around the "Why do people wash their hands" questions I started skimming for the next dialogue.

Confusing spot: Dr. Prentice talking to her mom through the door. "The talking is not six," "a composite that averages two very different pictures into a single score." I had to read that twice and I'm still not totally sure. I think he means she is way above her age in thinking but average at writing fast, so the average looks fine and the school will ignore it. A 14-year-old would get the gist but it is a lot of adult talk in a row. "Percentile" is explained later by her mom, which helped.

The part that got me: she asks her mom "What's a percentile?" and her mom says "He didn't give me a number." Then ice cream on a Wednesday. I could tell her mom was trying to be nice and also hiding something. And then "nothing changes." The doctor basically says she's gifted and fine and the school will not do anything for two years. That is infuriating. Her learning to "finish first, sit still, keep your answers short" is sad. By June she eats lunch alone.

I care about her more now. I also kind of want to yell at her parents, but they are not bad, just not hearing her.

Favorite: the fish tank and the Spanish poster. Weakest: the long testing middle.

Prediction: third grade gifted program, something good happens there. Or it still doesn't help. I hope it does.

## Chapter 03: The Letter

Now it gets interesting. A mystery letter addressed to a six-year-old, free school, nobody has heard of it. I perked up right away. This is the first chapter where I wanted to turn the page, not just feel bad for her.

What worked: the "Miss" thing. "It says Miss," Chloe says. "I think that's why." That is the best line so far. Nobody has ever treated her like a person and this letter does, and a kid would totally fall for that. It also explains why I should worry for her: the whole thing smells like a trap. The school's website has teachers who were in the Olympics and a SEAL, and not one other mention of the school anywhere on the internet. Creepy in a good way. The dad going quiet and re-reading the teacher page is the moment I got worried too.

I also liked the packing list in the train station voice and the shoe joke. It makes the family feel warm, which makes the "is this safe" feeling worse.

Where it dragged: the phone calls. Her mom calls the district, the neighbors, the school, and each call is a long chunk of her mom asking questions. I got the point after the first one (it is weird, no one has heard of it, the school answers everything too fast). The nine days of arguing is also told in a muddled way. "On the sixth day" shows up after the eighth day, and I had to stop and work out the order. That made me feel like I was missing something.

Also: the parents do the whole thing offscreen at the end. They just decide. I wanted to know what changed their minds, besides the overheard "she's been flat since Christmas." That bit was good, actually, it is the real reason.

Words: "pull-out group" (no idea, I think it means the gifted-ish kids who leave class), "district office," "charter" (I know it vaguely), "scouts" and "networks" in the school's answer. The mom says "networks" like it is sinister and I did not fully get why. Chloe's joke about "the network with cartoons" saves it, funny.

Do I trust Halstead? No. I think it is shady. But I also want Chloe to go, which is probably the point.

Favorite: "It says Miss." Weakest: the phone call stack and the confusing day numbers.

Prediction: it is a real school for gifted kids but with a catch, like someone is watching them or they never get to leave. Chloe will make a friend.

## Chapter 04: Pluto

This is where the book got fun. After three chapters of an adult world, there are finally other kids and they talk like actual kids.

What worked: "room-shaped object." I laughed out loud. Sam is my favorite already, with the "and a half" thing and the pool with a lid. Ruth correcting everybody and then going "I'm agreeing with you" / "You're agreeing at me" is great. I know a Ruth. Priya info-dumping about ponies, hands, and drafts was kind of funny too, and I liked that Chloe just lets her run. It shows Chloe is lonely and nice at the same time.

The mom at the hotel is the emotional center for me. Chloe calls and says "I want to come there" and her mom is dressed in the middle of the first ring. And the "same day" underlined twice on the map. That made me tear up a little, honestly, because the parents are scared too and trying to hide it.

The moment where Chloe's body does the weird panic thing outside the Pluto door (knees, fingers, back at the wood chips) was a bit confusing. I got that she is reliving the bad classroom day, but it was a lot of body parts and I had to re-read to see it was a flashback-feeling and not a real thing happening. Then the boy laughs and it ends. I liked the ending of it, not the middle.

Where I skimmed: the orientation speech (laundry bags twice), and Priya's horse thing, a little. Also, the volcano scene with Kavi felt like it was showing off facts. "Viscosity" is fine, I liked that Sam looks dumb and then just keeps drawing his volcano. That is relatable, I've been Sam.

Confusing: there are two sets of names early. Priya, Sam, Ruth, Owen, Kavi, "a fourth in Pluto," in about two pages. I had trouble tracking who was who. Owen is mentioned in passing three times doing a worksheet and I do not know why yet.

The end: Ruth interrupts the teacher, nothing bad happens, and Chloe STILL sits on her hands. That frustrated me in a good way. She has not figured out this place is different yet. That is the point, I think.

Favorite: "room-shaped object" and the hotel call. Weakest: the orientation and the body-panic bit.

Prediction: Chloe slowly stops hiding. Sam and Chloe become friends. Something creepy still hangs over the school, because the teachers are too good to be true.

## Chapter 05: Behind

This is a long one with a lot going on, and it mostly worked for me. The best scene is the fraction one at night.

What worked: Chloe figuring out why you flip the fraction when dividing. She draws it, gets six, and says "Oh" in the dark. I actually remember learning that and being mad that "just flip it" never got explained, so that hit. It is also the first time Chloe is the one behind (the chapter title makes sense now) and she hates it. She asks "Why do you flip it" first and then Ruth and the teacher go off arguing about zero over her head. I felt her ears go hot.

The bridge was good too. Ruth is right, the square folds, and Sam does not listen because Ruth is annoying. And Chloe also knew it and said "Okay" and held the corner. That is a real thing, you know the answer but you do not want to fight. The "I told you" fights between Ruth and Sam are a bit repetitive though, it happens in chapter 4 and here.

The Owen part is the moment that sat with me. His bed stripped to the plastic. Chloe cannot understand how anyone could not have fun, and Sam says "I know" flat. And Ruth saying she saw him crying in the stairwell and did not go in. Ruth is blunt to the point of kind of awful, but she is not lying. More doors open each week. That made the school feel a little ominous again, although the school did what it said (let him go home), so maybe it is not sinister, it is just hard.

The moon argument at the end was funny but I zoned out partway. "Tidally locked" is a good word to learn. The ending beat, that Chloe talked normally for a whole dinner and "the sentence comes back clean," was a nice quiet payoff after chapter 1.

Where it dragged: the bridge test has a lot of procedure (dowels, glue gun, buckets), the swimming bit is short but felt tacked on, and the writing timer scene near the start was confusing. I had to re-read "what the man in the story believes at the beginning and stops believing by the end." That reads like homework.

Do I care about Chloe? More and more. I'm worried about Owen too, and I want to know what happened to him.

Favorite: the fraction "Oh." Weakest: the writing-timer opener and the repeated Ruth-Sam fights.

Prediction: Chloe and Ruth end up being real friends. More kids leave. Someone is going to leave who matters.

## Chapter 06: The List

Mixed. The ending is great and the middle repeated stuff I just read.

What worked: the very end, in the car. Chloe pretends to be asleep with her breathing "slow and even, on purpose" (she did the same in chapter 1 after Christmas, nice echo) and listens to her dad tell her mom everything. "Meg, they taught her CPR." "I don't know what that is, Meg, and I was standing right there." I loved that. It is the first time her dad sounds really impressed AND a little scared. And the part where Chloe gives Priya her hair tie. Small, kind thing. I smiled.

I also liked the parents at the waffle breakfast, who all admit they thought it was a scam but nobody can say what Halstead actually is. "Here it buys you a paper plate and a seat at this table" is a sharp line. That kept the mystery alive. The "list" is the thing I want answered. Who makes it?

What dragged: the dinner scene where Chloe tells her dad everything. It is a very long list (Gary the CPR dummy, bridge, pool, fractions, Spanish, signing, puzzle, wood shop, eggs, computers, robot arm, chess, karate, nerf guns) and the bridge and fractions are things I literally just read in chapter 5 and she explains them again in nearly the same words. I skimmed most of it. I think the point is she talks and talks and dad cannot keep up, and the repetitions ("Then the wood shop again, and the goggles again") are on purpose, but as a reader it made me bored. I would put the book down at the computer room paragraph.

The hallway scene about getting in for the whole year was confusing too. I understood: there is a form, then a day in the fall where people look at them, then maybe a yes. But the arguing about who heard what through which door was hard to follow. I lost track of Ruth, Sam and Kavi in the middle of a bunch of "said / said" lines.

Words: "phishing scam" (I know it), "wire transfer" (kind of).

Do I care? Yes. What I felt: the signing and the Spanish and the friends were the first time Chloe is just happy. And I know that means something bad is coming, or at least a hard part: going home to boring school again.

Favorite: the car call at the end. Weakest: the long dinner list.

Prediction: she goes home, regular school is worse now, and she has to wait for the fall day. The kids will write letters. Ruth will not write.

## Chapter 07: The Same Room

This was the saddest chapter so far, and it worked really well for me. I was properly bummed out at the end.

The best part by far: Chloe asking her mom to call Sam, and then realizing she does not know anyone's last name. "First names were the only names anybody used there." She lists everything she knows about Sam (the "and a half," window open, swims two lengths) but no way to find him. That hit hard because it is a smart idea that I did not see coming: she has all the details and none of the useful ones. Then her wrist scrubbing her eye. Her mom's hand on her head. Great scene. Also the phone she keeps checking on the hook every night, ugh.

The time capsule scene was good, too. She writes something real, gets called weird again by Kayleigh and Bryce, covers it with her hand, scratches it out and writes "I hope you like fifth grade." That is the moment I wanted to reach into the book and hug her. The pencil going almost through the paper says it all.

The library card going into the drawer with the batteries is a callback to the train station book from chapter 1 (the key in the bowl with the batteries). I think that is on purpose and I liked noticing it. Without being told, I knew what it meant: she stopped reading. For a kid who reads cereal boxes, that is the scariest sign.

The ending with her crying at her desk, and she doesn't know why, felt right. It is also frustrating because the teacher is nice and still can't help. The cut-off line "I don't..." is a good place to end.

What dragged: the "she asks and asks" paragraph about the question is one super long sentence with a bunch of semicolons. I had to re-read it. And the part where she tells the librarian everything about camp AGAIN (Gary, the bridge, the corner). I was already tired of the Gary/bridge/fractions story by now. Third time. Please stop.

Also confusing: the opening paragraph about Sam and Kavi leaving early is hard to follow, because it is one tangled sentence. It reads like it takes place at camp but the chapter starts in the loop out front. It took me a minute.

The parents: mom is trying hard, calling schools. Third grade is "the floor." I think I get that the school will not move her up. Dad is almost silent here.

Do I care about Chloe? A lot. This is the first chapter where I felt properly worried.

Favorite: the last-name scene. Weakest: the repeated camp recap to the librarian and the one-sentence asking paragraph.

Prediction: they let her go. But I'm afraid the price of going will be missing her family or something being off about the year school.

## Chapter 08: The Asking

Heavy chapter. The "Nobody was mean" line is the best thing in the book so far, and it earned the whole chapter.

What worked: Chloe finally answers her mom's question about what was different at Halstead, after her mom has only gotten the bridge and the fun stuff. "Nobody was mean." Then the bullying question, and Chloe says "Bryce just thinks I talk weird. That's different from bullying." / "It's only true." I put the book down for a second after that. It is such a believable thing for a kid to say, and her mom's "you'll make friends, it just takes time" is exactly the useless adult thing, and I could tell even Chloe knew it was useless.

Also the fight over the jacket where she says "I hate you so much" and then stands behind the door, listening for the stair. And dinner after, where mom passes the bread and dad passes the salt before she asks, and nobody says anything. That felt very real.

The enrichment class was a good scene. She keeps waiting for the part where it gets hard ("When does it get harder?") and it never does. Mrs. Prahl is nice and it does nothing. That tells me "being in the fast group" is not what she wanted. She wanted to struggle with friends around. I did not expect that answer, and it made sense.

Where I got lost: the bit with Dr. Ammons. Mom is the one answering everything, which I think is the point, but I read the whole thing and Chloe barely says a word. The knights and knaves puzzle (Alan and Bob) is described and not solved, fine. But "the paper on the floor with the eighths ruled straight through it" and "the apples" (mom says "the bridge and the apples") are things I do not remember, and I wondered if I missed an apples scene.

Weird name thing: in chapter 7 Chloe tells the librarian "Mrs. Prahl kept ours on the windowsill" about the camp bridge. Now Mrs. Prahl is her enrichment teacher at the regular school. Same person? Or the same name by accident? I was confused and kind of hoped it was a clue. If it is just a repeated name it is distracting.

A few sentences also tripped me up. "So is the third, except that Mrs. Prahl puts them in pairs" has no clear start, I think a line is missing. And the timeline is blurry: the bike and the parents in the doorway felt like a pause button on the drama, but I liked that her mom has the line "I lie awake and go through the reasons to say no, and every morning I still don't have one good enough." That made me trust the parents more.

The end: she stops asking. The magnet holds a grocery list now. That is a quiet, awful ending. I felt defeated for her. Parents think she is "calmer." I disagree.

Favorite: "Nobody was mean." Weakest: the Dr. Ammons scene where Chloe is just sitting there, and the confusing missing-sentence bits.

Prediction: Something will pull her back into Halstead after she has given up, maybe the fall day Ruth talked about. I was wrong that they'd say yes quickly.

## Chapter 09: February

Okay, this is the chapter I would tell a friend to read. The first scene wrecked me.

The floor scene. Chloe in the gap between the bed and the wall, offering her mom everything: the dishes, "Dad's jobs," the bike, Christmas, "Take my birthday." She is seven. And "Please just let me go to that school." I was close to crying. It works because she isn't giving a speech, she can't even get a whole sentence out. And then her parents downstairs: "she offered up her own birthday like it was money she'd saved." I got chills. The dad standing in the doorway with the dishcloth, no water running, is a great detail too.

Then the yes. The screaming, the "Is that a true thing you're saying, or is it a thinking about it thing?" That made me laugh and cry at once. Then the bike comes back into the living room and she says it was a deal. And her dad says "You cannot buy this off us." That might be my favorite parent moment in the book. I like that her dad is the one who did the form in secret.

What I liked less: the house argument in April. Her parents fight about Halstead offering a free furnished house if she is enrolled, and her mom says no. I understood it: if you take the house, they own you. But it took a long back-and-forth to get there, and it was tough to follow whether the dad was for it or against. It felt like a lot of "Meg" and "Dave" lines.

Something is off, though. I have a bad feeling about the Halstead visit. Mrs. Okonkwo walks backward and says "I can't tell you who's enrolled here" and then says "I know you do." Ruth's rule about the fall evaluation is just "silly," so the girls were wrong or someone lied to Ruth. And then a man with a gray-ish beard sits down at her height and asks her about her day. A beard, like Dr. Prentice in chapter 2? Maybe it is just me, but that felt like a test with no test. "No test arrives, but she keeps waiting." I think that was the test. Creepy, and I like it.

The ending, with her mom sitting in the car with the engine off and not getting out, is such a quiet gut-punch. She is scared. I'm scared for her too. Chloe goes for toast.

Also: confusing time stuff. February to April covers a lot, and the chapter hops between months fast. I had to check the dates twice.

Favorite: the floor scene, then the bike. Weakest: the house argument.

Prediction: the free house is a trap and will come back. Mom is right to be afraid. I hope I'm wrong about Sam still being there.

## Chapter 10: April

Happy chapter, and the first time I felt the book relax. Also the first one where I noticed a real clue, so I'm hooked on the mystery again.

What worked: Sam running down the hall ("I told everybody you would"), Ruth hugging her, and then Ruth never having her address and apologizing for not writing. Fen and her rocks sorted by size is my favorite small thing in the book so far. "If I find another tomorrow the whole row moves again." I do not know why that is so good but it is. And I love that she is "taking them with me and starting the sill again wherever I go next." That sounds like a metaphor, but it never says so.

The no-card library is a big payoff from chapter 1, where she fought about four books at a time. Now she takes a dozen and the desk is empty at night. I smiled at that. Then the math problem about the wheel, and she and Ruth are working it out together at the board. That is the "it gets hard" moment she wanted from Mrs. Prahl. Good.

But here's the clue that got me: Kavi says Owen wanted to stay. Crying by the kitchens, then his mom's car outside Saturday. Back in chapter 5, Sam said Owen left because "he wasn't having fun" and "he wanted to be home." Those do not match. Somebody is wrong or lying. Also Ruth calls him "the boy who wouldn't do the water thing." What water thing? I also noticed there is a notice board with something called "Defensive Watch" that nobody explains. And the writing teacher says "People do go home sometimes... which makes this a school rather than a prison, and certainly rather than a competition you can be knocked out of." That is a very strange thing to say if nobody asked about competitions. A smart reader goes: why bring that up?

What dragged: a LOT of listing. Subjects, the swim teacher who went to the Olympics, the cooking teacher saying "triangle" jokes, how many subjects you can carry (seven, max ten). I skimmed the whole schedule part. Also, so many names show up (Fen, Priya, Marcus, Ruth, Sam, Kavi, Mrs. Okonkwo) that I'm sorry, I lost a few.

Also a thing that bugged me: Chloe's mom asks "are you happy there, actually happy" and Chloe says yes and talks in one breath. That should have been a bigger moment, and instead the call gets cut short because someone is waiting. Maybe that's the point. It's realistic.

Do I care more? Yes, I'm glad she is happy. But I am not trusting this place.

Favorite: Fen's rocks. Weakest: the subjects-and-schedule dump.

Prediction: the "water thing" is important. And the "Defensive Watch." I think kids are being watched or tested secretly. I was half right earlier about the "no test" visit being the real test.

## Chapter 11: Eight

This one is a big wide stretch of a year, and I liked the first half a lot more than the second.

What worked: the summer at home. The hot dog is a sandwich argument is funny, the "hinge is a fact about bread" line made me laugh, and watching it go on for eight days in the car and at breakfast is relatable. I also loved her teaching her mom Spanish at the counter and both of them wheezing. And the parents' talk afterward, where her mom says "She used to hum in the car, and only in the car." That is the quiet kind of detail that makes a family feel real.

The part I did not expect: the grandmother says Chloe "was so thin at Christmas" and Chloe eats half her plate and says "I've had enough." So the sad part of her year is still there under the happy stuff. Also her mom says "she has never fought me, not once... she always stops the moment she knows." That is a really sad line to me, because it means she already learned to give up on arguing at home. So the "good kid" thing is not good.

Then the school. Politics class is so weird: you learn the leaders of every country, their spouses, kids, "who they owe." A kid asks "Why do we need their kids" and the teacher says nothing is secret, it is just nobody writes it down. That sounded creepy to me. Why are eight-year-olds learning who owes money to whom? My guess is the school wants these kids to run things someday. Also "Nerf is eight," the dart game, is cool and I liked how Coach Bell explains it. But "the day it turns over goes past like any other Monday" lost me. What turns over?

The thing I did NOT like: the ninetieth of ninety. She is dead last in drawing and dance, and she reads the number "twice." I wanted more reaction. After the whole book about being fastest, here she is last and it barely rattles her. Then she goes to Mrs. Okonkwo to quit dance and gets "No." I actually liked that, the school does not cave, but the ending of that scene is just a list of columns. And then her hand drawing, the fortieth hand, is a good small win with Ruth: "Last term you were drawing mittens."

Skimmed: the elective list, the choir bit, the apples at the drainpipe. Fun but I lost the thread of what the chapter was about. It feels like a school catalogue.

Confusing: "Nerf is eight" as a title and the opening of the Nerf section. Title is the grade/age? I guess it is the age: "Eight." OK. But then "Nerf is eight" in the text reads like a code. Also lots of "and then and then" sentences.

Favorite: the hot dog argument. Weakest: the long school-catalogue middle.

Prediction: the ranking list is going to matter. Ninetieth of ninety is a flag.

## Chapter 12: Nine

I liked this chapter more than the last, mostly because of two scenes. The rest was a pile of classes.

The best moment: Bex Alcantar and the bridge. Bex finishes your sentences, and when the teacher asks how the bridge held, she says "we" in the first sentence and then "I" for everything after it, so she gets all the credit for Chloe's two weeks of work. And Chloe doesn't say anything. She goes home and rehearses the right answer into her mattress. Then next time she sees Bex she says "thanks" and "the word comes out thin." That is so true to life. Then Ruth says she should have said it while the teacher was writing. And the last line: Chloe "stops saying we out loud about anything of hers, to anybody, for the rest of the year." Ouch. That is the same thing as in chapter 1, where she took the long answer down to one word. She still does this. The author did not say "she is scared to speak up," I saw it myself.

Sam and the overhang was great too. He spends the whole year on the climbing wall, falls off, then asks a younger girl how, and it was his left foot. He says "that's still Monday." I was cheering when he got over it, and Chloe tearing the corner off her book without noticing is a nice detail. Sam is still my favorite.

The scene with her dad's vendor contract was a nice surprise. Chloe reads a boring business paper and says "that's not actually a promise." Her dad says "I would have signed it on Tuesday." I like that she is useful to him now, and that it is her dad and not the school that notices. But I did not totally follow what the contract trick was. I got the idea: nothing in it can be checked.

What dragged: the class tour again. Mandarin tones, chemistry (the gray vs. white), first aid with the radio, textiles, the fish tank, riding. I could feel the author wanting me to know how much happens in a year, and it blurs. I skimmed Mandarin and Textiles. The chemistry gray/white test was okay because there was a clear problem and answer. Also too many new names: Bex, Iyad, Anne, Amara, Vasquez, Mrs. Sun. I forgot who Iyad was by the time he came back. His "your accent" announcement at dinner was mean, in a quiet way, but that scene is short.

Confusing: "Block two is hers, and she takes riding" came out of nowhere. And in the first scene, something on Sam's sheet is "different" and nothing more is said. I'm guessing it matters.

Chloe quitting riding because it is "easy" and Priya liking it even when nothing is happening. "I kept waiting for it to turn into that for me too." That is a really honest line. It made me think about things I stopped doing.

Favorite: Bex and the bridge. Weakest: the Mandarin and Textiles stretches.

Prediction: Sam's different sheet is a big clue. I still think they are being sorted and ranked.

## Chapter 13: Ten Pages

This is the chapter where the book finally showed its teeth. The Defensive Watch sheet made me sit up. Also a few parts I had to fight through.

The big thing: the scoring sheet. "They do not enter a building. They do not learn anything about you. You take their comms." Adults come over the fence with a bag, and the kids are scored on how fast they take them down. And everybody starts at ten. Combine that with 10v1 sparring, paintball, self defense every day and "adults in ordinary coats who walk in the front door." That reads like they are training child soldiers or bodyguards for something, and I do not know for what. Chills. I am genuinely worried for these kids now. The teachers keep calling it a "school rather than a prison," and I do not buy it. Also, wait, the school said its goal in the letter was gifted summer program. When do the parents find out about all this?

The Bex thing happened again. Chloe figures out the paintball "barrel is the hose" trick with Ruth, Bex hears it and tells Bell like it is hers, and Bell says "the sheet is already written." I got angry. Twice now. It is so unfair that I started to dislike Bex, even though she doesn't seem mean on purpose. Then Iyad turns Chloe's own good line ("Nobody scores the drill") into a joke at her expense, and the whole table laughs. I wanted to throw the book. Poor Chloe carries her tray out the far door. That is a real middle school feeling and I felt it.

What worked quietly: the tariffs conversation with her dad at Thanksgiving. He asks about her essay and actually argues back, "what happens if it's five years and the war's two?" And her mom bringing in pie. Warm and also kind of funny. I also liked the grandmother line about the chair: "Your mother did exactly that."

Hearn is interesting. He tells Chloe she's "only comfortable," not right, and the first real feedback she gets that she doesn't just win. That's a good moment, and the later essay that she writes "towards the place where she is going to be wrong" sounds like she is growing. I did not totally follow the reading list of Federalist numbers. Too many numbers.

Where I skimmed: the Hamilton and Federalist details, the Russian and Arabic bit, the music paragraph, and the 10v1 timing numbers. ## Chapter 25: Forty Targets

Best surprise in the book for me: this chapter is about Sam, not Chloe. I did not expect to leave Chloe for a whole chapter and I did not mind at all. It is funny, then it gets tense, and the last page made me gasp.

Do I still care about Chloe? I notice she is not here, and I miss her, but I care about Sam more now. He is more than the joke friend.

Favorite: "You're shooting the bullet." Weakest: the paperwork paragraph.

## Chapter 27: Nadia

Another side-character chapter, and I liked this one a lot better than Sam's. Nadia is the only person in the book whose life looks like something I could end up in, which makes it hit harder than the soldiers and the spies.

Ruth was searching for the truth in public and everybody made a joke inside a minute. "What she posts reads, to everyone reading it, like Ruth being Ruth." Then she stops posting in April because "she has understood what the numbers add up to and would rather somebody else say it first." That is the answer to the Ruth mystery, and I think it's a good one. It is not a kidnapping or a death, it's something worse in a way: she has realized that her friends are not like everyone else, and she can't stand it.

The chat is the key scene. "i dont think we're the same kind of thing." "put any kid off my street in that building at seven and you get this. thats the part nobody will say out loud." It reframes the whole book for me. They were not picked because they were gifted. They were picked because someone drew a line through a list of six-year-olds, and the building did the rest. And she asks "i want to know what the line was." She's right to ask. It makes me think of Chloe at six, "You talk weird." If the school made them, then she was never weird. She was six. And then the plot connects: the pill, the file, the "list is usually pretty good," and the scam of the first letter. I think I have been reading this book wrong. I thought it was about a gifted girl, and it's about a made one.

Honest critique: I liked the chat part more than the long narrative section before it. Much of the narrative is explaining Ruth's feelings in paragraph form. The book repeats "she keeps it in the folder" a few times. And I'm still not sure what Ruth's "diagnostic in August" tested. It would have helped to see it.

Favorite: "put any kid off my street in that building at seven." Weakest: the walking through offices.

Prediction: someone will try to find out who picked them, which means the list, which means the school's donor. And Chloe is the one with the clearance to look.

## Chapter 32: The Money

Half of this chapter was a computer lecture I could not follow, and the other half was a detective story that I loved. I would cut a lot of the first half and keep every line of the second.

What lost me: the build. Eli builds a program "that has to be boring" and Kavi builds "the thing that watches it." Polling interval, reconciliation job, worm, the "watcher," deployment target. Page after page of it. I got the idea: they made a program that slowly looks through bank records without anyone noticing, like a bored accountant. The idea is clever. But the writing keeps telling me about desks and coffee cups and monitors instead of what the program does. I skimmed to the chat. Also, they are breaking into financial systems. The book never stops to say it's illegal. Kavi, Eli, Nadia, all of them just do it. I think the story knows it's wrong, and it expects me to feel the same way I did about the mugging chapter. I still didn't like how easy it was. Nadia saying "if this comes back to my company i will end all of you" was funny, though.

What worked: Ruth proving it was one person. She sits on the floor of her apartment with the cards in a ring around her "the arrangement she used at six," and walks a strip of paper along the floor on her hands and knees. Then the three clues in the chat:
1. There are gaps, three to five days with nothing happening. "Institutions don't have gaps like that. Committees don't get flu."
2. The decisions all land in the same seven-hour band, which moves forty minutes over twenty years. "That's aging." / "That's a person getting older."
3. There are two tools that do the same job, one slightly worse, and "he's" used the worse one every time since 2003. "No reason. It's just what he does."
That's a great piece of detective work, and I could follow it. It's the first time in a while the book let me do the thinking with them. And then "whats your number" / "99.99" / "and the other 0.01" / "is me being humble" is the funniest line in the chapter and also so Ruth.

It also matters that this is Ruth's chapter-after-chapter again, and she's out of her slump. After her silence in the last one, it's great to see her working at full speed on something that finally matters to her. She's the same kid from chapter 4 who would not shut up about triangles, and this time she's right in a way that actually gets taken seriously.

The other big thing: Theo's message. "nobody repeats any of this... they've had a file on the school since 2013." He tells them about the man planted on staff, and the four operators at four in the morning. Sam: "WHAT." Chloe: "theo that was a drill." Theo: "it wasnt a drill. i read the file." I knew this from chapter 29 and I still got a thrill watching them find out. Chloe asks "theo which four," because she was one of the four. Chloe's reaction is the most interesting: she doesn't say "no way," she asks which one. She knew all along it was something. "She reads that twice, sets the phone face down next to the badge." I felt that.

Also, Kavi says "a state doesn't leave four operators walking. That's not their risk tolerance," meaning a real government would have done something worse. And Nadia says "they caught four operators once" and then "widen the polling interval again" in the middle of it. That is how people really act when their heart is pounding: they go back to the task.

The ending line is "so lets go and read it" and that is Eli, and I do not know what "it" is: the file? Theo's file? I guess they plan to read the government's file about the school, which is a bold, risky thing. Chloe has a clearance and a badge. This is going to get dangerous.

Where I was confused: the timeline ("1998 or 2001, depending which thread") and whether "one man" means the school's secret owner, or the funder. I think it's the funder. The chapter calls the financial "one" without saying what they're looking for, and I had to piece it together from the chat. A short sentence would have fixed it.

Favorite: Ruth's three clues. Weakest: the build.

Prediction: they'll find who the man is and it will tie to the pill and the list. Also I think Chloe will be asked to choose between her new job and her friends.

## Chapter 33: The Other One

A planning chapter. I know the book is setting up a big heist, but this part is almost all talking about talking, and I'd call it the quietest chapter since the Whitaker one. Still, two things about it made me smile.

The first is a payoff from chapter 19. When Nadia says "name whos actually touching the code" and then "write that down, chloe," Chloe answers "already did. its clause four." Chloe, who got blamed for not writing down her reasoning on the bar exam and who failed Marek for the same thing, is now the person who writes the rules for a crime. She writes the page on what "noticed" means, and she says it twice in different words because she knows which of them will be "squinting at an ordinary blip at two in the morning." She learned the lesson and then she became the best at it. I smiled at that and I don't think the book points to it. That's a good bit of storytelling.

The second thing I liked is the stop clause. One line: any one of them can end it, no vote, no reason attached. Ruth wants a sentence of reason "for the record." Nadia says "a sentence is a case. If you're building a case you're not stopping, you're asking. Whoever's scared enough to pull it is scared enough." Ruth says "fine, strike it." That is a smart rule and I liked seeing why. It reminded me of the safe word thing in the trust falls joke. Also Nadia's sleeves going up "when something is going to cost her."

And Ruth's legal numbers: "section 1030... five years first offense, ten if there's a financial angle." Eli: "everyone speeds." Ruth: "thats not the same." I like their different instincts, and "give me the actual number of cameras youve counted, not the number youre confident about." That's a very Ruth thing to say, because she is right and Eli is dodging. It's a tense bit, and I felt it.

But I want to be honest that I was bored. It is a lot of pages about a document. Eli writes twelve pages, Theo reads it and closes it, rereads it, closes it. Everyone says "in" or "fine" one at a time, which is a list of signatures, five or six in a row. Theo's "still the stupidest thing any of us has ever done" is funny once, and then twice. And the second paragraph about the target is vague: "the unit Theo told them exists." I am not sure what "the second worm" does and I'm not clear who the target is except "the people who have the file." It's fine, but I would want one clean sentence about the plan.

Also there's something wrong with the ethics, and I don't think the book is fully facing it. Chloe has a clearance, which she just got, and she is going to use it to read a sealed government file. The last paragraph says "What that costs her she works through once... and the thought ends in about as long as it takes a light to change." That's the one line where she doubts, and then she's done. As a reader I wanted her to hesitate more. This is the person who failed Marek "because she was the one who had to write it down." She knows rules. Why doesn't she wrestle with this? I felt the book skipped over the best moment.

Another quick note: the book keeps jumping dates, and it was hard to tell this chapter happens after the last one. "May 2026 – June 2026." Chloe turns twenty-one in August: "two months after she adds the last page," but this section is also called "weeks into the job" earlier. I can't track the calendar. It doesn't hurt, but I keep checking.

Favorite: "already did. its clause four." Weakest: the long signature list and the vague target.

Prediction: the break-in will go wrong in a way the "noticed" clause didn't plan for ("a gap closing again before anyone can point to it"). That last line of the page-three paragraph is a hint. I also think Theo is going to be the one who gets hurt.

## Chapter 34: The Files

This is the chapter where I felt most like the book had finally paid off. It is short, tight, and every few lines gave me a new chill. I read it in one go and wanted to start over.

What worked first: the idea of seven people opening their own files in seven places at once, alone, and the chat starting up after. The opening paragraph is long, but each person has a different way of reading: Ruth on the lab floor "because the chair is across the room," Sam on a phone in thirty-second bursts while a sergeant talks, Theo standing the whole way through. It's neat, and it shows each person's personality. I didn't skim it.

The files themselves are creepy. There is one on every single one of the ninety-one of them. Case number opened 2013. Address, family, where their parents work, where everybody banks. Eli's says he found five vulnerabilities in his employer's systems in one afternoon and flags it "as worth continued attention rather than as anything resembling praise." That is so funny and so cold. Nadia's file spells her name differently on every page. Kavi's is the thinnest, "a paragraph a year," with the word "continuity" written in the margin. I don't know what "continuity" means here and that bothered me in a good way. Theo's has a note with a name in it that he already knows, and he refuses to tell Sam. I'm dying to know who that is. Chloe's file notes she accepted a job requiring a background check, "dated to a Tuesday she remembers clearly, because it's the week she signed the lease." It makes me feel like someone has been sitting on her shoulder for the whole book.

The line "the graduates are believed to be in contact with one another... collection against the channel comes back empty every time it's tried, but the recommendation is still to keep attempting it." That's a great detail. The kids' homemade encrypted chat from chapter 24 has beaten the government for years. And the line about how the sentence keeps getting retyped "by a clerk who has stopped expecting the retyping to change anything." I read that three times.

The Waffle House incident is the big thing. I remember chapter 20, with the seven men and the 22 seconds. The file says: all the footage from the Waffle House, the units next to it, a traffic camera, and the town camera: "unrecoverable wherever the students appear, but intact everywhere else. No known method." And the government team assigned to watch the school had its own recordings that night, "gone too." Ruth's box! The thing she built "in about a weekend so we wouldn't get detention" beat a federal unit they didn't know existed. Ruth: "i was thirteen and it was a weekend and i wasnt trying." That's the emotional core. And Kavi: "ruth, read that back." He wants her to hear herself. I did, too.

The ending is good: Ruth asks "how good are we" and Eli says "i dont know. i have never once been at the top of a room in my life." (A great, human thing to say.) And Chloe: "were about to find out." / "thats not an answer" / "it wasnt supposed to be one." That's the best last line of any chapter.

Honest complaints: it makes me ask why Chloe and her friends never noticed a federal team watching them. They did lots of cameras stuff for years, and I wanted that to matter earlier. Also "the unit assigned to observation of the school had their own recordings." I didn't follow how Ruth's box got those too, but I get that it was in range. And the part about a "translation problem": Chloe wonders if "blank" means the men can't recall faces or that they didn't match. It's a smart thought, but it sits in a long paragraph and I almost skipped it.

I also noticed: Sam says "is that the one where you lost a shoe," which is how they'd talk. I'm glad the book let one joke through.

Do I still care about Chloe? More than ever. She's carrying a clearance she shouldn't be using, and she says "were about to find out" in a chat. I'm worried about her and proud of her.

Favorite: Ruth "i was thirteen and it was a weekend." Weakest: the second paragraph's long list of whose file says what.

Prediction: the thing they find out is the real reason for Halstead. And the "name Theo already knows" will be a person from Halstead who has been working for the other side.

## Chapter 35: Nine Minutes

This is a slow-burn chapter that I think works as a setup, but nothing really happens in it. I was tense and a little bored, which is a weird combination.

What worked: the idea. Their worm, the program they built to quietly watch bank records, goes silent for exactly nine minutes. All three logs say it was running the whole time. "No error, no restart, no gap." Somebody stopped it, started it again, and cleaned up so well "the cleanup isnt there either." Then it happens a second time, to the second: nine minutes. Kavi's line, "different would say clumsy, but the identical number twice says measured," is the best sentence in the chapter. I thought about that for a while. Nine, nine. It's not a glitch. It's a signature.

Then the argument between Chloe and Eli is the real scene. Chloe: "hes telling us hes there. he could have killed it and he gave it back. thats a message." Eli: "no. chloe im sorry but no... if you find a tool like this you do one of three things. you kill it, you follow it, or you feed it garbage. you dont put it back." Chloe's answer is interesting and Eli's is practical. And then Eli says "hes very slightly wrong about how good we are." I love that line. The unknown person might be wrong about them, and that's their one advantage. I also like Ruth saying "so he doesnt know we know" / "thats the only thing we have" / "dont lose it then."

I like that Theo says the last line: "and if hes made one mistake, how many has he made that we didnt catch." That's a really good question and I wish it were the last line of the chapter.

What dragged: a lot. The opening is a full page of Eli checking logs. I had to reread it to understand there were three logs and they all agree. The chapter does the same thing over and over: "He checks the noticed clause again... it reads exactly as it did." "Ruth goes through them slowly... It holds." I think I read the "noticed" clause four times and it never changed. I understand why: the rules are the point. But it makes the chapter feel like a loop.

Also, I noticed the book keeps using the same body habits for characters, and now it feels like filler. Eli's two-finger tap on the desk, Kavi turning a cable or "whatever's on the table" over in his hand, Nadia's sleeves going up, Theo closing his laptop carefully. They were cute the first few times, and now I could write them myself. The tap shows up again in this one twice. I have started to skip the sentence with the tap.

And I wanted more from the "he." Who is "he"? The chapter just says "he" the whole time and assumes I remember it's the one man from the money chapter. I did, but only barely. Also "the financial worm" versus "the second worm" confused me again, because I forgot which one went dark. I think it's the first one that was still running. A line saying "the first worm, the one watching the bank" would have helped.

The end: Chloe says she's "out for a few hours" and asks Ruth to watch the thread. Then "her eyes close on the version of tonight that ends here, not on the one that keeps going somewhere she'd have to stay awake to follow." I did not understand that sentence at all. I think it means: something might happen tonight, and she chooses to sleep. Then the next day, she texts to ask if the night held. It's a quiet ending, and it makes me nervous because the last thing I know about her is she's asleep. I think the author wants me to know something happened. I do not.

Do I still care about Chloe? Yes. But I have been waiting for something big for a few chapters now, and I notice I'm more curious than invested.

Favorite: "different would say clumsy, but the identical number twice says measured." Weakest: the long opening with the logs.

Prediction: the nine-minute person is the one who actually runs Halstead. And on the last night something will go wrong with the thing Chloe is asleep through.

## Chapter 36: Seventy-Five

I read the last page and said "wait, that's it?" out loud. It is a strong chapter with a great twist, and then it just stops, with the biggest questions still open. I'm not sure if I'm mad or impressed.

The opening is hard to follow. Priya goes quiet and the first page explains that everyone else is "where the summer left them" in a long list: Ruth's card, Kavi's second bench, Eli's job title with "a word in it he finds funny," Theo's blinds. Those are cute, but I wanted the chat. The second paragraph is a fragment about "a bus with a cracked window, then a second bus, then a room over a feed merchant's," and it took me a minute to realize it was Priya, not Chloe. Too many "she"s with no name.

Then the chat, and it's great. Priya says people keep turning up to take her since March: two men in a van, then six, then a dozen, then on Tuesday night seventy-five. "give or take. there were more behind the line i never got to." Sam: "we did seven in a car park." Priya: "you were sixteen and chloe said they were retarded." That line hit me, because it's the same word I complained about in chapter 20. Priya quotes it back, so the author clearly knows it is in there and chose to bring it up. I'm not sure it works as a joke or a bit of shame, because Chloe doesn't answer and nobody remarks. I still think the word should not be in the book, and I think readers my age would feel the same.

The big reveal is in what Priya says about the seventy-five. "They came in like thirteen year olds. a couple of fourteens. one of them was a fifteen and he put me into a wall." "Nobody did anything advanced. Not once, not one thing I have not had done to me on a mat." So they fought like Halstead kids at thirteen, fourteen, fifteen. They are not experts. Somebody has been training people to fight like Halstead students, just worse. That means there is another school, or a copy of Halstead, or another program. "so either they are lying about who they work for, or somebody has spent a lot of money on people who are not very good at it." That sentence gave me chills and also explains the chapter title for 33, "The Other One." I think this is the main point of the end: Halstead isn't the only one. I wanted to read more about it right away.

The best part is Ruth and Priya arguing. Ruth: "at the things you were doing on tuesday you are somewhere in the top ten percent of people alive." Priya: "ruth i finished sixty-fourth out of ninety-one. bottom third four years running." "but thank you for the heads up, genuinely." That's a perfect joke, and also the thesis of the book: even the "bottom third" can beat seventy-five. And Ruth's arithmetic: "seventy-five people, arm's length apart, is a hundred and ten meters. ive walked that distance a thousand times between buildings. i can see the whole of it straight through." It made me see it as a line across a field. And then it ends with Ruth writing down where everybody sleeps and realizing she started at the bottom, with her own address last. I loved that.

Also Priya taking two prisoners and treating them like houseguests: the one who puts every piece of onion on the edge of his plate, the one who says good morning first. That is kind of beautiful and strange, and it is the only softness in the chapter. Priya is such a good character. I'm glad she gets the last chapter. The last line, "the mare is due and the mare keeps her own hours," is a quiet joke that ties back to chapter 4, where she was telling Chloe about horses on day one.

What I did not like: no answers. We never learn who the nine-minute person is, who the "one man" behind Halstead is, what the pill is, why Ruth is on a list. I do think the book is saying: these are the questions now, and the answers are not in this book. But I wanted at least the pill. I keep thinking about "the small white one, you took it twice a day" in chapter 24, and nothing happens with it again. Chloe's still got it in her bathroom cabinet. I feel like the author gave me a really good clue and then forgot it.

Also: Chloe barely does anything in this one. She "types a paragraph on the bus, reads it back, deletes all of it, but sends a single line instead." That's very her. But it is the last chapter and the main character is on a bus. I would have wanted her to say something to Priya that mattered.

Favorite: Priya's "sixty-fourth out of ninety-one" and Ruth's hundred-and-ten meters. Weakest: the confusing opening and the lack of answers.

Prediction: there's a sequel. Maybe the next one is about the other school, or about Chloe at her job. But I want to know who pays for it all.

## Whole book

Would I finish it? I finished it, because I had to. But I'll be honest: I wouldn't have finished it without the first nine chapters. The first third hooked me hard, and the last six had me by the throat, but the middle (chapters 12 to 19 and a few after) lost me over and over. If I had picked this up in a bookstore and was an average 14-year-old, I think I'd have quit around chapter 14 or 16, when the book turned into long paragraphs about archery, markers, signal clocks, mark schemes and the bar exam.

Would I recommend it? Yes, to one specific kind of friend: someone who reads a lot, likes puzzle stories, and doesn't mind slow parts. Not to most of my friends. I'd say "skip the lists, read the dinners and the dialogue."

What stayed with me:
- Chloe at six getting called weird, and cutting her answer down to "Water." That's the whole book in one word, and I thought of it again at "Fine, quiet" in chapter 28.
- The library fight ("Four"), which comes back in chapter 10 when the library has no card.
- The floor scene in chapter 9: "Take my birthday." I still feel it.
- "Nobody was mean." Best line.
- Fen and her rocks sorted by size.
- Ruth: "put any kid off my street in that building at seven and you get this." That changed how I read everything before it.
- Nadia's window lettering sitting half an inch low, and "Out at a tire place."
- Sam shooting forty for forty and saying "You're shooting the bullet."
- The operator report from the file, where the four men from chapter 15 were real, and the kids graded themselves.
- Priya's "sixty-fourth out of ninety-one."

What dragged:
- The class lists. Almost every chapter has a paragraph that is a list of subjects and teachers and what she learned. After the first two or three, it all blurs. I skimmed them from chapter 12 on.
- Technical talk. Archery angles, milliseconds, signal clocks, ciphers, contracts, the financial worm, polling intervals. If you know it, fine. If you don't, it's just a wall.
- Too many names. I lost track of Bex, Iyad, Eli, Theo, Marek, Odile, Aurel, Dov, Ferris, Tomas, Bev, Manny, and so on. Some were important, many weren't.
- The Bex credit-stealing was used maybe six times (chapters 12, 13, 14, 17, 19, 21, 22). It was frustrating the first two times, and then it felt like the book was repeating a point.
- Some chapters repeat information I already knew (chapter 6's dinner recap, chapter 7's librarian recap, chapter 22's offer read twice, chapter 30's Whitaker interview).
- The same character tics: Eli's two-finger tap, Kavi turning an object in his hand, Nadia's sleeves, over and over.
- Long dense paragraphs and very long sentences. A lot of the prose reads like it was written for a college student. I often had to reread a sentence to find out who the "she" was.
- One word in chapter 20 that I flagged and that comes back in chapter 36. It should go.
- The ending gives no answers. The pill, the one man, the nine-minute person, who is behind the seventy-five.

How my feelings about Chloe moved: At the start I liked her and felt for her, in a "that poor kid" way. In chapters 4 to 11 I loved her, because she was funny and stubborn and wanted so badly. During the middle she turned into a person who was good at everything and I started to care less. I wanted to see her fail or fight with someone, and when she failed the bar in chapter 19 I woke up again. In the adult chapters I felt close to her again because she started to look like the six-year-old: saying "fine, quiet" at work, not telling her parents, hiding her pill, and still keeping Marek's sheet in a drawer. By the end I care more about her than anyone else in the book. I just wish she'd had more to do in the last chapter.

One more thing: the book says its characters talk "weird" and then makes everyone sound the same, long and precise and polite. A 14-year-old would say it is "an author's voice." The dialogue works best when someone cracks a joke (Sam, Priya, Eli). More of that, please.

Bottom line: a strong idea, a heartbreaking first third, a drag in the middle, a hard-hitting last quarter. I'd give it a 7 out of 10, and I'd read a sequel.
