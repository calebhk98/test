# Reading journal: the line editor

Lens: the prose and the craft for a 9th grade audience, watching for the narrator talking to the reader, register drift, tics, dialogue, and pacing.

## Chapter One: Before

This is a strong opening in terms of character. Chloe's voice comes through the arguments she makes, not through the narrator telling us she is smart. The library "four at a time" exchange is the best thing in the chapter: it is funny, it is concrete, and the librarian's flat "Four" lands. The cloud line, "Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else," and the short word she swaps in ("Water") show the whole problem of the book in two beats. A 9th grader would get both without help.

Talking to the reader is mostly light here, but it is present as trailing clauses. Examples: "Chloe can tell her mom isn't really paying attention or listening anymore, since her eyes have gone somewhere past Chloe's left shoulder" (the since clause explains what the eyes already showed, and it also says the same thing twice with "paying attention or listening"). "A chair squeaks behind her while Peanut keeps washing his face, so they must not have heard her" (the so clause is the reader's job). "which is when she hears her name" is fine as rhythm. "The word lands somewhere Chloe was not expecting it to land" is a narrator naming a feeling the next sentences (going back over the sentence twice) already carry. Cut it. "The parking lot, the boxes, the question that had already happened without her: all of it is true, but Chloe watches the streetlights" is the most essay-like sentence in the chapter and is also confusing. "Kayleigh cheated. Chloe is sure of it, because the same catch put Maddie on the wall" is a because tail that restates the argument we just heard her make aloud.

Register: mostly plain. A few places climb. "unbothered," "motionless," "continuing long after Chloe stops watching him," "the volume it had before" read a notch above a 9th grade voice, and "Chloe has decided against finishing it" is formal. The doctor's "everything I can measure in this room looks the way it ought to look" is stiff for dialogue. Sentences are often very long and chained with while, before, and and, such as the recess paragraph ("She plays two more rounds... at the whistle the line forms without anybody looking around for her"). It is a single breath of eighty-plus words.

Tics: heavy use of "while" and "before" to chain actions ("while the librarian works down," "while he types," "while her dad makes his usual noise"). Time stamps open paragraphs: "In October," "In November," "In December," "In January." "Still" and "keeps going" recur. The coffee cup on the roof is described twice in the same paragraph, nearly word for word.

Dialogue: Chloe's argument speech is believable for a precocious six-year-old only just. The train-station book summary is long, but the locker key detail is great. Her mom's phone monologues run long and have the same thought restated twice ("what is the next step" appears twice in one speech). The parents are not yet very distinct from each other.

Pacing: the middle (conferences, doctor, Christmas) is a steady run of small scenes with a section break between each. The Christmas scene with cousin Dana is thin. The ending on the sticky note is good because it is just a plain object, though "where her mom checks it every morning... and Chloe reads it whenever she catches the kitchen empty" is fine.

Overall: a warm, specific, slightly overlong opening. The strongest thing is the character logic. The weakest is sentence length and the occasional because-tail.

## Chapter Two: March 7th

The best parts: the empty fish tank, the NO CELL PHONES sign joke ("the people who can't read the sign are looking at a phone with a line on it"), the juice, and the car ride where Chloe asks about "percentile" and her mom says "He didn't give me a number." The line "because they are having a nice time" is exactly the kind of plain sentence that carries a feeling without naming it. Ben's "That is a question I skip" is also good, and the answers "They're the covers for the ends of you" and "Something not hurting you. It just won't stop" are the right kind of kid-logic.

The testing sequence is where the chapter slips. It runs roughly two thirds of the chapter and reads as a list: blocks, similarities, digits, picture pairs, symbols, words, letter-number strings, matrices, comprehension, cancellation. Many beats are one short sentence in a row: "The lid goes on the bin." "Ben writes." "She points, he turns the page, she points, he turns the page." That is the flat, choppy end of the register problem. Several sentences also describe a test the reader cannot picture and do not need to (the letter-number rule paragraph, "The pieces stop being shapes and start being rules about shapes," which is also a literary line). I would trim by a third; the blocks and the coding sheet are the two that matter.

Talking to the reader: the overheard speech by Ben is the main problem. It is the book explaining its own premise through a mouth. "a composite that averages two very different pictures into a single score" and "bright kid undersells the room I sat in this morning" are too polished for a man talking in a doorway, and the one that starts "Right, and here is the thing about the report" is one very long sentence. "a bad number is the kind that gets a school to do something, and hers is going to read as good" and "the only lever the school has for her" are thesis statements. "The talking is not six" is confusing. Also in narration: "What she works out that month is bigger than the pencil trick: finish first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the room leaves you alone" explains what the previous scenes showed. "She still knows all twenty-eight of their names, but not one of them has needed to learn hers" is well made, but it is a narrator summing up, and a bit literary. "because that question is off limits to children" tells us a rule instead of letting her swallow the question. "which Chloe decides means she is telling the truth" is fine.

Tics: "There is a version of this where she finds out and a version where she doesn't," and earlier "There might be a version of this where the mark can be scribbled": same construction twice, and it will probably come back. Lots of "Then" openers and "and she" chains. "Ben writes" appears over and over. British spellings ("colours," "forwards," "towards," "behaviour" in ch 1) in an American school story; the author may or may not want that.

Dialogue: Chloe's questions ("Can you fail it? Say I get all of them wrong") work. Ben is warm, but his instructions are very even and adult. The mom is more distinct here than in chapter one: short, protective, a bit evasive.

Pacing: the testing drags in the middle and the aftermath rushes. The "nothing changes" section is a short summary over three months and it is correct to keep it short, but it is told as summary, not scene. Ending on the strawberry magnet where the sticky note used to be is a clean image.

Compared to chapter one: same strengths, more list-like prose, and now dialogue doing the narrator's explaining.

## Chapter Three: The Letter

Chloe is the best thing here again. "It's got my name on it." and "It says Miss," are the emotional core, and they work because she says them flat and her mom just waits. "Is the network the one with cartoons" is a good small joke. The stair that "announces her every time" and the skipped fourth stair are good plain detail. The dad's train-station announcer voice is a nice, specific bit of family life, and the packing scene with the shoe joke is the warmest stretch in the book so far. The last line, the envelope in the front pocket with her name on it, is clean.

The problem is how much of the chapter is adults being suspicious in complete, polished sentences. The mom says "I have no memory of applying to anything of the kind, and I would remember," and later "a building I have yet to see." Neither sounds like a mom at a dinner table. The speech starting "Usually pretty good is the vaguest possible way anybody could answer that question. A phrase is what you reach for when you haven't got a number." is an essay paragraph in a person's mouth, and the same goes for "Nobody has that ready unless people ask it a lot." The mom and dad also repeat the same argument back and forth (free things exist, free things do not exist), then again two beats later: "Sometimes they are, if somebody's got the money..." followed by "Sometimes somebody has money and wants to spend it on something." That is a restatement of a line the reader just heard.

Talking to the reader, trailing clauses: "since there is a hotel half a mile from the campus with rooms held for them, free as well"; "The free part is the part Chloe can't place, since her mom says things cost too much about once a week, standing in a store with something in her hand"; "since past a point it stops being research and turns into a thing you have to explain to your neighbours, so that by Saturday half the street knows the Kesslers got something in the mail" (two tails stacked); "because a child opens it, and that's how you find out which houses write back." Narration that sums up: "Every single question gets answered straight away, quick and complete, as though the questions had all been expected" says what the phone call already showed. "For a school that has put over a hundred children through a summer and done it again every year since, that is what she finds" is the narrator pointing at the suspicion. "with the school, the list, and who is paying for it left for some other night" is a tidy wrap-up. "Behind her the bills are clipped to the refrigerator... but the letter upstairs says the month is free" is the camera, and works better than all of those.

Pacing: the phone calls and web searching go on too long, and the second call is mostly "Another wait" and "The voice continues." The day count is confusing: "It takes them nine days," then the fourth and eighth day, then "until on the eighth," then "On the sixth day," then the ninth night. A 9th grader will trip on that. Packing and the librarian bit are the right size.

Tics: "the right way round" four times (letter, letter again, Chloe's chair, KESSLER tags); it may be a motif, but it is too many. "her feet swinging free" and "comes up onto her knees on the chair" are repeats from chapters one and two. "Eyes on the page" twice in one sentence. Many long sentences chained with "then" and "so." British "neighbours" again.

Dialogue: the library conversation echoes chapter one nicely and the librarian is dry and distinct. Dad is warmer and looser than mom, which helps.

Overall: good ending third, slow and over-reasoned first two thirds.

## Chapter Four: Pluto

The strongest chapter so far, and the reason is the kids. "room-shaped object," the Pluto argument ("That's worse than just taking it off the list." "It isn't worse, it's what happened." "No but it's worse though."), Sam's "and a half," "You're agreeing at me," and Chloe having "a sentence about the lid ready" all sound like children, and each is different from the others. Priya's horse monologue is funny because she never checks whether Chloe cares. A 9th grader would enjoy this chapter. The hotel night, with her mom talking "word for word" the third time while Chloe falls asleep, and the "looking at the doorframe" ending of the Sunday goodbye, are plain and good. The not-calling night, ending on "Tomorrow. After dinner, when she's back at the hotel," is the best small scene so far. "Chloe lies on top of the covers with her shoes on." and "Chloe has been doing the same thing for six days." do their job in one line each.

Where it falls short: the physical-reaction paragraph, "something goes wrong with her... the backs of her knees go loose, her fingers curl stiff, refusing to close all the way, her ears do what they did in October," is hard to follow and the October reference is vague; the next sentence resolves it, so the explanation adds nothing. "words blur into one long hum, still going" is a literary note. "the sound that many people make in a hallway with hard floors is enormous" is a narrator stepping back to comment. Trailing clauses: "because there is another later for the families still driving"; "so the school puts Chloe in the first group until August"; "having said all of it once already, downstairs, in front of the woman at the desk" (the tail explains what her mom's words already showed); "which she has already told Chloe twice, word for word both times." "Chloe has a very clear idea of what is supposed to happen to a child who does what Ruth just did, so she watches for it" tells us her fear and then lists the camera version, which is enough on its own. "That night it catches again: Ruth said it" is unclear.

Register: orientation is rendered as a run of flat reports ("The laundry bags are green. They go outside your door on Tuesday and Friday nights.") and it is fine as a summary, but "no attempt to talk you round" and "got cross when I interrupted" are British phrasings in dialogue, along with "straightaway" and "round." A seven-year-old American kid would not say them.

Characters and clarity: six or seven named kids arrive in a few pages (Priya, Sam, Ruth, Owen, Kavi, plus the unnamed boy and girl). "There's a fourth in Pluto who stays silent" does not tell the reader who the fourth is, and he only becomes Kavi in the last lines of the volcano speech, where he has been "the boy." Owen appears twice working at a worksheet with no payoff yet. Kavi's viscosity lecture and Ruth's mass and weight lecture are the same kind of scene back to back, a kid correcting an adult or a peer; the second one is a little redundant, though Chloe's reaction to Ruth gives it a point.

Tics: "quiet about it" and "quietly about it" for two different crying boys; thumbnail pressed into something (ch 2 and here); "sits on her hands" (ch 1 and here); "keeps going" and "going straight on." Long run-on openings that stack "because" and "and" clauses.

Pacing: quick and varied, with a good split between scenes and summary. Chloe's wish to go home but not call is the quietest and best part.

## Chapter Five: Behind

A good opening line ("Everything here moves at the speed of whoever finishes first, and in the first week that person is Ruth.") and a good chapter overall. The bridge scene is the best use of show-don't-tell in the book so far: Ruth is right about triangles, Chloe half sees it, says "Okay" and holds the corner while Sam glues. The reader gets the point that she is going quiet again without being told. The Owen scene is also strong ("a mattress with the sheets gone," Sam's flat "I know," Chloe's pile of questions to Ruth). The moon argument ("You can't lie if you don't have a mouth") is funny and sounds like kids.

The long fractions epiphany is the weakest stretch. It works as a moment, "Six." followed by "Priya's light goes off." is a good beat, but the paragraph before and after it explains the math in full: "dividing by an eighth is asking how many eighths fit inside the thing you started with, and that is all it has ever asked" and "which means that multiplying by eight is the only thing turning the eighth upside down was ever doing." A 9th grader will follow it, but it is narrator lecture. The paragraph before it ("draws it a third time with her own numbers, because his numbers have been sitting in her head") is one very long sentence chain. "Oh," she says, out loud, in the dark, late at night" repeats itself.

Talking to the reader: "The bridge was fun. Owen must not have liked it. The two facts still won't sit next to each other, and no answer from Ruth is going to move them." The last sentence names the idea the scene already carried (and "the gap between those two facts" was used a few pages earlier for the fractions, so it is a tic of the narrator). "so that by the fourth time Chloe reads the event off the corridor" is a literary phrase for a plain observation. The closing two lines ("the check she has run on every sentence since November" and "She checks for the damage anyway, out of habit, and the sentence comes back clean") explain the arc of the chapter, which the argument at the table had already shown. I would end on the argument. "Chloe, wrong by three pounds, writes that the corner went first, because every corner of a square carries its angle alone and an angle alone gives way" is a trailing explanation, and an abstract one.

Register and dialogue: the kids are supposed to be gifted, but Ruth's speech about Owen ("I'm not going to pretend I didn't see it just because everyone else is pretending") and Mr. Baptiste's "a rule that survives every case you can throw at it is what a rule is, negatives included" are too polished and explain the theme. The zero speech ("zero was never a real number to begin with, and it can't be both") sits at the top of what a 9th grader could follow. Some lines lack attribution and are hard to follow, especially the table exchange after "Ruth." where the speaker of "She didn't ask you, specifically, she asked the whole table" is unclear. Also, everyone ends lines with "though" ("I did though," "It's not a race though," "That's what I'm asking though," "No but it's worse though" in ch 4), so Sam, Ruth, Chloe, and the teacher's answers start to blur. The moon scene opens in the middle of an argument ("That's why it's a liar") with no setup, so the reader has to catch up.

Prose shape: semicolon lists ("Sam gets it, announcing the fact with one loud grunt; Kavi says he already knew it...; Ruth, well down the packet...") and "Chloe writes a low number; Sam wants a hundred..." feel formal. The paragraph about the writing prompt has a fragment ("Where in the chapter that change happens, and how a reader is to know.") and a muddled sentence ("which would have been the longest answer in Mrs. Aldana's room by half a page, and that meant the class listening to it...").

Repeats: "lining the pieces along the edge of her tray" is chapter one's cafeteria image again. "Ruth says it to the room" appeared in chapter four. Ruth's "I told you" scene is the same as chapter four's. Swimming is a thin section.

Overall: a lively chapter with real scenes, held back by narrator lecture about the math and a closing that explains.

## Chapter Six: The List

The dinner monologue idea is good: a kid so full of a month that she cannot stop, while her dad watches. The "Then..." run (goggles, the robot arm, "Then chess, which Sam is also better at.", "Then karate: mostly learning how to fall over safely.", the nerf guns that "arrive out of order") is the best-paced stretch here, and the painting that "cost more than a house, which baffles her, because it is a painting" is a kid's thought. "what he has found is people eating" and the dad's "Cool," are excellent and plain. The ending phone call is the strongest part: the dad listing the month back to the mom, "Meg, they taught her CPR," and then the two of them repeating "I was standing right there." That is two adults not knowing what to do, said without anyone naming it.

The first scene, the breakfast room, is more adults speculating about the list. We did that in chapter three and the lines are nearly the same: "I told Dave I was keeping her home from a building I had yet to see" repeats the mom's phrase "a building I have yet to see" from chapter three, now from a stranger, so the parents all sound like one person. "which explained the paragraph, but not the list" and "which leaves the whole question of good at what" are cute but essay-like for people holding paper plates. "stayed a rumour" is the British spelling again.

The monologue repeats the bridge and the fractions in nearly the same words as chapter five, including "Every corner of a square carries an angle on its own, and an angle on its own gives way" and "A triangle can only fold if something actually breaks." The reader just saw both scenes, so this is the story restating itself. It would work if she compressed it or got it wrong. "which is a different way of running out" is a literary line in a six-year-old's mouth. Then, "Anybody can do the flipping... But the rightness belongs to him and not to you. All you actually know is that he said so." This is the theme of the whole book, stated by a child to her dad; the napkin going into his pocket carries more than the speech. Narrator tails: "and explains the corner twice to make sure he has it"; "on purpose" at the end of "her breathing slow and even, on purpose" (chapter one handled this with just the breathing).

The hall scene with the four kids goes in circles. The half-a-phone-call argument ("half a fact still beats a whole guess") is cute, but it runs eleven lines and the point is only that there is a form and a day in the fall. Kavi's "a form is a completely different object from a promise" and "a day only means somebody looks at you, and I think that's still a long way from anybody saying yes" explain and sound like an adult. "you know perfectly well" shows up for both Ruth and Sam. Odd grammar that looks like a typo: "an whole month," "an whole year," "an whole room" (three times; if it is a kid tic, it is never marked as one). Then the narrator paragraph, "Chloe runs the numbers again... four hours in the car each way," is followed immediately by Chloe saying the same numbers aloud: the same content twice in a row.

Tics: "keeps going" and "straight on" (the dinner monologue says "keeps going, straight through it" and "talks straight on through it" in two consecutive paragraphs), "while" chains, "the cold part of the window" and the eyes-shut car ride, which is a good echo of chapter one if intentional. Priya's hair tie is a nice small detail but is not explained, and Chloe's gesture lands softly.

Pacing: the middle of the chapter is slow because the same ideas are told three times (the month in dinner speech, the hall argument, the phone call). The phone call is the one worth keeping in full.

## Chapter Seven: The Same Room

This is the first chapter where the emotional shape works almost all the way through. The scene where Chloe learns she does not know anyone's last name is the heart of it: the mom keeps asking, Chloe goes through what she does know about Sam (the "and a half," the window, "a sister, or possibly a cousin"), and her mom's hand comes off the phone book to rest on her head. The waiting section (the chair that faces the phone, the debt collector call, "renew them please") shows a kid shrinking without anyone saying so. The time capsule is very good: Kayleigh's "It's like you're reading it off a card or something," Bryce's tone ("the way you would tell somebody their shoe was untied"), Chloe's hand coming down over the page, and then the last line she writes instead. A 9th grader would feel that one. The ending, the crying at the desk with "I don't..." and nothing more, is the right size.

The first paragraph is confusing. It tries to cover Sam leaving, Kavi leaving, and a crowd in one long sentence ("while Kavi is over by the double doors with adults who give him a hand up, until a family walks between them and by the time they pass he has gone"). I had to read it twice, and it is the opening of the chapter. Ruth's goodbye after it is fine.

Talking to the reader and tails: "putting the envelope down and looking pleased about it in a way that puzzles Chloe" (names the effect instead of showing the mother and the confusion); "and her mouth goes tight at the corners, the way it does right before she cries" (tail that names what the next line shows); "for a reason she could not give if anybody asked her for one, cries" in the last scene (narrator explaining that the feeling is vague, and then the very next paragraph does it again with "Whatever it is, she can't get the answer out"); "relieved to find it still works like it worked in first grade" (the camera already showed her checking all thirty answers). "A number was extra." is an odd phrase that I could not parse on first read.

Dialogue: the mom sounds like a script. "Give me the whole thing, start to finish," "so that if I give you our number you can pass it along to them, and then it is entirely up to those families whether they do anything with it," "I think that's a decision your father and I have to make together, not something we settle standing in a hallway. We're not going to decide something this big with your backpack still on your shoulders, and neither should you want us to." Real parents under pressure are shorter. Ms. Vance, in the crying scene, uses therapist lines ("tell me one thing that's true right now," "that's the first thing I have to rule out"). Chloe's speech to the librarian is the third time we hear Gary, the bridge, and the fractions (chapters five, six, seven), and it now contradicts chapter five slightly (here "right at the corner... the only corner that did"; there it folded sideways all at once). Also "Mrs. Prahl" appears with no introduction, after Mr. Doyle and Mr. Baptiste. The librarian's "That's a real month, that is." is a good voice.

Tics: "Chloe is already talking before..." appears three times across chapters six and seven (dinner, kitchen, library). "have yet to": the mom said "a building I have yet to see" in chapter three, a stranger said "a building I had yet to see" in chapter six, and now Chloe says "a person you have yet to meet." Every adult in the book seems to own the phrase. "though" at line ends again ("But when, though?"). "on the second ring." The mom opens lines with "honey, sweetie, sweetheart" in rotation.

Style: "Having taken the third library book to bed on the Monday, she makes it..." is a dangling start. The long "she asks" sentence, with six semicolons, is deliberate and works as a rhythm, but it could lose two items. Sentence chains of "and... and... then" continue.

Pacing: six short scenes of waiting; the rushed one is the call with the school about testing, which is mostly plot logistics.

## Chapter Eight: The Asking

This chapter has the best single exchange in the book so far, and it is the one at the end of the bedroom scene: "Nobody was mean," then "Nobody was mean to me," then the bullying question, then "That isn't bullying. It's only true." Chloe's answers are small and flat and the mom's confusion is real. The bike on Christmas morning is nearly as good: the parents in the doorway saying "She does." and "She really does, look at her go," while the reader knows what is behind it. The magnet holding a grocery list "instead of the note," and the envelope under the socks "where it has been since August," are quiet, plain endings to a long stretch. The Dana callback ("Chloe says it's good") is a good shorthand.

The best sentence to cut in the chapter, then, is the one that comes right after "Nobody was mean": "Her mom waits, sitting very still on the end of the bed; that is all of it: a hallway with a brass plate on the last door that says PLUTO, a boy honking with laughter about a room-shaped object... and being allowed to be wrong for a whole afternoon." That is the narrator unpacking what the line means, and it is the most "talking to the reader" sentence in the chapter. The line was strong by itself. The same goes for "Chloe sits up and answers straight away, the answer already sitting in her mouth before the question finishes, and it is one thing, and it is small", which pre-announces the line. And "What she is waiting for is the turn, because at camp there was a point in every single thing where it stopped being easy..." explains the feeling, and then Chloe says it out loud ("When does it get harder?"), so we get it twice. The paragraph starting "She can't work out what's wrong with it, because it's faster than her class..." is a long explanation of her feelings, ending with a tail: "a thing she has learned to keep to herself." "There are more of them than will go in a line" and "the rest of the hour goes quiet around it" are literary.

Clarity: the transitions are bumpy. After the car scene, "Her mother starts on the school in October, every week" runs on with no section break and steps back in time. "At the counter, with her back to the room and Chloe at the table behind her, she says the words in writing twice in one sentence" is a puzzling line; I could not tell what it meant. "So is the third, except that Mrs. Prahl puts them in pairs" reads as a fragment of something missing. "Mrs. Prahl makes a real thing out of it" is vague. "The jacket is Friday, and any excuse would have done as well" is nice, but then "something about the Thursday that needs answering and gets it" is another vague one.

Dialogue: Dr. Ammons ("loving a place and something happening there can both be true at once") and Mrs. Prahl ("Some children get there in October and some get there in May. Both of those are the same speed as far as I'm concerned") talk in tidy adult paragraphs. The mom's fight line, "I lie awake and go through the reasons to say no, and every morning I still don't have one good enough," is the mom explaining her state, which is a little too neat. Chloe's fight ("You want me to be like this") is raw and works.

Tics: "keeps it to herself" three times (the bike under the sheet, the packet, the thing she has learned to keep to herself); Chloe "says it to" something (the couch arm, the ceiling, the wall; in earlier chapters the desk and the tray); mom sitting "on the end of the bed" with her hand flat; "keeps going anyway"; "mouth goes tight."

Pacing: this chapter covers four months and handles it with scenes that are each small and well chosen. The Thursday program section is the least alive, mostly a list of things Mrs. Prahl says that are nice and empty, which is the point, but it is told with narrator commentary where the reader would have got it from "I'm still working on it."

## Chapter Nine: February

The floor scene is the strongest writing in the book so far. Chloe offering to give up the dishes, the bike, Christmas, "Take my birthday," in broken sentences ("You can have. You can have Christmas," "That's the last thing. That's the.") is real, and it works because she cannot finish the lines. The father's feet on the kitchen tile "no pan, no water" shows his state with no comment. The wheel-the-bike-back-out beat, and the parents saying it is not a deal, then her answer ("you don't get to keep only the half of it you like"), is a child's exact logic. "Is that a true thing you're saying, or is it a thinking about it thing?" is great. The ending, the mother sitting in the dark car while Chloe has toast offered from the other room, leaves the feeling unspoken.

Talking to the reader is still the main flaw, but in smaller lines: "Chloe can't answer that. The word for it is nowhere in her." (names the state, and "nowhere in her" is literary); "and it is a whole sentence, and she forces it out twice" (explains what the quoted line shows); "The crying is ahead of her, and she is sitting in the gap with a fistful of carpet" (unclear); "having worked out beforehand what order he wants it in" and "which he sent off days ago and kept to himself" (tails); "the building is a different building in April than it was in July, because it is raining and the halls are full of people" (a literary line with a because-tail, in one sentence); "running each one into the next before he can answer" (tail on the drive talk); "but she has yet to open the door on her side" (a narrator pointing at the image; the picture alone was fine, and "yet to" again). The overheard lines "like it was money she'd saved" is a simile, but it is the mom speaking and it fits.

The opening is a single paragraph of about 230 words: weeks of February in one block. It has good details ("the carton stands on its own now," coloring "going right up to the lines and stopping exactly there"), but the block is dense, and "past where the talk reaches" is a literary phrase. British items slip in: "colour in" and then "colors" two sentences later, "a fortnight ago," "arms round her," "went round, I expect."

Dialogue: the parents' kitchen argument, and the later house argument, are full of long polished speeches. "A lease that ends the day we take her out. That is not somewhere to live, that is a hold on us, and the people who wrote that offer know it, and so do you, because you read contracts for a living and you have not once told me I have this one wrong." is a lawyer's closing, and it is mostly plot exposition (the house offer) delivered as argument. They say each other's names at the start or end of most lines ("Dave," "Meg"), which stage dialogue does and people do not. "since August" appears four times in two speeches. "I was standing right there" is the dad's line from chapter six, now with the doorway and dishcloth. "I'm not saying it a third time" and "You can say it a third time" are used again in separate scenes.

Point of view: the April house argument happens while Chloe is at the Petersons', so it breaks from her point of view; the narration also changes from "her dad" to "Dave" in the middle of it. It is the only scene so far where this happens, and the chapter does not need it, since we already know the house issue from the mom's end. The earlier "Chloe is out on the landing... catches only the end of it, clear enough" is also muddled in the same way.

Pacing: the first half is gripping and fast; the second half is plot logistics (form, house, tour) and loses tension, though the drive home picks it back up. The tour is rushed, with the beard man and the "entire day" question left hanging as a single sentence.

## Chapter Ten: April

This is the first chapter set inside the school, and it reads more like a tour than a story. The best things are small and specific: Fen and her rocks (the row sorted by size, "I'm taking them with me and starting the sill again wherever I go next," the white line "a different rock that got into the first one while it was still soft"), Sam eating standing because "his chair is Ruth's now," the argument over a spoon nobody remembers, the afternoon hours "Fen calls the fun ones but everybody else calls the afternoon," Ruth finally giving her last name ("Aymar. A, Y, M, A, R."), which pays off the last-names scene in chapter seven, and the wall at the phone alcove "gone smooth and pale at shoulder height where everybody leans." Ruth's "Though not before ten" is a perfect small line.

The problem is that so much is told in summary, in very long paragraphs. The library paragraph is about 280 words and ends in a muddle: "The first time she takes a dozen... but she manages a few of them." (what was managed?). The math-at-Halstead paragraph defines the school in abstract terms ("Here you get given the thing the formula is about, plus a week, and you come back with whatever you have. The teacher tells you whether it is true and stops there. Good is a different question, one he leaves alone."). The big math breakthrough, the wheel problem, is a summary sentence ("It occurs to her in the shower on the Wednesday") and we never get to feel the idea, so it feels lighter than the fractions night in chapter five. The paragraph that starts "The second term starts and the subjects change over" is a list of subjects and counting on fingers, and the dining hall paragraph ("of the ninety who were at camp, twenty-some came back that first fall") is hard to follow.

Talking to the reader: "Her mother laughs at that, properly, for longer than the answer deserves, but it takes Chloe a beat to work out that it was funny" explains the laugh; "She knows the number, because she counted them herself the week it happened, before Ruth ever said so" is a vague tail; "because he is the one she has decided will simply answer her" and "since there's no other sport that teacher looks like he'd have done" are because/since tails; "The glass has the approach in it: the gravel loop, the front of the building..." is an odd camera line dropped into the Fen paragraph, and "She came in under all of that an hour ago and is only looking at it properly now" explains it. "for a month she hates it, though the first weeks go badly in every subject she has" is rushed and reads like a note.

Register: the narration says "mathematics" while the kids say "math," uses "second term," "carries on cutting," "carries on eating," "knocked about," and "hours" for periods, so the school voice drifts British and formal. Teacher speeches are tidy: "People do go home sometimes... which makes this a school rather than a prison, and certainly rather than a competition you can be knocked out of" is a statement of the book's thesis, delivered in a case-packing voice. The literature teacher's "There's no list at all, and I know that sounds like me being clever with you, but it's the answer" is good.

Repetition: Owen is asked about again, and the exchange with Kavi ("you asked Ruth in April, at the trays, while I was standing right there") is a replay of the Owen scene from chapter five, and again nothing is resolved. "hugs her hard enough to hurt" is Ruth's chapter-seven line word for word. "keeps going long after anyone needed him to." The third-floor, rooms, and bread details are charming but are one more pile of detail.

Dialogue: Fen is distinct (short, factual, dry). Ruth and Kavi are consistent. Sam, who had the best lines in chapter four, has almost none here.

Pacing: lots of cheerful catalog, little at stake until the Sunday call. It is a rest chapter after nine, which is fine, but at this length it needs one scene played out in full.

## Chapter Eleven: Eight

The home summer is the best part and a relief after chapter ten. The hot dog argument ("Nobody has ever bitten into anything and tasted the hinge") is a funny, believable father and daughter fight, and the day count ("On the eighth he concedes on a technicality about structural intent") is a good joke. The parents downstairs after, with the shower still running and the singing, work well, especially "She used to hum in the car... like it was something she needed the engine noise to cover." The grandmother scene is quiet and good: the remark about weight, the mom's "I know" kept low, and Chloe at dinner with "I've had enough." The school half has the best individual sentences of the book so far: "Nerf is eight." "Last term you were drawing mittens." "so she is at least flat somewhere useful." "The others wanted off the team you're on." Chloe being ninetieth of ninety at drawing and dance is a good, honest thing for the book to let her be, and the oval mallet she keeps using is a quiet image with no comment.

Problems start with clarity. In the curfew scene, "three of the things Chloe says over the next twenty minutes she has been building since the day before" and then "Then there is the thing Chloe has not built at all, then the stairs, then the door" skip the part the scene is about: we never hear the unplanned thing. The mom's recap to the dad ("she had it all loaded before I opened my mouth... she always stops the moment she knows") is the mom explaining her daughter and the scene we just saw, and it reads as the author's summary. "Obviously I won, I'm the one who picks the number" is better. Some parent lines say the feeling outright: "The whole summer, and I already know I'm going to count every single day of it," "I know how long it is, I've had the number in my head since the day she left." The dad has done this before, in chapter six.

The school paragraphs again slide into abstract statements. The French teacher's "It carries on being yours, which is the entire answer. It stays where you put it, or it goes, and from here that's your call rather than mine" says almost nothing, and the reader cannot tell what "it" is. "Logic is an hour of being shown a sentence and asked what it is actually claiming, which she can do from the first week. On the phone she tells her mother it's like taking a lid off" is a phrase repeated from Sam's pool lid earlier, unconnected here. The politics teacher's port authority in Piraeus speech is fun but is an adult-sized sentence about banks and spouses. "The day it turns over goes past like any other Monday" means nothing on first read. "Bell" appears as a bare surname before he is introduced as Coach Bell. The bracket game on the grass uses a "bracket" that is never shown, so the whole joke needs a setup the text skips. "while names are on the climbing line and one on beekeeping" is garbled. "With drawing she found out at the end of term, whereas with dance she can feel it while it is happening" explains the difference between two scenes the reader just saw. "but the matching is a different job from the thing she came in for. What she liked was being loud in a small tiled room, alone" is an explaining pair of sentences, and "a different activity wearing the same word" is a literary phrase.

Tics: "an whole building of eight-year-olds" (again "an whole"); "different thing/job/sentence/activity" appears five or six times; "reads it twice" three times; "keeps going." British: "pavement," "colours," "towards," "carries on," "went spare," "a shade darker," "turns the block sheet round." Walking backward is now both Mrs. Okonkwo's and Sam's habit.

Pacing: the summer at home is scene-rich; the school year is a calendar of one or two sentences per activity with one longer scene each. It is cheerful, but many of the subjects blur together. Chloe barely speaks in the apple scene, and the hour on the grass is mostly Kavi and Ruth.

## Chapter Twelve: Nine

This is the longest montage so far and the one where the pages start to blur. Subjects, teachers, and small experiments pass one after another: Mandarin, statistics, chemistry, first aid, textiles, biology, two more blocks, riding. A few land hard: Kavi's complaint about the form (funny as a numbered speech, "The third is cryptography"), "I liked dance, and I was good at dance, and I'm going to keep saying it," the biology teacher's "That is a long life for that fish, and I've been expecting it since Christmas," the shirt that drags in the same place ("because the second is the green shirt"), and Sam's overhang. The overhang is the best section: "Then thirty people watch me not do it, and that's still Monday," Chloe tearing the corner off her book "with no memory of doing it," and "Sam eats left-handed for days and keeps the other hand under the table." That is the camera doing the work, and the feeling is plain.

The Bex and Iyad sections should be the heart of the chapter (someone takes Chloe's work and says "we"; someone repeats the stranger-loses-her line at the far end of the table), but they are told as a report with almost no spoken lines. "Bex has it before Chloe has her mouth open... she says we in her first sentence, finds Chloe over the top of the desk before she finishes it, and gives everything after it in I" is very hard to picture. We never hear Bex say a word to Chloe or Chloe say a word to Bex, so a 9th grader gets the idea but not the sting. "the word comes out thin" and "Chloe goes back over the fortnight that evening looking for the sitting where she said any of it out loud" are literary and stiff. "Chloe works with Iyad in chemistry for a term after that, during which he is careful and fast but gets more out of the hour than she does" says little.

Talking to the reader and tails: "In November Mrs. Sun stops the hour and does it on the board for the room, because half the room has it" (unclear); "Chloe reads her own sheet again on the stairs, for a different reason, then a third time" (vague); "which she marks by dog-earing the corner"; "By February they have a name for that from statistics" (the name is withheld, so the line says nothing); "Priya... gives it back, 'I know, and that's the bit I kept waiting for the whole time, for it to turn into that for me too'" repeats chapter eight's "I keep waiting for the part where it gets hard" and states the point of the riding section outright. "Something on Sam's own sheet is different from everyone else's, and by Thursday there are theories about what" is a hook that never pays off in this chapter.

Register: the language is carrying a lot of adult school and office vocabulary. "p-value," "reagent list," "integration work," "deliverable," "milestone," "inboard," "ablative." The kids' jokes with these words work for gifted children, but a 9th grader will skim them. The ending, the dad and the vendor proposal, is the weakest scene here: it is business jargon ("Every deliverable is worded so it can't be checked against anything, and every milestone describes the effort") and it is the book's thesis about promises and checking, spoken by an adult. The fact that Chloe notices and he agrees is the point, but it is dull to read. "a different kind of point... a different kind of score entirely" is the "different" tic again.

British and school voice: "the head," "a fortnight," "film," "queue," "term," "alright," "round" and "carries on" keep appearing. Teachers again finish their line or sentence before they look up ("finishes the line she is on," "eyes on the next bench," "keeps his eyes on it"), and kids "square the sheet" (also Mrs. Okonkwo in eleven).

Pacing: no through-line. Many pleasant, similar paragraphs ("the Friday after that she runs it wet... the Friday after that she runs it dry") and Chloe is mostly absent as someone who wants anything, apart from the Bex pain, which is under-written. I would cut half the subjects.

## Chapter Thirteen: Ten Pages

A stronger chapter than twelve because there are more scenes, and the best ones have something at stake. The paintball moment is the best: the hose picture ("You watch where the hose is pointing before anything comes out of it") is concrete and a 9th grader can use it, the two paintballs meeting "with a flat wet crack," orange and blue hanging in the air, is good camera work, and then comes the pay-off in which Bex announces it to Bell. The post-drill corridor with Ruth ("Six is me." "Six is all of us, Chloe.") is good, and Iyad turning Chloe's line into a laugh at her expense ("on account of Kessler was standing in it") is a sharp, believable bit of school cruelty with no narrator comment. The grandmother ("Your mother did exactly that, floor, back against the bed") is warm and short. The ending, "Then how come you're not miserable about it any more?" / "I don't know," is exactly right: she does not explain herself.

Problems of clarity are worse here. Several time jumps go backward without warning: "In the second week of September a man from the office spends an hour on Watch" appears after October and November events and after "by Christmas." The self-defense paragraphs use shorthand the reader cannot decode ("The first term is ones... in November it goes to twos"; "Chloe is on eleven seconds by June, but two of them she wasted"; "Sam gets fourteen seconds in February, a mark that stands alone for the rest of the year"). The "three things" Bex says are the barrel, the feet, and the single speed the first time, and "the barrel, the shoulder, the single speed" the second, without comment. Hearn's advice ("the third page of an essay is doing the work of the pages in front of it") and the later payoff ("all the pages do what only the third used to") are too abstract to follow, and the sentence "The shape has a place in it where she is going to be wrong, but she writes towards that place" is vague, essay-about-essays writing.

The Bex theft happens a second time, and the paragraph that tells it ("People start asking on the Friday...") restates Chloe's whole insight in a long summary sentence after we just read it in her own words. It is also the third in a row of "the sheet is already written" beats (chapter twelve's bridge, now Bell). The line "like the sheet is the thing that happened and the Thursday isn't" is the best of them, and it does not need the follow-on "Bex is like that with everything."

Talking to the reader: "A line she could argue with. A mark with no line under it leaves her only her own doubt to argue against." (explains what the clean page did; keep only "She would rather have had a line telling her it was bad"); "She has all of it, and she says it at the volume she says everything at" (the opening line of the chapter, about Hearn, reused for Bex); "so that a drill nobody could have held comes out looking like a year that couldn't hold it" is Chloe's speech and is polished enough to sound like an essay ("Nobody scores the drill" is the real line). "because the question is harder and she spends most of a night stuck" is a tail. The paragraph about languages and music ("Russian starts in September... Algebra starts in mathematics in the second week, on the sheet, in the slot where the mathematics was") is filler, and "Music runs both ways at once" is a literary opener.

Dialogue: Kowalczyk's lecture ("Five people can reach you at once, six if they're good, seven if you're standing badly...") is clear and works. The tariff argument at Thanksgiving is accurate to a gifted kid, but a 9th grader will not care about steel and wars; "Go on then," "turns round in his chair," "any more" are British again. The dad's cut-off "That's the b-" is nice.

Tics: "a boy called Wes," "a girl called Hana," "a boy called Dev," "a girl called Marisol," "a boy called Marcus," "a girl called Marguerite": a new minor name with the same "called" setup almost every chapter. "says the number out loud twice" (Sam in five, Kavi here); "ears hot"; "reads it twice, then a third time."

Pacing: the first half is a rich run of scenes; the second half thins into summary sentences again.

## Chapter Fourteen: Sixty Degrees

The best moments are specific and physical. Odile's collision: "a small dry click a long way up, before anything is visible," then two arrows coming down wrong, hers whole, hers "spinning," Odile's broken across the middle, and the pair of them yelling in the grass about a sound neither hears the other describe. The area bombing essay is strong because of the dinner argument: "It's a stupid question because it's got an answer," then Sam's pushback and Chloe's "That's annoying. Now I have to go and build the thing I came in here to say was wrong." The B note is a good line ("An argument you apologize for is a request. Make it or leave it out."), and Hearn, caught last in the library, asking whether the boy was done with the Burke is funny. The grandmother scene ("once in an whole year is hardly a lot, is it") and Chloe folding the envelope and keeping it is the kind of ending the book does best. The intercom ("Every member of staff currently in the building is now hostile... Good luck") is a clean, plain scene opener.

The first third, though, is hard to follow. The archery setup is geometry told by Coach Bell and the narrator: "two firing lines seventy meters apart, side by side, both facing north, each bending in towards the other until they meet at a point where the angle holds at sixty degrees." A 9th grader cannot picture this, and "Your target is coming the other way at sixty meters a second... Say target and everybody here will know what you mean" is a puzzle. "At the top the arrow has stopped climbing and has yet to start dropping, so it hangs there. If you're late, it's barely moved while you were being late, and that's the only thing you've got going for you" is a muddle. Kavi's speech about the shoulder is long and technical.

The Bex thing happens a third time: chapter twelve the bridge, thirteen the paintballs, now the age of the universe ("Bex... gives it to the boy on her other side complete, beginning to end, Sam's name on the front of it"; "at the volume she says everything at," the same phrase for the third time). The beat is the same, the paragraphs are long and muddled ("and asks it again twice more, until she has both ways of measuring it in full"), and the reader is now ahead of the book. Chloe's quiet reaction ("keeps her bag zipped under the desk, and does not turn round") is the good version of it.

Talking to the reader: "because she spent those hours on a landing while the number of teachers upstairs went uncounted" (tail that explains why she stares at number seven); "Marked out means done, whether by a knee in your back or paint on you" (a definition for the reader, fine, but the sentence before it does it too); "Chloe opens her mouth, then works out that the answer starts with the hat, and after the hat it needs the deadline..." (narrator summary where a short line would do); "The walk down to the field is long and empty either way" (literary and unclear). "Rhetoric is an hour a week of dismantling a speech to find the place where the person giving it is being paid, usually well down the page" is a quip in narration. The essay-writing paragraph ("There is a hole in the middle of what he says, but he walks straight past it, so she writes the missing piece in herself, on his side of the page, since knocking down the weak version afterward would be doing his job for him") is essay about essay-writing and dry. Sam, the clown, says "If you can't say why the man signed it, what you've got is a feeling that it was wrong and a blank where his reason goes," which is the book's theme again in a polished sentence, and it is out of character for him.

Order and clarity: the Rhetoric, art history, and Kavi paragraphs are in a muddled order (Kavi "asks whether she got the essay in all right" in the middle of the art history, long before the essay). "People say afterward that Ruth ran the east stairwell... while in minutes Nadia takes the third floor" switches tense; "People say afterward" is used twice in the Watch scene. The B note says she hedged "twice on page eight, once on page twenty-two," and then Chloe says "he found both." The chisel exchange has a stage direction used as a line ("Filing the edge.").

British and word tics: "an whole" (twice here), "yet to" (twice), "towards," "backwards," "for ever," "bannister," and "thirty pounds" in an American school. "says it to" again.

Pacing: long and heavy with mechanics; the three scenes that work (collision, essay dinner, Watch) are worth a clean chapter by themselves.

## Chapter Fifteen: Twelve

This is the first chapter where the humor and the dread run together and both work. The bread test is the best set piece in the book so far: Ruth's "which is a room agreeing with itself before anybody has chewed anything" (a little literary, but the next line, "You can taste knowing what day it is," lands), Kavi at the oven glass writing numbers on his hand ("That's a heat exchanger problem and I want to know which part of it is the limit"), Mrs. Adeyemi's flat "It's a bread test... Go to bed," and "Five out of eight is what a coin does." The stock count ("Sam... They're by your foot") is a good small bit. The 4 a.m. scene has great short lines: "The box always says AES-256," "That's not very twelve," Ruth going down the row with "He's fine. This one's fine. This one's, yeah. He's fine," and Sam asking a tied-up man "Why did you even bother encrypting?" and then "That's a C right there." The Thanksgiving table is the best parent scene since chapter six: the spoon sliding into the gravy, the dad's hand over his mouth until his wife's look makes it drop, the mom's "Fine as in fine, or fine as in the subject is closed?" The ending ("You mostly can't," "Huh," and the hand held out for the ham) is understated and right. "the space kept for her" in the common room is the plainest way the book tells us she has friends now, and it is good.

The weak parts are clarity and the narrator's explaining. The Marek paragraph is nearly unreadable ("the chapter he has been going through since the summer, the line the author skips over, the pages he has had to write out himself to get past it, all of it fast and with his finger keeping the place"), and the hooks, "That evening the sixth is in the fire, but she stops there, scroll half open, and puts it back in the coals," do not make clear what happened or why it matters. "the girl at the next bench walks over with a punch in her hand and admires how straight the hammer marks run" is a stray image. The Chloe and Odile paragraph is procedural ("which Chloe establishes with a watch behind her over several sessions"). The physics and psychology lines are filler. Priya's speech about the corridor kid doing the plates is one long sentence and hard to follow.

Talking to the reader: "she lets him, because moving would be wrong" and "doing exactly what she needed him to do, in front of three men who are all looking at one thing" (both explain the plan we just heard); "Without stopping, he leaves her to work out what he meant, which takes a few minutes and another attempt" (tail); the paragraph that starts "Chloe sits against the wall with her arms around her knees while Sam talks, working at the wrongness of it" is the biggest offender, a very long sentence that explains the book's central doubt ("the direction a school would get it wrong in... or the direction four real people would get it wrong"), followed by "A man's chest going up and down a few feet away sits on that list with the bolt cutters," which is a literary line. This is one of the key moments in the book and the narrator explains the stakes instead of letting the reader feel the oddness of the bolt cutters. "The other thing they discuss is the drugs: the kit has both" reads like a report.

Repetition: the Watch scoring list appears again, almost word for word from chapter thirteen (here without numbers and with "in that typeface"). The "she opens her mouth but the answer needs the hat/the release, and the release needs the count" device appears here and in chapter fourteen (the hat, the deadline). It works as a way to show the gap between school and home, but it is now a pattern.

Dialogue: the jargon in the 4 a.m. scene (AES-256, "salt," "derivation," "four seconds of compute") is above a 9th grader, but Kavi and Sam both undercut it with jokes, so it reads as texture. "It'll cost us, but we don't have much choice" is a flat line. "to nobody in particular" is used for both Sam and Ruth within twenty lines.

Word tics: "keeps to herself" (the burn on her wrist) again; "at a volume that turns the far end"; British items "tinned tomatoes," "trolley," "trousers," "in a shop," "pigeonhole," "towards," "carries on."

Pacing: slow opening, then a run of strong scenes. The two-line coda about thirtieth out of ninety is a fair last note.

## Chapter Sixteen: Thirteen

The most crowded and least clear chapter so far. Almost every paragraph introduces a new subject, a new minor character, or a new piece of the school's machinery, and I had to reread several to follow what happened. Still, the good lines are good: Marek ("a receipt is a different object from work," the blank sheets, apologizing for the ruler, "That's the problem, in full"), the epistemology professor ("luck wearing a good coat," then "Office hours"), fencing ("arriving a fifth of a second after it has closed"), Kavi in game theory ("You'd worked out I go left, so left was the surprise... I'm playing you"), the plan that "works, all of it, including the kitchens," and the ending with the dad and the step. "this stays between the two of us" is a father teaching a kid to hide a mistake with no comment, and the mom bouncing twice on the top step with "Great improvement on the old lot" is a plain and perfect last beat. "Chloe knows all their names" is a quiet echo of the first grade.

The Marek section starts clear and then goes abstract. "Marek does the work and keeps it. The sheet has a column and the column is for paper. The number that comes out of the column counts on her record as well as his, and she has been asking him for it since October; asking him is the whole of what she is allowed to do about it." and the closing paragraph ("whatever a fail costs a student here, expulsion is no part of it. Whatever it costs him, whether it follows him onto anything that matters, whether anybody sat him down about it, stays outside her reach for years") are the narrator telling us what the story means and looking ahead years. It is formal and unclear for a 9th grader, and the earlier scene already carried it. "in the flattest words she can find for it and with no name anywhere in the question" and "It takes under a minute and it answers everything she asked" are tails.

The covert-network material is where the prose loses the reader. "Everything gets arranged on their phones; in October the duty teacher stops at the end of the long table, says she is sure Mrs. Okonkwo would be delighted to hear what her handwriting reminds them of, and carries on to the servery. What she has done is read three messages off the top of a screen over somebody's shoulder" is a bundle of facts with no clear scene. Ruth's box speech ("eight ugly things in a box and not one clever thing... Make it noisy for a bit and that's a camera being a camera") is a technical lecture, and the log speech ("Knowing what we said is beside her point. What she wants is that this table said something...") uses "her" with no clear person. A reader cannot tell who is watching whom or why. The lab and press paragraphs ("somebody stopped and kept the stopping to themselves"; "Both describe a single report, so one of them is wrong") are literary and dry. Amberg's three-point speech about locks is clear, but it is an adult lecture.

Talking to the reader and register: "Teaching starts at thirteen and you take the year below, so her cohort, being the oldest in the building, is the only cohort doing it, which makes the twelves the only year taught by another student and the thirteens the only year with no teacher of their own" is a puzzle sentence. "By the end of the meal the table's five opinions have settled on hers" is a summary. "Nadia is there most nights, saying what she thinks in flat words on principle, so half of it lands as an accusation" (so-tail). "which makes" and "which means" tails appear in many sentences. "She'll not say" in Iyad's speech is dialect that none of the other kids uses.

Repetition: Iyad at the volume of "the far end of the table" again; "different object" (Kavi in eleven, Marek here); "the back of a spare timetable" twice; "keeps to themselves" again; Chloe quietly drops away from the people who take credit (chapter twelve, fourteen, now: "Chloe does not sit on that side of the table again for the rest of the term"). The napkin scene is the third time someone retells her work wrongly, with the extra twist that Iyad makes her sound sly rather than shy; that twist is the interesting part and deserves more space.

Dialogue: the napkin scene has many unattributed lines and the reader must guess who is speaking. Too many named dinner kids appear in one list (Sam, Ruth, Kavi, Priya, Odile, Eli, Nadia) for a 9th grader to hold.

Pacing: compressed to the point of shorthand. Marek, the napkin, and the steps could carry a chapter by themselves.

## Chapter Seventeen: Fourteen

The strongest scene is the dinner at the end of the chapter, and the reason is the list. Chloe has told her mom a story about a scar, her mom asks "You're shooting rifles?", and then the book gives us what Chloe cannot say: the girlfriend, the pencil mark on the door frame, "a clamp that closes on the wrong axis, three hinges in a bin," then "Which one was she supposed to have opened with? Why is it the rifle?" That is the book's best use of a list so far. The dad's "I thought you knew. I thought it was fine," the mom's "What in God's name is clays?", the pie warmed up by way of an answer, and the Sunday questions about the man behind the line are good because everyone avoids the sentence. Other good bits: Voss and the stopwatch ("Firing point four, you're up"), Sam asking a girl out ("he'd think about it and get back to her"), Pruitt's "a seam, not a hinge, and stop throwing away good work," Kavi's number and Ruth's "That's a hope with a schedule on it," Ruth looking up at Sam's height ("You've been eating like a man with a farm"), and the girl at the sinks ("You said the second week. Nobody gets a week out of the air. Somebody handed it to you." then "Chloe already knows who sits there."). That last scene is the sharpest the book has been on the Iyad subject because Chloe acts instead of the narrator summing up.

Where it falls down is the sound-array project, roughly a thousand words of milliseconds, clocks, calibration, thresholds, and hand's widths. Priya's line ("everything wrong with the boxes has gone into the clocks and you've written calibration on it") is a precise sentence about something the reader cannot picture. The emotional point (Kavi sat on a known error for two weeks, and Ruth is angry) is clear once you reach it, but a 9th grader will skim ten paragraphs to get there. The no-libraries paragraph ("sixteen milliseconds or the letters tear") is another list of unexplained terms. This is "register" in the sense of an expert writing for experts. Also the list of subjects ("Abstract algebra arrives with probability beside it, contracts spends a term..., logistics spends a term...") is another filler run, though "no way to test any of them" is a good phrase.

Clarity: "It's seven and about a fifth," Aymar says" is hard to follow: Ruth's last name is Aymar, there is another student named Aymar, and she says "He's got my last name... So he's mine. Those are the rules." It is a funny exchange but the reader has to work out that this is a different person. The teaching error is vague ("She taught them the ordering reversed... everything before the test came out identical either way round, it sat there for months"); I could not tell what the ordering is. "The real sentence stays exactly where it's been all evening, buried under the one about pie... that turns out to be as much of the sentence as the room can hold" is a metaphor the narrator builds and then explains; the dinner works without it. "That's a hold," her mother says. "That is a hold, that's been a hold all night" is cryptic unless one remembers "a hold on us" from the house argument in nine.

Talking to the reader: "where it is funny and nothing else, and it is carried from there to Iyad within two days" (narration spells out the rumor chain); "All of it went past this table in silence" (the list already did that); "Chloe has the arrival sheets and nothing yet to put into them, so she sits in the room..." (flat summary); "She finds Ruth at breakfast and tells her the round herself, ahead of the story going round again" (explains what her action means). The fourth Bex incident ("By the week after, the round the year has is a round Bex called from the edge of the mat") is now a pattern the reader recognizes, and the chapter handles it in a single paragraph, which is the right amount.

Dialogue: Kavi, Ruth, Priya, and Nadia now speak with the same cadence (precise, long, a clause-by-clause hedge: "so the answer is a meter wide before Ruth has heard anything and before Chloe has done any arithmetic at all"). Nadia's "A wire is people. A wire is a morning putting it down and a morning taking it up" is the one voice that stands out. Odile's joke about Ruth being bored in the briefing is good.

Tics: "standing right there," now Odile (it was the dad in six and nine); "walking backward," now Kavi (Mrs. Okonkwo, Sam, Chloe on the stairs before); "with people behind her waiting for a turn," word for word from thirteen; "says nothing about it" twice about Nadia; "keeps going" again; "a boy called Dov / Ferris," more minor names; British "synthesised," "round," "fortnight," "servery."

Pacing: long middle of technical summary, then a very good last scene and a flat final line about calls home.

## Chapter Eighteen: Fifteen

The plateau at sixty is the best sustained idea here, and the prose serves it when it stays concrete: "At dinner she clears the plate, but it could have been anything on it," the wind-pattern card she bends "until it comes off in her fingers," Ruth's toffees ("Have a toffee, it helps me think"), Sam's "I think I sleep fine on it... Go and be miserable at Ruth," and Chloe sitting beside Odile after her record round with "the damp coming through her shirt." The Iyad and Aurel rumor is the book's most accurate piece of school cruelty so far: "She has never heard the name in her life," "does not look up either time," "Odile says all right, then asks it again, differently, in May," and "She stops using the language-block stairs." Chloe's reply ("Four... I could give you the number for everybody else on your list too") works. The driving scenes are very good: "I said look ahead, not Tennessee," Chloe calling the truck's every move, the full coffee, the dad laughing into the cup. The ring ending ("I'm not telling you which corridor") is the best last line since chapter fifteen.

The weak parts. Voss's and Kowalczyk's speeches read as statements of theme: "a gun you can only point at a man is a weapon, but a gun you can put a bullet onto a bullet with is a tool," and "you're fighting people who have been doing this for years... they're coming up off a standing start, which is the first actual challenge you've had." After Voss's speech the narrator spends a paragraph on where the line comes from, and it goes nowhere. "Point six... Put another two hundred rounds behind it" and "Some things you have to work out on your own. I can't teach you everything" are evasive in a way that sounds written. "eleven people who have stopped moving, their curves flattening into a single narrow band" is hard to read. Sandoval's request ("a request and not an instruction," "I would refuse to put a federal agreement in front of a fifteen-year-old, because asking you to sign would be wrong and would fail in court besides") is a long formal speech. The week after it ("builds the case against first... From her own position and from that of the names on the form, she makes that side as hard as she can before she builds the other") is thinking described in the abstract. Hark's scene turns on a metaphor ("You bought a piece of somebody else's ceiling") and Chloe's "he was capped and I wasn't," which I could not decode. The loan paragraphs are a page of rates and motors.

Talking to the reader: "Ruth calls the wrong sentence... The right sentence is that a single lock stood between a lectern drawer and everything the table has said to each other" (the narrator spells out Ruth's point); "so the instructor writes steady on her sheet and then the time beside it" is fine, but "the laugh that started for Chloe finishes somewhere else" explains the turn of a laugh; "which is the first actual challenge you've had" is Kowalczyk explaining. The phone and login section ("puts a window on the handset," "a clean pane of glass in your pocket") is technical poetry that blurs what Kavi built.

Repetition: "That's not an answer" or "Not an answer" now appears three times in this chapter alone (and in chapters three, six, and thirteen). "at the volume of the far end of the table" is Iyad's fourth appearance of the phrase. "leaves X exactly where it was" shows up four times (the offer, both boxes, the sheet). "yet to" (Kowalczyk). Priya's "girl called Fen" is introduced as if new, though Fen is the rock girl from chapter ten. Chloe's long conversations at dinner are summarized ("She hears the run of zeroes, the November card, what the wind does...") instead of played.

Register: "carry on," "round," "towards," "pudding," "a telephone" in Sandoval's speech. A list of subjects again (surgical block, accountancy, Hindi, topology).

Pacing: too many threads. The range plateau, Iyad, and the driving lessons could stand alone.

## Chapter Nineteen: Sixteen

A good chapter about failing, and the book is braver here than anywhere else. "Fail," she says. "By four points, out of two hundred." Kavi's slip ("I passed it by six"), Bex arriving loudly to comfort her, and "I can do the fraction myself, Bex," said at her back, are all believable and hurt. "Lights off, she runs the fraction again anyway... to find the one sentence that would have stopped Bex halfway down the hall" is the right size of thought. Sam's "I'd like to withdraw ten of my pages" is funny. Marek returning the stack unread, with the shed question rewritten and "That's better than theirs... Do that," is the best comic beat. Delacroix with the cup on his knee ("at a speed where the cup is the more impressive achievement," "You hit what you look at") pays off the dad's driving lessons. Chloe filling in the April paper's four blank rows and saying "I'm finishing it" is quiet and strong. Pruitt's "Tongs on the rack... The ones you made. Take them with you" says goodbye without saying it. The last question to her dad is a good, short close.

The problem is the same lesson delivered four times. Amberg explains the mark scheme twice, Kavi explains it again with the "single idea wearing two coats," and Nadia's "said nothing" in the margin carries it a fourth time. The first Amberg speech is nearly one hundred words in a single sentence, ending in "whatever they feel like from inside your own head while you're doing the first and not the second." The tone is a lecture in every one, and the reader has understood by the first. "In a single fast paragraph she writes the shed burning down, with a sentence in the middle that makes her jaw set" is a quick, vague summary of the moment the lesson actually lands. The contract and shed material also sits in a legal register ("who carries the risk while the goods are sitting in a shed waiting to be collected") that a 9th grader will not warm to.

Register and dialogue: almost everyone now speaks in finished, balanced sentences. Chloe to the drama teacher: "I'm choosing, and there's a difference between skipping a line and deciding the line has to earn its place before it stays." Kavi after the board: "I knew it the moment it left my mouth, and now it's already out there whether I like it or not." Chloe's long speeches to her father about Sam and Ruth (about two hundred words each) are retellings of material from chapter eighteen ("go and be miserable at Ruth," Ruth on ninety-one), so the reader gets the same fact twice, and they run as monologues, not talk. "bores her absolutely rigid the entire time, she has never once said so out loud" is a long sentence with a story inside it. The line "Ruth... told me the whole point of a day off was choosing what to do with it, so this was what she'd chosen" is good, and would be better if the speech around it were cut. Mom's "That's not the girl who left here for that school in the first place" says the point outright.

Talking to the reader: "Bex has both of those right, and she has them out to the corridor rather than to Chloe, at the volume she says everything at" (the phrase is now a refrain; this is the fifth use); "by August the rate has come down and settles there, declining to improve" (formal, dry); "the work down there staying between her and Pruitt, his entire commentary across the years having come to a handful of sentences" (a summary where the scene had already done it). The first sentence of the chapter is about eighty words and ends in a "so that" clause about the sound of the room.

Tics: "exactly where they were/it is" appears again (Chloe's own line and "leaving every line of it exactly where it is"); "turned the right way round" (Amberg's slide of the paper, as in chapter three); "yet to" (Delacroix); "walks backward" (Sam, again); "wearing two coats/a different hat"; "turns the glass a quarter turn" twice. "sleeps through most of the first week" is a returning-home beat from chapter twelve.

Pacing: the bar, the failure, and the retake are a clean arc; the middle has driving, drama, and a stack of practice papers that read as summary. The ending question is well placed.

## Chapter Twenty: The Parking Lot

The most fun chapter in the book and one of the cleanest in terms of what happens. It is one night with four kids and a clear shape: sneak out, diner, shopping cart, mugging, home. The opening line ("They are the first to sneak out since the school opened.") works. The diner details are good: Sam eating around the crisp edge "before he touches the syrup at all," the waitress calling them honey "twice for Ruth," Nadia asleep sitting up who "wakes and finishes her sentence," the exact change "split to the cent." The cart paragraph ends on a very good image ("a wheel still turning"). Nadia's "You take the thing you're worst at and you stay in it... It's the only pattern you've got" and Chloe's "Pick something you've actually watched me do before you hand me a pattern" is the best personal exchange between kids in a while. "Let me have this one." and "None of them were adults, and none of them were strangers" are strong. "Left," Nadia says, "I know," Sam says, is the best beat in the fight. "That somebody robbed them of a robbery?" is a great line. Chloe counting her own ribs afterward ("finds nothing. Only then does she get under the covers") is quiet and right.

One thing needs fixing right away: in Chloe's speech after the fight, "that's retarded" is used as an insult. For a book aimed at 9th graders it reads as a slur, and nothing in the scene marks it as a character flaw. It should come out, and the sentence is better without it.

The fight itself is hard to follow: "Sam puts the first through the second, which drops both. The third gets an arm out and Sam takes it and uses it," and "Chloe's weight goes onto her hands once, when the fourth man gets round behind Sam's shoulder, and comes off them again before she's committed to the drop." A reader cannot see these. The strip of seven men, three guns, "a clump" is clear, but the action is mostly shorthand. "from four meters away it looks like three people walked backwards out of the world" is a literary line in the middle of action.

Talking to the reader: "Flat, not unkindly, she counts it off" and then a speech by Chloe explaining why the fight was nothing (bunched between two cars, locked elbow, "the worst-paid dangerous job there is"). It is the right thought, but she explains the whole analysis, and the later sentence "Whoever taught them to stand like that never taught them what standing like that is actually for" does the job alone. "She says it evenly, but she is looking at the cup and not at any of them" tells us the feeling behind Ruth's brother. "Sam hasn't moved, looking at the seven of them on the ground the way you look at a problem where the answer came out wrong" is a simile that explains the line he says next. The opening plan paragraph, "preparation divides itself between them," is formal, and "Kavi turns it down... from Kavi this always means he has already started it" is a tail that explains him.

Banter is good, but Ruth's "hither" speech (about ninety words, "dictionaries everywhere would need rewriting every few years") is a long sentence that kills the joke's pace, and the whole exchange is a longer run than it needs. The ending paragraph is muddled: "Kavi sends a laughing face... but he lets it sit every time it comes up" does not make clear whether "he" is Kavi or Sam, and the last sentence is a sprawl. I would end on the ribs or on the waitress joke.

Tics and spelling: "quarter turn" of a cup, "clockwise" (a glass in nineteen, now a cup); "counting under her breath out of habit" twice in a few pages; "curb" and then "kerb" in the same chapter; "gas station" with "fortnight," "towards," "pudding"; "walked backwards."

Pacing: lively and short; the best-sized chapter since chapter four.
