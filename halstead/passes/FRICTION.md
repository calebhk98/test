# Friction: who Chloe dislikes, and where else they could rub

Two parts. Part 1 is the finding: everybody in the book who frictions Chloe,
sorted by what the friction is actually made of, so the author can overrule the
sorting. Part 3 is the proposal: where else each of them could rub against her
across the twenty years.

Part 2 is three new sheets in `characters/`: `MAREK.md`, `BEX.md`, `IYAD.md`.
Nothing in `chapters/` was touched.

---

# Part 1: the people

## A. Sustained antipathy. These are the real ones.

**Bex** and **Iyad** are the only two the book states outright, in one paragraph
in chapter twelve. **Marek** and **Kayleigh Burns** are not stated and are the
same thing in practice: both of them cost Chloe something repeatedly, over
months or years, and she cannot put either down.

| person | where she has them now | what the dislike is made of |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Bex** | ch12, one paragraph; ch21, one clause | She takes the ends of Chloe's sentences. The ending is the one that was coming, and there is no way to object to being agreed with. Chloe's counter is to get to the point faster, so the cost is paid in how Chloe talks rather than in anything Bex ever hears about. |
| **Iyad** | ch12, one paragraph | He agrees loudly around the second sentence and hands the idea back at dinner with no origin on it. Chloe would still take him as a bench partner tomorrow, which is the sharpest thing in the paragraph: the dislike survives knowing he is good. |
| **Marek** | ch16, most of a section; ch21, one clause | He does the work, keeps it, and the blank column lands on her record as well as his. She has to write the failure in herself. Asking him is the whole of what she is allowed to do, and she is still careful about how she asks years later. |
| **Kayleigh Burns** | ch1, ch2, ch7, ch9 | Two flat public verdicts on how Chloe talks, a rule argued away on a blacktop, and a hug for everybody at a door. She is named in ch9 as one of the pieces of the thing Chloe cannot name. She is the reason a whole answer becomes one word, and the shortening then works, which is what makes it permanent. |

Sheets written: `characters/MAREK.md`, `characters/BEX.md`, `characters/IYAD.md`.

**Kayleigh already has a sheet** (`characters/KAYLEIGH.md`) and the brief bars me
from editing it, so she is reported here instead. She belongs in this group and
she is the most under-used person in it. See Part 3.

### A note on the three new sheets

The author's complaint is that every existing sheet reads as logical, silent,
direct and honest. All three of these were written against that:

- **Marek** is written warm, fast, distracted, apologetic and hopeless about
  time. The reviewers' profile has him as a cold uncompromising monk making a
  principled stand, and that is precisely the calm reasonable antagonist the
  brief rules out; his MBTI is INTP on the sheet rather than the reviewers'
  INTJ. His one stillness has one trigger, an adult holding a form, and it is
  written as a learned exit from a conversation. Everywhere else he is in
  motion. His refusal is an absence rather than a stand, which makes him much
  harder to be angry at and much harder to move.
- **Bex** is loud, generous, popular, physical, and mortified every time she is
  told. She apologises properly and does it again in ninety seconds.
- **Iyad** is warm, anxious, over-groomed, and structurally necessary to
  everybody. He is the one who keeps the list and takes the worst job. He is
  not tracking attribution on anything, including his own ideas.

None of the three is silent, none is direct, and two of them talk more than
anyone at the table.

## B. Sustained friction, sheet already exists

Real, recurring, unresolved, and the author may want any of these promoted.

- **Nadia** (ch16). She reads Chloe and says the reading out flat, at a table,
  and Chloe takes her hand off the napkin and puts it in her lap. The book
  already tells us Sam sits between them most nights and neither of them has
  ever said a word to him about it. That is the best piece of unused friction in
  the manuscript and it appears once.
- **Chloe's grandmother** (ch1, ch8, ch14, ch18, ch23). Every appearance
  diminishes the thing Chloe has just described, kindly, and moves on to the
  next person. Chloe hands her the hand with the burns on it and lets her hold
  it. This is friction Chloe never resists, which is what makes it usable.
- **Cousin Dana** (ch1, ch8). Asks how school is, gets an answer, turns away.
  Twice, seven years apart, identically. She is not disliked. She is a machine
  for making Chloe feel unaskable, and she has appeared exactly the right number
  of times to make a third landing.
- **Sam** (ch4, ch14, ch18). He agrees at her, tells her to go and build the
  argument she came in to demolish, and tells her to be miserable at somebody
  else. Not dislike. Worth listing because he is the only person who frictions
  her and gets thanked for it.
- **Ruth** (ch4, ch5, ch14, ch15, ch21). The told-you-so, the Latin-is-cheating
  argument, the height, the four-pages-for-six-hundred remark in front of Nadia.
  Real abrasion, no antipathy at all.

## C. Annoyed once, or annoyed at a situation rather than a person

None of these should get a sheet, and none should get more friction.

- **Mrs. Aldana** (ch1, ch2). Takes the book by the spine. Chloe's grievance is
  with the day, and she rationalises the teacher inside a paragraph.
- **Mrs. Prahl** (ch8). Nice to her every single week, and the hour is wrong.
  Chloe cannot locate anything to object to, which is the point of the scene.
- **Mrs. Okonkwo** (ch11). Says no to dance and no to a team swap. Chloe holds
  the edge of the desk and loses, and does not carry it.
- **Coach Bell** (ch14), **Voss** (ch18), **Kowalczyk** (ch18). Each declines to
  give her the answer she came for. She goes to the next thing every time.
- **Maddie** (ch1). Named twice, both stings. Too thin to build on.
- **Aiden R.** (ch1), **Wes** (ch13), **Marisol** (ch5), **Rustem** (ch18),
  **Dov** (ch17), **Delacroix** (ch19). Single-scene irritants at most.
- **Priya** (ch4, ch12). Talks at her for a month and asks why she quit riding.
  Affection throughout.
- **The three-percent figure, the registrar, the man who asks about her research
  team** (ch21, ch28, ch30). Friction with institutions and strangers, not with
  people. Chloe files each one and moves on, and that filing is characterising.

## D. Disagrees with, respects

Do not add friction here; it would flatten people the book is using well.

- **The librarian** (ch1, ch3, ch7). Will not budge on four at a time and gives
  her the cart on a Thursday and holds a book for a month. Chloe argues with her
  every visit and likes her.
- **Mr. Hearn** (ch13, ch14). The essay back with no note at all is the worst
  thing anybody does to her in the middle of the book, and she never once
  resents him for it.
- **Mr. Amberg** (ch19). Makes her sit down before he finishes. She reads the
  rows twice.
- **Mr. Baptiste** (ch5), **Mrs. Sun** (ch12), **Hark** (ch18), **Sandoval**
  (ch18), **Kavi** (throughout), **Whitaker** (ch30).

## E. Hostile to her while she is indifferent

- **Bryce Dolan** (ch7). Tells her she is weirder than she was, with interest,
  the way you would report a shoe was untied. Chloe puts her hand over her page.
  She never thinks about him again and the sheet is already right about this.
- **The boy who says lucky** (ch13). One word, and Ruth does the impression.
- **The men in the parking lot** (ch20). Chloe's account of them is the flattest
  thing she says in the book.

## F. The childhood-to-adult question

**Nobody Chloe dislikes as a child appears again after chapter eleven.** Not
Kayleigh, not Bryce, not Maddie, not Aiden R., not Mrs. Aldana, not the
librarian, not Ellie Peterson. Cousin Dana gets to chapter eight and stops. The
home town is a place the book visits for summers and holidays from chapter
eleven to chapter nineteen, and it is populated entirely by her parents, her
grandmother and a reservoir.

That is the resource. Chloe spends eleven years being measured against ninety
geniuses and never once stands next to the girl who told her she talked weird.
Three sightings of Kayleigh across those summers, each shorter than the last,
would do more for the book's argument than any new antagonist, because Kayleigh
will be pleasant, will remember almost nothing, and Chloe will have nothing at
all to say to her. Dana lands the same blow from inside the family.

Both are in Part 3.

## G. One thing noticed in passing

Chapter seventeen calls Ruth by her surname at the range and then, two
paragraphs later, has Ruth telling the year whose stopwatch it was, also by
surname. It reads on a second pass but a reader can take it for a second Aymar
on the first. Not a friction question; flagging it because it was in the way.

# Part 3: the friction proposals

## The rules I held these to

- Small, recurring, unresolved. No confrontations and no arcs. A person still
  doing the same small thing eight years later is the product.
- **Chloe does not win any of them.** She has nothing to say, or she is late, or
  she gets the procedural answer, or she takes her hand off the table.
- **Nothing tells the reader what she feels, and the feeling is never named.**
  Every beat below is written as what she does. Where a beat needs the reader to
  connect it to something earlier, it connects by repetition of an action, not
  by a sentence explaining the connection.
- She never looks down on anybody. In the home-town beats she assumes the other
  person's life is the interesting one, and nothing in the prose corrects her.
- Every beat rides on a scene already in the chapter. None of them needs a new
  section, a new location, or a change to what happens.

## Notes per person

### Marek

Seven beats, from twelve to twenty. The shape is that the thing never resolves
and never escalates: she asks carefully, gets a procedural answer, and is still
carrying it into a federal background interview eight years later. He is warm to
her in every single one of them, which is the whole engine. **The ch19 beat is
the load-bearing one**: he borrows her papers, does not read them, and gives her
something better instead, in good faith, and she cannot object to a gift.

One continuity question for the author. Chapter sixteen puts Marek in the year
below Chloe; chapter twenty-one has him applying to universities alongside her
cohort. The proposals above assume the year below and none of them breaks if he
is in her year instead, except the ch23 beat, which becomes him standing in the
alphabetical line behind her.

### Bex

Eight beats, from ten to twenty-one, and the run is deliberately monotonous.
The same move every time, always accurate, always agreeable, and always in a
scene where somebody else is watching. The ch30 beat is the only one that
happens without her in the room, and it is the one that says she has been the
same person for a decade.

Do not let her do it twice in one scene. Twice reads as spite and she becomes a
different character.

### Iyad

Seven beats, from ten to eighteen and then once as an adult. Vary the
mechanism: two loud agreements, two unattributed returns, three pieces of pure
arranging. **The ch24 beat is the best of them** and it rides on a scene the
book already has: Nadia asks the group for a date and gets nothing, and a month
later Iyad asks the same question with everybody's name in it and the group
answers.

He is never present when Chloe is alone. He only exists at tables.

### Kayleigh Burns

Two beats, at fifteen and sixteen, plus the option of a third at eight in ch11
if the author wants the run to start earlier. She is friendly every time. She
asks where Chloe goes to school and accepts the answer. She has no memory of
anything she said when they were six, and the prose must not say so.

**The hard constraint here is House Rule 1.** No line may point at the earlier
chapters. No epithet, no callback, no clause explaining who this is. Her name is
enough; a reader who has read chapter one has her, and a reader who has not gets
a nice woman outside a tire place. Chloe says almost nothing in both beats and
stalls the car in the first.

Chloe should not privately assess her. If anything crosses Chloe's mind it is
that Kayleigh's summer sounds like the more interesting one.

### Dana

One beat, at ch23. The third instance of the same forty-word exchange, twelve
years after the first, on the day Chloe graduates. Nothing about it should be
different from the first two. That is the joke and it is the author's joke, not
the narrator's.

### Nadia and the grandmother

Both already have sheets, so these are proposals only. Nadia gets four beats
because the ch16 material is doing nothing and is the best friction in the book:
somebody who reads Chloe correctly, says so flatly in company, and has never
been objected to. The grandmother gets one, at graduation, because ch14 and ch18
have already built her and one more landing finishes the pattern without
overplaying it.

## What I did not propose

No beat where Chloe out-argues any of these people. No beat where somebody
tells Bex or Iyad what they are doing. No scene where the friction is named,
resolved, or discussed by the group. No new antagonist invented from nothing.
No beat that requires a chapter to change what it is about.
