## The institutional-routine question, answered

The brief asked me to confirm or overturn the earlier pass's decision to
leave chapter 10's institutional-routine passages alone. I checked all
three named cases plus the other routine passages of the same shape in the
chapter.

- **`chapters/10_april.md`, line 73:** "The bell rings a little past the
  hour, every hour..." This is not even a passive to begin with; "rings" is
  what a bell does on its own, with no missing agent to restore (nobody
  "is rung" here). **Confirmed correct, no change.**
- **`chapters/10_april.md`, line 55:** "The corridor lights go out..." Same
  shape: an unaccusative verb ("go out"), not a passive construction, and
  the one thing that could be named (whoever operates the switch) is exactly
  the unknown-and-irrelevant agent rule 8 part 2 describes. **Confirmed
  correct, no change.**
- **`chapters/10_april.md`, line 17:** "...the rooms all get reassigned in
  August..." A genuine get-passive. The dropped agent is the school's own
  room-assignment process, which nobody in this scene could name even if
  asked, and Fen (the speaker) stays the topic of her own sentence either
  way. This is the same shape as rule 8's own worked example, "Polish is
  added to the autumn schedule." **Confirmed correct, no change.**

Three more of the same shape, checked for the same reason, all landing the
same way:

- Line 81, "plus being told for the fourth time that your onion is uneven
  all over" (agent is the cooking teacher, generic and about to speak in the
  very next line, but the sentence is a parallel list of what the class
  *is*, not an account of what the teacher does to Chloe specifically).
- Line 91, "you get given the thing the formula is about" (same: the
  teacher is named two sentences later, but this line is describing the
  subject's method in general, not a specific act toward a specific
  student).
- Line 123, "a third about the kitchens, who is allowed in them, and when"
  (agent is the administration; the corkboard notice is the topic).

**Overturned nowhere.** All seven read as function-2 or function-3 passives
by the real test: the missing doer is not a specific person already live in
the scene whose relationship to Chloe the reader needs, it is the building
itself running on its own rules. None of them erase a person the way the
book's own bad examples do (a parent, a teacher, a specific named adult
doing something to Chloe that the scene is actually about). The earlier
pass's reading holds.

## Call-out

### chapters/10_april.md, line 169

**Current:** "South America is done by the end of May and Europe soon after
that, but she keeps putting the -stans in the wrong order, all five of
them, in a different wrong order every time she tries."

**Why the current voice might be wrong:** every other sentence in this run
keeps Chloe as the subject: "She still has to stop and think her way
through Central Asia" just before it, "she keeps putting the -stans in the
wrong order" and "she counts her own on her fingers under the table" just
after. This is the one sentence in the stretch that drops her and puts the
continent in her place, which is topic continuity broken in the direction
rule 8 part 1 warns about: the thing the paragraph is following (Chloe's
progress) jumps out of the subject slot for one sentence and jumps back.

**Why it was not auto changed:** genuine doubt, not a rule blocking it. Two
lines earlier the teacher's own dialogue frames the whole exercise as being
about when "a country's actually finished," which gives a real reading
where "South America is done" is tracking that established
country-finished frame rather than dropping Chloe, in which case the
passive is correct under rule 8 part 3 (the receiver, the map, is what the
clause is about) and touching it would be the wrong direction entirely. I
could not settle which reading the author intended, so this is reported
rather than changed.

**Active:** "She has South America done by the end of May and Europe soon
after that, but she keeps putting the -stans in the wrong order, all five
of them, in a different wrong order every time she tries."

**Active:** "She finishes South America by the end of May and Europe soon
after that, but she keeps putting the -stans in the wrong order, all five
of them, in a different wrong order every time she tries."

**Passive:** current wording is the passive option and is included above
for comparison: "South America is done by the end of May and Europe soon
after that..."

## Chapter 9 in full

Read start to finish, sentence by sentence, for both directions (passive
wrongly dropping a person, and active wrongly promoting an unimportant or
inanimate doer). This chapter is almost entirely two scenes: a
psychologist/school-decision montage and two pieces of overheard parental

their actions actually matter to the scene (line 5, line 7, line 17, line
75, line 99, line 113, line 139). Dialogue was checked separately and reads
as natural, unforced speech throughout; nothing in it needed reporting
under rule 8 or under any other voice question.

No changes. No call-outs. This is the honest result, not an underworked
one: the chapter's few institutional passives are all the same
unknown-agent shape confirmed above for chapter 10, and its family scenes
are already active with the actual parents doing the actual things.
