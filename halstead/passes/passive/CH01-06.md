## Instance 1

**01_before.md:99-105, age 6.**

> The board still has yesterday's spelling list chalked half erased in the
> corner. In November, Mrs. Aldana asks what clouds are made of, and Chloe
> knows the answer. Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else, and the whole
> sentence is in her mouth, her hand already up, until she looks at the back
> of Kayleigh Burns's head and takes it down to one word.
>
> "Water," she says, and Mrs. Aldana says good and moves on.
>
> The exchange is over before anybody looks up. In December, when called on
> for the water cycle, she names the stages: evaporation, condensation,
> precipitation. She hears Mrs. Aldana say exactly.
>
> The full answer comes first, every time, and she has to catch it before it
> gets out and swap it for the smaller one, the one that matches what is
> already on the board. Some days the catching is late and half a sentence is
> already out of her before she gets to it. But the short version works every
> time she uses it.

**Shape 3, declining, repeatedly**, told as a summary rather than lived once.
Two instances are dramatized (clouds, water cycle) and both land clean; the
paragraph that follows asserts a whole year of the same swap, including days
when "the catching is late," without ever showing one of those late catches.
It is the book's central masking habit reported as a statistic.

**What she does now:** the swap is total and invisible on the page - we are
told it sometimes goes wrong, never shown it.

**What a six-year-old would do:** not manage a smooth downshift every time.
A kid catching herself mid-answer catches herself audibly, with a half-word
already out.

**Proposed:**

> The full answer still comes first, and one January morning it gets partway
> out before she catches it. Asked why the moon looks different some nights,
> she starts, "Because it's not really -" and stops with her mouth open on
> the next word, her hand only halfway back down. Mrs. Aldana waits. Chloe
> closes her mouth around the rest of it and says, "it changes shape,"
> and Mrs. Aldana says good.

## Instance 2

**02_march_4th.md:285, age 6. Minor - one sentence, easy fix.**

> Chloe pulls the seatbelt out and lets it snap back against her collarbone,
> against the rules, which her mom lets go. There is a version of this where
> she finds out and a version where she doesn't, and she can't work out
> which fast enough.
>
> "No," she says.

**Shape 5, the considered non-action, stated.** The sentence hands Chloe
credit for weighing two named outcomes; nothing about the weighing is shown,
only asserted, and then she answers anyway.

**What she does now:** the narrator states the two branches and the
inability to choose between them.

**What a six-year-old would do:** not run parallel hypotheticals. She'd do
the thing with her body again, and the answer would come out ahead of her
rather than after a weighing she doesn't have the patience for at six.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe pulls the seatbelt out and lets it snap back against her collarbone,
> against the rules, which her mom lets go. She does it a second time, then
> a third, and on the third the word is out of her before she has decided to
> say it.
>
> "No."

## Instance 3

**04_pluto.md:195, age 6.**

> On the fourth night she gets as far as the phone and stands there with her
> hand on it, until a kid comes up behind her to ask if she is using it, so
> she says no, then goes back upstairs, where Priya is still awake.

**Shape 3, declining.** She reaches for the one lever her mother told her
works at any hour, and gives it up the instant someone else wants the phone
- no hesitation, no negotiation, total and immediate retreat, at the exact
point in the chapter where her mother is about to stop staying at the hotel.

**What she does now:** relinquishes the phone on the first interruption.

**What a six-year-old would do:** stall, half-finish what she started, or
claim the small territorial right of having gotten there first, rather than
folding on contact.

**Proposed:**

> On the fourth night she gets as far as the phone and has the number half
> dialed when a kid comes up behind her and asks if she is using it. Her
> finger stays on the last button a second too long before she hangs it up
> without finishing.
>
> "No," she says, and goes back upstairs two steps at a time, where Priya is
> still awake.

## Instance 4

**04_pluto.md:257-265, age 6.**

> Mr. Doyle is already onto the next thing, brisk about it, while a kid
> glances at Ruth before looking back at the board. Chloe presses her
> thumbnail into the edge of the desk until it leaves a mark.
>
> She has done this herself, in January.
>
> Chloe has a very clear idea of what is supposed to happen to a child who
> does what Ruth just did, so she watches for it the whole rest of the
> period: Mr. Doyle's face the next time Ruth's name comes up, the door on
> the way out in case somebody is waiting there to have a word, and at
> lunch, the end of the table, to see whether anybody comes over.
>
> But nothing comes, all day.
>
> That night it catches again: Ruth said it, so she sits on her hands
> through the next class, and the one after that, but answers only when Mr.
> Doyle calls on her direct.

**Shape 1, auditing instead of reacting.** Three separate checkpoints get
tracked across a full day - a teacher's face, a door, a lunch table - in
place of any actual move on Chloe's part. The thumbnail mark is a good
physical tell at the start of the passage, but everything after it is pure
surveillance of somebody else's fate, run like a small research protocol,
rather than anything Chloe does about it.

**What she does now:** watches three abstract checkpoints for a punishment
that never lands, then privately restricts her own hand-raising afterward.

**What a six-year-old would do:** not track a teacher's face and a doorway
and a lunch table like three instruments. She'd ask the person sitting
right there.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe presses her thumbnail into the edge of the desk until it leaves a
> mark. She has done this herself, in January.
>
> All through lunch she keeps one eye on the door, waiting for somebody to
> come and get Ruth. Nobody does. At the end of the table she leans over and
> asks her straight out. "Are you in trouble?"
>
> "For what," Ruth says, already onto her sandwich.
>
> That night it catches again: Ruth said it, so Chloe sits on her hands
> through the next class, and the one after that, and answers only when Mr.
> Doyle calls on her direct.

## Instance 6

**05_behind.md:101-103, age 6.**

> "Good, watch. Flip zero and you get one over zero, and one over zero has
> no answer at all, so the whole thing breaks right there. Same rule, same
> answer, and it's the one case where the rule tells you something is
> impossible," Mr. Baptiste says, doing it on the board as he says it and
> tapping the break with the marker. The bell goes before Ruth has finished
> arguing the point with him.
>
> Around them the rest of the class is already standing, chairs scraping,
> Sam's bag over his shoulder before Mr. Baptiste has finished the sentence,
> while Chloe sits in the middle of it and watches a conversation go on over
> her head about a question she asked first. Her ears stay hot for an hour
> afterward, through the break and through most of the lesson after it.

**Shape 1 with shape 4.** The question was Chloe's ("Why do you flip it"),
but Ruth takes the follow-up over completely, and Chloe's only response to
losing ownership of her own question is a physical blush that runs for an
hour. Nothing reclaims the thread, even privately.

**What she does now:** sits with hot ears for an hour and does nothing else
about a question that started as hers.

**What a six-year-old would do:** go back for it once the room empties -
write it down where it's hers again, or ask the one friend who'll listen.

**Proposed (append after "Her ears stay hot..."):**

> At the end of the lesson she stays behind at her desk until the room
> empties, and copies the zero line into the back of her own packet in her
> own words: you can't split nothing into pieces, so the rule breaks on
> purpose right there. Then she closes the packet and goes to find Sam.

## Instance 7

**05_behind.md:173-175, age 6. Top priority.**

> Chloe's own fork stops halfway to her mouth, questions backed up behind
> it: whether anybody went into the stairwell after him, whether he had to
> say it out loud to somebody before they would let him go, and how long it
> was between the saying and his mom being at the door.
>
> Owen, then the bridge, then Owen again, and the loop refuses to run
> further than that. So Chloe picks her roll apart, one piece at a time,
> lining the pieces along the edge of her tray, going back through the two
> facts: the bridge was fun, but Owen must not have liked it, which sit
> crosswise, and neither will move over for the other. Long after Ruth has
> gotten up and taken her tray, she starts from the beginning, checking them
> a third time, trying to work out how a person could stand in front of a
> bridge but want to be somewhere else.

**Shape 1, the purest case in the span.** Three full questions get
generated and none of them gets asked; the passage names its own structure
outright ("the loop refuses to run further than that") and then runs the
same two facts a third time at the same table, next to the one person - Ruth
- who already said she saw the thing Chloe most wants to know about. It is
the longest pure-audit passage across all six chapters, over the highest
stakes in the chapter: a friend who left because he was unhappy, and nobody
around the table treating that as strange.

**What she does now:** reruns the same two facts three times without moving
her mouth.

**What a six-year-old would do:** ask. Directly, out loud, of the person who
already brought it up.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe's fork stops halfway to her mouth. "Did you go in after him? In the
> stairwell?"
>
> Ruth looks up from her plate. "No. I was walking past. You don't go in
> after somebody doing that."
>
> "Why not?"
>
> "It's worse. They know you saw." Ruth goes back to pushing her food
> around.
>
> Chloe picks her roll apart anyway, one piece at a time, lining the pieces
> along the edge of her tray. The bridge was fun. Owen must not have liked
> it. The two facts still won't sit next to each other, asking or not.

## Totals and answers

**Instances found: 9** (6 full, 3 minor) across the six chapters.

**Worst chapter: 5 (Behind), age six.** It holds four of the nine instances,
including the single worst one in the span (Instance 7, the Owen loop) and
the only instance where Chloe reasons out a correct answer and never spends
it (Instance 5). It is also the chapter with the most going right elsewhere
- the fractions homework scene and the moon argument at dinner are both
fully active - which makes the audit passages read as a specific recurring
gap rather than a chapter-wide problem.

**Initiates-a-scene ratio:** counting bounded scenes across the six chapters
by section break and distinct incident (roughly seventy of them), Chloe
opens about twenty-two - **just under one in three**. It is not flat across
the span: chapter 2 (the evaluation) and chapter 6 (the closing days) sit
lowest, for opposite reasons - chapter 2 is examiner-led by its nature, and
chapter 6's own strongest Chloe material (the dinner recap, the interrogation
of Kavi about the form) is still opened by a parent's question or Ruth's
proposal rather than by Chloe. Chapters 1, 3, and 4 sit closest to even,
carried by the library counter, the letter at the table, and the phone call
that brings her mother to the hotel. Chapter 5 sits in between: high when
she argues (the moon, the flip-it question), low in the two group scenes
listed above.

**Three to fix first:**

1. **Instance 7 (05:173-175).** The purest and longest audit passage in the
   span, at the highest stakes in its chapter - a friend leaving because he
   was unhappy - and the easiest of the nine to fix without disturbing
   anything else on the page, since Ruth is already sitting right there with
   the answer.
2. **Instance 9 (06:195-201).** The closing beat of the whole section, where
   a specific, sourced worry gets swallowed whole on the last night of the
   month; fixing it costs nothing structurally, since the lights already
   give the scene a clean, external reason to cut her off rather than
   leaving the silence unexplained.
3. **Instance 3 (04:195).** The cleanest single-beat failure in the span,
   sitting at the exact hinge of the homesickness arc - the last night
   before her mother stops staying at the hotel - where a total, instant
   retreat from the one lever she was given undersells everything the
   chapter has built up to that point.
