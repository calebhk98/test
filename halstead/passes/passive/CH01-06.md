# Passivity pass, chapters 1-6

Span: 01_before.md (age 6), 02_march_4th.md (age 6), 03_the_letter.md (age 6),
04_pluto.md (age 6), 05_behind.md (age 6), 06_the_list.md (age 6). Chloe does
not turn seven anywhere in this span; every instance below is a six-year-old.

**Revision note.** This file was rewritten after the brief changed. The
earlier draft treated deliberate stillness, and a scene belonging to another
character, as exemptions, and it held up the chapter 3 staircase-listening
beats and the chapter 2 corridor wait as already meeting a standard. Both
moves are wrong under the current brief: there is no stillness exemption,
and no scene in this book is a model to match. What follows judges every
scene from first principles - what she wants, what she does to get it, what
it costs or fails at, and whether the scene ends somewhere different because
of something she did - and reports short of that bar, including several
scenes the previous draft waved through.

**Not flagged, per direct instruction on this span:** chapter 1's recess
scene (lines 67-81) and Icarus scene (lines 83-95), ruled settled and
standard. Chapter 4's corridor episode outside the Pluto door (lines
119-129, the knees-and-ears flashback set off by the room name), ruled
deliberately unwitnessed - and separately, it is not a decision Chloe makes
or declines; it is an involuntary physical reaction, outside the five
shapes on its own terms.

Nineteen instances follow.

## Instance 1

**01_before.md:5-13, age 6.**

> Chloe's mom is on the phone in the kitchen most evenings now, standing at
> the counter with a hand pressed flat against her forehead.
>
> [...]
>
> On the other side of the wall Chloe sits on the living room floor with a
> book open in front of her, listening.
>
> [...]
>
> Chloe's thumb sits on a line she stopped reading minutes ago. She turns a
> page, but her eyes drift to the wall between her and the kitchen, where
> her mom's voice is coming through.

**Shape 2, receiving a scene.** This is the book's opening scene, and it is
entirely about Chloe's future, on the far side of a wall, and she does
nothing about being shut out of it: no move toward the kitchen, no question
once the call ends. She wants to know what is happening to her and the
scene ends exactly where it started - her on the floor, pretending to read.

**What she does now:** fake-reads a line she stopped following, eyes on the
wall instead.

**What a six-year-old would do:** get up. Six-year-olds do not have the
patience to sit still through a call about them; she'd drift toward the
doorway on some pretext and get caught at it.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe's thumb sits on a line she stopped reading minutes ago. She gets up,
> book still open in one hand, and drifts as far as the kitchen doorway
> before her mom notices her there and covers the mouthpiece.
>
> "Five more minutes, baby."
>
> Chloe goes back to the couch, but she doesn't open the book again before
> the call ends.

## Instance 2

**01_before.md:99-105, age 6.**

> The board still has yesterday's spelling list chalked half erased in the
> corner. In November, Mrs. Aldana asks what clouds are made of, and Chloe
> knows the answer. Clouds are the ocean going somewhere else, and the whole
> sentence is in her mouth, her hand already up, until she looks at the back
> of Kayleigh Burns's head and takes it down to one word.
>
> "Water," she says, and Mrs. Aldana says good and moves on.
>
> The exchange is over before anybody looks up. In December, when called on
> for the water cycle, she names the stages: evaporation, condensation,
> precipitation. She hears Mrs. Aldana say exactly.
>
> The full answer comes first, every time, and she has to catch it before it
> gets out and swap it for the smaller one, the one that matches what is
> already on the board. Some days the catching is late and half a sentence
> is already out of her before she gets to it. But the short version works
> every time she uses it.

**Shape 3, declining, repeatedly**, told as a summary rather than lived
once. Two instances are dramatized and both land clean; the closing
paragraph then asserts a whole year of the same swap, including days when
"the catching is late," without showing one of those late catches or what
it costs her when it fails.

**What she does now:** the swap is total and invisible on the page - we are
told it sometimes goes wrong, never shown it.

**What a six-year-old would do:** not manage a smooth downshift every time.
A kid catching herself mid-answer catches herself audibly, with a half-word
already out.

**Proposed:**

> The full answer still comes first, and one January morning it gets
> partway out before she catches it. Asked why the moon looks different
> some nights, she starts, "Because it's not really -" and stops with her
> mouth open on the next word, her hand only halfway back down. Mrs. Aldana
> waits. Chloe closes her mouth around the rest of it and says, "it changes
> shape," and Mrs. Aldana says good.

## Instance 5

**01_before.md:165-173, age 6.**

> On the way home Chloe has her head against the cold part of the window
> with her eyes shut and her breathing kept deliberately even, so that the
> conversation in the front seat goes on over the top of her.
>
> "She was quiet tonight," her dad says.
>
> "She was fine," her mom says.
>
> "I didn't say she wasn't fine."
>
> Chloe presses closer to the cold glass and keeps her eyes shut for the
> rest of the drive.

**Shape 3, declining, by way of stillness.** She fakes sleep specifically
to hear herself discussed, then does nothing with what she hears - not
even when her mother mischaracterizes the whole evening as fine, which
Chloe herself would know is not true. The scene ends exactly where it
started.

**What she does now:** holds still and lets "she was fine" stand.

**What a six-year-old would do:** six-year-olds are bad at keeping up a
performance under provocation. Being talked about inaccurately while
"asleep" is exactly the kind of thing that breaks the act.

**Proposed:**

> "She was fine," her mom says.
>
> "I didn't say she wasn't fine."
>
> "I wasn't," Chloe says, eyes still shut, and the front seat goes quiet
> enough that she can hear the blinker before her dad turns it off without
> using it.

## Instance 6

**02_march_4th.md:227-273, age 6.**

> Then he talks to her mom, and Chloe waits in the corridor on a chair too
> tall for her feet, holding her jacket in her lap.
>
> [...]
>
> Chloe looks at her shoes and holds still, in case moving is the thing
> that makes the door close.

**Shape 3, declining, by way of stillness.** She is shut out of a
conversation that will decide the next two years of her school life, and
her one strategy for getting closer to it is to not move, on the theory
that stillness keeps the door open. It is a real six-year-old piece of
magical thinking, but it is also passivity end to end: she never tries to
actually hear more, and the door closes regardless of what she does.

**What she does now:** sits rigid, banking on a superstition, and gets
nothing extra for it.

**What a six-year-old would do:** treat the chair, not just her body, as
something she can use - inch it closer, test how much noise it makes.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe looks at her shoes, then slides the chair an inch closer to the
> gap in the door, testing whether the legs will scrape. They don't, so
> she does it again, twice, until Ben's words come through whole instead
> of in pieces. She stays there, silent, until the door opens the rest of
> the way.

## Instance 8

**02_march_4th.md:311-319, age 6. Top priority.**

> On March 5th it is the same worksheet, the problems sitting after the
> one that tells you how it works, and Bryce Dolan counting on his fingers.
> Mrs. Aldana keeps grading at her desk, and when she asks Chloe once, in
> April, whether everything is all right at home, Chloe says yes.
>
> Some days she asks to play four square and gets a round in before the
> ball goes somewhere else without her, so she sits on the wall with her
> library book until the bell.
>
> What she works out that month is bigger than the pencil trick: finish
> first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the room leaves you
> alone, the teacher included. It works in reading and in math, every day
> until the end of the year.
>
> [...]
>
> She still knows all twenty-eight of their names, but not one of them has
> needed to learn hers. By June, Chloe is quiet, she does what she's told,
> and she eats lunch by herself.

**Shape 3, declining, at book scale.** This is the chapter's closing
statement, and it is the single largest passivity claim in the span: a
whole school year of choosing invisibility, stated as strategy and
described as working, with not one scene in it where the strategy is
tested or fails. She is asked directly if things are all right and lies.
She loses a spot in a game and doesn't ask for it back. None of it costs
her anything on the page - we are told she eats alone, we never watch it
happen to her.

**What she does now:** goes fully quiet for a year and the book reports it
as clean success.

**What a six-year-old would do:** a strategy this total has bad days. A kid
running it for nine months slips at least once, and the slip should cost
her something we see rather than a fact we're handed.

**Proposed:**

> What she works out that month is bigger than the pencil trick: finish
> first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the room leaves you alone,
> the teacher included. It works in reading and in math, every day until
> the end of the year, except the afternoon in May when Mrs. Aldana asks
> the class what they want to be when they grow up and Chloe's hand is up
> before she has decided to raise it. Her answer runs four sentences longer
> than anyone else's. She sits through the rest of the period wishing she'd
> said doctor and left it there.

## Instance 9

**03_the_letter.md:205-213, age 6.**

> On the ninth night Chloe is supposed to be asleep, having already been
> sent back once, but comes out a second time in her socks, skipping the
> fourth stair.
>
> "She's been flat since Christmas, and you've seen it, because she comes
> home and she says it was good."
>
> "Kids say it was good, which is a kid being polite rather than a kid
> hiding something. Ours has always been quiet. You were quiet."
>
> "Not like that. That's a kid gone quiet, and you know the difference as
> well as I do. She used to come in the door talking."
>
> A minute goes by before her mom says something too quiet to make out
> from the top of the stairs, her dad says he knows, twice, and somebody
> starts the dishwasher. At that, Chloe goes back up the rest of the stairs
> on her hands and knees, to keep the boards quiet, and gets into bed with
> her socks still on.

**Shape 2, receiving**, with a real physical attempt (the second trip
downstairs, the sock feet, the hands-and-knees retreat) attached to a
result that changes nothing: she hears her own father confirm, out loud,
that she has gone quiet and unhappy, and does nothing with that confirmation
- doesn't come down and say so herself, doesn't ask a single question the
next morning. This is also the third time in the chapter she creeps down
these same stairs (lines 69-73 and 89-107 are the earlier two); by the
third trip the pattern is a habit the scene has stopped remarking on, which
is shape 3's own definition.

**What she does now:** gets the confirmation she needs and goes back to bed
with it.

**What a six-year-old would do:** having just heard her dad say the true
thing out loud, a kid this age uses it - comes down and says "I heard that"
rather than sitting on new leverage all night.

**Proposed:**

> A minute goes by before her mom says something too quiet to make out from
> the top of the stairs, her dad says he knows, twice, and somebody starts
> the dishwasher. Chloe comes down the rest of the way instead, in her
> socks, and stands in the kitchen doorway until her dad sees her.
>
> "I heard that part," she says. "The quiet part."
>
> Neither of them answers her right away, and it's her mom who crosses the
> kitchen first.

## Instance 16

> Chloe's own fork stops halfway to her mouth, questions backed up behind
> it: whether anybody went into the stairwell after him, whether he had to
> say it out loud to somebody before they would let him go, and how long
> it was between the saying and his mom being at the door.
>
> Owen, then the bridge, then Owen again, and the loop refuses to run

moving her mouth.

**What a six-year-old would do:** ask. Directly, out loud, of the person
who already brought it up.
