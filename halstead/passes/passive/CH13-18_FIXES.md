# Passivity pass, second pass: chapters 13-18

Span: 13_ten_pages.md (age 10), 14_sixty_degrees.md (age 11), 15_twelve.md (age 12),
16_thirteen.md (age 13), 17_fourteen.md (age 14), 18_fifteen.md (age 15).

Starting count from CH13-18.md: 21 instances. Kept and fixed: 15. Dropped: 6,
one of them defended rather than merely cut.

Worst chapter by instances changed: **chapter 17 (Fourteen)**, four fixes
(Instances 4, 15, 16, 20), edging out chapter 18's four (5, 17, 18, 21) because
17's four are spread across four different relationships (Ruth, Ruth again,
Pruitt, a stranger at the sinks) rather than concentrated on one antagonist.
The single highest-cost fix, independent of chapter, is Instance 21 below.

---

## The worst instance

**Instance 21, 18_fifteen.md, the Aurel confrontation.** This is the scene the
original brief held up as the standard and the author has since rejected
outright. The old version gave Chloe a real want (make it stop) and a real
public attempt (telling Iyad to stop at the table), then let the whole thing
evaporate: he asks a worse question, the table laughs, and she "eats the rest
of her breakfast without tasting any of it" while the text a paragraph earlier
already states the rumor "is still going in the summer" regardless. A
confrontation that changes nothing about the thing it was fighting, in the
chapter that is supposed to prove she has learned to push, is where the
passivity costs the book the most: it isn't just an instance, it's the
instance the brief pointed to as proof the book had already solved this, and
it hadn't.

## The best instance, defended

**Instance 9, 14_sixty_degrees.md:193-197, Chloe is eleven** (grandmother,
envelope, "once in a whole year is hardly a lot"). Dropped from the fix list,
not because it is minor, but because it already does what it is there to do.
Chloe corrects the number once, out loud, plainly: "October was the first one,
but I've done it a dozen times since then, more than a dozen." Her grandmother
hears it and still reads it her own way. That is the whole point of the
beat: it is not testing whether Chloe can assert herself (chapter 14 already
tests that harder, twice, in the same chapter), it is establishing how this
one relationship works, across the span, alongside the "there's a chair"
scene in 13 and the hand-reading scene in 18. Escalating an eleven-year-old
into a second round with her own grandmother over the angle of an envelope,
when nothing in the plot turns on the record being corrected, would spend a
confrontation on a relationship that is supposed to read as warm and settled,
not adversarial. The four-part test asks whether the scene ends somewhere
different because of what she did; here the answer is no, and that is
correct, because the scene was never about her getting a different outcome
from her grandmother. It is about the reader learning that this is what her
grandmother is like, and Chloe already knows it, which is why she does not
push twice.
