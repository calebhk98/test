**Revision note:** this file was rewritten after the brief changed. The
stillness exemption is gone, and no scene in the book is being used as a
model. Everything previously excused as "earned stillness" — including
chapter 35's whole three-quiet-incidents structure, and the two scenes the
original brief pointed to as the standard — has been re-read against the
corrected definition and logged where it qualifies. The count below is much

## Chapter 35, re-judged

Told to judge this chapter against the corrected definition: the
three-quiet-incidents structure is exactly the pattern being complained
about, and the fact that the group's inaction is argued and costed on the
page does not exempt it. It is now the chapter with the single most explicit
instance in the span (Instance 35-4, below) — the group naming its own
policy of doing nothing, three times over, without a single character
proposing an alternative that has to be argued down. See the full instance
list below; this chapter is no longer cleared.

## Cross-cutting pattern: the tic instead of the reaction

Before the per-chapter list: the same physical gesture recurs across the
span as the default substitute for an actual response to news — a two-finger
tap against a desk or counter (32:77, 34:5, 34:117, 35:7, 35:57), a pen or
cable turned over and set back exactly where it was picked up (32:13,
33:89, 34:5), a held stillness timed in seconds (31:87, 34:67, 34:91,
36:29). Each single instance reads as characterization. Stacked across six
chapters it is the mechanism by which "receiving a scene" gets dressed up as
business rather than logged as nothing happening — a character is given a
hand to occupy so the page doesn't sit empty, and the hand does the work an
actual decision should be doing. This is not itself one of the five shapes,
but it is the delivery vehicle for several of the instances below (32-1,
34-3, 35-1, 35-4, 36-1), and it is worth the author's attention as its own
line item: not zero, per house rule, but currently the reflex the prose
reaches for every time someone would otherwise have to choose.

## Chapter 31 (Ruth, POV — viewpoint character throughout; Chloe is 18-20
across the chapter's span)

### 31-1 — deferring an escalation she has already framed as urgent

**chapters/31_ruth.md:19**

> The mistake, she decides, is bigger than one office can fix, something
> upstream of advising and the registrar both. It goes in the folder under
> problems she'll solve once she works out who actually owns them.

**Shape:** 3/5. She names a specific plan (find who owns the mistake) and
then takes no step toward it inside the chapter. The folder is where the
plan goes to wait, not where it starts.

**What she does now / what she'd do:** Files it for later. Someone who has
just spent three weeks learning the building's stairwells and door-locking
habits would spend one afternoon on the next lead, not table the whole
question.

**Proposed:**

> The mistake, she decides, is bigger than one office can fix, something
> upstream of advising and the registrar both. That afternoon she emails the
> department chair directly and gets an out-of-office reply back within the
> hour. She prints that too, and it goes in the folder with the rest, dated.

### 31-2 — reading silence as proof rather than checking it

**chapters/31_ruth.md:21**

> When a preceptor hands back a problem set with a perfect score and no
> comment, she reads the blank space where the comment should be as
> confirmation: a correct answer earns silence, and so does a class already
> pitched at the right level for the room. The sequence will catch up to her
> eventually, she tells herself... The reading stays two weeks ahead so
> she'll have something to do once it does.

**Shape:** 1. A silence gets interpreted rather than tested, and the
interpretation is convenient. This is the single most repeated engine of the
chapter.

**What she does now / what she'd do:** Assumes. Someone this literal
elsewhere in the same chapter (the fifth-grade question, the "off the point"
directness with Devansh) would ask the preceptor outright rather than build
a theory off his silence.

**Proposed:**

> When a preceptor hands back a problem set with a perfect score and no
> comment, she catches him at the door of the section room and asks him flat
> out whether a class this easy is normal for the room or just for her. He
> says he hasn't graded enough of these yet to know, and goes back to his
> stack. She reads the shrug as confirmation anyway, and the reading stays
> two weeks ahead so she'll have something to do once it does.
