# Passivity pass: chapters 7-12

*Report only. No chapter edited.*

**Revised.** The first version of this file was written under a brief that
exempted deliberate stillness and pointed at chapter 12's bridge scene as a
model. Both are gone. The author: *"Deliberate stillness IS a form of
passivity. It's super common, annoyingly too much imo,"* and, on the standard
scenes, *"I have yet to read a good chapter anywhere here that is a good
standard for the fix."* Everything below was re-read against the current
`passes/PASSIVITY_BRIEF.md`: watching, waiting, declining, holding back,
choosing silence, and registering something accurately and moving on are all
logged now, with no exemption for a quiet ending or a well-written moment.
Each instance is judged from first principles — does she visibly want
something, does she do something toward it, does the attempt cost her or fail
or create the next problem, does the scene end somewhere different because of
what she did — not against any scene in this book.

**Still out of scope, on separate authority, not this brief's stillness
question:** the Ruth/languages scene (`chapters/12_nine.md:126-127`) and the
named state "which she finds restful" (`chapters/12_nine.md:167`) are ruled
deliberate by the author directly, outside this pass; the "finishes it,
checks it, waits" motif is addressed on its own terms at instance 9 below
rather than silently excluded. The chapter 12 Iyad/Mandarin scene
(`chapters/12_nine.md:121-123`) already has a full write-up and proposed fix
in `passes/BEX_AND_IYAD.md` and is counted here but not re-drafted, to avoid
filing two competing proposals for the same lines.

Every quotation below is copied from the chapter file and checked against it
a second time.

## Chapter 7 (age seven)

### 1. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:29,33` — the last name

> Chloe's mouth opens, then stays open a beat too long. "A number was extra."
> Her mom is still waiting for it. "I always just asked him myself, in
> person."
>
> [...]
>
> Chloe opens her mouth, then closes it, and goes upstairs to sit on the end
> of her bed and try to remember.

**Shape:** 2/3. Asked a plain question she can't answer, she says nothing at
all and leaves the room, rather than say "I don't know" out loud.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Closes her mouth and
disappears mid-conversation. A child this talkative elsewhere (she narrates
half of Sam's biography unprompted two pages later) is more likely to say she
doesn't know, out loud, before going to work it out.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe opens her mouth, then closes it. "I don't know his last name," she
> says, to the counter, and goes upstairs anyway to sit on the end of her bed
> and try to remember it herself.

### 2. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:53` — the phone call

> Somebody at the school picks up on the second ring, and Chloe gets close,
> straining to hear. Her mom gives their name, then Chloe's, confirms yes,
> that's her, and settles into a long stretch of listening, her weight going
> onto one foot and then the other.

**Shape:** 2. Pure reception. She hovers and strains to hear but takes no
part in the call that is nominally being made for her.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Hovers silently. A child this
invested would try to get a word into the call, not just get close to it.

**Proposed:**

> Somebody at the school picks up on the second ring, and Chloe gets close,
> tugging once at her mom's sleeve to make sure Ruth's name goes in as well as
> Sam's. Her mom gives both names, then Chloe's, confirms yes, that's her,
> and settles into a long stretch of listening, one hand coming back to rest
> on Chloe's head to keep her from tugging again.

### 3. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:63-65` — waiting for the phone

> Chloe waits for the phone, day after day.
>
> She takes her book down and reads it in the kitchen, in the chair that puts
> the phone in her sightline, and she is up and across the floor before the
> first ring finishes if she is anywhere near it. [...] on the ninth day her
> mom tells her that these things can take weeks.

**Shape:** 2. The whole passage organizes itself around waiting; even the
anticipatory seating is in service of pure reception.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Positions herself to intercept
calls that are never hers, then keeps waiting. A child this impatient
elsewhere would try to shorten the wait rather than just sit closer to it.

**Proposed:** add after "these things can take weeks."

> On the tenth day she asks her mom to call the school back and check whether
> they actually reached Ruth's family or only said they would, and her mom
> makes the call while Chloe sits on the counter swinging her legs to watch.

### 4. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:147,149` — the library books

> Having taken the third library book to bed on the Monday, she gets a page
> and a half in, reads the same sentence twice, and starts the page over with
> no memory of what it said the first time.
>
> On the Tuesday she opens it where she stopped, reads the top of the page,
> reads it again, gives up, and puts it down on the floor. On the Wednesday
> she leaves it there.

**Shape:** 2. The decline is shown entirely as failed attempts with no
decision point and no word to anyone.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Quietly gives up, day by day.
A child this age more often tries something else first — reading aloud,
switching books — before a book is simply abandoned on the floor.

**Proposed:**

> On the Tuesday she opens it where she stopped, reads the top of the page,
> reads it again, gives up, and puts it down on the floor. On the Wednesday
> she tries reading it out loud to her stuffed rabbit propped against the
> pillow, gets through half a paragraph, then stops, and the book stays open
> on the floor where it fell.

### 5. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:189` — the doorway

> Coming down for water, Chloe gets the end of a call, and the sentence she
> arrives on has two pleases in it. Her mom is at the counter with one hand
> flat on it, and Chloe stops in the doorway with the glass still empty.

**Shape:** 2. She registers an unusual, charged detail and does nothing with
it: doesn't approach, doesn't ask, just stands there until she is sent to
bed several paragraphs later.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Watches silently from the
doorway. A child this size would more plausibly cross the kitchen for the
water she came down for in the first place, rather than freeze in the frame.

**Proposed:** add immediately after the quoted sentence.

> Chloe crosses the kitchen anyway and fills the glass at the tap, loud
> enough that her mom's free hand lifts an inch off the counter without her
> turning round, the sign for not yet.

### 6. `chapters/07_the_same_room.md:209,217` — the crying scene

> The answer, whatever it is, refuses to come out of her.
>
> [...]
>
> Chloe moves her head from side to side with it still down on her arms.

**Shape:** 2. Direct, caring questions get headshakes and silence; the scene
ends with nothing decided or offered.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Produces no answer at all,
verbal or otherwise specific. Even wordless, a child this distressed usually
manages one concrete gesture toward the actual trouble.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe moves her head from side to side with it still down on her arms, one
> hand coming out from underneath to push the math sheet an inch across the
> desk, toward Ms. Vance, without lifting her face to see if she takes it.

## Chapter 8 (age seven)

### 7. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:5,9` — Ms. Vance's call

> Ms. Vance calls the house on the Friday, which Chloe hears from the stairs,
> though most of it is her mom listening and saying she knows, again and
> again, to things Chloe can't make out from where she is sitting.
>
> [...]
>
> Chloe goes back up before the call is finished.

**Shape:** 2. She positions herself to overhear a call about herself and
leaves before it resolves, asking nothing before or after.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Retreats mid-call. A child
this curious would come closer to hear better rather than withdraw from a
conversation about her.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe comes down two more steps to try to catch her mother's half of it,
> and stays on the stairs until the call actually ends.

### 8. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:45,49,81` — Dr. Ammons

> Every question goes to her mother, and Chloe sits through it.
>
> "Were the same adults with her the whole time, or did they change over?"
>
> Chloe's hand stops on the thread.
>
> [...]
>
> "Okay, whatever you think is best," Chloe says, and then the wall of the
> building slides sideways past her window.

**Shape:** 1/2. A question that is actually about her safety passes entirely
through her mother; her only recorded response is a hand going still. Later,
offered a real say in her own treatment, she hands the whole decision back.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Freezes at the one question
that's actually about her, and says nothing; later, defers completely rather
than ask for any say at all. A child pressed this directly, even once, tends
to answer for herself instead of letting the answer be supplied.

**Proposed:** after "Chloe's hand stops on the thread."

> "The same," her mom says.
>
> "The same," Chloe says too, to the couch arm, a half beat after her mother,
> loud enough that Dr. Ammons's pen pauses on the pad.

And replace the "whatever you think is best" line:

> "Do I get a say in it, though, or is 'a few weeks' just a thing you already
> told her?" Chloe says, and only lets it go when her mother says the day is
> still hers to move if she hates it. Then the wall of the building slides
> sideways past her window.

### 9. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:119,121` — Mrs. Prahl's packet, first pass

> Chloe has it before Mrs. Prahl has finished reading it out, so she waits,
> because you wait. [...]
>
> There are twelve on the sheet, of which Chloe does most in the first
> stretch before she makes herself slow down, because the twelfth is the last
> one, and there won't be a thirteenth.

**Shape:** 3. Two separate, stated acts of self-imposed restraint: holding an
answer back on principle, then deliberately throttling her own pace to make
a packet last.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Silently rations herself
twice. A genuinely bored seven-year-old with a fast answer more often blurts
it, or bargains for more, than quietly manages her own supply.

**Proposed:** add after "and there won't be a thirteenth."

> Halfway down the last page she puts her hand up anyway. "Can I have a
> thirteenth one if I finish? I won't tell anybody how fast." Mrs. Prahl says
> she'll see what's in the cupboard, and Chloe finishes the last three
> problems considerably faster than the first nine.

### 10. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:133-139` — three days of the same question

> "Is it today? Can we call them today?" she asks in the car on the Monday.
>
> "We're still thinking about it, sweetheart."
>
> Chloe asks again on the Tuesday and gets the words back in the same order,
> as if her mom has them memorized too now.
>
> "Not now, Chloe," her mom says on the Wednesday, before she has gotten the
> question all the way out.

**Shape:** 3/4. The identical question, the identical deflection, three days
running, with no escalation or change of approach until an unrelated fight
finally breaks it open on Friday.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Repeats herself verbatim for
three days. A child would more plausibly try a different target or a
different question by day two.

**Proposed:** add after "before she has gotten the question all the way out."

> On the Thursday she skips her mom entirely and asks her dad at breakfast,
> low, while her mom is still upstairs. Her dad looks at the door to the hall
> before he answers, and says only that it isn't only up to him.

### 11. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:169,171` — the fourth packet

> On the fourth Thursday there's a new packet, which Chloe takes home,
> finishes in about half an hour, but keeps to herself, so that it sits in
> the bottom of her bag all week under the folder for reading. When Mrs.
> Prahl asks the following Thursday how she got on with it, Chloe says she's
> still working on it, and Mrs. Prahl says that's absolutely fine, take your
> time.
>
> She can't work out what's wrong with it, because it's faster than her
> class, everybody keeps telling her it's the fast group, Mrs. Prahl is nice
> to her every single week, and there are nine of them in that room instead
> of twenty-six. Chloe wants to say that this is the wrong thing, out loud,
> to Mrs. Prahl, on a Thursday, in front of the others, but the rest of the
> sentence stays stuck behind that one, and she is fairly sure the first half
> already sounds like she thinks she's better than everybody, a thing she has
> learned to keep to herself.

**Shape:** 1. A full audit of why speaking up would sound bad, ending in a
direct lie to a kind adult about her own progress. This is also the exact
hazard `HOUSE_RULES.md` names: the reasoning is laid out for the reader
instead of one thing happening.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Hides the packet, lies about
it, reasons herself into silence. The same girl argues her father to a
standstill over hot dogs three chapters later; a bored, capable
seven-year-old is at least as likely to act out at the material as to reason
her way into silence about it.

**Proposed:**

> On the fourth Thursday there's a new packet, which Chloe takes home and
> finishes in about half an hour, standing every answer on its head so the
> sheet only reads right held up to the lamp. She puts it in the bottom of
> her bag under the folder for reading. When Mrs. Prahl asks the following
> Thursday how she got on with it, Chloe hands it over without a word and
> watches her turn it around twice before she works out what she is looking
> at.
>
> Mrs. Prahl laughs once, out loud, and writes something on the corner of it.
> Chloe still cannot say the actual sentence, the one about this being the
> wrong thing entirely, not with nine people in the room and Mrs. Prahl this
> nice about all of it, so she keeps that part exactly where it was.

### 12. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:199,201` — "nobody was mean"

> Her mom says it carefully. "Sweetheart, I need you to tell me the truth. Is
> somebody bullying you at school?"
>
> "No," Chloe says, shaking her head against the pillow.
>
> "You can tell me if somebody is. I wouldn't go to the school with it unless
> you said so."
>
> "No," Chloe says again, to the wall this time. "Nobody at that school is
> bullying me."

**Shape:** 3. Technically true, and twice offered as the whole answer, when a
truer and more complicated one (a boy calling her weird for the time
capsule, a program too easy to say so about) sits right behind it.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Answers the narrow, literal
question and stops. A child this vocal elsewhere might hand over the
adjacent true thing unprompted, since she's shown still carrying it in
October.

**Proposed:** add after the second "No,"

> "Nobody at that school is bullying me." She works a thread loose from the
> pillowcase with one finger. "Bryce just thinks I talk weird. That's not the
> same as bullying."
>
> Her mom's hand goes still on Chloe's shirt. "What does Bryce say, exactly?"
>
> "Never mind. It's not the thing you're asking about."

### 13. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:215` — cousin Dana

> Her cousin Dana asks how school is, and Chloe says it's good. Dana asks
> whether she's still reading all the time, but Chloe says some. Then Dana
> turns to talk to somebody else, so Chloe eats her potatoes.

**Shape:** 2. Two direct questions, two minimal deflections, no attempt to
correct the impression or hold the conversation open.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Gives the smallest possible
true-ish answers and lets the exchange dissolve. This is a kid shown talking
at length to anyone she thinks is actually listening; going flat with Dana is
a choice worth showing, not just cutting away from.

**Proposed:**

> Her cousin Dana asks how school is, and Chloe says it's good. Dana asks
> whether she's still reading all the time, but Chloe says some. Last
> Thanksgiving Dana asked the same two questions in the same order and
> stopped there too. Then Dana turns to talk to somebody else, so Chloe eats
> her potatoes.

### 14. `chapters/08_the_asking.md:223` — the envelope

> The envelope is in the top drawer under her socks, where it has been since
> August, and she gets it out sometimes, holds it, and reads the part with
> her name typed across the front of it, then puts it back under the socks.

## Chapter 9 (age seven)

### 15. `chapters/09_february.md:5` — the whole opening paragraph

> February goes by with the telephone silent, and the sheet in the morning is
> the same sheet as Monday's with different numbers in it, so she finishes
> it, checks it, and sits behind it with her hands in her lap for the rest of
> the period. At lunch she carries her tray past empty seats to get to the

**Shape:** 2. An entire day rendered as pure endurance — finish, check, sit,
isolate, color with exaggerated care — with no decision anywhere in it.

**Note:** the "finishes it, checks it, sits/waits" phrasing echoes a motif
`DO_NOT_FLAG.md` calls partly deliberate on its return specifically in
chapter 7. This chapter 9 occurrence is not the one that ruling addressed, so
it is logged here rather than silently folded into that exemption; the
author can rule on it directly.

**What she does now / what a child would do:** Performs each task
competently and withdraws further. A low mood at this age more often shows
up as something disruptive or attention-seeking, not uniformly careful
compliance.

**Proposed:** add after "stopping exactly there."

> On the Thursday she colours one petal red on purpose, the only wrong colour
> on the page, and hands it in without a word about it.

**Shape:** 2. Asked to define her own state, she has nothing, and the scene

**Proposed:**

### 28. `chapters/12_nine.md:113-119` — the bridge, judged from first principles

> Bex Alcantar arrives at the end of your sentence before you get there and
> finishes it for you, and the ending she puts on it is usually the ending it
> was going to have.
>
> In the second term Chloe and Bex build a bridge that carries nineteen
> pounds and comes down a plank at a time: the joints, a fortnight Chloe
> spent getting them wrong in the hour before dinner. The teacher asks how
> they held. Bex has it before Chloe has her mouth open, the whole fortnight
> in order and every part of it right, and she says we in her first sentence
> but I in the rest of them. The teacher writes it on the sheet and moves
> along the table.
>
> Chloe goes back over the fortnight that evening looking for the sitting
> where she said any of it out loud, and finds the Thursday, when she had it
> half built and was talking fast and had it wrong twice before she had it
> right, with Bex at the next bench. Then she gets her head under the pillow
> and runs the whole thing again the way it should have gone, out loud into
> the mattress, the version where she says the joints were hers and the
> teacher hears her say it.
>
> She says none of it to Bex. On the Monday Bex catches her in the corridor
> and says good job about the bridge, warmly, meaning it, and Chloe says
> thanks, and the word comes out thin.

**Shape:** 5, as clean a case as the span has. Run against the four tests
directly:

- *Wants something visibly:* yes, credit for the joints, made explicit by the
  audit and the pillow rehearsal.
- *Does something to get it:* no. Everything after the audit is spoken into a
  mattress. Nothing leaves the room. This is the named shape exactly — "there
  is a version of this where she does Y," and the book gets credit for it.
- *Costs her something, fails, or creates the next problem:* the only cost is
  a private, invisible thinness in one word of dialogue. Nothing in the world
  registers that anything happened.
- *Ends somewhere different because of what she did:* no. If she had stayed
  in her room that evening, Monday's corridor exchange is identical. The
  scene the plot would reach either way.

This fails all four, which is a harder failure than most instances in this
span: it substitutes a fully written, emotionally convincing non-action for
an action.

**What she does now / what a child that age would do:** Performs the
correction to a mattress and thanks the person who took it. A nine-year-old
who has rehearsed a version this fully usually says a version of it to
somebody, however badly aimed, however late, however useless once said.

**Proposed:** keep the audit and the pillow scene exactly as written — the
noticing is real and worth keeping — and follow it with an actual, spoken
attempt that can cost her something in the world:

> She says none of it to Bex. On the Monday Bex catches her in the corridor
> and says good job about the bridge, warmly, meaning it, and Chloe says
> thanks, and the word comes out thin.
>
> At dinner she tells Ruth the whole fortnight over again, in the right order
> this time, out loud, the way she practised it. Ruth says she should have
> said so to the teacher on the day, not to Ruth on a Monday, and goes back to
> her plate. Chloe doesn't tell the teacher either, not that week and not the
> one after, but she stops saying we out loud about anything of hers, to
> anybody, for the rest of the year.

This keeps the loss (she never gets the credit corrected, with Bex or the
teacher) while giving her a real spoken attempt, a real and unhelpful
response, and one lasting, external, attributable change.
