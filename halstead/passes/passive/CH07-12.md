## Instance 1 — chapter 7, age seven (`chapters/07_the_same_room.md:135-141`)

**Passage, quoted exactly:**

> Chloe's hand comes down over what she has already written, the whole page
> gone under her palm.
>
> "I just meant it's a long time to wait for anybody to actually read it," she
> says, to the desk.
>
> "Yeah," Bryce says, and turns back around.
>
> Her hand stays where it is for the rest of the period. But when Ms. Vance
> calls time she scratches out the lines underneath it until the pencil almost
> goes through the paper, then, while Ms. Vance is over at the cabinet with the
> tape and looking up for none of it, writes I hope you like fifth grade
> instead, folds it in half twice, and puts it in the box.

**Which shape:** Shape 2, receiving a scene, shading into shape 3. Two
classmates call her weird for the letter she is writing to her older self; her
only recorded response is to erase the real one and replace it with something
safe, then hide it in a sealed box for three years. Nothing survives the
scene. Ask what she decides: nothing, except to disappear.

**What she does now:** Total capitulation. The genuine, characteristically
overlong thought gets scratched out; the substitute is one flat, compliant
sentence, and it goes into the box exactly like everyone else's.

**What a child that age would do:** A seven-year-old stung like this rarely
goes all the way to erasing herself. She keeps something back, out of spite or
face-saving, even while outwardly folding. A scrap kept in a pocket, a mark
made somewhere the box won't see, is well inside what a seven-year-old does
with a moment like this.

**Proposed:**

> Chloe's hand comes down over what she has already written, the whole page
> gone under her palm.
>
> "I just meant it's a long time to wait for anybody to actually read it," she
> says, to the desk.
>
> "Yeah," Bryce says, and turns back around.
>
> Her hand stays where it is for the rest of the period. When Ms. Vance calls
> time she scratches out the lines underneath it until the pencil almost goes
> through the paper, then writes I hope you like fifth grade. Before she folds
> it she tears a strip off the bottom of the page where nothing is written,
> folds that separately into a square small enough to close inside her fist,
> and puts it in her pocket, not the box.

## Instance 2 — chapter 8, age seven (`chapters/08_the_asking.md:169-171`)

**Passage, quoted exactly:**

> On the fourth Thursday there's a new packet, which Chloe takes home,
> finishes in about half an hour, but keeps to herself, so that it sits in the
> bottom of her bag all week under the folder for reading. When Mrs. Prahl
> asks the following Thursday how she got on with it, Chloe says she's still
> working on it, and Mrs. Prahl says that's absolutely fine, take your time.
>
> She can't work out what's wrong with it, because it's faster than her class,
> everybody keeps telling her it's the fast group, Mrs. Prahl is nice to her
> every single week, and there are nine of them in that room instead of
> twenty-six. Chloe wants to say that this is the wrong thing, out loud, to
> Mrs. Prahl, on a Thursday, in front of the others, but the rest of the
> sentence stays stuck behind that one, and she is fairly sure the first half
> already sounds like she thinks she's better than everybody, a thing she has
> learned to keep to herself.

**Which shape:** Shape 1, auditing instead of reacting, textbook. She runs the
entire case for why the program is wrong (too easy, everyone kind, the room
too small to matter) and the entire case for why saying so would sound bad,
and the audit itself is the whole response. She then lies to an adult about
her own progress rather than say either half out loud. This is also, this
chapter, the specific hazard `HOUSE_RULES.md` calls "spelling out a logical
joint" — the two clauses of her reasoning are laid out for the reader instead
of one thing happening.

**What she does now:** Hides the packet, tells Mrs. Prahl a specific untruth
about it, and talks herself out of the one honest sentence entirely inside her
own head.

**What a child that age would do:** This is the same Chloe who, one chapter
earlier, hides a diary page rather than confront a bully, and, three chapters
on, wins a four-day argument with her father about hot dogs. A bored, capable
seven-year-old is at least as likely to act out at the material as to reason
her way into silence about it: do it wrong on purpose, do it backward, do
something to make the mismatch visible without having to say the sentence she
has already decided not to say.

**Proposed:**

> On the fourth Thursday there's a new packet, which Chloe takes home and
> finishes in about half an hour, standing every answer on its head so the
> sheet only reads right held up to the lamp. She puts it in the bottom of her
> bag under the folder for reading. When Mrs. Prahl asks the following
> Thursday how she got on with it, Chloe hands it over without a word and
> watches her turn it around twice before she works out what she is looking
> at.
>
> Mrs. Prahl laughs once, out loud, and writes something on the corner of it.
> Chloe still cannot say the actual sentence, the one about this being the
> wrong thing entirely, not with nine people in the room and Mrs. Prahl this
> nice about all of it, so she keeps that part exactly where it was.

## Instance 3 — chapter 11, age eight (`chapters/11_eight.md:153`)

**Passage, quoted exactly:**

> Team sports is where the teams are assigned, and she gets a team with two
> people on it she has already decided against, but plays out September and
> October on it.

**Which shape:** Shape 3, declining, repeatedly, stretched past the point the
brief allows ("once is character, four times in a chapter is a habit"). She
has already made the judgment (decided against these two teammates) in
September and does nothing with it for two full months on the page. It is
eventually resolved, at `chapters/11_eight.md:175` in the same chapter, when
she asks Mrs. Okonkwo to swap teams, so the pattern does get an active ending;
the gap between deciding and acting is simply too long left unmarked.

**What she does now:** Nothing shown between the September verdict and the
February office visit. Nine words carry two months of enduring people she has
already judged.

**What a child that age would do:** Say something, badly, to somebody who
cannot fix it, well before the eventual right channel occurs to her. A kid
who has already privately decided a teammate is a problem usually complains
about it out loud long before she finds the actual office to complain to.

**Proposed:** Add one clause where the verdict is first reached, so the two
months of silence are not simply absent:

> Team sports is where the teams are assigned, and she gets a team with two
> people on it she has already decided against, which she says to Ruth once
> at dinner in September and then not again, and plays out the rest of
> September and October on it anyway.

## Instance 4 (minor) — chapter 9, age seven (`chapters/09_february.md:163-165`)

**Passage, quoted exactly:**

> Out the kitchen window, her mother is still sitting in the car with the
> engine off and the lights with it, but she has yet to open the door on her
> side.
>
> Chloe watches her through the window, until her dad says something from the
> other room about whether anybody wants toast, and she goes.

**Which shape:** Shape 2, receiving a scene. Chloe notices something plainly
wrong (her mother sitting motionless in a dark, engine-off car after the
argument earlier in the chapter about the campus house) and the scene simply
ends when somebody else calls her away.

**What she does now:** Watches, then leaves when redirected. No word, no
question, no move toward the door.

**What a child that age would do:** At minimum, ask about it out loud rather
than drift off toward toast. This is a lower-priority instance than 1-3: it
closes the chapter, the stillness could be read as the intended note to end
on, and the fix below is small on purpose.

**Proposed:**

> Out the kitchen window, her mother is still sitting in the car with the
> engine off and the lights with it, but she has yet to open the door on her
> side.
>
> Chloe watches her through the window, then calls through to ask why Mom is
> still sitting out there. Her dad says something about toast without
> answering that, so she asks it again, louder, and this time he comes and
> looks out the window himself before he answers her at all.
