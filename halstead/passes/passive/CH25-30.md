# Passivity pass, chapters 25-30

Span covers 25 (Sam, POV), 26 (Sam, POV), 27 (Nadia, POV), 28 (Chloe, 19), 29
(Theo, POV — flagged below, this is not one of the three the brief named), 30
(Chloe, 20).

## Chapters 25, 26, 27, 29: no instances

Read in full against the five shapes. None of the four produced a passage
that met the bar, and the reason is the same in each case: the viewpoint
character pushes.

**25/26 (Sam).** Sam argues with a captain about what his own qualification
score actually measures, corrects his own squadmates' bearing rather than
just pointing at the map, holds a pack open for a second weigh-in when
accused of cheating it, and tells a major to his face that the exercise gave
him too little contact. He is wrong more than once (the culvert, the tone he
should have stood still for) and the book lets him be wrong, but every wrong
call is a call he made, not one he failed to make. Nothing here is passive;
if anything the fourth-shape ratio (below) runs the other way; a soldier
answering to drill sergeants and a captain is a structural fact of the
setting, not the character declining to act.

**27 (Nadia).** The most active viewpoint character in the span by a wide
margin. She deletes the fraudulent accounts before she sits down, drives out
alone to the scam office, states her terms standing in a room with four men
who close a door behind her, and builds and then re-tunes her own hiring
pipeline over a year. No instance.

**29 (Theo).** Worth flagging as a finding on its own: this chapter is Theo's
viewpoint, not Chloe's, and the brief's note about 25/26/27 needing the
"whoever it belongs to" reading applies here too. Theo is the still one in
this chapter, deliberately: he reads a file on his own school implicating an
"unidentified adult" purge and a home-invasion drill that ends with children
grading the intruders' response time, and for two days he does nothing
visible about it. But the stillness is earned exactly the way the brief
describes for chapter 12's bridge and chapter 18's rumour scene: it costs him
(hand over his mouth, the folder that has to go back in a drawer because
leaving it open is worse, the phone call to his grandmother where neither of
them says the real thing), and it ends in an action, however guarded: he
drafts and deletes several versions of a question before he sends the one
that finally goes out to the group. This is the standard working correctly.
No instance. (Chapter 29's "Bloods drawn at the debrief" paragraph is
untouched and not part of this review, per the protection notice.)

## Chapter 28 (Chloe, 19): 3 instances

### Instance 1

**chapters/28_nineteen.md:23**

> Twice before her second week is out, she volunteers to do the filing for
> the whole floor. Deb, across the desk from her, watches her carry an empty
> tray back to the shared bin a third time. "Did somebody actually ask you to
> do that," she says, not unkindly, "or did you just decide?" She'd assigned
> it to herself, so she stops offering.

**Shape:** 3, declining. Deb hands her a genuine question about her own
behaviour and Chloe's whole answer is to stop. Nothing on the page shows her
taking the correction; it is reported as an instant, frictionless switch.

**What she does now / what a nineteen-year-old would do:** She just stops.
A nineteen-year-old caught over-helping by someone she wants to think well of
her goes red before she has a comeback ready, gives a flat answer she
half-regrets, and privately keeps score of whether the thing she stopped
doing actually gets done by somebody else.

**Proposed:**

> Twice before her second week is out, she volunteers to do the filing for
> the whole floor. Deb, across the desk from her, watches her carry an empty
> tray back to the shared bin a third time. "Did somebody actually ask you to
> do that," she says, not unkindly, "or did you just decide?" Chloe's face
> goes warm before she has an answer ready. "I decided," she says, and sets
> the tray down harder than she means to. She does not offer again that
> week, but for the rest of the month she counts, without telling anyone, how
> many days go by before a tray sits unfiled long enough for somebody else to
> notice.

### Instance 2

**chapters/28_nineteen.md:33**

> When a project runs long and Deb has to leave sharp for a game, she says so
> plainly, packs her bag while she's still finishing the sentence, and is out
> the door. Chloe is usually still working out whether she was supposed to
> offer to cover the rest.

**Shape:** 5, the considered non-action, stated. The sentence narrates an
unresolved deliberation as though the deliberation itself were the beat. It
recurs ("usually") without ever landing on a decision either way.

**What she does now / what a nineteen-year-old would do:** She stands there
turning the question over, chapter after chapter, and the book moves on
before she answers it. A nineteen-year-old who has just started noticing this
about herself picks a rule and tests it, badly at first, and adjusts.

**Proposed:**

> When a project runs long and Deb has to leave sharp for a game, she says so
> plainly, packs her bag while she's still finishing the sentence, and is out
> the door. The first two times Chloe stays at the stack until it is finished
> herself, filing the last page after the building has gone quiet around her.
> The third time she stops at nine sheets, takes the rest home in a folder,
> finishes it at the kitchen counter, and has it back on Deb's desk before
> Deb sits down the next morning. On the inside cover of her own notebook she
> keeps a running count of the nights, six by December, not for Deb to see
> and not for anyone else either.

### Instance 3 (continues into chapter 30 — see below)

**chapters/28_nineteen.md:67**

> Once more through his message, then she closes the tab. The truth is what
> she has told him. It's just arithmetic.

Full passage for context (lines 61-67):

> A man emails in February to ask, politely, whether she has a research
> team. He's specific about it: he cites pieces by title and asks how many
> people it takes to turn around something that long twice a week alongside
> a full-time job. The reason he gives is that he genuinely can't work out
> the arithmetic.
>
> Her reply says she doesn't. It's her. Six thousand words is about a day's
> work, and she was doing ten of these a week at school on top of everything
> else, so this is less than she's used to.
>
> He doesn't reply.
>
> Once more through his message, then she closes the tab. The truth is what
> she has told him. It's just arithmetic.

**Shape:** 1, auditing instead of reacting, with a flavor of 2. Something
odd arrives, a stranger unusually specific about her operation who goes
silent the moment she gives him a straight, checkable answer, and Chloe's
entire response is to re-read it, decide her own answer was correct, and
close the tab. She never turns the same scrutiny on him that she turns on a
treaty text. Given what this book has already shown happening to
Halstead-trained people who attract this exact shape of attention (the
captain's memoranda in chapter 25, the file in chapter 29), a stranger
cataloguing her output and vanishing on a straight answer is precisely the
kind of thing she is established, elsewhere in this same chapter, as good at
noticing.

**What she does now / what a nineteen-year-old would do:** She marks the
question closed because her own answer to it was true. A nineteen-year-old
who runs a blog by cross-referencing two governments' press releases against
each other keeps a record of a pattern like this the same way, out of habit,
long before she has any theory about what it means.

**Proposed:**

> He doesn't reply.
>
> She reads the message a second time, then a third, and opens a spreadsheet
> that night with three columns: the date, the piece he asked about, and
> whether he wrote back after she answered. Two more emails go into it before
> spring, both asking the same question in different words, neither one
> answering when she asks in return what paper or company they write for. By
> April the spreadsheet has five rows, and the column for who somebody
> writes for is empty on every one of them.

## Chapter 30 (Chloe, 20): 1 instance, plus a note on the investigation scene

### Instance 4

**chapters/30_cleared.md:11**

> Roughly a third of those conversations end there, with a reply that
> trails off into something polite, "right, well, if the team ever wants to
> talk about expanding," and then the thread goes quiet. The pattern repeats
> often enough, the question and then the drop-off, that she decides it's
> people being funny about credentials and stops turning it over between
> emails.

**Shape:** 1, auditing instead of reacting. This is the same pattern as
instance 3, continued a year later at higher volume ("roughly a third of
those conversations") and now closed off explicitly: she reasons her way to
the least alarming explanation and then states, on the page, that she stops
thinking about it. Of the three flagged passages this is the one line I
would fix first if I could fix only one; it is the book handing itself the
credit for noticing a pattern and then spending that credit on giving up.

**What she does now / what a twenty-year-old would do:** Settles on
"people being funny about credentials" and drops it. Someone who has spent a
year building the spreadsheet habit from chapter 28 keeps feeding it,
whether or not she has an explanation that satisfies her, because the
absence of an explanation isn't the same as the absence of a record.

**Proposed:**

> Roughly a third of those conversations end there, with a reply that
> trails off into something polite, "right, well, if the team ever wants to
> talk about expanding," and then the thread goes quiet. Each one goes into
> the same spreadsheet she started in February, and by the following winter
> it runs to eleven rows, none of them ever naming an outlet or a company
> when she asks. There is no next step available for eleven rows with no
> name attached. She leaves the file open on a second tab and keeps working
> the one she is actually writing.

### Note, not an instance: the Whitaker interviews

Per the brief for this span, I read the background-investigation scenes
(30:29-121) as a scene an investigator by definition runs, and judged Chloe
on what she does with the questions rather than on who is asking them. On
that standard the scene holds up. She gives a direct answer and gets a
direct one back on the record-sealing question before she decides to trust
him with more than the minimum ("Chloe likes him for it"); she takes
personal ownership of the one student she failed to reach ("Mine," flat,
no hedge); and on the one question she can't answer alone, she doesn't
guess and doesn't stall, she names exactly what she'd need before answering
("I'd want to talk to her first"). Each of those is a real decision inside a
format that hands her very little room to make one. No fix proposed here.

## Summary

**Instances found:** 4, all in Chloe's own two chapters (28: three, 30: one,
with instance 3 running across both). Chapters 25, 26, 27 and 29 produced
none; each has an active viewpoint character (Sam, Sam, Nadia, Theo) who
pushes against what's put in front of him or her, including in 29 where the
stillness is deliberate and is shown costing something before it resolves
into action.

**Worst chapter:** 28, on instance count (three sites against one). The
single worst line in the span, though, is in 30: "she decides it's people
being funny about credentials and stops turning it over between emails,"
because it explicitly narrates the noticing and the giving-up in the same
sentence.

**Initiates-a-scene ratio:** Counted by section break, chat threads excluded.

| chapter | POV | self-initiated | other-initiated |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | Sam | 2 | 4 |
| 26 | Sam | 2 | 4 |
| 27 | Nadia | 4 | 0 |
| 28 | Chloe | 3 | 2 |
| 29 | Theo | 4 | 1 |
| 30 | Chloe | 1 | 4 |

Span total: 16 self-initiated against 15 other-initiated, close to even, but
that number is misleading if read as one figure. Sam's chapters run
other-heavy because he is answering to drill sergeants and a captain, a fact
about the setting rather than about him; Nadia's and Theo's chapters run
self-heavy because both of them go looking for what they're dealing with.
Chloe's own two chapters are the ones worth watching on this measure taken
alone: 4 self-initiated against 6 other-initiated, about 40:60, and chapter
30's skew toward "other" is largely the investigation interview, which the
brief rules out of scope for this measure by design.

**The three I would fix first**, in order:

1. **30:11** ("decides it's people being funny about credentials and stops
   turning it over") — the single line, the flattest give-up, and the one
   most out of character for someone who runs a fact-checking operation on
   her own evenings.
2. **28:67** (the research-team email) — the same failure the first time it
   happens, before the pattern has a name; fixing it here means 30:11 is a
   continuation of a habit already established rather than the first sign of
   it.
3. **28:33** (the Tyler-game departures) — the clearest case of a
   deliberation stated instead of resolved, recurring ("usually") across
   what reads like most of a year without ever landing.

Instance 1 (28:23, the filing tray) is real but the smallest of the four;
it would be the fourth fix, not the first three.
