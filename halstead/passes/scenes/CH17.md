# Chapter 17 — Fourteen: scene audit

Read-only. Nothing in `chapters/17_fourteen.md` is touched by this file.
Every quotation below was checked against the current text of the chapter by
direct search before being included.

This chapter covers a full school year (September 2019 – April 2020) across
roughly a dozen mostly-unrelated activity threads (marksmanship, self-defense,
teaching, coursework, an engineering project, a sound-localization project, a
no-libraries project, the forge, physical growth, a classmate's dating
subplot, and a family dinner). Section 4 below flags, up front, that this
produces a very high scene count for a nine-page chapter, and asks whether
that is the vignette problem the brief warns about or a deliberate
year-in-survey design. My answer is hedged and comes at the end.

---

## 1. Scenes

The chapter has four sections, marked by `________________`. Scene numbering
below runs straight through all four; it does not restart at each break,
because several scenes cross a break (Scene 5 spans the gap between Sections
1's ending is inside Section 1, but the same run of scene-splitting logic
continues into Section 2 without resetting) and at least one section (2)
alone contains twenty-one of them.

### Scene 1 — Dry drill (September, presumably the armory or a classroom) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: a compressed account of weeks of unloaded weapons
training before live fire starts.

**Content:** Marksmanship replaces archery. Weeks of dry drill: naming weapons
off a silhouette, stripping and reassembling them by feel under a cloth,
timed by Voss. Chloe fumbles the bolt release for weeks until, in the third
week, her hand finds it without her looking.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing a training rhythm (weeks of
invisible failure before a skill clicks) that the chapter's later plateau
scene (Scene F, see below — actually Scene "range plateau," numbered 24-ish
in the walk) seems to invert or echo; hedged, since I can't be sure that
echo is intended rather than my own pattern-matching.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be setting expectations for how
long mastery takes here, against which "the count starts to matter again" in
Scene 3 can be measured.

### Scene 2 — Live fire begins (October, the range, prone line) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe's first live-fire session, with banter among
Sam, Kavi, Odile and Ruth about how long the sport stays boring.

**Content:** Live fire starts at fifty metres, prone. Chloe is down before
Voss finishes handing out ear protection. Sam asks how long it stays boring;
Kavi predicts six weeks based on archery and paintball's timelines; Sam notes
Ruth is already bored; Odile agrees, and Ruth puts three rounds through one
hole and asks for water.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to establish the ensemble's voices early in a
new activity and to set Kavi's "it escalates every year" expectation, which
the chapter's design-project arc later appears to bear out.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that for this group, boredom
with a new physical skill is the default expectation rather than the
exception, which could be read against Chloe's own later plateau.

### Scene 3 — The season's progression (October–December, the range) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: a compressed run through kneeling, standing, wind
reading, and the swinging target, plus Odile's hand injury.

**Content:** Kneeling and standing added before month's end; then a hundred
metres, then a hundred metres in crosswind, then, in November, a swinging
target on a rope. Odile burns her hand on an ejection port that week and is
still holding the palm up at breakfast in December.

**Possible purpose:** may be pure compression to get the reader from October
to February without dwelling; the Odile injury detail may be there mainly to
mark time (it is "still" bothering her weeks later).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may just be pacing; I did not find
anything else load-bearing in it.

### Scene 4 — The stopwatch dispute (February, the range) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: a mechanical clay trap is introduced with a printed
timing card; Aymar disputes the card's accuracy with Voss.

**Content:** Voss hands out a card with the trap's interval printed on it.
Aymar says the card is wrong by a fifth of a second because he has timed the
mechanism himself; Voss tells him the card says what it says; Aymar explains
why (a new throwing-arm head slows the reset); Voss, after visibly weighing
it, moves on to the next firing point without conceding out loud.

**Possible purpose:** may be introducing Aymar's specific competence
(precision timing) ahead of his role in the sound-localization project later
in the chapter, and may be showing Voss as someone who acts on being
persuaded without saying so.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that being right here does not
come with acknowledgment, which is possibly a small preview of how the
sound-project team's later successes and failures also go mostly
unremarked-on by the adults running things.

### Scene 5 — The card, and the path (later that week, the range bench, then a path) — 6 paragraphs

What appears to happen: a corrected card is quietly taped up; Ruth needles
Aymar (who shares her surname) about it; Kavi catches up with Chloe walking
and raises a design problem about scaling the timing device to a moving
bullet.

**Content:** A different card appears with a new number; Ruth spends the term
crediting Aymar's stopwatch for it and tells Chloe why she keeps bringing it
up ("He's got my last name... so he's mine"). Kavi, walking backward with his
kit, argues that the old arrow-based approach relied on two people sharing a
fixed, forgiving cycle, and a bullet removes both of those properties; Sam
suggests they might stay at clays; Kavi predicts escalation.

**Possible purpose:** may be planting, well before the sound-localization
project starts, the specific technical problem (shared timing across
distributed people) that the project spends the rest of the chapter solving.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Kavi is already thinking
several steps ahead of the current exercise, which is possibly consistent
with how he is the one who eventually builds the network's clock.

*Note on this scene's boundary:* the single paragraph carrying "A different
card is taped to the bench later that week... Kavi catches up with Chloe on
the path afterward" moves from the range bench to a path with no paragraph
break in between. See Feedback.

*Note on names:* Ruth's line — "He's got my last name... So he's mine" — is,
I believe, the chapter's one acknowledgment of Ruth and Aymar sharing a
surname, which `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md` already rules is not a continuity
error and says gets exactly this much acknowledgment. Not raising it as a
defect.

### Scene 6 — The self-defense hold and its retelling (a Tuesday in February, the mat room, then the sinks) — 7 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe holds fifty seconds against ten opponents, is
put down before the whistle, and the story of the hold spreads through the
year over the following days via Bex's retelling.

**Content:** Chloe holds fifty seconds, then is taken down laughing.
Odile calls it the longest hold of the week; Chloe wants another go;
Kowalczyk assigns pairs instead. Afterward Chloe re-enacts the hold by hand
for Ruth, Sam, and a younger boy who witnessed it; Bex, arriving late,
retells it accurately to the next person through the door, plus a tactical
note Chloe should have used. By the next week, the story the year tells is
Bex's version, not Chloe's own retelling.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing how a personal moment becomes public
property once it passes through a better storyteller, without Chloe losing
anything visible over it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that ownership of an experience
is not guaranteed to the person who had it, in a school where everyone is
being trained to narrate technical events precisely; the final image (hands
under running water) may be there to give Chloe something to do with her
hands rather than a reaction to name.

### Scene 7 — The backward-taught lesson (September–November, a classroom) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe, now teaching a group of younger students,
discovers she taught them a mathematical procedure in reverse order for
months without anyone noticing.

**Content:** Seven of the year below are on her teaching list; her hour is
differential equations. In November, all of them get one question wrong, and
wrong the same way. Reviewing her own old notes, she finds she taught the
steps in reversed order the whole way through, and the error was invisible
because everything up to the test came out identical either way. A student
asks if the test counts; another says maybe they're not actually bad at it;
Chloe corrects them both: their answers followed correctly from what she
taught, and what she taught was wrong.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show her handling being wrong about
something she taught with more composure than she might have a few chapters
earlier, and may set up the gossip in Scene 8.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that teaching, for her, carries
a specific kind of risk (being wrong at scale, invisibly, for months) that
her other activities in this chapter don't.

### Scene 8 — The rumor at dinner (that week, the dining hall, then the sinks) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: Iyad spreads the story of Chloe's error at dinner
while she isn't there to hear it; a girl in the year above asks her directly
whether it's true.

**Content:** Iyad tells the story table to table over several days. Days
later, a girl at the sinks asks whether it's true Chloe taught the whole
year backward; Chloe says yes; the girl says she thought so and leaves.

**Possible purpose:** may be tracking how a piece of information about Chloe
moves through the school without her control over it, which recurs at least
twice more in this chapter (Scene 27's dating gossip, the "Aurel" rumor in
Chapter 18).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that this school's information
economy runs on retelling regardless of whether the subject is present; see
Feedback for the exchange itself.

### Scene 9 — The rest of the curriculum, survey (September–November) — 2 paragraphs

What appears to happen: a summary of her other classes that year.

**Content:** Japanese moves fast enough that she checks her own old notes on
Russian and Mandarin to confirm it, and confirms it; the grammar slows her to
half an hour nightly by November, still ahead of where Mandarin had her.
Abstract algebra, probability, contracts, logistics, and comparative religion
are each given a sentence or two of what they cover.

**Possible purpose:** may exist purely for pacing and texture — establishing
the breadth of the curriculum without dramatizing any of it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that by this point checking her
own competence against her own historical record is a habit rather than a
performance for anyone else, which (given she is fourteen, in a specialist
program, and has done comparable checks elsewhere in the book) does not read
to me as the "Chloe files that" kind of overly computational note the
calibration guidance warns about — but flagging the comparison since that
guidance exists.

### Scene 10 — The clamp (November, the engineering workshop) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: a specification Chloe wrote for a clamp is built
exactly as written and fails because her spec never said what the clamp was
supposed to hold.

**Content:** She writes a two-page spec; a boy named Dov builds it; it closes
on the wrong axis. The teacher shows her it works as specified — squeezing
hard, but not for any particular object, so it holds anything equally
poorly. She checks her own pages and finds the backs blank; she summarizes
the problem herself: page and a half on how hard it squeezes, nothing on
what's being squeezed.

**Possible purpose:** may be another instance (after Scene 7) of Chloe being
shown a mistake that was invisible from the inside, this time about
specification rather than teaching.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building a pattern across the
chapter — teaching, specifying, timing — where her competence is real but
incomplete in a specific, recurring way (not checking what the work is
actually for), though I'm not confident this is a designed throughline
rather than three separate anecdotes that happen to rhyme.

### Scene 11 — Trading an evening (a Tuesday in February, the dining hall) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: everyone in the group wants the same free evening and
has to negotiate around the table for hours.

**Content:** Most schoolwork now involves other people's names alongside
hers. On a Tuesday everyone wants the same evening; she trades hours around
the table and ends up moving an hour to Sunday, giving up an evening she'd
already planned mentally.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to establish, briefly, that the workload has
become genuinely collaborative and contested before the sound-localization
project (which needs the same scarce evenings) begins in the next scene.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be a small, unglamorous cost of the
increasingly complex curriculum, distinct from the more dramatic costs shown
elsewhere in the chapter.

### Scene 12 — The brief and the brainstorm (February, "the room," Tuesdays and Thursdays) — 12 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the engineering-and-design groups are announced; this
group's brief is to locate a sound on a field to within a hand's width; the
group spends an evening arguing through the physics of the problem.

**Content:** Names go up on a noticeboard; Eli jokes that the school has
"put your dinner table in a room and given it a number." Kavi raises the
network-timing problem; Ruth raises the harder problem of picking the same
instant on the sound's leading edge at every box; Chloe proposes arrival
differences and argues against a straight-line box layout; Nadia proposes
wireless and states its practical cost in labor; Odile notes the far end of
the field is colder; Kavi connects temperature to the speed of sound and
proposes a person with a thermometer at every post; Priya raises that
calibrating against a known spot will make that spot read well and lie
everywhere else, which the staff won't allow anyway since a fixed
calibration point defeats the point of the exercise. Nobody has an answer by
Thursday.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to let each named character contribute one
specific, technically real objection, functioning as a group characterization
device more than an advancing-the-plot scene.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that this group solves problems
by identifying every way the naive approach fails before building anything,
which the rest of the project (see the scenes below) seems to bear out.

### Scene 13 — The number that isn't dropping (mid-March, dinner) — 7 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the wireless network's timing lands at "a couple of
milliseconds" and won't improve, and Kavi admits he's known for two weeks.

**Content:** Kavi announces the number with his hands flat on the tray; Ruth
asks how long it's been sitting there; "a fortnight"; Ruth is furious that
they built and tuned against a number he sat on. He says he thought it would
come down; she calls that "a hope with a schedule on it."

**Possible purpose:** may be showing Ruth's specific competence (calling out
a decision cost immediately, in numbers) and Kavi's specific failure mode
(hoping instead of reporting).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that in this group, withholding
a bad number costs other people's time specifically and measurably, which is
possibly the chapter's clearest illustration of the stakes behind Voss's
February silence in Scene 4.

**Content:** Nadia puts the wire back on the board: the clock goes down the

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly as the pivot line between the crisis

**What I think it tells the reader:** N/A — see the merge proposal below.

### Scene 15 — Laying the wire (Saturday, the long field, in rain) — 2 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the group lays a hundred and sixty metres of wire in

**Content:** They lay and peg the wire on Saturday; Sam helps with the
lifting because Odile asked him to; they lift it Sunday because the mowers

**Possible purpose:** may exist to make the labor cost of the wireless-to-wire

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be underlining that Nadia's
decision in Scene 14, while solving Priya's calibration problem, creates a
new recurring cost (people-hours) that nobody in Scene 12's brainstorm
priced in.

### Scene 16 — Recording in the dark (that same week, the field at night) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Odile and Sam go out after dinner, repeatedly, to
produce the sound on the field so Ruth can record it.

**Content:** Kavi's new clock lands Tuesday; every prior recording is now
useless and has to be redone. Odile and Sam make the sound over and over,

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give a physical, bodily cost (red hands,
repeated trips into the cold) to what would otherwise be an abstract data
problem.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that the unglamorous, repetitive
part of the project falls on Odile and Sam rather than on whoever is
theorizing about it, though I don't see the prose commenting on that
division one way or the other.

### Scene 17 — The midnight edit (after midnight, the workroom) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe, working alone on the geometry, moves one of
Ruth's data thresholds without asking, and Ruth catches it and is angry.

**Content:** Chloe opens Ruth's thresholds and moves the far box's mark
because she believes it's picking up the road as the sound's leading edge.
Ruth tells her to put it back: if the road is being picked up, it's
happening at every box, and Chloe's edit has now made comparing them
impossible. Ruth tells her to redo her own end, go to bed, or get food, and
leave Ruth's data alone. Chloe goes to get her food.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's clearest instance of a
collaborative failure that is Chloe's fault specifically (not a shared
unknown, not someone else's withheld information, but her own unilateral
edit).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing a limit to her
competence that isn't about being wrong on the facts (she may even be right
about the road) but about process — editing someone else's instrument
without asking. I'm not fully sure this reads as a flaw the text intends the
reader to register as a flaw, versus a neutral disagreement between two
people under the same pressure; noting the uncertainty.

### Scene 18 — The run (the last Thursday in March, the field, afternoon) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the actual measurement happens once, and the group's
placement is off by about a hand's width and a half.

**Content:** A staff member carries out the plate (made from stock Chloe cut
in February) and disappears over a rise; there is nothing to do for twenty
minutes; the sound plays once, thin and distant. Ruth gets onsets inside a
quarter hour; Kavi's clock agrees with itself along the wire; Chloe reads a
point off the sheet. Odile and Sam measure the actual mark and find it a
hand's width and a half from Chloe's prediction.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to deliver the single concrete payoff of
Scenes 12 through 17 in one compressed, low-drama paragraph sequence, in
contrast to how much narrated setup and conflict preceded it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that after all the process
drama, the actual result is close but not exact, which the chapter does not
editorialize about one way or the other.

### Scene 19 — The grade (Monday, a corridor) — 1 paragraph (see note)

What appears to happen: the group gets a B, with named failures listed

failure named here is the same one Ruth was furious about in Scene 13 —

*Note:* the paragraph containing this scene also contains, in its second

### Scene 21 — The renderer (spring, presumably her own workspace, demo day) — 2 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the no-libraries project is introduced; Chloe builds

**Content:** The brief: build something real, entirely from scratch. Her

it, and re-lays-out a page live within a sixteen-millisecond budget while the

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to establish the assignment's premise
and give Chloe's specific entry, ahead of Ruth's entry in the next scene.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that of the two projects shown
in this pairing (hers and Ruth's), hers is presented as already working by
the time the reader sees it, while Ruth's (next scene) is presented mid-crisis
— possibly a deliberate asymmetry, possibly just which stage of each project
happened to be dramatized.

### Scene 22 — Ruth's attack transient (spring, Ruth's room) — 9 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Ruth is stuck on a live-synthesized sound effect and
Chloe helps her find that she's been cutting off part of the sound without
realizing it.

**Content:** Ruth does live sound synthesis and spends days replaying a few
seconds until Priya bangs on the wall. She says fixing the attack breaks the
tail; Chloe tells her to play it from the actual start; Ruth says she's
played it hundreds of times and it always does it; Chloe tells her to

more times, then sits up so fast the machine slides off her: there are thirty
milliseconds of silence on the front of the clip that she'd been cutting off
as "empty space," moving the perceived attack every time.

**Possible purpose:** may be a second, quieter instance of the "invisible
error that was there in the data the whole time" pattern from Scenes 7 and
10, this time solved collaboratively rather than discovered alone.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Chloe's specific
contribution here isn't the technical insight itself (Ruth finds the actual
silence) but the instruction to change how she's testing it, which is
possibly a different kind of competence than the show-your-work correctness
seen elsewhere in the chapter.

### Scene 23 — The fracture tests (autumn through March, the materials lab, then a corridor) — 1 paragraph

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing a discrepancy between
the size of the discovery (five weeks, a real methodological error) and the
size of its acknowledgment (a name in a corridor, said in passing, from
someone already walking elsewhere), without the prose naming that gap
itself.

### Scene 29 — The dinner disclosure (winter break, second night, the dinner table) — 24 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe's mother learns, mid-story, that Chloe has been
shooting live rifles since September and that her father already knew; the
family argument that follows covers who kept what from whom.

**Content:** Chloe is telling the table about Odile's scar when her mother
asks where the burn came from and hears "on the rifle." Her mother's hands
stop; she asks "You're shooting rifles?"; Chloe confirms, since September.
Her mother repeats "Since September" pointedly; Chloe tries to reassure her
about the safety procedures; her mother asks why this is the first she's
hearing of it. Chloe runs through, silently, everything else that happened
this year that also went unmentioned (teaching, the backward lesson, the
languages, the clamp, the hinges) and has no answer for why the rifle is the
thing that surfaced. She tries to explain she didn't mean to hide it, just
that a lot started in September and she skipped all of it. Her mother turns
to her father, who admits he knew from a phone call in October and thought
her mother knew too. The exchange escalates between the parents; her mother
gets up, goes to the kitchen, and starts stacking still-full plates.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's central scene — showing the cost
of a whole year of selective non-disclosure landing all at once, on the one
topic (weapons) her parents were least prepared for.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Chloe's habit of not
mentioning things at home, established gradually across the book, has by now
scaled up to a genuinely dangerous-sounding omission, and that neither she
nor her father can produce a good reason for it in the moment — which, if I
am reading the calibration note in the brief correctly, is consistent with
"masking... should cost her something visible, and it should sometimes
fail." I'm flagging this as an observation, not proposing any change to it.

## 4. The chapter whole

**Scene count and average length.** I found 32 scenes across 129 paragraphs,
