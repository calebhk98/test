## 1. Scenes

The chapter has seven `________________` breaks, giving eight sections, but
the sections are not the scenes. I found **19 numbered units** below, of
which **14 are staged scenes** (a located time and place with continuous
action) and **5 are summary or reflection passages** that compress a
period, a habit, or a train of thought rather than dramatizing a single
moment — I've marked each of the five as such where it occurs, per the
brief's instruction to say so rather than count them as scenes on equal
footing. The chapter runs August 2023 to November 2023 and reads, on the
whole, as a training-year montage that periodically slows down into a fully
staged scene — most heavily for qualification, which is both the chapter's
title and by far its longest scene (27 of the chapter's 102 paragraphs).

### Scene 1 — Arrival night (the bay, the first night of August) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the bus arrives after dark; Sam falls asleep and
wakes to the bay's first full morning.

**Content:** The bus reaches the company area after dark; Sam, first off,
spends twenty minutes standing somewhere he's told to stop standing. That
night he falls asleep to the bay finding its racks; he wakes before the
lights to a bay in the dark he calls a footrace.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to set the clock running and to give
one image (the footrace) for what every subsequent morning will feel like
without having to restate it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be establishing that Sam, who
we're told likes the Army "immediately," starts from a position of already
being slightly behind everyone else physically (last off, twenty minutes
in the wrong spot) — which the fitness test in Scene 6 will then upend.

### Scene 2 — Rack inspection under Ruiz (the bay, the second morning, extending across the week) — 6 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Ruiz fails Sam's rack repeatedly; Okoro starts
demonstrating alongside him.

**Content:** The schedule reappears on the wall. Ruiz inspects racks by
eye, stops at Sam's, pulls it onto the floor, says "Again." This repeats
across several days until Okoro, already familiar with the folds, starts
making his own rack slowly beside Sam's. After that it takes Sam about
ninety seconds, putting him in the back half of the platoon.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing Okoro as the person who actually
teaches Sam things, ahead of the sinks conversation in Scene 3 where Okoro
also corrects Sam's reading of Ruiz.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing that whatever Sam is
good at (which the chapter spends the rest of its length establishing), it
isn't this — a small, deliberate area of ordinary incompetence before the
fitness test and qualification scenes complicate that picture.

### Scene 3 — Sinks with Okoro (the bay sinks, the same week) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam tells Okoro he thinks Ruiz likes him; Okoro
disagrees, dryly.

**Content:** Sam offers his theory about Ruiz at the sinks. Okoro, shaving
with the water off because the bay lost water privileges on Tuesday,
tells him to build "a considerably smaller theory." Sam pushes back once;
Okoro considers it and drops the subject.

**Possible purpose:** may be introducing Okoro's function for the rest of
the chapter — a flatter, more skeptical reader of the same events Sam is
over-reading — which recurs in the chow hall (Scene 8) and the closing
scene (Scene 19).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be an early instance of Sam
building a theory that doesn't survive contact with someone else's plainer
account of the same facts — the same shape the "screen" theory in Scene 10
runs on a larger scale.

### Scene 4 — "How long, then what" (unstaged, composite, by the end of the first week) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam asks around about the length of the program;
one exchange goes further than the others.

**Content:** By the brief's own definition this isn't quite a scene — it
names no place and summarizes an unspecified number of conversations
("a succession of people") before dropping into one representative
exchange about what infantry service looks like after training.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to set up the specific question ("what's
this for, when does the real one start") that Scene 5 gives Ruiz's answer
to.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be less a scene than connective
narration wearing a scene's shape — see Section 4 for why I'm not counting
it as a full scene and how I'd fold it into Scene 5.

### Scene 5 — Friday formation with Ruiz (the parade ground, Friday evening) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam asks Ruiz directly what training is for; Ruiz
gives a non-answer that Sam treats as partial rather than final.

**Content:** At parade rest after evening formation, Sam asks his question.
Ruiz says the real one is every day from Tuesday onward and will still be
running after Sam has forgotten this conversation, then dismisses him. Sam
keeps asking afterward.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's first statement of a question
(what is any of this measuring, what is it for) that recurs at the fitness
test, the chow hall, and qualification, each time with a different
authority figure declining to give the answer Sam wants.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be establishing Ruiz as someone
who answers a question honestly without answering it usefully — which the
company commander in Scene 15 does the opposite of (answers usefully by
not answering the philosophical question at all, only the security one).

### Scene 6 — The fitness test (the gravel range, the second week, early morning) — 11 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam maxes a test built on events he's never done
before; nobody believes him about it, including the grader.

**Content:** Six events, cold morning. Sam scores the maximum on all of
them (six hundred). The grader double-checks the addition and asks if he's
done this before; Sam says no. Sam spends the rest of the day unable to
reconcile a fixed ceiling with his own experience of always having room to
improve. On Sunday he has Okoro time him again privately to see if the raw
numbers under the capped score move; they move a little.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to introduce the "cap on the scale" problem
that recurs at qualification — a score that reads as maximal to everyone
else and as an unresolved question to Sam.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing Sam's specific
discomfort with being measured by an instrument he can max out, since a
maxed-out instrument stops being able to tell him anything, which seems to
matter more to him than the achievement itself does.

### Scene 7 — The record retest (the range, the ninth week) — 1 paragraph, summary

**Content:** In the ninth week Sam retakes the record test and posts the
same maximum score again.

**Possible purpose:** may exist only to close the loop opened by Scene 6's
"keep them, they're just better" line, confirming the six hundred wasn't a
one-off.

**What I think it tells the reader:** a seven-week jump compressed into one
sentence; I'm not counting this as a staged scene. See Section 4.

### Scene 8 — Chow hall interrogation (the dining facility, that afternoon) — 14 paragraphs

What appears to happen: word of the fitness score spreads by chow; a
soldier presses Sam on it and doesn't believe the answer.

**Content:** Someone asks whether Sam played sports before enlisting. Sam
says boarding school, offers his self-deprecating shooting number (forty
percent) as a counterweight, and tells two stories about Odile and about
losing something from his boot. Several soldiers end up laughing; one
doesn't believe any of it. A third voice says "Boarding school" and Sam
gives a rehearsed-sounding deflection ("Small one. You wouldn't have heard
of it."). The subject changes. Later at the same meal he complains about
the length of the serving-line window.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing the "forty percent" figure and
the deflecting "small one, you wouldn't have heard of it" line before both
get reused, nearly verbatim, by the captain at qualification (Scene 15) —
so the reader has already seen Sam give this exact performance once when
it's repeated under higher stakes.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing that Sam has a
practiced routine for explaining himself away, one that works on
soldiers his own age and, later, does not work at all on an officer who
has the standing to keep asking.

### Scene 9 — Weapon issue and first zero (the range, weeks three and four, compressed into one paragraph) — 1 paragraph

**Content:** The rifle is issued in week three. First live rounds come the
week after; Sam puts most of his group inside the target's center ring on
the first morning and can't understand why the scorer wants to talk to him
about it afterward.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to plant, quickly, that Sam's shooting is
also unusually good before the chapter turns to why that bothers him
(Scene 10) rather than to him enjoying it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** compresses two separate weeks into
one paragraph; I'm treating this as intentional pacing rather than a
scene-boundary problem, though see Section 4 for how it affects the count.

### Scene 10 — Sam's three theories (no fixed place, weeks following, reflection rather than scene) — 6 paragraphs

**Content:** Sam notices he is outperforming his own history at every
physical and technical task. He considers and discards two explanations
(fatigue, time already invested) and settles on a third: that the testing
is a screening process for something not yet revealed to him, and that his
peers are holding back. He stops examining the theory after a week.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give the reader Sam's own account of why
none of what's happening to him feels like what it looks like from
outside — which the reports in Scene 16 then confirm is at least partly
true, though not in the way Sam has guessed.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing a specific kind of
overconfidence: Sam trusts his own inference (a "screen") more than he
trusts the plain evidence in front of him (he is simply better prepared
than the men around him, which his own "second thought" already worked
out and then discarded in favor of the more dramatic third one). This
reads to me as a variant of the calibration note about a character
building an elaborate account rather than accepting a plainer one, applied
to Sam rather than to the book's protagonist — I flag it for the author to
weigh rather than treat it as confirmed, since Sam is not the character the
calibration note is about.

### Scene 11 — Foot march weigh-in (the trailhead, September) — 1 paragraph

**Content:** Packs are weighed before a foot march. Sam is accused of
underweighting his; he opens it for a second reading, which comes out the
same, and keeps walking.

**Possible purpose:** may be a smaller-stakes rehearsal of the
disbelief-then-verification pattern that structures the fitness test and
qualification scenes.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing Sam's default response
to accusation is to offer verification rather than argument — consistent
across this scene, the fitness test, and qualification.

### Scene 12 — Land navigation (the course, October, day and night) — 3 paragraphs

**Content:** Sam completes the land navigation course perfectly on the
first attempt, then walks back out to help squadmates who are a point
short, correcting their compass bearing and walking it with them rather
than just telling them the answer.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show the same instinct Okoro showed Sam
in Scene 2 (demonstrate alongside, rather than just correct) now running
the other direction, from Sam to somebody else.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be the chapter's clearest example
of Sam being generous with what he's good at rather than possessive of it,
which sits against the "screen" theory's assumption (Scene 10) that
everyone around him is holding something back.

### Scene 13 — Mail call and evening routine (the bay, ongoing, summary) — 2 paragraphs

**Content:** Mail call happens on schedule, with push-ups charged by the
letter. Sam writes his grandmother weekly in block capitals, reporting the
cold and the food but not the six-hundred score.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show what Sam chooses not to report
home, mirroring what the chapter later shows the Army choosing to report
upward about him (Scene 16).

**What I think it tells the reader:** a habitual routine rather than a
single dramatized moment; not counted as a staged scene. See Section 4.

### Scene 14 — Okoro's grandmother (the bay, an evening) — 3 paragraphs

**Content:** Okoro asks who Sam's letters go to. Sam says his grandmother.
Okoro mentions his own grandmother sends him newspaper word searches, which
he's started doing.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to keep Okoro present as a
counterweight character between the larger set pieces, and to give him one
detail of his own rather than only reacting to Sam's.

**What I think it tells the reader:** No firm reading; a small, low-stakes
beat that may just be pacing between larger scenes.

### Scene 15 — Qualification (the range, the second Thursday of October) — 27 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam shoots a perfect qualification score; the
company commander presses him on where he learned to shoot, and the
conversation moves from disbelief to an official report.

**Content:** Forty pop-up targets, timed. Sam hits all forty. The scorer
and tower both verify it. The company commander comes down to the line,
signs the card, and asks where Sam learned to shoot. Sam volunteers his
"real" number (forty percent, on live-fire intercept work at Halstead) as
a correction to the "good sheet." The captain, who has served sixteen
years and knows no school and barely any unit runs that kind of training,
presses him on the school's name and location. Sam names Halstead and
repeats his "small one, you wouldn't have heard of it" line. The captain
takes the card, folds it, and keeps it rather than filing it, then sends
Sam off to pick up his brass.

**Possible purpose:** the chapter's title event and clearly its centerpiece
by length; may exist to be the specific incident that starts the reporting
chain in Scene 16, which the rest of the chapter (and, per the passes
directory's chapter 26 material on "considerable," at least one later
chapter) follows forward.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be the point where Sam's habit of
offering the "real," worse number as a corrective (established at the
fitness test and the chow hall) meets someone with the authority and the
specific knowledge to recognize what the "real" number actually implies,
rather than someone who simply doesn't believe him.

### Scene 16 — The captain writes the reports (the company desk, that evening) — 6 paragraphs

**Content:** The captain writes out four documents by hand that evening: a
memorandum for record, a referral to counterintelligence, a request for
Sam's school records, and a behavioral health referral, in that order.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show the machinery a single conversation
sets in motion, in the same flat, itemized register the chapter uses for
the fitness test's six events and the qualification course's five
positions.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be underlining that what Sam
treats as a private correction to a scoresheet becomes, to the person
receiving it, a security question with an institutional answer.

### Scene 17 — The desk above the captain's (higher headquarters, the following Thursday) — 1 paragraph

**Content:** A week later the memorandum reaches a woman positioned well
above the captain, who reads the fourth paragraph twice, sets it on top of
a stack rather than into it, and pulls out a folder older than the
captain's commission.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to signal, without saying so, that Sam's
file connects to something that predates this chapter and this captain
entirely.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be doing a lot of implying in one
sentence (an existing folder, a woman "positioned well above" an already
senior captain) without confirming what it implies — which reads to me as
consistent with house rule 1 rather than in tension with it, since nothing
here states a meaning, only shows an action (the folder coming out) whose
significance is left to the reader.

### Scene 18 — Grandmother's reply (unstaged, summary) — 2 paragraphs

**Content:** A letter arrives from Sam's grandmother; his reply repeats
that the food hasn't changed and the real difficulty has always been the
serving-line clock.

**Possible purpose:** may exist as a callback closing Scene 8's complaint
about the nine-minute meal, and to show the October-to-November time
passing.

**What I think it tells the reader:** a summary passage rather than a
staged scene; not counted as one below. See Section 4.

### Scene 19 — "Whether the screening is finished" (the path to the range, the first week of November) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam asks Okoro whether he thinks the sorting
process (his "screen" theory from Scene 10) is over yet.

**Content:** Walking down to the range, Sam asks Okoro if he thinks the
screening is finished. Okoro laughs at the question; Sam lets him. On
Thursday, Sam asks somebody else. The chapter ends there.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to leave Sam's own theory from Scene 10
explicitly unresolved and still being asked, going into whatever chapter
follows.

**What I think it tells the reader:** the chapter ends mid-thread rather
than closing it, which given the surrounding chapters seem to be part of a
continuous account of the same training cycle, I'm reading as a deliberate
chapter break rather than an incomplete scene.

## 4. The chapter whole

**Scene count and average length.** I found 19 numbered units across 102
paragraphs, an average of about 5.4 paragraphs per unit. Of those 19, 14
are staged scenes and 5 are summary or reflection passages (Scenes 4, 7,
10, 13, and 18) that compress a period or a train of thought rather than
dramatizing one located moment. Restricting the average to the 14 staged
scenes gives about 6.2 paragraphs per scene — close to the four-to-six
benchmark the brief gives for a chapter this length, until Scene 15
(qualification, 27 paragraphs) is set aside as an outlier; the other
thirteen staged scenes average closer to 4.6 paragraphs.

**Is this the vignette problem?** I don't think this chapter is a string of
disconnected short pieces in the way the brief's example (four unrelated-
feeling scenes for one continuous request) describes. What it is instead:
a four-month training-year summary that periodically slows into a fully
staged scene, with one scene (qualification) taking up more than a
quarter of the chapter's total length and functioning as the clear
structural payoff. The part of the chapter that reads closest to true
vignette trouble is the opening section — Scenes 1 through 5 are five
separate location or time shifts inside the chapter's first fifth, two of
which (Scenes 2 and 4) already compress several days or an unspecified
number of conversations into one paragraph rather than staying inside a
single moment. I'm hedging this: the author's own account of the four-
scene Chloe example in the brief involved one continuous request across
one short span of time, which this chapter's opening section is not — it
covers a full week of in-processing — so I'm not confident the same
diagnosis applies at the same strength.

**Concrete merges, with what carries the seam:**

1. **Scenes 2–3** (rack inspection; sinks) are bridged by Okoro, present in
   both, and by the same throughline — Sam misreading Ruiz, then Okoro
   correcting the reading. The two happen in the same general period (the
   first week) and at adjacent locations (the rack row, then the sinks).
   Recommend treating the sinks exchange as the back half of one scene
   about what Okoro teaches Sam in week one, rather than two.

2. **Scenes 4–5** (the composite "how long" summary; Friday's formation)
   are already bridged directly in the text — the composite paragraph is
   explicit setup for the pinned Friday exchange, asking the same
   question. Recommend folding paragraphs 14–17 in as the lead-in to the
   Friday scene rather than counting them separately; this also resolves
   Scene 4's lack of a fixed place, since it would no longer need to stand
   alone as a scene.

3. **Scenes 6–7** (the fitness test; the ninth-week retest) are bridged by
   the same test and the "keep them, they're just better" throughline.
   Recommend making the ninth-week line the closing sentence of the
   fitness-test scene rather than its own time jump.

4. **Scenes 16–17** (the captain writing the reports; the woman's desk)
   are bridged by the memorandum itself, the object that travels from one
   desk to the next across the seam. These already function as two beats
   of one continuous "the report climbs" movement; recommend treating them
   as a single scene with an internal jump in place and time at the point
   paragraph 96 already marks it.

Applying these four would take the chapter from 19 numbered units to
roughly 15 (the 5 summary/reflection passages still counted separately).
That is above the four-to-six range the brief names for a nine-page
chapter, but I don't have a confident view on whether that benchmark is
meant to apply at the same strength to a chapter that is explicitly

other chapters' scene-audit reports I've seen filed in this same directory
(Chapter 18, for one) raise a similar question about their own high counts
without resolving it, so I'm flagging rather than asserting.

**Scene specifically not proposed for change:** Scene 15 (qualification).
