## 1. Scenes

The chapter has four sections, marked by `________________`. Like Chapter
17, this chapter covers a full school year (September 2020 – July 2021)
across several mostly-independent activity threads (the turret and the long
plateau on the range, self-defense, money, a rumor, an NDA, phone security,
and finally a driving-lessons arc that closes the chapter). Scene numbering
runs straight through.

### Scene 1 — The turret (October, a new range lane) — 6 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Voss introduces a new, harder shooting discipline —
firing across the path of a moving live round — with a speech that becomes
famous around the school.

**Content:** A machine fires a live round across sixty metres on a fixed
cycle; students shoot across its path from behind glass. Voss tells them the
target is the bullet, not the turret; Aymar asks why, on behalf of everyone;
Voss says a gun aimed only at a man is a weapon, but a gun that can hit
another bullet is a tool, and this year is the second kind. He tells them to
put the rifle down when their arm shakes and pick it back up when it
doesn't.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to establish both a new difficulty tier and
the specific line ("put the rifle down when your arm shakes...") that later
scenes (the plateau, Scene 5) seem to test her against.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be setting up Voss as someone whose
authority comes from having said the same true thing to many students before
her, which the next scene's tracing of the quote's origin may be testing.

### Scene 2 — Where the line comes from (through November, the library, then the range office) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: Voss's speech becomes a repeated, almost-scriptural
line around school; Chloe investigates where it originated.

**Content:** The line is repeated at dinner tables within days, "quoted with
the settled confidence of something printed in a manual." In November,
mid-plateau, she looks for its source in the library and then the range
office; the oldest instructor on staff says the first time *he* heard it was
from Voss.

**Possible purpose:** may be suggesting the line has no deeper source than
Voss himself — it isn't handed down from some larger tradition, it
originates with the one man saying it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be a small comment on how
authority in this environment gets built (repetition, not provenance),
though I'm not confident the text means to make that point versus simply
recording that she checked.

*Note on this scene's boundary:* the paragraph moves from "round the year by
Thursday" (days after the speech) to "In November" (weeks later, mid-plateau)
without a paragraph break. See Feedback.

### Scene 3 — Weeks of zero (October, the range) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: every card in the year reads zero for weeks; Kavi
does the math on when someone will hit something.

**Content:** The range is "the sound of a great many people missing" for
weeks. In the second week Kavi calculates and announces at dinner that
someone will hit something around March; Priya tells him to eat.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to establish the scale of the difficulty
before individual progress (Scene 4) begins.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building comic relief ahead of
the increasingly serious plateau material that follows; see Feedback on how
Kavi's line is rendered.

### Scene 4 — First hits, then the climb (November–January, the range) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Odile, then Chloe, hit the target for the first
time; Chloe's count climbs steadily through January; then stops.

**Content:** Odile gets the first hit on November 11th. Chloe gets hers days
later — a dry crack, the paper unchanged at first, her pulse against the
stock, still sighting when told to clear. By end of November she's on
twenty; by late January, sixty. "Then it stops."

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing the climb specifically so the flat
plateau that follows (Scene 5) reads as a real reversal rather than just a
pause.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be the chapter's clearest use of
plain numbers to carry an emotional arc without naming any feeling — the
sequence zero, twenty, sixty, stopped, does the work by itself.

### Scene 5 — The plateau day (mid-February, the range, then dinner) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe shoots the same score three different ways in
one session and can't move past it; Voss takes the rifle from her.

**Content:** She scores sixty-one, fifty-nine, then sixty after adjusting her
count; she's loading again before the card is marked. By the sixth series
Voss is at her shoulder with his hand out. She asks for one more; he tells
her, by name, that she's shot the same number four ways in ninety minutes and
it isn't changing tonight; send her to dinner. She clears her plate without
registering what was on it.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's central turn — the climb from
Scene 4 meeting a wall the rest of the chapter's range material is about
trying to get past.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that persistence alone (more
series, faster) isn't the answer here, which the following scenes (old
tricks, the card archive, Ruth's diagnosis, Voss's own answer) seem to bear
out one at a time.

### Scene 6 — Old tricks (February–March, the range and her room) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: she tries several things that worked on other
skills; none of them move the number.

**Content:** Counting on her leg on the walk down (worked on archery)
doesn't change Friday's number. Holding her breath at different points in the
cycle makes her dizzy instead. By the second week of March she has weeks of
cards fanned across her floor; a pattern she finds on Tuesday turns out to be
wind through the open end of the lane, and she worries the corner of that
card until it comes off in her fingers.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show elimination as a method — ruling out
plausible causes one at a time — rather than a single insight arriving.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be establishing that she's now
working the problem alone, in her room, before Scene 8 shows her escalating
to institutional data (everyone's cards).

### Scene 7 — The archive (later in March, the range office) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: she asks for and receives every range card in the
year.

**Content:** A staff member hands her a box of ninety cards with no lock on
it, off a shelf next to the year below's identically unlocked box.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly as a transition into the analysis in
the next scene, and may be a small note about how little security surrounds
information at this school (which the chapter picks up again later with the
NDA and the phone-lock scenes).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be foreshadowing the phone/lock
material later in the chapter — an institution that doesn't lock a box of
scores is possibly of a piece with a lectern drawer that holds a phone with
years of chat on it — though I'm not confident this echo is intended rather
than coincidental.

### Scene 8 — Reading the cards (that month, dinner) — 4 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe spreads the range cards out at dinner and
finds eleven people, including herself, who plateaued the same way.

**Content:** Nadia identifies what she's doing without being told ("You're
looking for people who stopped where you stopped..."); Sam asks what the
cards are. Over several evenings she finds eleven people whose curves flatten
into the same narrow band regardless of how long it took them to get there,
and one of them — herself — sitting exactly where she landed since November.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show her converting a personal problem
into a dataset before taking it to an authority (Voss, next scene), which is
a method she uses elsewhere in the book (the loan ledger, the sound-project
geometry).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be underlining that eleven other
people hit the same wall, which could be read as either reassuring (not
uniquely bad at this) or more frustrating (a known, unsolved problem); the
prose doesn't signal which.

### Scene 9 — Voss's answer (that month, the range office doorway) — 7 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe brings Voss her findings; he confirms the
plateau is real and known, but gives her only a cryptic-sounding number in
return.

**Content:** She tells him what she found; he says it's real and she's far
from the first to find it. She asks what it is, since he says it's real; he
taps the pages straight and hands them back without answering that. He tells
her, "Point six, Kessler... Your block started without you." She leaves,
running the numbers again; the box goes back on the shelf, squared on top of
the year below's, and she leaves both where they are.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing an authority figure declining to hand
over an answer that would shortcut the work he clearly thinks she needs to
do herself (or that he doesn't have to give).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that "point six" and "your
block started without you" are meant to read as genuinely cryptic to the
reader as well as to Chloe — I don't have a confident read on what they
mean, and the chapter doesn't explain them anywhere else that I found.

### Scene 10 — Ruth's diagnosis (a Saturday in April, the range) — 6 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Ruth spends an afternoon timing Chloe from behind and
rules out the one variable she can check.

**Content:** Ruth checks Chloe's count against the clock and confirms it's
clean; Chloe says that's a different question from the one she asked. Ruth
clarifies she means the release itself — timed, Chloe's is tighter than
Ruth's own (who is at ninety-one) by about a hundredth of a second — so
whatever is costing Chloe points is somewhere Ruth can't see from behind her.
Chloe asks where, then; Ruth says she'd have said already if she knew, and
offers a toffee.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to rule out the most obvious mechanical
explanation (the count, the release) so that whatever is actually wrong
stays genuinely unresolved by the end of the chapter's range material.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing a second character
(after Voss) declining, or unable, to hand Chloe the answer, which by this
point in the chapter reads as a pattern rather than a single scene's choice.

### Scene 11 — What Sam thinks about it (that week, dinner) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Sam brings up the fact that everyone is talking about
Chloe's score, and she pushes back on why he's bringing it up.

**Content:** Sam mentions it's the talk of breakfast; Chloe asks what his
actual point is, plate full in front of her. He says he's on forty and
sleeps fine; she asks how, if he's just fine with it; he says it's because
he's where he is and does other things, then tells her to go be unhappy at
Ruth instead, since Ruth's the one on ninety-something.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give a third, contrasting response to the
same plateau — not an authority declining to answer (Voss, Ruth) but a peer
who has simply made a different peace with a worse number.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Sam's contentment with a
lower score functions, in this scene, as its own kind of answer Chloe
doesn't want — not a diagnosis but a demonstration that the plateau doesn't
have to be a crisis.

### Scene 12 — The bad Tuesday (November, the mat room, then dinner) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe has an unusually short self-defense round and
spends the day recalibrating against her peers' numbers.

**Content:** Her average that year is forty-one seconds against forty-five
the year before; a bad Tuesday session in November gets her only thirty-
eight on the first round. Kavi says the first round is always short and
recovers by the third; forty comes on the second, thirty-nine on the third.
Afterward she works the tape on her wraps, answers Odile badly on the way
out, then finds her at dinner to answer properly; she works out that Odile
is up seven on last year and Sam up four, while boys who used to finish
behind her are now ahead, including one she's beaten every year since age
ten.

**Possible purpose:** may exist as the self-defense section's own version of
the range plateau — a number going the wrong way for reasons that aren't
immediately about her own technique.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building toward Scene 13's
answer from Kowalczyk about why (puberty timing, not skill loss).

### Scene 13 — Kowalczyk's answer (Friday, the mat room door) — 6 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe presses Kowalczyk for an explanation and gets
one, though not one she finds satisfying.

**Content:** Kowalczyk confirms "that's about the size of it"; Chloe presses,

again at eighteen when the boys finish growing. Chloe calls this "not an
answer" and asks what she's supposed to do; Kowalczyk says the only answer
there is: learn faster than they do, then sends her back in.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give Chloe (and the reader) the one
explanation in this whole chapter — range plateau included — that actually
resolves into something concrete, in contrast to Voss's and Ruth's
non-answers.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that unlike the range plateau,
which stays unsolved through the end of the chapter, the self-defense
decline has a named, external cause (growth timing) that isn't really about
her at all.

## 4. The chapter whole

**Scene count and average length.** I found 34 scenes across 101 paragraphs,
an average of about 3.0 paragraphs per scene — lower than Chapter 17's
already-high 4.0. Twenty-two of the thirty-four scenes are two paragraphs or
fewer; fourteen are a single paragraph.

**Is this the vignette problem?** As with Chapter 17, I think the raw number
says yes far more strongly than the reading experience does, for a similar
reason: most of the single-paragraph "scenes" here are compact stops along a
longer, coherent thread (the range plateau runs from Scene 3 through Scene
11, nine scenes total, and each one is a different person's attempt at
answering the same open question — Voss, Ruth, Sam — none of whom actually
answers it). The money thread (Scenes 16–21) and the phone-security thread
(Scenes 27–28) work the same way. Where I think this chapter comes closer to
true vignette trouble than Chapter 17 is the driving arc at the end (Scenes
29–33): five scenes, each a different Sunday, with comparatively little
change in what's being shown beyond incremental competence — this is the
one stretch of the chapter where I'd say the brief's actual warning
("a string of short disconnected pieces") comes closest to applying,
though even there a repeated object (the coffee cup, present in four of the
five scenes) already does some of the work a merge would do. Hedging this
conclusion at the same level as Chapter 17's.

**Concrete merges, with what carries the seam:**

1. **Scenes 1–2** (the turret; where the line comes from) are already
   bridged by the line itself, which is the one thing present in both
   scenes — said on the range, then repeated at dinner tables, then traced
   back to its source. One scene, rather than two, would cost nothing.

2. **Scenes 3–4** (weeks of zero; first hits and the climb) are already one
   continuous progression with no real break — "For weeks every card...
   reads zero" flows directly into "Odile gets the first" with the same
   subject (the group's collective progress) carrying across.
