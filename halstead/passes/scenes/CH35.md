# Scene audit: Chapter 35, "Nine Minutes"

Method follows `passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. Read alongside `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`,
`passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, `CLAUDE.md`. Everything below is a guess at intent, not a
claim about it, and no verdict here is praise. All line numbers refer to
`chapters/35_nine_minutes.md` as currently written; every quotation below was
copied from that file and checked against it with a direct grep before this
document was finalized (see Section 5).

## Rule used for scenes inside the chat lines

This chapter alternates embodied narration with short runs of name-prefixed
chat lines (`eli:`, `ruth:`, `chloe:`, `kavi:`, `sam:`, `theo:`). The rule I
used, matching how earlier scene-audit passes in this project (CH24, CH27,
CH32, CH34) appear to have treated the same device: **a run of chat lines is
one scene, not several, for as long as it reads as one continuous, live
exchange, even though the people typing it are shown in different physical
rooms** — the shared thread is the "place," the same way a single phone call
is one scene despite two people in two rooms. A new chat scene starts only
where the text marks a real gap (a new date, a section break with time having
passed, or a return to embodied narration followed later by an unrelated
chat). A narration paragraph that leads directly into a chat with no gap, no
new location for the same character, and no new action beyond the act of
typing (Ruth's paragraph at line 63, ending "she types into the chat instead
of the clause," immediately followed by her own first chat line at 65) is
treated as the opening of the same scene as the chat that follows it, not as
a separate scene, since nothing changes but the medium. A narration paragraph
that instead reports a distinct, separately located character's own action in
response to the same event (Kavi's own logs, lines 17-19, following Eli's
paragraph at lines 5-15) is treated as its own scene, since it is a different
place, a different person's independent action, and a real gap in time (hours,
not the pause between messages).

**Scene count: 9. Total paragraphs across the nine scenes: 40 (42 total
paragraph-lines in the chapter, less the 2-paragraph frame at lines 29-31).
Average scene length: 40 / 9 ≈ 4.4 paragraphs.** See Section 3 for how
paragraphs were counted, including chat lines counted one-per-line as
dialogue.

This is a short chapter (2,000 words) built almost entirely from very short
units: five of the nine scenes (2, 3, 4, 5, 7) run three paragraphs or fewer.
Read on its own, cut into nine pieces at under five paragraphs apiece, this
looks like it could be the vignette problem the brief warns about. But the
shortness here looks patterned rather than accidental: three near-identical
incidents (June, the second Tuesday of July, "the third time") each get a
Kavi-beat and/or an Eli-beat and a chat, and the beats appear to get shorter
each time the incident repeats — incident one gets a full multi-paragraph
scene for Eli (Scene 1) and a full one for Kavi (Scene 2); incident two
compresses each character's reaction to a single paragraph (Scenes 4 and 5);
incident three drops to one paragraph total, Eli only, no separate Kavi beat
and no chat immediately attached (Scene 7). I'd call this a possible
compression device tracking the group's own habituation to the anomaly
(each repeat costing the characters, and the prose, less attention) rather
than a vignette failure, though I'm hedging that reading; see Section 4.

### Scene 1 — The first missed check-in (lines 5-15)

**What appears to happen:** Late on a Thursday in June, Eli notices the
worm's routine check-in is nine minutes late. He pulls three independent
logs, finds nothing wrong in any of them, checks the project's own
"noticed" clause against what happened, and concludes on his own reading
that nothing here trips it. He messages Kavi.

**Content, in order:** Eli's desk and the project described; the missed
check-in and its on-time correction nine minutes later; his reasoning for
wanting more than one log before saying anything; three logs pulled, no
error found in any; the "noticed" clause reread against the night's events;
his own conclusion that the clause doesn't apply; he opens the thread to
Kavi.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing Eli's habit of verifying before
reacting, and the clause itself, as things the rest of the chapter is going
to keep testing.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; possibly setting up that the
in-world legal/procedural definition of "noticed" is going to matter more
than the emotional read of the event, since Eli goes to the document before
he goes to a person.

### Scene 2 — Kavi rereads his own watcher (lines 17-19)

**What appears to happen:** Hours later, at three in the morning where he
is, Kavi checks the independent tool he built specifically to catch a
falsified clean log. It comes back clean. He starts reading his own code
instead, in case the report itself is wrong, and leaves the file open when
he goes to sleep.

**Content, in order:** the watcher's report, described as healthy; Kavi's
hour and his awake state noted as ordinary for him; the watcher's original
design purpose recalled; the report checked and found clean; he switches
to reading his own code; he decides this doesn't go on his years-long open
question list yet; the file stays open when he sleeps.

**Possible purpose:** may be a second, independent check placed right after
Eli's, letting two separate verification methods both come back clean before
the chapter moves to the chat.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, may be showing a difference in
method between Eli (checks a legal document) and Kavi (checks his own
tooling and then his own code), without either being told to the reader as a
contrast.

### Scene 3 — The chat, first incident (lines 21-25)

**What appears to happen:** Eli reports his theory to the chat: someone
found the tool, ran it, and cleaned up well enough to leave no trace of the
cleanup. Ruth asks if it could be a government actor; Eli says no, reasoning
that a government would have moved to custody by now rather than handing the
tool back.

**Content, in order:** Eli states the theory; Ruth's question; Eli's answer
and reasoning.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to put the group's working theory on
the page in the fewest possible lines before the chapter moves to the six
weeks between incidents.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 4 — The second incident, Kavi's count (line 33)

**What appears to happen:** Six weeks later, on the second Tuesday of July,
the check-in misses again. Kavi, no longer trusting the watcher's own
schedule, catches it within two minutes himself. The gap is nine minutes
again, exactly. He logs the timestamps and finds no pattern in the interval
between incidents.

**Content, in order:** the date; Kavi's own polling; the identical
nine-minute gap; the timestamp log; the two intervals compared; no pattern
found; the negative result recorded anyway.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing that the repeat of the exact same
number, not a different one, is what unsettles Kavi specifically, since he's
the one built to notice a pattern and here there isn't one.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 5 — Eli rechecks the clause (line 35)

**What appears to happen:** Eli checks the "noticed" clause again. It reads
the same as it did in June. He goes back to work.

**Content, in order:** the recheck; the same result; back to work.

**Possible purpose:** may be a deliberately minimal beat, showing the second
check costing Eli almost nothing next to the first one's multi-paragraph
treatment in Scene 1.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; see the chapter-whole note on
the three incidents shrinking as they repeat.

### Scene 6 — The chat, second incident (lines 37-55)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe reads the pattern as a message: whoever
this is could have killed the tool and didn't, and gave it back. Eli
disagrees at length; his read is that whoever it is made a mistake and
underestimates how carefully the group is watching. Kavi backs Eli's read,
noting that only a tool built the way his was would have caught this at
all.

**Content, in order:** Chloe's message theory in two lines; Eli's flat "no";
Eli's reasoning, three options he says a person who found a tool like this
actually has; his conclusion that whoever it is doesn't expect to be caught;
Kavi's confirmation that ordinary tooling wouldn't have caught it, his
watcher being the exception; Eli's restated conclusion, "he's not
signalling. he made a mistake."

**Possible purpose:** may be laying two competing readings of the same
event, unresolved between the characters, side by side.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 7 — The third incident (line 57)

**What appears to happen:** In hotter, later July, the check-in misses a
third time. Eli catches it before Kavi's watcher does. The gap runs longer
than before, then resolves the same way as always. Eli keeps working through
the day regardless.

**Content, in order:** the heat and the hour described; Eli catches the
miss; the longer-than-usual gap; the report lands unchanged; he keeps
working the rest of the day.

**Possible purpose:** may be the compressed final iteration of the
three-incident pattern, given only one paragraph and one character, no chat
attached to it directly (the chat about it, if any, isn't shown before the
chapter moves on).

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 8 — Ruth's evening and the chat (lines 61-83)

**What appears to happen:** Ruth, on the floor of her apartment, rereads the
group's own shutdown clause (any one of them can end the project
unilaterally) looking for a flaw in it and doesn't find one. She then types
into the group chat instead. The group agrees, in the chat, to do nothing:
not patch the tool, not move it, not act on knowing they're being watched.
Theo raises the possibility of other mistakes they haven't caught. The chat
closes with each of three people (Theo, Sam, Ruth) shown briefly in their
own separate rooms.

**Content, in order:** Ruth's location and the rules document described;
her close rereading of the shutdown clause; it holds; she opens the chat
instead; "so he doesnt know we know"; agreement to do nothing, spelled out
by Eli in three parts; Theo's question about other missed mistakes; the chat
ends; Theo closes his laptop, Sam sets his phone down and returns to
folding laundry, Ruth's screen dims while she is already back on the rules
document.

**Possible purpose:** may be pairing the in-world safety mechanism (the
shutdown clause) with the group's actual decision (do nothing, on purpose),
letting the reader see that the clause exists and is deliberately not being
used yet.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; possibly that Ruth checks the
exit door exists before agreeing to a plan that doesn't use it, without the
narration saying that's what she's doing.

### Scene 9 — Chloe reads and goes to work (lines 87-93)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe reads the chat at one in the morning. She
reads it once, puts the phone down, and goes to sleep on the version of the
night that stops there. The next day she goes to her job as usual.

**Content, in order:** the warm night, window cracked; Chloe reading at 1
a.m. with a short night ahead; her badge and building routine described;
the phone goes face down after one read; she chooses sleep over following
it further; the next day, she goes to work.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's intended closing beat, mirroring
Eli's own two "goes back to work" beats (lines 15... actually not stated
there, but lines 35 and, implicitly, 57) with Chloe doing the equivalent for
her own job — each of them absorbing the incident and returning to ordinary
routine rather than acting on it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, possibly extending the
same pattern of normalized non-reaction that Scenes 5 and 7 already show for
Eli, now shown once more for Chloe.

### Scene 1 (lines 5-15)

**P1 (5).** *"The worm goes quiet on a Thursday in June."* No issue. Thin
(single short dateline sentence); this matches a pattern of short dateline
openers used elsewhere in the book (e.g. the chapter 7 audit notes the same
device at several Part openings), so I'm not recommending a merge; see
Section 3.

**P2 (7).** *"...the thing usually catches itself inside a minute, quietly,
the way it always has. The sentence he's typing gets finished first, the
two-finger tap against the desk edge running ahead of it the way it always
does..."* Both clauses are camera-plausible habit description, not glossing
a meaning, so I'm not flagging either alone. The two "the way it always
[has/does]" constructions land in the same paragraph, close enough together
that it may read as a tic on a second pass. Low-confidence flag, redundancy
category.

**P3 (9).** *"A check-in doesn't arrive. But nine minutes later, everything
is precisely where it should be. State, position, byte for byte what it
was."* No issue; concrete, camera-level.

**P4 (11).** *"The gap reads as a question rather than a verdict."* This
reads as Eli's own framing of the event rather than a camera image, closer
to free indirect thought than to a narrator statement about what the reader
should conclude; I'd call it a borderline case, hedged, and would not force
a fix without more certainty. *"Because a single log is a story somebody
wrote, and logs that agree read closer to a fact."* This sentence opens with
"Because" and has no independent main clause of its own; on its own terms
this is a fragment, similar in shape to the no-main-verb defects chapter 1
turned up, though it may be intended to read as a continuation of Eli's own
clipped reasoning rather than a slip. Flag, grammar category, moderate
confidence given the possible stylistic intent.

**P5 (13).** *"Eli pulls the logs. No error, no restart, no gap."* Clipped
fragment list, reads as a deliberate technical-log rhythm rather than a
grammar defect (compare the protected "lists" device in `DO_NOT_FLAG.md`,

inventory). No issue.

**P6 (15).** *"...he reads the clause the way he'd read a spec he was trying

*"On the only reading he can defend to himself, that's where tonight

## 4. The chapter whole

Nine scenes at an average of 4.4 paragraphs is short even by the standard
of a chapter this size (2,000 words, near the book's floor), and five of the
nine scenes are three paragraphs or fewer. Read in isolation this is the
shape the brief calls vignettes. I don't think it's the accidental version
of that problem here, for the reason given in Section 1: the chapter runs
the same incident three times (June, the second Tuesday of July, the hot
afternoon later in July), and the paragraph count given to each iteration
drops each time — 6+2+3 paragraphs for incident one (Scenes 1-3), 1+1+10 for
incident two (Scenes 4-6, though the ten includes the group's whole debate
about what it means), and 1 for incident three (Scene 7) with no immediately
attached Kavi beat or chat. If that shrinking is deliberate, it reads like
the group's own attention to the anomaly thinning out across the summer
even as the anomaly itself stays exactly as regular (nine minutes, twice to
the second). I'm hedging this as a possible pattern, not a confirmed one.

**A real combination candidate: Scenes 4 and 5 (lines 33 and 35).** Both are
one-paragraph reactions to the same specific event (the second incident),
back to back, with no time gap stated between them and no section break.
The only thing marking them as two scenes under the brief's test is the
implied change of location from Kavi to Eli. A merge here would read as one
paragraph narrating both checks in sequence rather than two separate
single-paragraph beats — for instance, letting Kavi's timestamp-and-pattern
work run directly into "Eli checks the noticed clause again anyway" in the
same paragraph, since nothing currently bridges them but the shared subject.
I'd flag this as a considered, not fully confident, merge candidate: it
would match the shrinking pattern I've described above (folding the two
already-thin incident-two beats into one even thinner one), but it would
also erase the one structural thing that currently distinguishes incident
one's treatment (two full separate scenes, Eli then Kavi) from incident
two's (already two much shorter scenes) — merging them removes a rung of
that ladder rather than just shortening it. I'd leave this to the author's
read of whether the ladder is supposed to have three rungs or two.

**Not a merge, a correction already reflected above: Scene 8 is one scene,
not two, despite crossing the medium from embodied narration into chat.**
Ruth's own paragraph (61-63) ends "she types into the chat instead of the
clause," and the very next line is her own first chat message (65); nothing
changes but the medium, so I've counted this as one scene throughout
Section 1 rather than splitting it, consistent with the chat rule described
above.

I looked again at whether Scene 1 and Scene 2 (Eli's initial check, then
Kavi's) should also merge, on the same logic as the Scene 4/5 candidate
above. I don't think they meet the same bar: Scene 1 ends with Eli opening
the thread to Kavi, but Scene 2 opens hours later ("It's three in the
morning where Kavi actually is"), a real time gap rather than an immediate
sequential beat, and Scene 2 is a full multi-sentence scene on its own
rather than a single thin paragraph. I'm not proposing to touch this pair.

I don't see a scene here that seems removable outright; the three-incident
structure and the two chat debates each look like they're carrying distinct
content (a first theory, then a competing pair of readings of the same
event; a first check of the shutdown clause and the group's actual decision
to leave the tool alone).
