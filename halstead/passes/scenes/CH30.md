# Scene audit: Chapter 30, "Cleared"

Read before writing: `CLAUDE.md`, `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` (rule 1 in particular), and
`passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`. Nothing in this chapter looks to me like it touches a
`DO_NOT_FLAG.md` ruling directly.

Settled going in, per instruction, not raised below: the dialogue-only ", because"
construction; questions punctuated as periods (fixed book-wide); the six retired
trailing-clause examples in `HOUSE_RULES.md` (repaired, not hunted, not flagged for
resemblance); "content" at line 59 as the noun it is. Also per instruction: the
"Hebrew is the second year's" paragraph (line 19) and the two added languages in the list
Chloe gives Whitaker (line 57, "Polish and Hebrew") are newly added and protected — I've
described their place in the chapter below but proposed no change to either, and neither
appears in Feedback.

One method note up front: I did not take the three `________________` marks as scene
boundaries. A scene is one time, one place, one action, whichever way the break marks
fall. Two of this chapter's four sections turn out to hold more than one thing happening
in them.

## Scene count, up front

**6 scenes, plus 9 paragraphs of connective/summary material that I don't think are
scenes** (the blog-growth summary at the top, the protected language-study paragraph, a
one-sentence frame at the start of the background-check section, and the closing coda
line). 58 total paragraphs in the chapter; 49 of them sit inside the 6 scenes, the other 9
are connective. Average scene length before merges: 49 / 6 ≈ **8.2 paragraphs**. That
number is carried almost entirely by one outlier: the October interview at 35 paragraphs
against a November interview at 8 and four short scenes at 1-3 paragraphs each.

I don't think this chapter has the vignette problem as the brief describes it. It reads to
me as a fairly deliberate shape: a wash of summary narration (blog, languages, the exam)
establishing that a huge amount of unremarkable competence has piled up, funneling into
one long dramatized scene (the October interview) that cashes all of it in line by line,
with a shorter second sitting and a one-line payoff after. The risk isn't fragmentation —
it's that the summary material at the front is doing very similar work in several
different registers before the real scene starts, which is where I'd look for a cut.

## Scene 1 — The Foreign Service exam (lines 21-25)

**Location:** the testing center; the parking lot outside it.
**Time:** one day, three weeks after Chloe's twentieth birthday.

**Content:** Chloe sits the exam in a room of carrels, works through it without visibly
checking the clock the way the candidates around her do, and afterward drinks a coffee
standing in the parking lot. The result arrives weeks later by mail.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mostly as a hinge — closing out the languages-and-study
material that precedes it and opening the door to the background check that follows,
without itself needing to carry much weight.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that the exam itself has stopped being an
event that could go either way for her by this point in the book — the room and the other
candidates are described in more detail than her own experience of the questions is.

### Paragraph walk

1. "Three weeks after she turns twenty she sits the Foreign Service exam...before letting
   her sit down." — No issue.
2. "It's four hours, in a room built for finishing a thought all the way through...the
   morning already behind her by the time the cup is empty." — "The way you enjoy a
   crossword" is the only second-person aside I found in either of my two chapters; it may
   just be an idiomatic "you" rather than an address to the reader, but it's a genuine
   outlier in voice and worth a second look. Low-medium confidence.
3. "The result arrives by mail weeks later, and she reads it as having sat an exam." —
   Unclear to me on a plain read: "reads it as having sat an exam" doesn't parse cleanly
   as a description of her reaction to a result. It may be reaching for flatness (getting
   the result costs her no more than sitting it did), but the sentence doesn't land that
   reading without effort. See Feedback.

### Paragraph count

3 paragraphs, none thin enough to merge on their own. Resulting count: **3**, unchanged.

## Scene 2 — Preparing for the visit (lines 31-33)

**Location:** the kitchen counter, then the apartment generally.
**Time:** one evening for the paperwork; the Friday night before the visit, in October, for
the tidying.

**Content:** Chloe fills out the SF-86 questionnaire alone at the kitchen counter one
evening. Whitaker calls the second week of October to set a time; the Friday night before,
she straightens the apartment on her own initiative.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show her preparing for the file to be checked before
the reader sees anyone doing the checking — one long form filled out carefully, one
tidying pass nobody asked for.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that she takes the process seriously on her
own terms before Whitaker ever arrives, rather than only in front of him.

### Paragraph walk

2. "Then he calls, the second week of October, to set a time; the Friday night before goes

   with no working subject or verb for its apparent subject). See Feedback.

### Paragraph count

## Scene 3 — The October interview (lines 35-103)

**Location:** Chloe's kitchen table, in her apartment.
**Time:** continuous, one sitting, a morning into noon in October.

**Content:** Whitaker arrives at eight and interviews Chloe for the background
investigation: how the file will be handled, her residency, when and how she came to
Halstead, the languages she speaks, the classes she took and the one student who never
handed in his work, the school's internal bar exam, two published papers, and a third,
unpublished one she declines to describe on another researcher's instruction. He leaves at
noon having told her the file is otherwise complete once she's answered the last question.

**Possible purpose:** may be doing most of the chapter's real work — establishing, through
one long recorded conversation, everything about Halstead and Chloe's history that the
clearance process would actually need to verify, in a form that reads as an interview
rather than a recap.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that Chloe's candor with Whitaker is
conditional rather than blanket — she gives him more than the minimum on almost
everything, and is precise and immovable on the one thing she's been asked to hold back,
which may be the point being tested against her character generally.
