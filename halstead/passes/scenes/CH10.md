# Chapter 10 — April: scene audit

Read-only. Nothing in `chapters/10_april.md` is touched by this file. Every
quotation below was copied from the current text of the chapter and checked
against it before being included. Per the standing rulings I was given: the
", because" construction in dialogue is not raised anywhere below (it appears
often in this chapter, roughly eighteen times, but it is already catalogued
elsewhere), and I looked for a question punctuated as a period and did not
find one in this chapter (the one line that reads like it could be, "When you
can do Central Asia in one go.", is the teacher's answer to a question, not a
question itself, so I read the period as correct).

## Scenes

This chapter has three `________________` marks in it, but they do not line
up cleanly with where the real scene boundaries fall — one break sits at a
genuine boundary, the other two sit inside a single scene. The chapter also
covers far more calendar time than most (an arrival day in April through a
departure in late June), so several of what follow are less single dramatized
scenes than compressed, iterative survey passages with a dated exchange or two
dropped into them. I have numbered those too, and flagged which ones I think
are doing that job rather than functioning as a scene proper, because I think
that distinction matters more for this chapter than for a single-day one.

### Scene 1 — Arrival (16 paragraphs)

Corridor, then the third-floor room. One afternoon in April, the day Chloe
arrives.

**Content:** Sam runs down the hall and hits her hard enough that she drops
her bag; Ruth needles her about having said "probably not" back in February
and takes the bag anyway; they climb the stairs to the third floor, past a
bowl of apples. The room has a bed, desk, window, and a roommate, Fen, mid-
arranging a windowsill of rocks by size. Fen explains the wall-decorating and
radiator facts, then goes back to her rocks. Ruth talks Chloe through camp
gossip while she unpacks; Chloe looks out at the grounds for what seems to be
the first time since arriving; the envelope from home goes into the desk
drawer under a folder. Chloe starts a notebook page of names, spellings, and
phone numbers, gets Ruth's surname and number, and leaves a line for Sam. Kavi
arrives an hour later, aggrieved that Sam let him search the whole building
for her instead of telling him she'd arrived; Ruth says Sam forgot; Kavi
argues that forgetting, in this case, was itself a decision. Chloe asks for
his surname to finish the list.

**Possible purpose:** may be introducing the four-person unit (Sam, Ruth, Fen,
Kavi) and the two physical objects — the notebook, the envelope — that the
chapter comes back to at the end.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that the first thing Chloe does
in a new place is build a small piece of order around it (the list of names),
which is possibly meant as a character note, though see Feedback on how close
this and a couple of later paragraphs come to the "Chloe files that" problem
the brief calls out.

### Scene 2 — First dinner (4 paragraphs)

The dining hall. That evening.

**Content:** A description of the hall's acoustics; Chloe counts sixteen
familiar faces and a note on how many of the original ninety camp kids came
back over the years. A boy asks if she did the camp bridge; she says no. Sam
shows her a stuck window, then walks past the noticeboards without looking at
them. On the way back with the trays she asks Ruth about Owen, who tells her
he left in the second week, wouldn't do "the water thing," and that more left
after him. The scene ends on a line saying Chloe already knew the number
Ruth's about to give her, because she'd counted it herself before Ruth said
anything.

**Possible purpose:** may be opening the Owen thread that Scene 7 closes, and
may be a second instance (after Scene 1's notebook) of Chloe privately keeping
a count of something.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that she let Ruth tell her
something she already knew rather than say so, which could be an early,
low-stakes instance of the compression the calibration note describes,
though the prose does not say this is what is happening.

### Scene 3 — First night (8 paragraphs)

The dorm room, lights out. That same night.

**Content:** The corridor lights go out; Fen appears to drop off mid-sentence.
Chloe lies awake waiting for the promised radiator noise and tests whether Fen
is actually asleep. Fen answers and demonstrates, in the dark, how she orders
the rocks by size and (when two are close) by weight. Chloe asks why she
bothers if the room turns over every year; Fen says she's not leaving them
behind, she's taking them and starting the sill again wherever she goes next.
Fen falls asleep; the radiator performs twice while Chloe stays awake for
both.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give Fen her own logic (portable, ordered,
hers alone) as something distinct from Chloe's, and to close day one on a
concrete sensory beat rather than a summary line.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Fen's answer about the
rocks — not staying, just taking them along — has some resonance for a girl
who has just left her own house, though nothing in the scene draws that line
itself, and I could be reading a parallel into it that isn't there.

### Scene 4 — The first-term routine (11 paragraphs, largely a survey)

Multiple locations (dining hall, pool, kitchen classroom, Spanish room, math
room) across the following weeks, not one time or place.

**Content:** The daily bell schedule and subject list. Breakfast is louder
than dinner; a running bit about tunneling out; Sam and Ruth's chair-and-spoon
history. Swimming: the pool is warm, the retired-Olympic-swimmer teacher
narrates her stroke before he says it, and gets in to demonstrate in week two;
Priya's aside about the teacher's Olympic background. Cooking, chosen because
Ruth is in it: knife work, and a running joke about Chloe's onion being cut
unevenly, with the teacher and (apparently) Chloe exchanging lines and Ruth
explaining afterward why he says it every week. Spanish, described as
immersion from the first minute, with two dated instances (that Monday, on
Wednesday). A general account of how math is taught here (the teacher tells
you whether an answer is true and stops there; "good" isn't his question),
contrasted once with how fractions were taught at camp. A closing line that
the first weeks go badly everywhere, resolving by the fourth week into being
behind in four subjects and caught up in one — literature — which sets up
Scene 5.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to compress an unshowable stretch (settling
into a full course load over weeks) while keeping a few small, dated
exchanges dramatized rather than only reported.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be less about any one subject and
more about establishing that everything is hard here at first, as a setup for
the two subjects (literature, math) that get full scenes of their own next.

### Scene 5 — The literature exchange and the library (6 paragraphs)

The literature classroom on "the eighth day," then the library, general,
over the following weeks.

**Content:** Chloe waits for a reading list that never comes; on the eighth
day she asks about it and is told there is no list — read whatever she wants,
write about it, bring back what she borrows and admit it if she loses one. A
description of the library itself (much larger than her home library, no
card, no borrowing limit) and how her own habits with it change over the
following weeks, settling into whatever she can carry in one hand.

**Possible purpose:** may be the clearest instance in the chapter of an adult
answering Chloe's intensity with an actual answer instead of a rule, which
this reads to me as a deliberate contrast with the corkboard/writing-teacher
half of Scene 7, where the institution stays vague on purpose.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that this is one of the places
at Halstead that gives her more than she was asking for, rather than a
boundary — hedged, since the scene doesn't say this outright.

### Scene 6 — The math epiphany (6 paragraphs)

The math classroom, a specific Wednesday in the third week of May into the
next morning.

**Content:** A wheel-rolling problem occupies her for days; it comes to her in
the shower; she gets to the classroom early the next morning, where Ruth is
already at the board. They compare methods — Ruth's is shorter but only works
if the mark starts at the top; Chloe's is longer but works from any starting
point. Ruth tells her to do it again from the start, slower. The scene closes
on a line that this becomes the hour Chloe gets up for.

**Possible purpose:** may be paired with Scene 5 as the chapter's two
positive academic scenes, and may exist to show Ruth correcting Chloe's work
on its merits rather than only being a friend to her.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that getting the right answer
isn't the end of the exchange here — Ruth still sends her back to redo it —
which could be part of establishing what rigor looks like at this school,
though again this is a guess at intent.

### Scene 7 — Working out who gets sent home (10 paragraphs)

Two locations under one continuous inquiry: the second-floor corkboard and
the writing teacher, then — later — a stairwell with Kavi.

**Content:** Chloe tries to work out from a corkboard (three notices, none
about anybody leaving) what actually gets a student sent home. She asks the
writing teacher, who tells her people leave because their families want them
home, not for "being bad at something." Later she asks Kavi on the stairs; he
describes seeing Owen crying by the kitchens on a Thursday and his mother's
car outside on the Saturday, and is short with her for having already asked
Ruth the same question, in April, at the trays.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to close the Owen thread Scene 2 opened, from
two angles — the institution's general policy, then one peer's specific,
eyewitness account — and Kavi's irritation at the end may be there to
register that her asking has become noticeable to the people around her.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may connect to the calibration note
about a strong sense of fairness that sometimes reads a situation and lands
on the wrong question, since what she's actually stuck on ("what happened to
him") is a smaller question than the one everyone keeps answering ("that his
mom came"). The text doesn't flag this gap itself; I'm inferring it from
Kavi's answer not actually landing.

### Scene 8 — Sunday phone calls (9 paragraphs)

The first-floor alcove phone. General routine (the calls shortening over
several weeks), narrowing into one specific, dramatized call.

**Content:** A note that the first Sunday call ran fifty minutes and the
fourth ran twenty. Then a specific call: her mother asks if she's really
eating (Ruth makes her finish her tray); asks if her roommate is nice (Fen's
asleep early, so Chloe basically has the room to herself, which makes her
mother laugh longer than the line seems to deserve); then asks if she's
happy, not just doing fine. Chloe sits on the alcove floor and, before
answering, the prose lists the pool, Ruth, and the library rather than naming
a feeling. She says "Yes."

**Possible purpose:** may be a checkpoint on the decision chapter 9 ended
on — specifically on the mother's own unresolved hesitation at the end of
that chapter, since it's her question and her silence afterward that the
scene is built around.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that the answer to "happy" is
delivered as a list of concrete things rather than a named state, which
looks to me like it's doing the work the house rules ask for instead of
naming the feeling directly — though I want to flag that as a read on a
formal choice, not something the text states.

### Scene 9 — Second-term subjects (4 paragraphs, a smaller survey)

The history/geography classroom. Weeks, running up to the end of May.

**Content:** Second term starts; history splits into political history and
freehand map drawing, with no textbook and no test. The teacher's only stated
standard is "when you can do Central Asia in one go." Chloe struggles with
the order of the five "-stans"; Ruth can already do the whole map, including
small islands. Chloe starts a full list of subjects, hours, and dates in the
back of her notebook, and notices that asking other students how long their
own list would be always sounds like she's measuring herself against them, so
she doesn't ask.

**Possible purpose:** may be a smaller, quieter echo of Scene 4's survey,
timed to show incremental competence (the map) against Ruth's greater one,
and it returns to the notebook object from Scene 1.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing her editing a question
before asking it because of how it would land on the people around her,
which reads to me as the "reads social situations" side of the calibration
note rather than the rule-bound side — again, my inference, not a stated
point.

### Scene 10 — Departure (7 paragraphs)

The dorm room, then the front steps, then the car. The last week of June.

**Content:** Fen checks whether the newest rock is still fourth from the end
while Chloe finishes packing; Fen's rocks go into a shoebox. Chloe retrieves
the envelope from under the folder in the same drawer it went into in Scene 1
and packs it. Ruth walks her down to the front steps, where Sam is already
outside; Ruth talks about her summer at her cousin's; Chloe promises to call
on Wednesdays, not before ten because of Ruth's father's sleep. They trade
"September" back and forth. In the car, Chloe reopens the notebook to check
the three names and numbers before holding it on her lap for the drive home.

**Possible purpose:** may be closing the chapter by paying off the two
objects Scene 1 opened (the envelope, the notebook), giving the year a frame
that closes where it started.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be suggesting continuity across the
summer gap (the promised Wednesday calls, the rocks going with Fen rather
than staying behind, the envelope surviving the whole year unopened and
unmentioned in this chapter) rather than an ending — hedged, since the prose
never comments on any of this directly, and I don't know what's in the
envelope or whether it matters to this chapter at all.

looked wrong to me. I have not repeated a note every time a paragraph
contains a ", because" construction in dialogue; it's in a large share of the

left it out entirely rather than flag it as new each time.

## The chapter whole

This chapter's structure is different in kind from a single-day chapter like
the one the brief describes chapter 1 becoming (thirteen sections down to
five): it spans an arrival day in April through a departure at the end of
June, and roughly half its scenes (4 and 9, and parts of 5, 7, and 8) are
iterative survey passages rather than one-time dramatized scenes. Applying
the "nine pages wants four to six scenes" guideline directly would, I think,
undercount what's actually happening here, since a chapter covering a school
term is a different shape of object than a chapter covering one evening.

That said, does each scene need to exist:

**Scenes 4 and 9** are both curriculum-survey passages doing structurally the
same job (a tour of what's being taught, with a small dated exchange or two)
at two different points in the year. On a first pass this looked to me like
the kind of "two scenes doing the same job" the brief flags for merging. I
don't think a literal merge works, though, because they can't sit next to
each other without cutting everything chronologically between them (Scenes
5–8), and because Scene 9 is doing real, separate work: it's the one place
Ruth's greater competence (the whole map, small islands and all) sits next to
Chloe's struggle (the wrong order of the same five countries every time), and
it returns to the notebook object from Scene 1. I'm not proposing this merge,
but flagging the echo since a reader moving quickly through the chapter twice
might feel like they're back in the same kind of paragraph.

**Scene 7's two halves** (the corkboard/writing-teacher exchange, then Kavi on
the stairs) are already written back to back with no break between them, so
there's no seam left to close — the prose has effectively already merged
them into one continuous inquiry. I'm not proposing anything further here.

**Scenes 2 and 7** both turn on Owen, from different angles (a policy answer,
then an eyewitness account), which reads to me as closer to chapter 9's
Scene 3/Scene 6 pattern (same territory, different job) than to a genuine
duplicate, so I'm not proposing a merge there either.

I don't think any scene here should be cut outright. If I had to name the one
place I'd look hardest before deciding that, it's Scene 7: the "who gets sent
home" inquiry runs across two locations and ends without Chloe getting an
answer to the actual question she asked (see the paragraph-walk note on Kavi's
closing lines), which might mean the scene is doing exactly what it's meant to
(showing her stuck on the wrong question) or might mean it's spending ten
paragraphs to arrive nowhere. I can't tell which from the text alone, so I'm
flagging it rather than recommending a cut.

On the calibration note specifically: this chapter has the clearest run of
"Chloe files that"-adjacent language I've found looking at just this chapter
— the private count in Scene 2, the "list she keeps only in her head" in
Scene 7, and the self-editing-a-question beat in Scene 9 all sit close to the
same computational register the brief says to watch for. The Scene 9 instance
reads to me as the one that's actually calibrated correctly (she's reading a
social cost, not running a rule), but the other two look like they could tip
the same character into the "rule-bound" reading the author doesn't want.
See Feedback 8.
