Read before writing: `CLAUDE.md`, `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` (rule 1 in particular), and
`passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`. Nothing in this chapter appears to touch a `DO_NOT_FLAG.md` ruling

", because" construction (catalogued separately), and the six trailing-clause examples in

One correction to my own method partway through: my first pass took the seven
`________________` marks as scene boundaries, which the brief specifically warns against.
What follows is redone on the basis that a scene is a stretch of one time, one place, and one
action, whether or not a break mark agrees.

## Scene count, up front

**7 scenes, plus one 2-paragraph stretch of connective tissue that I don't think is a scene at
all** (the paperwork paragraph after the AAR — no dramatized action, no single continuous
moment, just a summary of what happens to a form over the following days). That connective
stretch is walked below but excluded from the scene average, the same way chapter 2's audit
excluded its own non-scenic summary paragraphs.

Average scene length across the 7: 106 total chapter paragraphs, minus the 2-paragraph
connective stretch, over 7 scenes = 104 / 7 ≈ **14.9 paragraphs**, before my merges. That
number is skewed by two outliers at opposite ends — the AAR at 30 paragraphs and the group
chat at 32 — against a middle four scenes running 10, 5, 15 (culvert), 3, and 9. Take the AAR
and the chat out and the other five average 8.4.

I don't think this chapter has the vignette problem the brief describes. The four
night-by-night scenes read as four different data points feeding one argument (a clean
engagement, a rule violation, an unmarked stretch of nothing, then a death by patience), and
the AAR explicitly recaps three of those four by name and number ("a two, a four and a one").
The one scene that doesn't get recapped anywhere is the shortest of the four (Scene 4, the
third night), and that's my one chapter-level merge candidate below.

## Scene 1 — Staging (lines 5-23)

**Location:** the gravel lot behind the tents, the folding table under the work light.
**Time:** continuous, the evening the exercise begins.

**Content:** Sam draws his rifle and harness, gets equipment-checked by an armorer and a
specialist, is read the exercise's engagement and hand-to-hand rules by a sergeant off a
laminated card, gets his checkpoint card, and moves out toward the tree line.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may exist mainly to put the rules of the game in front of
the reader before anyone breaks one — the five-metre minimum distance and the ban on
hand-to-hand both get tested later, so the reader may need to have heard them stated plainly
first.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly establishes the exercise as a bureaucratic,
witnessed, signed-for process (a hand receipt, a controller gun, a checked serial number)
before it becomes a physical one — a contrast the culvert scene may be leaning on.

### Paragraph walk

1. "The exercise is called Winter Anvil...weeks of Sam reading it on his way past." — No issue.
2. "Staging is a gravel lot behind a row of tents...refuses to come on." — No issue, camera.
3. "At the head of the line he draws his rifle...only then lets go of either." — Possible
   loose pronoun: "either" needs two antecedents and the sentence only clearly supplies one
   noun phrase ("the weapon") for it to pair with "the adapter." Readable on a second pass,
   low confidence this is a real defect rather than a compressed but parseable sentence.
4. "A specialist runs a controller gun...the pen skipping twice before it takes." — No issue.
   The trailing "the pen skipping twice" is an absolute construction (no finite verb by
   design), not the "no main verb" defect that paragraph 6 below has; it reads as a
   deliberate house device rather than an error.
5. "A sergeant reads him the rules off a laminated card...to bring a casualty back." — Two
   sentences. First: reported speech rather than quoted dialogue, listing the actual
   engagement rules. This is possibly an instance of "narrated speech that should be spoken,"
   though converting a full rules recitation into quotation might cost the scene its pace;
   flagging at medium confidence rather than certainty. Second sentence explains a mechanic
   of the exercise (what a steady tone does) rather than a meaning of this scene, and reads
   to me as closer to necessary staging than to talking to the reader — no issue on that one.
6. "Then the card, carrying its grids of figures and listing the checkpoints between them in
   the order he is required to take them." — Grammar defect: no finite verb. "The card,
   carrying...and listing..." has a subject and two participles and never gets a main verb.
   Stands alone as its own paragraph, so there's no earlier clause to read it as elliptical
   against.
7. "'You'll be moving between these on foot, at night,'..." — No issue, dialogue.
8. "'How many of them, Sergeant?'" — No issue.
9. "'More than you.'..." — No issue.
10. "Sam checks the bearing against the card...enough to lose it." — No issue.

### Paragraph count

10 paragraphs. No thin ones I'd merge; the shortest (7-9) are dialogue exchanges between two
speakers and the new-speaker rule holds them apart. Resulting count: **10**, unchanged.

## Scene 2 — The fence line (lines 27-35)

**Location:** a fence line past a stand of pines, at the edge of a fallow field.
**Time:** continuous, a little after midnight, the first night.

**Content:** Sam approaches a fence line slowly, sees two men waiting on the far side, pulls
back into cover, watches one of them shift his weight on a repeating interval, and fires two
rounds when the pattern repeats a third time. Both harnesses go into a steady tone. He crosses
the wire and moves on.

**Possible purpose:** may be there to show a contact executed correctly and at range before
the next scene shows one executed wrong and at close quarters — the two engagements the AAR
later credits him with by name.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that Sam reads pattern and timing well when he
has distance and time to use them, which the fourth-night scene may be testing from the
opposite side (someone else's patience beating his own).

### Paragraph walk

1. "The first contact is at a fence line a little after midnight...the drill still felt like a
   game." — No issue. The closing clause about drill and childhood is background rather than
   a stated meaning of this action, and doesn't match the shape of the six retired
   trailing-clause examples (no "so," "which," "because," or "from" doing consequence work) —
   I'm reading it as a fact about Sam rather than the narrator glossing what just happened.
2. "Two men are waiting on the far side of it in the darkness...and so does he." — No issue.
3. "Then he withdraws back into the pines...stays with it through the third." — "Worth very
   little on its own" reads to me as a judgment about the weight of a piece of evidence,
   which could be the narrator evaluating for the reader, or could be Sam's own trained
   assessment rendered in free indirect style (a soldier watching for exactly this kind of
   repeat). Medium-low confidence it's a defect; "making it a rhythm" a few words later seems
   to me more clearly functional — it's the piece of information the reader needs to
   understand why he fires on the third repetition, not a stated feeling.
4. "Two rounds. The report goes out flat...too far away to hear." — No issue.
5. "Crossing the wire a hundred metres down...That is where he leaves it." — No issue. Reads
   as Sam's own flat non-reaction rather than narrator commentary.

## Scene 3 — The culvert (lines 39-67)

**Location:** a culvert under a service road, then the road directly above it.
**Time:** continuous, the following night, running maybe an hour end to end.

**Why I'm reading this as one scene and not two:** the location shifts from inside the pipe to
the road just above it, but it's the same cut of ground, no time gap opens between the
ambush and Ives's arrival, and one action runs through the whole thing — the fallout from a
single engagement. I don't think this clears the bar for a location change the way the
corridor-to-car cut would in a different chapter.

**Content:** Sam is ambushed by four men inside a culvert; a blank goes off next to his head
and every harness in the pipe (including possibly his own — the text doesn't let him tell)
goes into a steady tone at once. He fights his way out physically, injuring two men, and is
stopped on the road above by Staff Sergeant Ives. A medic treats the injured men and Sam's
split knuckles. Ives rules the engagement void and the physical contact a separate violation,
has Sam recite the contact rule, and sends him back out.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to put a real rule violation in front of the reader with
Sam clearly and admittedly in the wrong, ahead of the AAR scene where an authority figure has
to name it as one.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that the trained instinct that works at a
fence line (react to a threat with the weapon) is exactly the wrong instinct in a crowd at
zero range, and that Sam knows this before anyone tells him — his own line to the major later
("I was watching the clock instead of the pipe") is his own diagnosis, not one handed to him.

## Scene 4 — The third night (lines 71-75)

**Location:** unspecified broken ground between checkpoints.
**Time:** continuous, the third night.

**Content:** Sam moves cautiously between checkpoints, applying the caution the culvert
taught him, stopping twice for sounds that turn out harmless, and eats standing up while
still moving. His taped fingers and numbing hands slow his compass reading by the last
checkpoint.

**Possible purpose:** may be there mainly as an interval of nothing happening, between two
nights where something does — a demonstration that the caution from Scene 3 has actually
taken, shown rather than just asserted.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that endurance and vigilance cost him
something even when nothing external happens — the taped fingers and the numb hands are the
visible price of the two nights before this one.

### Paragraph walk

1. "The third night is simply walking...the entire stretch of it passes quiet." — "Its own
   particular variety of long" reads to me as the narrator characterizing the quality of the
   night rather than reporting a fact of it. Low-medium confidence; it may be reading as the
   book's ordinary flat-ironic register rather than a violation on its own.
2. "The open ground he begins taking the way the culvert taught him to take it, stopping at
   the edge of every tree line to listen before he crosses it...until he is certain enough
   to move again." — "The way the culvert taught him to take it" names the causal link
   between this scene and the one before it. Medium-high confidence this is the kind of
   logical joint house rule 1 warns against spelling out — the actions that follow (stopping
   at tree lines, going wide of cover, standing still until certain) already show the lesson
   without the label; the reader doesn't need "the culvert taught him" to draw that line.
3. "Standing, he eats out of a pouch...before he trusts it." — No issue. ("Standing" reads as
   "without stopping to sit" rather than literally motionless, given "keeping his boots
   moving throughout" in the same sentence; I don't think this is a contradiction, but it's
   a fast enough read that a second pair of eyes might disagree.)

## Scene 5 — The fourth night and the finish (lines 79-95)

**Location:** an old drainage cut, then the last checkpoint, then a tent with cots.

**Why one scene:** the location moves three times, but there's no time gap between any of the

**Content:** Sam is shot from behind by a man who has been lying still in the weeds since

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that patience is being tested as its own
separate thing from the skills the earlier nights tested, which is close to what the major
says outright in the AAR — though there the line is his to say as dialogue, not the

## The chapter whole

**Scenes found: 7**, plus the 2-paragraph connective stretch above that I don't count as one.
I don't think this chapter has the vignette problem — the four night-scenes each do a
different, specific job (a correct engagement, a rule violation, an unmarked interval, a loss
to patience), and three of the four get named and numbered again in the AAR. The one scene
that never gets referenced again anywhere in the chapter is Scene 4, the third night, which is
also the shortest of the four at 3 paragraphs.

**One merge I'd make:** fold Scene 4 (the third night, 3 paragraphs) into Scene 5 (the fourth
night and the finish) as its opening beat, rather than treating it as its own scene. What
would carry across the seam: the taped fingers and the numbness in his hands, already present
at the end of Scene 4, would carry forward as the physical state he's in when the ambush
happens in Scene 5 — the same cost that slowed his compass reading on the third night is the
cost that may be part of why he doesn't hear the man in the weeds until it's too late on the
fourth. Concretely, that means dropping the section break at line 77 and letting "Standing, he
eats out of a pouch..." run on into "On the fourth night Sam crosses a stretch of ground..."
with a time cue in place of the break (something like "The fourth night starts the same
way," rather than a new header), so the numb hands and the taped fingers are the thing that
travels across it rather than a bare cut.

I don't see a second merge candidate I'd make with the same confidence. Scenes 1 through 3
are doing three different, sequential jobs (equip, succeed, fail) and Scene 6 and 7 are doing
two different kinds of review of the same material (official, then peer) — collapsing either
pair would lose one half of what looks like a deliberate contrast rather than a repeated
anecdote.
