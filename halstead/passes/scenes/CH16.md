# Scene audit: Chapter 16, "Thirteen"

Read before writing: `CLAUDE.md`, `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` (rule 1), `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`,
`passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. Also read the two chapter-specific notes I was given directly:
the Marek grading scene (roughly lines 15-51) was already audited, wrongly reported once as
contradicting his character sheet, and confirmed to match the sheet almost word for word —
I have not re-opened that question, and I have not proposed edits inside that span. Aymar's
shared surname is a settled, deliberate choice and doesn't come up in this chapter anyway (no
Aymar appears here). Two other things I was told are closed and I haven't raised: the ",
because" construction inside dialogue, and periods standing in for question marks.

This chapter is dated *September 2018 – July 2019* in its own epigraph — it is built to cover
a school year, not a day, and that changes what "vignette problem" means here. I've still gone
scene by scene rather than trusting the three `________________` breaks (which give four
typographic sections for a chapter that turns out to contain over twenty real scenes).

## Scene count, up front

**97 paragraphs. 22 real scenes plus 8 passages that are connective summary/montage rather
than scenes** (no single time-and-place, no one action — a year-in-general statement, a
term-in-general statement, or a narrator coda closing a thread). 30 narrative units in total.

- Average length of the 22 real scenes: 88 paragraphs / 22 = **4.0 paragraphs per scene**.
- Average across all 30 units (scenes + connective passages): 97 / 30 ≈ 3.2.

Twenty-two real scenes in an ~11-page, 3,940-word chapter is more than the four-to-six the
brief's rule of thumb gives a chapter this size, even granting that a year-survey chapter
earns more scenes than a single-day one. Section 4 below is the merge proposal; the strongest
candidates are two Amberg first-day speeches doing the same job forty pages apart, and a
five-scene surveillance-and-countermeasures thread that currently reads as five separate
anecdotes instead of one escalating one.

## Section I (lines 5-53): the Marek arc

### Scene 1 — The stairs (lines 5-7, part)

**Location:** the stairs. **Time:** the first fortnight of September.

**Content:** A timetable line announces the new class. The narration explains why her cohort
is the only one teaching itself. On the stairs, Chloe works the same fact out and tells Ruth,
who shrugs and hands over half a sandwich.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may exist to open the "assessing other people" thread of
the chapter (Chloe as examiner) with the mechanics of why she's the one doing it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hard to say — the shrug-and-sandwich beat may be doing
work establishing that Ruth's register for this kind of thing is unbothered/practical, which
recurs later in the chapter (the shoebox scene).

#### Paragraph walk

1. (l.5) "A line appears on her timetable in September, new since June: *Mathematics, 12s,
   Thursday, room 4.* Under it sit seven names, and behind them a grade sheet due at the end
   of the year with hers at the top and theirs down the side." — No issue. Camera: a line on a
   page, a sheet with names on it.
2. (l.7) "Teaching starts at thirteen and you take the year below, so her cohort, being the
   oldest in the building, is the only cohort doing it, which makes the twelves the only year
   taught by another student and the thirteens the only year with no teacher of their own. On
   the stairs, in the first fortnight, she works that out, tells Ruth, and gets a shrug and
   half a sandwich." — Two things, possibly. First, "you take the year below" switches into a
   generic second person to state the school's rule directly, which is a narrator addressing
   the reader in the most literal grammatical sense rather than staying in Chloe's own
   register. Second, the "so ... which makes ..." chain hands the reader the whole deduction
   before the next sentence says Chloe "works that out" on the stairs — so the stairs beat
   proves nothing the reader doesn't already have, which is close to house rule 1's "a sentence
   that restates what the scene just dramatized," just running in the other order (explain,
   then stage the character reaching the same conclusion). See Feedback.

   camera couldn't strictly render, and "comes back ... fine" edges toward a named state. Both
   are small enough I'm not confident they're worth a slot.

## Section II (lines 55-131): the widening curriculum

### Scene 7 — The proof mark-up (lines 55-65)

**Location:** the analysis classroom (implied — a page handed back). **Time:** early in the
year, one specific returned proof, with a two-sentence coda running to February.

**Content:** Her first analysis proof comes back struck through with a margin note. She argues

**Possible purpose:** may be setting up the "receipt is not the work" idea that recurs with

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly nothing more than a straight subject-by-subject
survey beat; if there's a design, it may be that we see Chloe on the receiving end of the
"prove it, don't just believe it" standard before we see her enforce that standard on someone
else in the same chapter.

#### Paragraph walk

   what a described surface causes or means, just filling in context needed to understand the

## Section III (lines 135-187): sources, law, and the table

**Location:** wherever she reads — unspecified. **Time:** spread across December through

## 4. The chapter whole

**Scene count: 22, plus 8 connective/summary passages, 30 units, 97 paragraphs, average 4.0
paragraphs per real scene.** For a chapter this length that is more scenes than the brief's
rule of thumb suggests, even reading the September-to-July dateline as license for a wider,
more anthology-like chapter than a single-day one. It reads less like a string of vignettes in
the disconnected sense (most scenes carry a clear location and action) and more like a chapter
trying to be a syllabus — one scene per subject, one scene per friend, each visited once and
moved past. The strongest evidence for that is that four different classes (analysis,
cryptography, epistemology, game theory, law) each get their own single-visit scene, plus two
separate lab/project threads (Sanders, the encryption arms race) and three separate social
threads (Marek, the shoebox/duty-teacher/encryption sequence, the dinner table).

**Where I'd merge, concretely:**

1. **Amberg's two opening-day speeches (Scene 9, line 73, and Scene 20, lines 137-141).** Same
   character, same rhetorical device (a short numbered or listed argument for why the subject
   is taught the way it is), same function (establish his teaching philosophy before using it),
   about forty lines apart with nothing connecting them. What would carry across the seam: the
   fact that he teaches both subjects to the same room. Concretely, I'd put them on the same day
   or in back-to-back sessions and let one physical detail travel between them — he's still
   sitting on the desk from the cryptography scene, or the state code from the law scene is
   already on the desk when he starts on locks — rather than reintroducing him from nothing
   twice.

2. **The duty-teacher scene (Scene 12) into the encryption-writing scene (Scene 13).** As
   written, nothing states that the October reading-over-the-shoulder is why Kavi and Ruth build
   a countermeasure "by the end of the month" — the two currently sit as separate anecdotes that
   happen to be about the same subject. What would carry across the seam: the "end of the month"
   already in the text could simply become "by the end of October," anchoring it to the same
   month as the teacher's remark, which turns two disconnected beats into one cause and effect
   without adding a scene.

3. **Bex's breakfast plan (Scene 15) into the workshop scene (Scene 16).** These already share
   a day and a cast; what's missing is an object crossing the seam. The "good lamp already
   signed out" that Bex names at breakfast could reappear in Chloe's hands at the workshop,
   which would make the two read as one continuous Saturday rather than plan-then-cut-to-result.

4. **Five thin one-line paragraphs that could fold into their neighbors without losing
   anything I can find:** paragraph 4 ("Her own mathematics stops being about the answer,"
   l.55) into paragraph 5; paragraph 21 ("The box goes in the drawer under Ruth's window and
   stays there," l.89) into paragraph 20; paragraph 35 ("The plan works, all of it, including
   the kitchens," l.117) into paragraph 34; paragraph 46 ("Then he starts on contracts," l.141)
   into paragraph 45; paragraph 60 ("Nadia waits for him to finish," l.169) into paragraph 59 or
   61. That's 97 down to **92** if all five are taken.

**What I would not merge:** the Marek arc (settled, not mine to restructure), and the dinner
napkin scene, which is already the chapter's one long continuous scene and reads like the
model the shorter scenes elsewhere might be measured against rather than a candidate for
cutting.
