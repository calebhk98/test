# Scene audit: Chapter 24, "The Chat"

Read in order before writing this: `CLAUDE.md`, `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`, `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, `passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. This chapter is not edited anywhere in this file; it is report only.

## Rule used for what counts as a scene here

Most of this chapter is a group chat, not embodied action, so before counting I worked out what "one time, one place, one action" means when the place is a phone screen. The rule I used: **the chat is the place.** A stretch of messages on one topic, with no explicit or implied gap in time, is one scene even if the people typing it are on four continents, because the shared space the reader is watching is the thread, not any one person's room. A new scene starts where the text itself marks a jump — a dateline sentence ("Days later.", "Halfway through September", "In October"), a switch out of the chat into a physically embodied scene (the bank), or a new topic opened after the prior one has visibly closed and time has passed. A section break (`________________`) on its own is not a boundary; several of the breaks below fall inside one scene, and one scene runs straight through a break (Scene 1).

The lines like `priya: does anyone know` are dialogue — a character speaking, one line per turn, same as a quoted line of spoken dialogue elsewhere in the book. New-speaker-new-paragraph holds for them and I have not counted them as candidates for merging or as "thin paragraphs"; a one-line text message is not a thin paragraph, it is a person sending one line, which is what texting looks like. The paragraph-count work in Section 3 is aimed at the narrative paragraphs around the chat, not at the messages themselves.

I did not count the opening four paragraphs (lines 5-13, "The chat is five years old..." through "...gotten past the first word") as a scene. They cover five years and eight people's locations in general and habitual terms — no single time, no single event. It is a frame, establishing the terms the reader needs before any scene starts. It is discussed under Section 2 because it is where several of the sentence-level problems are.

## Section 1: Scenes

I found **16** scenes (including one, Scene 13, that may not deserve to be a scene at all — see below), plus the four-paragraph frame that is not a scene. Total paragraph count across all 16, counting each chat line as its own paragraph: **229**. Average scene length: **229 / 16 ≈ 14.3 paragraphs**, though this average is not very informative here — a scene that is one paragraph of narrated summary (Scene 5) and a scene that is fifty-six chat lines and narrative sentences (Scene 12) are both "one scene" by the rule above, and the paragraph count mostly tracks how many lines the two or three participants happened to type, not how much editorial weight the scene carries.

For a chapter this size (3,722 words, call it nine or ten pages), sixteen scenes is well past the four-to-six the brief names as the target shape for a chapter this length, and past even the "twelve, vignette problem" alarm point. I think the honest read is two things at once: the chapter is built as a check-in montage across a large cast that has just scattered, which by its nature wants more, shorter units than an embodied chapter does — that may be the intended shape, not a failure of scene control the way it would be in a single-location chapter. But sixteen still looks like more than the montage needs, and Section 4 below names where I think real cuts and merges are.

## Section 4: The chapter whole

Sixteen scenes (or fifteen after the one clean merge above) across roughly nine to ten pages is above the four-to-six the brief sets as the target shape, and the chapter reads, in five or six of its middle scenes (3, 6, 7, 8, 9), as a run of short check-ins with limited connective tissue between them — closer to a mosaic than to a small number of developed scenes. Whether that is the vignette problem the brief describes, or the form a check-in-with-eight-scattered-friends chapter needs, looks to me like a judgment call for the author rather than something the text itself settles, and I'd rather say that plainly than force a verdict.

Concrete moves I can point to:

1. **Scene 13 into Scene 12** (above) — a clean merge, no content lost, one fewer break, one fewer scene, and it removes the only scene in the chapter that has no character present, no dialogue, and no discrete event of its own.
2. **Scenes 1 and 2** (Eli's bounty and its deflation) do the same job with the same character in a rise-then-flat-correction shape, which is the pattern the brief specifically asks about for chapters with two scenes doing one job. I'm less confident this one should merge, because the text itself marks a real clock jump between them ("Days later"), which by the rule above makes them two scenes rather than one. If a merge is wanted anyway, what would carry across the seam is the tally itself — the count of bugs found — rather than a new sentence: the found-count could travel directly from *"turning them in now. fingers crossed for 5"* into a shorter version of the deflation beat, cutting the standalone dateline and letting the confirmation (or lack of one) land inside the same scene instead of after a fresh break.
3. **Scene 9** (the unnamed performance-review post) is the shortest and least anchored scene in the chapter — six paragraphs, no named poster, no callback elsewhere. It could stand as a deliberate anonymous beat (matching the screenshot's own blacked-out name) or it could be folded into Scene 7 (Theo's reasoning complaint), which is on the same subject — an institution asking for a legible performance it may not actually be measuring — using the shared subject as the bridge rather than a new time cue. I'd flag this one for the author's call rather than execute it myself, since I can't tell from the text which reading is intended.

No scene in the chapter looks to me like it should simply be cut outright with nothing kept from it; even the thinnest ones (5, 9, 13) are each carrying a specific fact the rest of the chapter doesn't repeat elsewhere (the Aurel omission, the retention-play line, the nineteen-thousand-listings number).

## Feedback

**"Sam thumbs the keyboard with the economy he uses on everything else, a few words and stop, because someone behind him is already waiting for the phone."**
The "because" clause explains to the reader why Sam is terse, using a fact (someone waiting for the shared phone) the frame paragraph two sentences earlier and the later Sam scene (line 201) both already establish. Comma-plus-"because" is the tell the house rules name for a trailing explanatory clause.
Action: cut the clause.
Replace with: "Sam thumbs the keyboard with the economy he uses on everything else, a few words and stop."

**"Others answer in bursts, messages at once after a long gap, a reply to something asked in the evening landing after midnight because a shift only just ended."**
Same construction: a comma-plus-"because" clause explaining a timing the sentence has already shown by naming it.
Action: cut the clause.
Replace with: "Others answer in bursts, messages at once after a long gap, a reply to something asked in the evening landing after midnight."

**"Whatever he's in the middle of at the agency keeps running through a joke about somebody's boss, so Theo reads everything within minutes of it landing, but sometimes waits a full day to answer it."**
This looks to me like a genuine grammar problem, not a style one: the subject of "keeps running through a joke" is "whatever he's in the middle of," a task, which cannot run through a joke. It also carries a comma-plus-"so" clause explaining Theo's reading speed to the reader. This is the clearest single defect I found in the chapter.
Action: rewrite. I'm not certain which reading was intended, so two directions:
Replace with: "Theo reads everything within minutes of it landing, but sometimes waits a full day to answer it."
Or: "A joke about somebody's boss can sit half a shift in his head before he gets back to it. He reads everything within minutes of it landing and sometimes waits a full day to answer."

**"The branch is a converted storefront a few blocks from the shop, with desks behind a rope and a manager who keeps the retail policies laminated in the drawer of the nearest branch."**
"The nearest branch" doesn't parse — the scene is already at this branch, so there's no other branch for it to be nearest to. Reads like an editing leftover.
Action: fix the phrase.
Replace with: "...a manager who keeps the retail policies laminated in the drawer of his desk."
Or: "...a manager who keeps the retail policies laminated in the drawer nearest his hand."

**"Days later."**
A two-word fragment carrying an entire time jump alone, thinner than every other dateline sentence in the chapter ("Halfway through September, Nadia tries to pin an actual date," "In October the question comes round again"). May be intentional, matching the chat's own terseness for a smaller, flatter beat.
Action: no forced fix. Consider either expanding it to a full sentence to match the chapter's other datelines, or leaving it as a deliberate hard cut; I'm not confident which the author would want.

**"A regular customer asks her, not unkindly, when she's going to go do something with herself..."**
"Not unkindly" is the narrator supplying the customer's tone, which nothing else in the scene dramatizes directly. Low-confidence flag; a two-word qualifier is a smaller instance of the pattern than a full trailing clause, and could be a legitimate quick disambiguation rather than a violation.
Action: consider cutting.
Replace with: "A regular customer asks her when she's going to go do something with herself..."

**"...most of them go on the individual company career pages rather than on the boards, because a board publishes in a single format and thousands of separate companies publish in as many."**
Another comma-plus-"because" clause, explaining the business logic to the reader rather than showing it.
