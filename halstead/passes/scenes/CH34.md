## Rule for scenes inside a chat transcript

A name-prefixed line (`chloe:`, `ruth:`) is a character speaking, on the same
new-speaker-new-paragraph footing as quoted dialogue elsewhere in the book — except
finer-grained, since consecutive lines from the same name are separate sends, not one turn
artificially split, the way a real phone actually delivers a burst of texts. I'm not proposing
to merge any two chat lines into one paragraph on "thin paragraph" grounds, because doing so
would misrepresent the message as a single send when the page shows two.

A run of chat counts as **one scene**, not several, for as long as it is one continuous, live
exchange — even though the people typing it are scattered across a bedroom, a lab, a barracks,
and a shop counter. I'm treating the shared thread as the scene's "place" the way a phone call
is one scene despite two rooms, because everyone in it is acting on and reacting to the same
exchange in the same stretch of time. A narrated cutaway to what one person is physically doing
while typing (pouring water, staying at the counter) doesn't start a new scene on its own, any
more than a POV shift inside one room would.

What does start a new scene inside a transcript is what starts one anywhere else: the clock
jumps, the live exchange plainly ends and a different one plainly begins later, or the action
leaves the thread for something a character does alone and un-reacted-to. A stretch of pure
narration that cuts between several people, each doing something at a *different*, unstated
hour, not talking to or reacting to one another, is not a scene by this or any reading — it's a
montage, and I've marked the one in this chapter as such rather than force it into scene
numbering.

## Scenes found

By this rule, chapter 34 is almost entirely one scene. The two section breaks inside the chat
portion (lines 55 and 65) don't mark new scenes — the conversation they interrupt never stops
being the same live conversation, the same night, the same group finding out the same thing.
What isn't a scene is the long documentary passage before the chat starts, which covers seven
people reading the same file at seven different, unstated hours and is explicitly told that way
("each at a different hour, each of them alone with it when it happens").

- Lines 5-37: not a scene — the file, and how each of them reads it (montage).
- Lines 39-117: Scene 1 — the chat, that night. One scene across three section breaks.

That is a 1-scene finding for a roughly 2,000-word chapter, which is the opposite problem from
the vignette failure mode the brief describes — this chapter is about as consolidated as a
chapter can get rather than strung into short disconnected pieces. I don't think that's a
defect on its own; see "The chapter whole" for what I make of it and what I'd still ask about.

## Not a scene — the file, and how each of them reads it (lines 5-37)

**Location:** seven places at once — Chloe's desk, a lab in Cambridge, wherever Sam's unit has
him, a shop counter, a kitchen table, a lab down a corridor, somewhere Theo is standing.
**Time:** explicitly not one time — "each at a different hour, each of them alone with it when
it happens."

**Content:** each of the seven opens the file alone. The file's own structure is described —
case number, classification banner, the individual entries for Sam, Theo, Nadia, Eli, Kavi, and
Chloe, the standing "believed to be in contact" line rewritten yearly, and an incident report
from four years back (the parking-lot robbery) that ends in unrecoverable footage on every
camera that would have shown the four of them.

**Possible purpose:** may be doing double duty — delivering a large amount of plot information
(what's in the file, on all of them) while also establishing that each of them is genuinely
alone with it before the chat starts, which the ending of the chapter may be setting up as the
thing that changes.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but the staggered, solitary reading might be
there to make the chat that follows read as a relief — the first time any of them has this in
company rather than alone — rather than to convey anything about the file's contents on its
own.

## Scene 1 — the chat, that night (lines 39-117)

**Location:** the shared group chat; physically, Chloe's desk, Ruth's lab floor, wherever Sam
is on duty, Nadia's counter, Eli's kitchen table.
**Time:** continuous, that same night, running at least the twenty-some minutes it takes Sam to
get a free hand for his phone.

**Content:** Chloe, Ruth, and Sam recognize the Waffle House from the incident report. Ruth

Eli's "I have never once been at the top of a room in my life" ends the exchange; the chapter
closes on what each of them does with the silence after it.

**Possible purpose:** may be doing the job of turning an abstract file into something the group
has to sit with together — moving from "here is what the government has on us" to "here is what
it means that the government can't explain us," which seems to be closer to the point than the
file's contents on their own.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but Ruth's arc within the scene (deflecting,
being made to reread her own words, landing on "I don't think i had accepted it. i think i knew
the sentence") may be doing more work than the file summary does — possibly this is where the
chapter's actual subject sits, with the documentary opening as the occasion for it rather than
the point of it.

## The chapter whole

One found scene, averaging 37 paragraphs, wrapped in a 17-paragraph non-scene frame — 54
content paragraphs total, no section break of the chapter's three actually dividing anything.
That is not the vignette problem; if anything it's the opposite shape, a chapter built to read
as one sustained, uninterrupted event rather than several short ones. I don't think that's a
defect by itself, but I want to flag the judgment call plainly rather than bury it: whether the
frame (lines 5-37) and the chat (lines 39-117) are two units or one comes down entirely to
whether staggered, non-interactive reading counts as the same scene as the live, interactive
chat that follows it, and I've come down on "no" because the frame explicitly says the seven of
them are alone and at different hours, while the chat is explicitly one shared, live exchange.

**A possible merge, not recommended:** the frame and the chat could be treated as one
continuous "reading the file" scene if the chapter's "place" is understood as *the file itself*
rather than any room — everyone in both halves is oriented around the same document. I'm not
recommending this reading because the frame is explicit that the seven readings don't share a
clock, which the chat plainly does; collapsing them would erase a distinction the text itself
draws. I'm noting it because it's the strongest case I can construct for a different scene
count than the one above, and the brief asks me to be honest about where a call is close.

**No merges proposed inside Scene 1.** The 37 paragraphs already read as beats, not one every
two sentences — the shortest units are single chat lines, which carry real information each
and are already at the size the brief asks paragraphs to be cut to.
