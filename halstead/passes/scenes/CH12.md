# Scene audit: Chapter 12, "Nine"

Method follows `passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. Read alongside: `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md`,
`passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, `CLAUDE.md`. Everything below is a guess at intent, not a
claim about it, and no verdict here is praise.

Three items are out of scope by instruction, not by my own judgment:

- The ", because" construction inside dialogue is known, measured, and being
  catalogued separately. I do not flag individual dialogue instances of it below,
  though I do flag the same shape when it appears in narration rather than
  dialogue, since that was not part of the exemption.
- Questions punctuated as periods have been ruled a defect and fixed book-wide.
  This chapter still has at least two ("Or can I go." at line 27, and "This is
  four pages of the same trick, isn't it." at line 197). I am not raising the
  defect type again, per instruction, but naming the two lines in case the
  book-wide fix has not reached this chapter yet.
- Line 125 (the Ruth/Chloe languages exchange) is deliberate per instruction and
  I am not proposing to compress it. I describe it as a scene below and note
  anything else in it, but the length itself is not a finding.

All line numbers refer to `chapters/12_nine.md` as currently written. This
chapter is a full school year (September 2014-July 2015) told mostly as a
subject-by-subject and person-by-person survey rather than a small number of
continuous scenes, and that shape affects almost everything below; I flag it
directly in section 4.

## 1. Scenes

The chapter has three section breaks (`___`), making four sections. Inside
them I count twenty-one scenes by the brief's test (one time, one place, one
action). Several of the "scenes" in the subject-survey sections are really a
short summary passage wrapped around one dated, dialogue-bearing incident; I
have tried to say so rather than pretend the summary itself is a scene.

### Scene 1 — The office counter (lines 5-13)

**What appears to happen:** The first morning of term, Chloe finds one more
line on her course sheet than last year's had, and takes it to the office to
ask if it's a mistake.

**Content, in order:** she counts the line twice on the way down; walks it to
the office; the clerk finishes what she's doing before looking up; Chloe asks
if it's a mistake; the clerk explains the yearly increase and that biology
starts Tuesday.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing, before anyone else appears, that
Chloe checks things against her own memory of last year rather than taking a
sheet at face value.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but this might be setting the
book's recurring "sheet as evidence" device that Kavi's scene reuses four
lines later.

### Scene 2 — Breakfast, Kavi's complaint (lines 15-31)

**What appears to happen:** At breakfast, Kavi lays out, in order, three
separate objections to how his course sheet was handled.

**Content, in order:** Chloe's own new subjects are named in passing; Kavi's
sheet is spread on the table; his first objection (the form is decorative);
Sam asks if there's a third and is put off; his second (two classes he wanted
are full because of his age); his third (a class deferred to an age he'll
already be past when it starts); Ruth reads the back of the sheet twice and
hands it back as Chloe gets up with her tray; a line planting that something
on Sam's own sheet differs from everyone else's, with theories by Thursday.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be establishing Kavi's specific mode of
objection (a numbered case, evidence-based, delivered whether or not anyone
asked for it) as a trait that recurs later in the chapter (the fish tank,
statistics).

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. Might be doing double duty: Sam
wanting to leave the topic and Ruth taking Kavi's argument seriously enough to
read it twice, without either being told to the reader as a reaction.

### Scene 3 — The dance/drawing announcement (lines 33-49)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe rereads her own sheet, discovers dance and
drawing are gone from it, and announces this to the table; Sam objects that he
liked dance; Ruth tells him why.

**Content, in order:** Chloe rereads the sheet twice more, once on the stairs;
she runs into the dining hall and announces it; Sam looks up; Chloe repeats
the complaint with more force; Sam says he liked dance, twice; Chloe needles
him for being alone in that; Ruth explains that liking something you're bad at
is different from what Sam's done, and that being ninetieth (not first) would
have kept him off "the table"; Chloe laps the table and sits.

**Possible purpose:** may be a small, second illustration of the "checking a
sheet more than once" behavior from scenes 1 and 2, this time producing a
correction rather than a clarification, and may be setting Ruth up as the one
who names the harder truth in this friend group without being asked.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but this could be read as
establishing the "table" (the popular/successful kids' end of the table,
picked up again in scene 12) as a place with a real cost to not being at.

**Location/time note:** the exact geography is unclear to me. Chloe apparently
leaves the same breakfast (she "gets up with her tray" at line 31), goes
somewhere to reread the sheet "on the stairs," then "comes into the dining
hall" again — implying she left and re-entered a meal still in progress. See
the paragraph note at line 33 below.

### Scene 4 — Mandarin: the tone lesson (lines 53-65)

**What appears to happen:** A year-long summary of Mandarin class, anchored on
one November lesson where Mrs. Sun explains and then gives up on explaining
tone, and closing on a June assessment of how well it worked.

**Content, in order:** Mrs. Sun's background and teaching style; Chloe's
reading improves fast enough to reach novels by February and Mandarin essays
by March; her ability to hear/produce tone lags; in November, Mrs. Sun singles
out the room's shared problem, demonstrates a syllable four ways, explains the
English-emphasis habit that's interfering; Chloe says she already knew that
part; Mrs. Sun tells her to stop trying to fix it introspectively and gives
three concrete fixes; a summary of Chloe (with Ruth's help from December) doing
all three, closing on the fact that she still can't hear the difference
between two teachers' accents that Ruth can tell apart instantly.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be setting up a a specific, nameable
skill gap (perception vs. production) rather than a vague "struggles with
Mandarin," and pairing it against a nine-year-old auditory task she can't do
by effort alone.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but the closing comparison
(Ruth can tell two accents apart, Chloe still can't) might be there so a
specific fact recurs at line 119 without the reader needing it re-explained.

### Scene 5 — Statistics: the dinner argument (lines 67-77)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe brings a study to dinner; she and Kavi argue
about whether a small sample means the finding is worthless; Sam asks them to
stop.

**Content, in order:** statistics is introduced as a subject; the argument
starts in week two; sample-size dialogue back and forth; Sam covers his ears
and hums; Chloe tells him they're nearly done; the last line notes this is not
the first time this exact argument has happened.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing the same evidence-based arguing style
established in Kavi's sheet complaint (scene 2), now aimed at Chloe instead of
at an adult, and Sam as the one who has to physically opt out of it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 6 — Chemistry: the grey/white experiment (lines 79-95)

**What appears to happen:** A term-long chemistry vignette narrows to a
specific investigation: Chloe's result keeps coming out the wrong color, and
Vasquez has her isolate the variable across three Fridays.

**Content, in order:** the room's general fire-setting reputation; the smell
and scarring of the lab; Chloe burns a hole in a sleeve; her result comes out
grey instead of white; Vasquez identifies water as the contaminant and gives
her three suspects; the next Friday it comes out white; Vasquez lists the
three things she changed at once and tells her to isolate them; she runs the
test wet on purpose (grey again) and then dry off the old bottle (white
again); she writes "glassware" in her notebook with a date.

**Possible purpose:** looks like a demonstration of an actual experimental
method (change one variable, confirm the result), rather than Chloe simply
being told the answer.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; possibly meant to sit next to the
statistics scene as two different angles on the same idea (a result needs to
survive being checked) without either scene saying that out loud.

### Scene 7 — First aid: the drill (lines 97-101)

**What appears to happen:** A first-aid class scenario escalates by term, and
by the third term includes a distressed "father" character Chloe has to talk
over while counting.

**Content, in order:** the drill is described as happening on a timed clock
from week one; by the second term the lighting is dim; by the third term a
radio plays and a man asks the same question over and over; Chloe asks if this
is going to continue the whole time; the teacher explains the scenario and the
point of the drill.

**Possible purpose:** may be building toward a specific skill (staying on task
while someone in distress talks over you) that could be relevant to Chloe's
own family later in the book; hedged, since I can't check other chapters from
here.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 8 — Textiles: the shirt (line 103 only)

**What appears to happen:** Across two terms, Chloe makes three shirts, using
a house shirt as a pattern only on the third attempt, and keeps wearing the
badly fitting second one anyway.

**Content, in order:** the first shirt's shoulder seam is an inch off and
drags; the second, redrafted from the same measurements, drags in the same
place; in January she traces an already-fitting shirt from home instead; the
traced line is an inch and a half off from what she'd been drafting; the third
shirt fits by the end of March; she wears the second one anyway because it's
the green one.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be showing her solving a problem by
changing method (measure a working example instead of trusting the
measurements) rather than by trying harder at the same method, matching the
chemistry scene's variable-isolation idea in a different subject.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. This is the entire "scene" — one
paragraph, no dialogue, seven months of a single project. See the
paragraph-shape note below and the merge discussion in section 4.

### Scene 9 — Biology: the fish tank (lines 105-109)

**What appears to happen:** Kavi decides a fish is being bullied, argues with
Ruth about it for months, and the fish eventually dies of old age.

**Content, in order:** Kavi's theory that the small striped fish is bullied;
he watches the tank for most of October and diagrams it; Ruth's competing
theory (he picked a favorite and reads the tank backward from it); by
February they have a statistics term for that; in March the fish dies of old
age; the fish-tank man tells Kavi at breakfast that it was a long life and
he'd expected it since Christmas.

**Possible purpose:** may be a second illustration (after the sheet complaint)
of Kavi committing to a theory and defending it past the point the evidence
supports it, this time with Ruth naming the bias directly instead of just
disagreeing with the conclusion.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 10 — Bex: the bridge review (lines 113-115)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe and Bex build a bridge together; when a
teacher asks how the joints held, Bex answers first, correctly, in a way that
splits credit ("we" once, then "I" for the rest).

**Content, in order:** Bex's general trait of finishing sentences correctly;
the two-week bridge project; the teacher's question; Bex's answer, complete
and accurate, phrased mostly as "I."

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be planting a specific, concrete
example of a credit-taking pattern rather than telling the reader Bex takes
credit.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 11 — Chloe's evening reconstruction (line 117 only)

**What appears to happen:** That evening, Chloe checks her own memory of the
two weeks against Bex's account and finds the one moment (a Thursday) where
she said any of it aloud, with Bex nearby.

**Content, in order:** she searches the fortnight for when she talked through
her work; finds the Thursday, half-built, wrong twice before right, Bex at the
next bench.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing that Bex's "we" was earned by actually
being present and listening, not fabricated, which would complicate a simple
reading of scene 10 as Bex stealing credit.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. This is one paragraph and reads
to me as the second half of scene 10 rather than a fully separate scene; see
the merge note in section 4.

### Scene 12 — Iyad: the dinner callout (lines 119-121)

**What appears to happen:** Iyad, established as knowing everyone's numbers,
announces a Mandarin fact about Chloe at dinner in March; afterward Chloe
starts entering meals by the kitchen door and taking a less visible seat.

**Content, in order:** Iyad's general trait of reciting everyone's data
unasked; in March, his fact about Chloe (a stranger loses her by the second
sentence), delivered loudly down the table while she's still walking it with
her tray; a note that it matches what "the man from the university" said,
word for word; Sam asks him something unrelated and he answers that too; a
term working with Iyad in chemistry, where he gets more from the hour than she
does; Chloe's changed dinner entrance.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing a second, less flattering side of Iyad's
total recall (it's delivered at volume, about her, before she's even sat down)
and a visible, if unexplained, behavioral cost to Chloe from it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but this reads as one of the
chapter's better examples of "masking costs something and isn't explained" —
the new kitchen-door entrance and end seat are shown, not glossed. See the
sourcing note on line 119 below, which is a separate question from this.

### Scene 13 — Sam, planted (line 123 only)

**What appears to happen:** One sentence: Sam spends the whole year on the
gym-wall overhang.

**Possible purpose:** looks like a placeholder/plant for the wall scenes at
lines 127-135, which don't otherwise begin from a clean setup.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. It sits between the Iyad scene
and the Ruth/Chloe scene rather than next to the wall scenes it's setting up,
which may be a deliberate interleaving of several kids' threads at the dinner
table before the chapter commits to Sam's arc, or may be a paragraph that
drifted from where it was meant to sit. I can't tell which from the text
alone.

### Scene 14 — Ruth and Chloe: the languages exchange (line 125 only)

**Per instruction, this scene is deliberate and I am not proposing to
compress it.**

**What appears to happen:** Ruth asks how the languages are going; Chloe gives
a full account (what's moving, what's stalled, where, what she's tried, the
thing Mrs. Sun said that just clicked); Ruth listens through all of it, then
gives back an account of her own struggle with the ablative at about the same
length. Nobody at the table looks up.

**Content:** as above, entirely in summary, no direct dialogue quoted.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but this may be the chapter's
clearest instance of an exchange between equals that the rest of the table
finds unremarkable, which contrasts with scene 12's Iyad announcement landing
loud enough that Chloe is still walking to her seat when it happens.

### Scene 15 — The wall, months of falling (line 127 only)

**What appears to happen:** A four-month summary of Sam failing to clear a
climbing-wall lip, ending when a younger girl clears it and gives him the

**Content, in order:** the wall and lip are introduced; Sam falls in October,
twice in November, and hard in December; an instructor checks on him; in
January a girl in the year below clears it in one try; Sam is upset for two
days, then asks her how, and learns it's about left-foot placement.

**Possible purpose:** may be setting up the specific technical fact (left
foot) that gets repeated as the payoff line in scene 16's dialogue.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 16 — Sam's triumph (lines 129-135)

**What appears to happen:** On May 11th, in front of thirty people he told in
advance he'd succeed, Sam fails once and clears the wall on the second try.

**Content, in order:** the date and the audience Sam built for himself by
announcing it; Chloe asks what happens if he doesn't; Sam's answer (left foot,
not arms or grip, because the girl told him and she was right); the first
attempt falls a foot short; the second clears it; the reaction (Ruth
screaming, Kavi's arms up, Chloe tearing a book's corner without noticing);
Sam eats left-handed for days afterward.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the chapter's clearest payoff scene, and one
of its cleanest examples of showing intensity through physical detail (the
torn corner, the left-handed eating) rather than naming it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; no issue found in the technique
here, see paragraph notes below.

### Scene 17 — Riding: the walk and Cricket (lines 137-147)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe takes up riding to be near Priya; Priya
teaches her the horses and their habits; by spring Chloe is competent at
riding but gets nothing out of it, unlike Priya.

**Content, in order:** Chloe's reason for choosing riding is given directly;
the walk to the stables; Priya naming every horse and their bite risk within a
fortnight; a specific exchange about a horse's blind spot; Chloe's competence
by February/March contrasted with her lack of enjoyment; in April the groups
split and Chloe rides twice more alone before stopping.

**Possible purpose:** may be building the specific contrast the next scene
turns on: competence without enjoyment, next to Priya's enjoyment of even the
boring parts.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 18 — Riding: the falling out (lines 149-159)

**What appears to happen:** Two days after Chloe stops showing up to riding
without telling Priya, Priya confronts her about it at dinner; Chloe explains
it was boring; Priya explains what she gets out of it that Chloe doesn't.

**Content, in order:** Priya's opening complaint (she heard it from someone
else); Chloe's apology for not saying it herself; Priya's question about what
actually happened; Chloe's answer (competent, bored, twenty-two minutes of
walking for it); Priya's answer (she likes even the boring, cold, empty parts);
Chloe's closing line, that she kept waiting for it to become that for her too.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the direct payoff of scene 17's contrast, and
possibly the chapter's most direct example of two characters correctly
identifying a real difference between them without either being right or
wrong about it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 19 — Home, first weeks (lines 163-165)

**What appears to happen:** Home for the summer, Chloe sleeps most of the
first week; her mother's concern fades into leaving water by the bed; Chloe
and her father watch films with no opinion required; she rides to a reservoir
and back in week two.

**Content, in order:** the extended sleep; the joke that shrinks and then
stops; the water on the nightstand; the film-watching with no opinion needed;
the reservoir ride.

**Possible purpose:** may be showing a recovery period after a demanding
school year, largely through what isn't required of her (an opinion, a
schedule) rather than naming exhaustion.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, with one exception flagged
below: line 165 does name a state ("restful").

### Scene 20 — The coast: cribbage (lines 167-175)

**What appears to happen:** On a July coast trip, Chloe's mother teaches her
cribbage on a motel balcony on her own father's old board; Chloe loses several
games before winning two.

**Content, in order:** the trip and the board's origin; scoring dialogue; a
question about runs; Chloe's early losses from double-counting a card; she
wins the fourth and fifth games; they play until the pool lights go off.

**Possible purpose:** looks like a small generational-object scene (a
walnut board from around 1961) alongside a specific, ordinary teaching
moment, without either being commented on.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged.

### Scene 21 — The contract (lines 177-203)

**What appears to happen:** In the third week of the trip, Chloe reads a
vendor proposal over her father's shoulder, asks the question that reveals the
whole document has no checkable commitments in it, and the two of them
confirm this across several days, ending in the car.

**Content, in order:** the proposal is introduced; Chloe asks what a paragraph
actually means; her father explains, then re-reads it himself and stalls;
Chloe names the actual gap (no way to check if they hadn't done it); her
father agrees and generalizes it to the whole document; that night he finds
more of the same; on Thursday he tests the vendor by asking for dates and
numbers; some come back, most come back reworded; in the car on Friday he
summarizes the results to her while she eats a sandwich.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the chapter's clearest example of Chloe
noticing something an adult professional missed, presented as a specific
question rather than a general cleverness.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. The location changes twice
inside this scene (the table that night, an off-page Thursday call, the car
on Friday) without a break or a new opening line marking either shift; see
the paragraph note on line 201 below.

## Chapter-wide notes

**Narrator "because," outside dialogue.** Distinct from the exempted
dialogue-only construction, narration itself uses a causal "because" clause
several times: line 57 (Mrs. Sun stopping the class), line 75 (Chloe
stopping), line 129 (the size of Sam's crowd), line 137 (Chloe's reason for
choosing riding), line 175 (why she loses at cribbage). Most of these connect
two directly observable physical facts and read as ordinary narration to me.
One (line 137) states an emotionally loaded motive outright rather than
showing it, and is the one I'd treat as a real finding; see Feedback.

**Second-person address.** Twice, at lines 113 and 139, the narration shifts
from third person into a generic "you" for an aphoristic description (of Bex,
and of the walk to the stables). Two instances in one chapter is not
necessarily a problem on its own — I can't tell from this chapter whether it's
a technique used at a similar rate elsewhere in the book — but it is the only
register shift of its kind I found, and I'd want it checked against how often
it appears book-wide before deciding whether it needs to come down.

**Short declarative scene-openers.** Several scenes open on one short,
factual sentence before staging the rest of the scene: "Block two is hers..."
(137), "Home for the summer..." (163), "In the third week, her father is at
the table..." (177). This looks like a consistent chapter-level pattern rather
than a defect, and I'm not proposing to change any of these individually.

**Compressed multi-month paragraphs.** The subject-survey sections in
particular (scenes 6, 8, 9, 15, and to a lesser extent 19) each pack several
months and multiple distinct beats into one or two paragraphs. This is the
mirror image of the "thin paragraph" problem the brief names — instead of one
sentence carrying a whole beat's weight, several beats share one paragraph —
and I think it's the more consequential paragraph-shape issue in this
particular chapter. See section 4.

## 4. The chapter whole

Twenty-one scenes, averaging about 4.6 paragraphs each, over roughly 4,000
words. Against the brief's rule of thumb (four to six scenes for a chapter
this size), this is well past the vignette problem the brief describes for
chapters found at twelve scenes under a page each — this chapter has
twenty-one, several of them a single paragraph. I think the honest way to
describe the shape is that this chapter is built as two different kinds of
survey (a subject-by-subject school year in section 2, and a person-by-person
set of dinner-table portraits in section 3) rather than as a small number of
continuous scenes, and the same reader experience the brief calls "vignettes"
follows from that shape whether or not it was intended.

Concrete merges I'd recommend, with what carries across each seam:

**Scenes 2 and 3 could combine.** Scene 2 ends with Chloe getting up from the
table with her tray (line 31); scene 3 opens with her rereading her sheet and
running back into the dining hall (line 33). Removing the break and running
these as one continuous breakfast — Kavi's complaint, Chloe leaving the table,
Chloe's own discovery, her return — would use the tray she's already holding
as the connective object, and would also let a rewrite clear up the geography
question I raised in the scene 3 note (where she went to reread the sheet).

**Scenes 10 and 11 could combine.** Scene 10 ends on the teacher moving down
the table (line 115); scene 11 opens "that evening" (line 117), a time cue the
prose already supplies. Running these as one scene — the bridge review,
followed directly by Chloe checking her own memory against it — would need no
new bridging text, just the removal of the implied break between two
one-paragraph units.

**Scene 8 (textiles) is a plausible candidate to fold into scene 9 (biology)
or scene 7 (first aid).** Both textiles and first aid are the two subjects in
section 2 without an extended dialogue scene of their own — everything in them
is summary. A transition like "the same term, in textiles, ..." at the head of
scene 8's current paragraph would let it run directly into whichever
neighboring subject block precedes or follows it, rather than standing as its
own single-paragraph scene between two more developed ones. I'd flag this as
the strongest candidate for combining two scenes doing a similar job (a
subject introduced, a struggle shown, a resolution reached) with a smaller
footprint than the rest of section 2 gives that same job.

**Scene 13 (Sam, planted) sits awkwardly between scenes 12 and 14 rather than
next to scenes 15-16, which is what it sets up.** I'm not confident enough to
recommend moving it — the interleaving of Bex, Iyad, Sam, and Ruth/Chloe
across lines 113-127 may be a deliberate weave of several kids' threads at the
same dinner table before the chapter settles on Sam's arc — but it's worth
the author's attention as either a deliberate structure or a paragraph that
drifted from where it was meant to sit.

I looked for two scenes doing the same job with the same character, as the
brief specifically asks about, and found one strong candidate and one weaker
one:

- **Scenes 6 (chemistry) and 8 (textiles)** both show Chloe solving a problem
  by changing method rather than by repeating the same method harder — isolate
  the variable in chemistry, trace a working pattern instead of the
  measurements in textiles. These read to me as the same underlying pattern
  told twice, in two different subjects, which might be exactly the kind of
  repetition worth noticing even if I wouldn't propose cutting either outright
  (they're in different subjects with different stakes). Worth the author's
  attention rather than an outright merge recommendation from me.
- **Scenes 2 (Kavi's sheet complaint) and 9 (Kavi's fish theory)** both show
  Kavi committing to a conclusion and defending it with a structured argument
  past the point the evidence clearly supports it. I don't think these are
  redundant — one is about an administrative slight, the other about being
  wrong and staying wrong — but I'd flag them as thematically paired rather
  than as separate character beats, in case the author wants to trim one of
  them rather than keep both at full length.

I did not find any scene I think should be cut outright; each of the
twenty-one seems to be doing distinct, specific work, even where several of
them are doing structurally similar work in different subjects.
