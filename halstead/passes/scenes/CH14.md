# Scene audit: Chapter 14, "Sixty Degrees"

Method follows `passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. Read alongside: `CLAUDE.md`,
`passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` (rule 1 in particular), `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`. Everything
below is a guess at intent, not a claim about it, and no verdict here is praise.

Two items are out of scope by instruction and not raised again here: the ", because"
construction inside dialogue is known and catalogued elsewhere, and questions
punctuated as periods have already been ruled a defect and fixed book-wide. I checked
this chapter for the second one and did not find an instance of it.

One item is settled specific to this chapter: the paragraph at line 85, where Kavi
asks a yes-or-no question and gets a long technical account back, then gives one back
at the same length with nobody in the scene reacting to the imbalance, is deliberate
and was added on purpose. I have not proposed compressing it, and I flag it below only
to confirm I saw it and left it alone.

All line numbers refer to `chapters/14_sixty_degrees.md` as currently written. Every
quotation below was copied from the file and checked against it a second time before
this was finalized (see Section 5).

## 1. Scenes

The chapter has three `________________` breaks, making four raw sections. Read for
continuous time-and-place, those four sections open out into thirteen real scenes and
four passages that look like connective summary rather than scene — three of the four
sections contain more than one scene, which is the pattern the brief warns about.

### Not a scene — opening frame (lines 5-7)

A camera description of the field and building, then a multi-week montage ("by the
end of the second week... bored by the middle of the third") covering the ordinary
stretch of archery training before anything specific happens. No single time, so not
counted as a scene.

### Scene 1 — Bell introduces the collision course (lines 9-23)

**What appears to happen:** Coach Bell stops the ordinary archery drill to explain the
two-line, sixty-degree shooting setup and the timing problem it creates. Kavi compares
it to paintball reading. Bell overrules the comparison and turns on a metronome.

**Content, in order:** Bell holds up an arrow and explains why this is unlike anything
in the gym; Kavi offers the paintball comparison; Bell rejects it as not what this
drill is for; a question from the line; Bell walks them to the lanes and the geometry
of the sixty-degree crossing point is laid out; a question about knowing when to
release; Bell turns on the metronome and tells them to count.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it exists to hand the reader the mechanism the rest of
the archery material runs on — the count, not the shot — before showing anyone
practising it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be setting up a contrast the chapter comes
back to more than once: an instructor naming exactly what skill matters (counting,
not watching), which Chloe and Ruth are then shown building on their own time in
Scene 2.

### Scene 2 — Counting practice in the corridor (lines 29-37)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe has been practising her release timing without a
bow. Ruth catches her doing it in the hall and they compare counts out loud until
they are late to dinner.

**Content, in order:** her release runs early or late depending on her state; she
starts practising against her leg on the walk to dinner; Ruth catches her at it and
asks; they compare; they are late enough that Sam has taken both their chairs.

**Possible purpose:** hedged, but this may be doing the same job for Ruth and Chloe's
friendship that appears elsewhere in the chapter (Scene 4, Scene 8) — a shared,
slightly competitive working-through of a problem, conducted standing up, in a
corridor, between two other obligations.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that the skill from Scene 1 gets
practised off the field and off the clock, which may be doing quiet work toward
Scene 3's payoff.

### Scene 3 — The Odile collision (lines 39-45)

**What appears to happen:** In late October, Chloe manages the first arrow collision
with Odile. The immediate aftermath is loud and confused. A forward-looking coda
covers dinner and then jumps to March and April.

**Content, in order:** the click, the two arrows coming down; shouting, banging on the
equipment box, Odile's run; Odile's word about her brother coming Sunday; a jump to
dinner where Sam wants to hear the sound imitated; a further jump to "by March" and
"in April," covering months in the same paragraph.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the scene the whole counting setup has been pointing
toward — the first successful demonstration of the thing Bell said was possible.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. The paragraph at line 45 does two jobs in
one breath — it finishes the day (dinner) and then summarizes five months forward — and
I don't have a confident read on whether that's meant to land as one beat or whether
the seam is worth separating. Flagged below at the paragraph level rather than treated
as a defect.

### Scene 4 — Latin is cheating (lines 47-73)

**What appears to happen:** On the first morning of the school year — earlier, in
story time, than the two scenes just before it — Ruth and Chloe argue over Chloe's
choice of Latin, walking from wherever schedules are read toward the stairs.

**Content, in order:** Ruth asks what everyone has; Chloe gets to Latin; Ruth calls it
cheating; an exchange about why (Latin sits inside Spanish and French, which Chloe
already has); Chloe reframes it as the year paying for itself twice; a swap to
Ruth's own Arabic and the point about being correctable; Chloe's last answer; Ruth's
line reframing it as reading rather than learning; Chloe concedes and leaves.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing the shape of one kind of argument Chloe and
Ruth have — Ruth needling a choice until Chloe either wins outright or is left holding
a much smaller claim than she started with. Scene 8 (the essay grade, on the stairs)
looks like the same shape run again later in the year, this time with Chloe
conceding the whole point ("Yeah") rather than holding a smaller one.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but if the two Ruth-argument scenes are
doing the same job, that may be intentional pattern rather than two anecdotes — see
the note on possible merges below.

### Not a scene — the year in survey (lines 75-83)

Calculus, astronomy, the age-of-the-universe unit, rhetoric, and art history, covered
across a school year in five sentences of narrator summary before the dinner-table
scene that follows. No single time; connective tissue rather than a scene.

### Scene 5 — The picture that got more expensive doing nothing (lines 85-95)

**What appears to happen:** At dinner, Chloe and Kavi go back over the art-history
auction numbers. Chloe works out that inflation doesn't explain the gap and that an
identical fake would be worth almost nothing. Kavi tells her to ask again.

**Content, in order:** (line 85, left as is per instruction above) Kavi's yes/no
question and the long answer and the long answer back; Chloe's case to Kavi
directly — same picture, untouched, priced six times higher; Kavi's "inflation";
Chloe's counter about a matching forgery; Kavi telling her to ask the teacher again,
properly; Chloe reporting that she already did and was told it was a question for a
different course.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be doing the same "adult gives an honest but
incomplete answer" work the ruled Ben scene in chapter 2 is noted for doing, this time
with a teacher rather than a parent.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, possibly showing Chloe testing an adult's
answer against her own arithmetic and finding it doesn't hold, without the narrator
saying that's what happened.

### Not a scene — wood shop, autumn to spring (line 97)

One paragraph covering two terms of chair-building and the March joint demonstration.
Summary, not a scene.

### Scene 6 — The soft chisel (lines 99-105)

**What appears to happen:** In May, Chloe's chisel keeps rolling its edge. She takes it
to the instructor, who tells her it's a soft one and points her to the forge.

**Content, in order:** the chisel dulls every twenty minutes; she brings it up; he
identifies it as soft; she asks where to get a hard one; he names the forge and its
hours.

**Possible purpose:** may be a small planting scene — a location (the forge) and a
detail (Thursdays) that could matter later, though I have no chapter after this one to
check that against.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, possibly nothing beyond itself; a short,
complete transaction.

### Scene 7 — The hat and the dinner table (lines 109-135)

**What appears to happen:** Essay topics are drawn from a hat. Chloe draws the area
bombing question. At dinner (the same sitting, on the reading below) the table reacts,
and Sam argues her into agreeing to build the case for the bombing before she attacks
it.

**Content, in order:** Iyad runs the hat; Sam, Kavi, and Priya each get a topic and
react; Chloe unfolds hers; the question, set off on its own line; Bex calls it a
proper question; Chloe calls it stupid; Kavi disagrees and would swap; Chloe's reasons
(there's an answer, people signed it); Sam predicts a C; a needling exchange; Sam's
"devil's advocate" argument that she has to say why the man signed it or she has
nothing but a feeling; a beat of silence; Chloe agrees to do it properly.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the scene the essay's structure (Wednesday: build the
other side properly, on purpose) is answering — Sam's argument here appears to be the
reason she does that instead of only attacking.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. Possibly shows Sam in a role — the one
who tells her the harder, correct thing bluntly, over food, without cushioning it —
that recurs with him elsewhere in the chapter (his bench story at the end).

**Note on staging:** the hat-drawing (Iyad "going round the table") and "at dinner"
(first named at line 117) may be the same sitting or two different ones. See paragraph
note below; I'm not confident either way.

### Not a scene — writing the essay (lines 137-145)

Three days of research and drafting (Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday), then the
submission and grade, then a paraphrase of Hearn's written note. Covers roughly two
weeks in nine sentences; connective tissue rather than a scene, though see the
paragraph note on line 145 below — some of this reads like it may be quoting Hearn's
note rather than only summarizing it.

### Scene 8 — The B, on the stairs (lines 147-157)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe finds Ruth on the stairwell and complains about her
grade. Ruth points out that Chloe planted the two hedges Hearn found on purpose, which
means he's right to have marked her down for them.

**Content, in order:** Chloe reports the B; Ruth notes the length didn't help; Chloe's
case that she put the hedges there deliberately and he still found them; Ruth's line
that this means he's right; Chloe agrees and sits down on the stairs.

**Possible purpose:** as noted under Scene 4, this may be the same argument-shape as
the Latin scene, run again with a different, sharper outcome — full concession rather
than a fallback claim.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; see the merge note in Section 4.

### Scene 9 — The kitchen, Christmas (lines 159-181)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe's mother asks what she's been working on. Chloe gives
a heavily compressed answer. Her father, drying dishes, asks about archery and then
sends her to talk to her grandmother.

**Content, in order:** the question; Chloe's answer, working out everything the full
answer would need first and cutting all of it; the mother's response ("A B's a B");
the father's question about archery; his instruction to go find her grandmother.

**Possible purpose:** the paragraph at line 169, where Chloe works out everything a
real answer to "what did you say" would require (the hat, the deadline, the reason
hedging counts against you) and gives none of it, looks like it may be showing the
compressing-for-family behavior in progress rather than naming it — the reader gets
the long version Chloe doesn't say, which her mother never hears.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but possibly consistent with the
calibration note that this is a skill Chloe is still learning to use on her own
family, and that it should cost her something visible — here, the cost may be that her
actual thinking about the essay never reaches her mother at all.

### Scene 10 — The front room, grandmother (lines 183-193)

**What appears to happen:** Chloe tells her grandmother about the archery and draws
the sixty-degree geometry on an envelope. Her grandmother, working from "you hit it
once," is gently reassuring about something that isn't the actual scale of what
happened.

**Content, in order:** Chloe explains and draws; grandmother's question about whether
she's any good; Chloe's answer (first hit in October); grandmother's response,
reading "October" as meaning once, ever; Chloe's correction (a dozen times since);
grandmother's revised, still slightly off, response; Chloe folds the envelope and
keeps it.

**Possible purpose:** may be a second instance of an adult getting a version of events
that undersells what actually happened, this time by miscounting rather than by not
asking — a gentler cousin of Scene 9's compression, from the other side (the adult
mishears rather than the child compresses).

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged. The kept envelope is the one object that
survives the scene onto the page; I don't have a confident read on what it's for
beyond itself.

### Scene 11 — The Watch (lines 197-213)

**What appears to happen:** A school-wide event: an intercom announcement declares all
staff hostile until 4pm or until none are left. Students scatter to defend the
building. Chloe holds a third-floor landing for the whole event. The staff lose the
building at 3:42.

**Content, in order:** the notice and Sinclair's announcement; the pause; "good luck";
the building-wide scramble and the students' local knowledge of the building; Ruth's
and Nadia's reported command of two stairwells; Sam's fall and elimination; Chloe's
stretch on the third-floor landing; the last four teachers, and Hearn's capture.

**Possible purpose:** looks like a set piece closing out the year — a single large
event standing in contrast to the string of small paired-off scenes that make up the
rest of the chapter.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, but Chloe's position (a landing, holding
a chokepoint, contributing to a total the reader never gets a number for) may be doing
some work toward Scene 12's line about the uncounted teachers landing on her
specifically.

### Scene 12 — The board, the next day (lines 215-217)

**What appears to happen:** The results board lists the time the staff lost and the
failures. Chloe reads item seven — that nobody counted the adults in the building —
and stands in front of it.

**Content, in order:** the board's verdict and grade; Chloe reading the failures;
item seven; she stands there until someone needs the space.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give Scene 11 a number it withheld in the moment —
what Chloe's landing work did and didn't add up to.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged; see the paragraph note below on the
"because" clause at the end of line 217, which I think states directly what this
scene otherwise shows.

### Scene 13 — Dinner, Sam's fourth telling (lines 219-221)

**What appears to happen:** Still thinking about the board, Chloe is at dinner while
Sam retells his bannister fall for the fourth time to people who watched it happen.

**Content, in order:** the board is still with her; Sam's retelling; his line about
the floor coming up at him.

**Possible purpose:** may be a closing contrast — Sam narrating the same small failure
over and over for an audience, against Chloe holding a much larger, uncounted one in
silence.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged, ending on Sam mid-sentence, without
resolution — consistent with the note elsewhere in this project that this book's
endings generally don't resolve.

## 4. The chapter whole

Thirteen real scenes plus four connective/summary passages, across roughly 3,900
words. By the rule of thumb in the brief (four to six scenes for a nine-page
chapter), thirteen is high, though I think this chapter is a specific case: it is
explicitly a year-in-survey chapter (September to April, stated in the dateline), and
several of the "scenes" are short by design — a five-line corridor exchange, a
three-line chisel transaction — doing the work that in another chapter a single
summary sentence might do. I'd call this vignette-shaped rather than confidently call
it the vignette problem the brief warns about, because the chapter reads to me as
choosing breadth (many small transactions across a full year) on purpose rather than
failing to notice it has fragmented. That said, two pairs look like genuine
candidates for combining into one scene each.

**Average scene length: 92 scene-paragraphs across 13 scenes ≈ 7.1 paragraphs per
scene** (this excludes the four non-scene connective passages, which together hold
14 more paragraphs).

### Merge candidate 1 — Scene 9 + Scene 10 (the whole Christmas visit)

Kitchen-with-parents and front-room-with-grandmother are two rooms in the same house
on the same evening, connected by the father's own instruction sending Chloe from one
to the other ("Go on, she's in the front room..."). That line is already the bridge —
merging these would mean treating "Christmas" as one scene with a walk between rooms
inside it, the same way a scene can hold a walk down a corridor, rather than two
separate scenes joined by a location change alone. Concretely: drop the scene break
between 181 and 183 (there isn't a `___` mark there in the file, only my own scene
numbering, so nothing in the manuscript itself needs to move) and read the two as one
"Christmas evening" scene running 159-193, twelve plus six paragraphs before the
count in Section 3 above, minus the one merge (175/177) already proposed inside it.

### Merge candidate 2 — Scene 12 + Scene 13 (the aftermath of the Watch)

The board-reading and the dinner conversation are one day apart from the Watch and
each other, and the text already supplies the bridge: *"It is still with her at
dinner"* (219) names the carry-over directly. Concretely: treat 215-221 as a single
four-paragraph "Aftermath" scene rather than two two-paragraph ones, with the merge
of 215/217 from Section 3 folding into it.

### Not proposed as a merge: the two Ruth-argument scenes (4 and 8)

Scene 4 (Latin, September) and Scene 8 (the essay grade, after November) both end
with Ruth needling Chloe into a concession on the stairs or in a corridor. They may be
doing the same relational job with the same two characters, which the brief's own
example (two anecdotes that could become one pattern) would normally flag for
merging. I'm not proposing it here because the two arguments are five sentences of
plot apart in the calendar and about different subjects, and `DO_NOT_FLAG.md`'s
general note on deliberate repetition ("repetition that is doing work") makes me
want to flag this as a pattern worth the author's eye rather than assume it's
redundant. Noted, not acted on.
