# Chapter 9 — February: scene audit

Read-only. Nothing in `chapters/09_february.md` is touched by this file. Every
quotation below has been checked against the current text of the chapter by
direct search; none is carried over from another document.

**Revision note.** An earlier version of this file used the chapter's time and
location shifts as scene boundaries but still treated the whole Halstead visit
day as one thirteen-paragraph scene. The corrected brief makes the same
"place changes / clock jumps / a new thing starts happening" test apply
inside that day as everywhere else, and doing that splits it into four real
scenes, three of them sub-page. The scene count, the per-scene purpose notes,
and the merge section below are rewritten for that. The paragraph-by-paragraph
walk, the paragraph-count section, the self-check, and the Feedback list are
unchanged from the earlier version — that work was at the sentence and
paragraph level, not the scene level, and none of it depended on where the
Halstead day gets cut.

One thing found before starting the walk that is worth flagging up front:
`passes/audit/SCENE_BREAKS.md` ranks this chapter's plea scene as its most
severe instance of narrated-then-quoted-then-narrated speech, and quotes a
summary-paragraph version of the plea ("She says she'll be good, and that
she'll do the dishes every single night...") that does not match the current
file. In the chapter as it now stands, the entire plea (Scene 2 below) is
already fully dramatized in dialogue. That finding looks stale against this
draft. Noted here because it affects how much weight to put on that other
report for this chapter; not something this brief asks me to resolve.

## Scenes

The chapter has no explicit scene-break marks (no `---` or blank-header
divisions anywhere in the book, not just here). Ten real scenes, numbered by
where the time, place, or action actually changes rather than by any mark on
the page. Two of them (1 and 4) are single-paragraph summary passages rather
than dramatized scenes; I've kept them in the count because they do have a
distinct place and time, and dealt with what that means for the chapter in
the merge section below.

### Scene 1 — A February day at school

**Location:** the school — classroom, then the lunch table.
**Time:** February, told as routine rather than one dated day.

What appears to happen: a compressed account of how the month is going for
Chloe at school — the same worksheet routine, lunch alone at the end of an
empty table, a coloring exercise, a passing mention of therapy visits since
October.

**Content:** The morning sheet is unchanged; she finishes, checks it, sits
with her hands in her lap for the rest of the period. At lunch she sits alone
at the end of the table, the library book she used to prop against her milk
carton gone now. In the afternoon, a plant worksheet: she finishes the words
fast, then colors carefully inside the lines. A note that she has been seeing
Dr. Ammons since October, and at the most recent visit, a fortnight back, she
said some of it had gotten easier.

**Possible purpose:** may be establishing that whatever leads to the floor
scene next has been building slowly for months rather than starting that
afternoon, and may be quietly tracking small losses since earlier chapters
(the missing library book) as evidence of that.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that her school life has gotten
smaller and quieter without anything dramatic happening in it, which is
possibly the point of contrast with what happens the moment she gets home.

### Scene 2 — The floor

**Location:** Chloe's bedroom, on the floor in the gap between the bed and the
wall.
**Time:** one afternoon/evening after school, continuing through dinner.

What appears to happen: Chloe's mother finds her wedged in the gap between the
bed and the wall and Chloe, unable to speak in full sentences, offers up
everything she has to trade in exchange for going to Halstead.

**Content:** Home from school, she goes upstairs in her coat; her mother finds
her on the floor. She can't get a full sentence out, only "please," in pieces.
In sequence she offers: doing the dishes every night for life, doing her
father's chores before he asks, giving back the bike, giving up Christmas
(this year and every year after), giving up her birthday. Her mother crouches,
then gets down on the floor and holds her. Asked what's wrong, Chloe can't
isolate one cause — school, the sheets, and a classmate are each a piece of it
— and says only "This." Her mother holds her through dinner; her father
appears in the doorway with a dishcloth and goes back down.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's central scene and the reason the
chapter exists — establishing the size of what Chloe is asking for by showing
what she is willing to give up for it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that she is negotiating with the
only currency a seven-year-old actually has, one item at a time, and that
nobody has told her yes yet by the time the scene ends.

### Scene 3 — Overheard at the kitchen table

**Location:** Chloe's bed (listening) / the kitchen table below (her parents).
**Time:** later the same night, after Scene 2.

What appears to happen: that night, Chloe overhears her parents arguing about
what she said on the floor.

**Content:** Her mother tells her father what Chloe said about the birthday.
He says he heard it, and has been trying to work out what to say back. She
says she has been the only one raising this since August and wants him to
actually say what he thinks out loud. A pause — a chair, a tap running. He
says he has thought the same thing since a car ride in July. She asks him to
say it out loud at the table, because she has been guessing at his position
for months. Chloe, listening from bed, can't make out her father's answer, but
catches her mother say "four hours."

**Possible purpose:** may be showing the imbalance in who has been carrying
this conversation, and may be showing that the actual decision-making has
mostly already happened before the chapter's announcement scene.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that the plain answer Chloe asked
for at the end of Scene 2 is not coming that night, and that the parents
already privately agree before either of them says so to her.

### Scene 4 — The rest of February

**Location:** home, in general, and Dr. Ammons's office.
**Time:** the remaining weeks of February, ending on an unspecified Thursday.

What appears to happen: the month runs out without either parent bringing it
up again, and Chloe stops expecting them to.

**Content:** Middle of February, then the end of it, dinner passing
unmentioned. At a Thursday appointment, Chloe says she is "fine" and has no
answer when Dr. Ammons asks what fine means.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to compress the waiting, and may be a
second instance of the same not-knowing-what-she-feels beat used in Scene 1's
Ammons line.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be underlining how long the silence
feels to Chloe, against the "four hours" her parents apparently discussed.

### Scene 5 — The fourteenth of March

**Location:** their house — living room, then the stairs and landing.
**Time:** one evening, March 14th.

What appears to happen: her parents tell her yes, and Chloe needs it repeated
and confirmed several times before the news lands.

**Content:** Parents sit her down; her father has a plan already worked out —
a form already sent, a visit day in April, a possible spring start. Chloe
demands the yes be restated three separate ways before she accepts it. She
screams, hugs her father and then her mother in turn, thanking each of them.
She runs upstairs, comes back down to ask about telling Ruth, is told yes, and
goes back up. Her parents, listening to her overhead, exchange a last short
line: her father says "that's her, that's what she's actually like," and her
mother says she knows.

I'm treating the movement between the living room and the stairs/landing as
one scene rather than a break, since there's no time skip anywhere in it —
it's one continuous stretch of Chloe reacting to the news, and she's still
inside the same few minutes when it ends.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's emotional high point and the direct
payoff of Scene 2's plea.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that Chloe needs an answer
confirmed multiple times before she trusts it, the same way she needed to list
every item she'd give up earlier rather than just asking once, and the closing
exchange between the parents reads to me as ambiguous rather than purely
happy, though nothing in the prose tells the reader to read it either way.

### Scene 6 — The house offer

**Location:** their kitchen.
**Time:** a Saturday in April, with Chloe away at a neighbor's.

What appears to happen: in April, the school offers the family free campus
housing tied to Chloe's enrollment, and her parents argue about it with the
positions from Scene 3 reversed.

**Content:** Her father brings home a letter offering a furnished house near
campus for as long as Chloe is enrolled. Her mother refuses immediately and
holds the refusal through several rounds of pressing. Her stated reason: if
they take the house, pulling Chloe out later would leave the family homeless,
so the school can have Chloe for the year but not the family's address. Her
father counters that he agreed to a summer camp, not to moving; she counters
that he'd already agreed to "the whole idea" in July. Nothing is resolved; the
scene ends on both of them returning to ordinary tasks.

**Possible purpose:** may be a second test of the same territory as Scene 3
(what the family is willing to hand over for Halstead) with the roles
swapped — the reluctant parent from Scene 3 is now the one pushing, and vice
versa.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that saying yes to Halstead in
March was not the same as agreeing to everything the school might later ask
for, and that the two parents don't weigh the risk the same way.

### Scene 7 — The Halstead tour

**Location:** the Halstead campus and buildings.
**Time:** a Friday morning into midday in April, raining.

What appears to happen: a staff member walks the family around the school for
about an hour, fielding Chloe's questions and her mother's.

**Content:** They tour the school in the rain with a staff member, Mrs.
Okonkwo, who won't confirm whether a boy Chloe knows from camp is enrolled
there but tells Chloe she knows the boy exists. Chloe's mother asks about the
nurse and about night staff and gets straight answers to both.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give the school itself some physical
presence in the chapter for the first time, after two scenes of arguing about
it in the abstract.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be showing an adult at the school
handling a question she can't answer (whether Sam is enrolled) without
brushing Chloe off, which could be read against how Chloe's own parents
handle questions they don't want to answer elsewhere in the chapter, though I
am not confident that comparison is intended.

### Scene 8 — The classroom

**Location:** a second-grade classroom at Halstead.
**Time:** that same afternoon; her parents are elsewhere in the building.

What appears to happen: Chloe is placed with a room of other kids and then
interviewed one-on-one while her parents are off doing something else.

**Content:** A boy at her table lends her a pencil before she asks. A man
interviews her about what she'd do with a day of her own and lets her talk as
long as she wants. No test is given, but she spends the rest of the afternoon
expecting one.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show Chloe handled well by an adult who
isn't her parent, and possibly to plant the expectation of a test that the
chapter then declines to pay off.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that being let finish, and not
being tested, are both new enough to her that she notices them, though the
prose doesn't say either directly.

### Scene 9 — The drive home

**Location:** the car, on the road.
**Time:** that evening, after dark.

What appears to happen: Chloe talks without a break for most of the drive and
falls asleep mid-sentence.

**Content:** She asks her dad question after question about the school,
running one into the next. She falls asleep partway through a sentence about
the pool and picks it back up, roughly where she left off, when the car stops
at home.

**Possible purpose:** may be there to show how much has changed in her since
the silent, contained kid of Scene 1 — a lot of unbroken talking rather than
staying quiet with her eyes on a tray.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be underlining a contrast with
Scene 1's silence, though I am inferring that contrast rather than finding it
stated.

### Scene 10 — Arriving home

**Location:** their house, then the driveway.
**Time:** that same night, immediately after Scene 9.

What appears to happen: Chloe goes in for water; her mother stays sitting in
the parked car.

**Content:** Her dad carries her bag in. She gets a glass of water at the
sink. Out the window, her mother is still in the car with the engine off, not
yet opening her door, until Chloe is called in for toast.

**Possible purpose:** may be there specifically to close the chapter on the
mother's hesitation rather than on Chloe's day.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that the one adult whose consent
still matters most hasn't arrived at hers by the chapter's last page, though
the prose does not say this and I could be reading too much into a parked
car.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph walk

Numbering restarts at 1 in each scene, matching the scene breakdown above.
"No issue" is used per the brief wherever nothing looked wrong to me. Where a
finding has a matching entry in the Feedback list at the end, I've named it
rather than numbered it, since the scene split changed after this walk was
first written and a numbered cross-reference would have been one more thing
to get out of sync.

### Scene 1

1. "February goes by with the telephone silent, and the sheet in the morning
   is the same sheet as Monday's with different numbers in it, so she
   finishes it, checks it, and sits behind it with her hands in her lap for
   the rest of the period. At lunch she carries her tray past empty seats to
   get to the seat at the very end, hers every day, past where the talk
   reaches. She used to keep a library book open against her milk carton
   there, but the carton stands on its own now, so her eyes stay on the tray
   until the bell. In the afternoon they do a worksheet about the parts of a
   plant, which has a diagram on it that you colour in once you have finished
   the words, and Chloe finishes the words in no time, but then colours the
   diagram very slowly, going right up to the lines and stopping exactly
   there. There have been a handful of visits to Dr. Ammons since October,
   and at the last of them, a fortnight ago, she was asked whether any of it
   had got easier and said some of it had."

   Four separate beats (sheet routine, lunch, worksheet, therapy) share one
   paragraph. Each clause is camera-level on its own — I did not find a
   trailing explanatory clause here, since "so her eyes stay on the tray
   until the bell" and "so she finishes it, checks it, and sits... with her
   hands in her lap" both show her doing something rather than telling the
   reader what it means. The last sentence (the Ammons visit) is reported
   rather than spoken — see Feedback, "Two therapy exchanges." Otherwise no
   issue, though see the chapter-whole discussion below on whether this
   single paragraph is doing too many jobs at once.

## The chapter whole

**Scene count and average length, as found: 10 scenes, 81 paragraphs, an
average of 8.1 paragraphs per scene.** That average hides a lopsided shape:
two of the scenes are 23 paragraphs (Scenes 2 and 5) and two are single-
paragraph summary bridges (Scenes 1 and 4), and the last four scenes of the
chapter — the entire Halstead visit day — run 6, 2, 2, and 3 paragraphs.
Thirteen paragraphs of the chapter's eighty-one, covering what is arguably a
whole day, are currently split across four scenes averaging 3.25 paragraphs
each. That is the vignette shape the brief warns about, concentrated in one
stretch of the chapter rather than spread through it.

**Does each scene need to exist?**

Scene 1 and Scene 4 are each a single connective paragraph rather than a
dramatized scene in their own right — Scene 1 zooms from the whole month down
to specific details without a hard break before Scene 2 begins, and Scene 4
does the same thing bridging into Scene 5. Neither reads to me as a
standalone unit so much as the lead-in to the scene that follows it. I don't
think this calls for any rewriting: both already flow directly into their
neighbor with no section break, no repeated information, and nothing that
would need to travel across a seam, because there is no seam — they're
already one continuous piece of prose. Folding Scene 1 into Scene 2 and Scene
4 into Scene 5, as their opening paragraphs, would cost nothing, since that
is functionally what they already are.

Scene 3 and Scene 6 are both parents' arguments about how far to go for
Halstead, which on a first read looked to me like a candidate for the kind of
merge Chapter 1 did — two scenes doing the same job with the same characters.
Looking more closely, I don't think it's the same job twice: Scene 3 has Meg
pressing and Dave silent, arguing about the decision to send Chloe at all;
Scene 6 has Dave pressing and Meg refusing, arguing about a specific new
condition (housing) neither of them anticipated in Scene 3. The positions are
reversed rather than repeated, which reads to me as the more likely reason
both exist — it would cost the reversal to merge them into one scene, since
the whole shape of the second argument depends on Scene 3 having already
happened with the roles the other way round. I'm not proposing this merge.

**The Halstead day (Scenes 7-10): the vignette stretch, and what I'd merge.**

Scene 7 (the tour, 6 paragraphs) is the one piece of this day with real
dramatized dialogue and a bounded unit ("takes them around for an hour"). I'm
recommending it stand on its own.

Scenes 8, 9, and 10 (the classroom, the drive, and the arrival home — 2, 2,
and 3 paragraphs) are where I think the actual merge belongs, and the prose
already supplies what would need to carry across each seam, rather than
needing anything invented:

- Scene 8 into Scene 9: Scene 8's last sentence already reads "No test
  arrives, but she keeps waiting for somebody to hand her one, right through
  the afternoon and out to the car" — the unresolved wait for a test is the
  thread that already crosses from the classroom into the car, so the seam
  is already written, just currently sitting on the wrong side of a scene
  break.
- Scene 9 into Scene 10: there is no gap to bridge here at all. Scene 9 ends
  "when the car stops in the driveway she wakes up and picks the sentence
  back up," and Scene 10 opens "Her dad carries her bag in" — the car
  stopping and the walk inside are one uninterrupted action, the same kind of
  continuous movement I treated as staying inside Scene 5 rather than
  breaking it (living room to stairs to landing, no time skip).

Combined, Scenes 8-10 would run seven paragraphs as a single scene — "the
rest of the day," or similar — carried by the waiting-for-a-test thread into
the drive and then by plain physical continuity into the house. I looked at
whether Scene 7 belongs in that same merge too, making the whole visit one
scene the way the "envelope at the mailbox" example folds several locations
and a day-later coda into one reported unit. The difference I found: that
example is entirely reported/summarized action with no scene proper inside
it, where Scene 7 here is bounded, dramatized, and separated from what
follows by an explicit time and configuration change ("In the afternoon,"
parents leaving to go elsewhere). I'm treating that as a real scene boundary
and not folding Scene 7 in, but flagging that this is the more debatable call
in this section — a reader could reasonably take the whole day as one scene
instead.

**Recommended scene count after merges: 6** (Scenes 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, and the
combined 8-9-10), folding Scenes 1 and 4 into the openings of 2 and 5 as
described above. Average length across those six: 81 ÷ 6 ≈ 13.5 paragraphs,
against the brief's four-to-six range for a chapter this length. I'm not
recommending any scene, or any content, be cut — every merge above is a
regrouping of paragraphs that are already written to flow into each other,
not a proposal to remove anything from the chapter.

## Feedback

**"The crying is still ahead of her, and she is sitting in the gap with a
fistful of carpet in either hand."**
The first clause reads as the narrator noting what's coming rather than
reporting what is visible in the room right now.
Action: consider cutting the forward-reference clause.
Replace with: "She is sitting in the gap with a fistful of carpet in either
hand." Or, if a lead-in beat is wanted before the dialogue: "Her breathing is
short and even, and she is sitting in the gap with a fistful of carpet in
either hand."

**"but from the floor there is only his feet on the kitchen tile, back and
forth, no pan, no water."**
Grammar defect: "feet" is plural and does not agree with "is."
Action: change "is" to "are."
Replace with: "but from the floor there are only his feet on the kitchen
tile, back and forth, no pan, no water."

**A repeated "because"-clause in adult dialogue, roughly eight instances in
this chapter alone** (examples: "Baby, breathe for me, just breathe, because
you have to breathe before you can talk about any of it." / "Do what, because
you have to tell me what it is and then I'll fix it, whatever it is." / "I
heard her, because I was standing right there in the doorway with a dishcloth
in my hand..." / "Then say it out loud at this table, because I need to
actually hear it from you...").
Nearly every line either parent speaks in the two argument scenes carries an
explicit "because" justification attached to a request or complaint. Some of
these earn their place (the reasons genuinely need stating), but the rate
across a roughly 2,600-word chapter looks high enough that it may be worth
cutting a couple of the least load-bearing ones so the device doesn't start
reading as a verbal tic shared by both parents.
Action: consider cutting or restructuring two or three of the weaker
instances, leaving the ones where the "because" clause is actually load-
bearing (for instance, "Then give me one reason, because I have been sitting
here trying to think of one, but I can't" needs its because).
Replace with, for the two clearest candidates: "Baby, breathe for me, just
breathe. Then you can tell me." (cutting the because-clause entirely) and
"Do what? Tell me what it is and I'll fix it, whatever it is." (same).

**""I can't do it anymore," she says, and it is a whole sentence, and she
gets it out twice."**
"and it is a whole sentence" tells the reader something already visible in
the quotation itself.
Action: consider cutting the clause.
Replace with: "'I can't do it anymore,' she says, and she gets it out twice."
Or, if the point is her regaining enough control to finish a sentence: "'I
can't do it anymore,' she says, and this time she gets all the way through
it, twice."

**"Home from school, she goes upstairs with her coat still on, so that when
her mom comes up to say that dinner is in ten minutes she finds her on the
floor in the gap between the bed and the wall, with her back against the bed
and her knees pulled up."**
The pronoun chain (she / her mom / she / her) makes it briefly unclear who
finds whom.
Action: consider naming the mother at the point of discovery rather than
relying on the pronoun switch.
Replace with: "Home from school, she goes upstairs with her coat still on.
Ten minutes before dinner, her mom comes up to get her and finds her on the
floor in the gap between the bed and the wall, with her back against the bed
and her knees pulled up." Or: "Home from school, she goes upstairs with her
coat still on, so that when her mom comes up to say that dinner is in ten
minutes, her mom finds her on the floor in the gap between the bed and the
wall, with her back against the bed and her knees pulled up."

**"until her dad says something she can't make out at all but her mom says
the words four hours, in the voice she uses about a thing she has already
lost."**
This is the closest match in the chapter to the named-tone construction the
house rules call out as recurring book-wide: it names the voice by what it
means rather than what it sounds like, and Chloe (the listener) doesn't get
an action beat in response before the scene ends.
Action: cut the meaning-clause; give the sound, and/or give what Chloe does
on hearing it.
Replace with: "...but her mom says the words four hours, quiet and flat."
Or: "...but her mom says the words four hours, and after that neither of them
says anything else Chloe can make out." Or, adding a reaction: "...but her
mom says the words four hours, and Chloe goes still under the pillow."

**"Twenty seconds after running upstairs she is back down, stopped on the
third step from the bottom with both hands on the rail."**
"Twenty seconds" reads as a number the narrator is measuring rather than one
a person in the room would have; nobody downstairs is timing her.

Replace with: "Before either of them has said a word, she is back down,
stopped on the third step from the bottom with both hands on the rail." Or:

bottom with both hands on the rail."

**"She runs back up." followed by "Her parents sit in the living room and

Two consecutive paragraphs describing one continuous movement (up the
