## 1. Scenes

The chapter has five sections, marked by `________________`. It does not
read as a single scene per section — the first section alone contains six
distinct scenes before Chloe ever sits down with Mr. Amberg. Numbering runs
straight through.

### Scene 1 — The interview period begins (staff office and corridor, the first Monday of April onward) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: the exit-interview process is described in general —
schedule, format, the growing roster.

**Content:** Amberg runs fifteen-minute interviews himself, a dozen students
a day, for a run of days starting the first Monday of April. A roster goes
up outside the staff office each morning; a runner calls names in. By the
sixth day the list needs a second sheet. His contracts class still meets
around it, and the second-week test goes out on schedule.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly to establish the process itself
(format, scale, duration) before narrowing to specific interviews later in
the chapter.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be setting up the ordinariness of
the machinery — a roster, a runner, a clock — against which the actual
content of the interviews (starting with Scene 3) can read as unusual.

### Scene 2 — The rest of April stays on schedule (the whole school, the same weeks) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: everything else at the school continues exactly as
planned, unaffected by the interviews.

**Content:** History, the range's Saturday relays, the forge on Thursdays
and Saturdays, lunch — all unchanged.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to set up the contrast the next paragraph
names directly ("What changes is dinner") — establishing what does *not*
change so the one thing that does stands out.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be nothing beyond that contrast;
this paragraph has no single location of its own (it moves across a
classroom, the range, the forge, and the dining hall in one sentence), so I
am not confident it functions as an independent scene rather than a list
doing setup work for Scene 3. Per `DO_NOT_FLAG.md`, the "and X, and Y, and
Z" list itself is a deliberate device and not raised as an issue.

### Scene 3 — Dinner talk turns to the interviews (the dining hall, the first week) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: two students' exit interviews become the subject of
dinner-table conversation, in different registers.

**Content:** A boy from the materials wing announces at dinner that he
turned an offer down, in what the narration calls the tone of someone
rehearsing a line for home; the table laughs and lets it go. A younger girl
comes back grinning and repeats her number and the car twice; the table
stays on it for the rest of the meal.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give two contrasting reactions (private
deflection versus public repetition) before the specific interviews that
follow.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building the general mood of
the two weeks before Chloe's own slot; see Feedback for two phrases in this
paragraph that read to me as the narrator supplying an interpretation rather
than reporting only what a camera could show.

### Scene 4 — Iyad's nightly roll call (the dining hall, then the corridor next morning, ongoing) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: Iyad tracks and recites the next day's interview
order from memory, and checks in on people who have already been in.

**Content:** He gives the table the running order by name before the sheet
is posted, then asks everyone already interviewed how it went and whether
they've told anyone at home.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to establish Iyad's habit (naming and
tracking outcomes) ahead of Scene 12, where he does the same thing with
Chloe's own result.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be establishing, alongside the
runner's "identical cadence" in Scene 5, that this information travels
through the school in the same flat, undifferentiated voice regardless of
whose name is in it — though I am not confident the echo between the two is
intended rather than coincidental.

### Scene 5 — Waiting outside the office (the corridor, Wednesday of the second week, before her slot) — 5 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe waits for her own interview and watches
another student's take barely any time at all.

**Content:** She arrives early to a corridor lined with waiting students —
one re-reading a textbook page, one staring at the closed door — and a rail
at chair height she has passed since childhood. A boy from the year above
goes in and is out again almost immediately: he already had a position
since he was fifteen, he says, and this is just making it official. The
runner calls her name and holds the door with his foot.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show, before Chloe's own interview, that
for some students this process is a formality rather than a decision —
setting up her own interview (Scene 6) as the harder case.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be establishing the range of what
"eleven years, anything for the file" can mean for different students,
which the chapter goes on to show twice more (Nadia's version in Scene 9,
Kavi's decision in Scene 11).

### Scene 6 — Chloe's interview (Amberg's office, the same slot) — 13 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe turns down a full-time research job offer from
the school, explaining that staying would keep her forever inside the same
measuring stick.

**Content:** Amberg opens with "Eleven years... Anything for the file." He
lays out an offer: full-time research, $135,000 to start, housing, food, a
car, medical, a paid degree on her own schedule with the post held open,
standard reviews. She reads the sheet in silence — recalling teaching the
twelves, the forge, the acceptance letter she still keeps — and says no. He
sets his pen down, which she notes she has seen him do perhaps twice in all
her years there. Asked for a reason, she compares staying to sitting the
same exam every year against the same ninety people: she'd never find out
if she were good at the exam or just good against that room. He writes it
down, closes the folder exactly when the clock reads zero, and the next
student is already waiting.

I am not proposing a fix to the offer itself (see the note at the top of
this file); the two items I am raising from this scene are both about
narrator clauses appended to observations, not about the offer's content.

**Possible purpose:** may exist as the chapter's central scene — the one the
title refers to — establishing both the offer's exact terms (repeated twice
more later, to Nadia's version and to Chloe's parents) and Chloe's stated
reasoning for refusing it.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that her reason is explicitly
about measurement rather than the money — a reading consistent with the
brief's calibration note that she reads social/institutional situations
rather than following a rule for its own sake. I'm not confident this
scene alone settles the question of whether that reasoning reads as
"computational" in the way the calibration note warns against; see Section
5.

### Scene 7 — The aggregate results (no single location, the same two weeks) — 3 paragraphs

What appears to happen: the chapter pulls back from Chloe's own interview to
report on all ninety-one.

**Content:** Thirteen say yes, all but one already somewhere in the building
on staff. The exception is a boy from the language block with no prior
position, who says yes anyway and spends the week asking around to find out
what the job even is. The eighty who say no return to finishing out the
school year.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give the reader the statistical frame
(thirteen of ninety-one) that both Nadia's interview (Scene 9) and Chloe's
own decision sit inside.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be setting up the boy who says yes
with no prior position as a small counter-case to both Chloe (who says no
specifically because she already has a position there) and Nadia (who says
no for a different, business-shaped reason) — though I don't have a
confident read on why this particular figure is singled out rather than
another of the eighty saying no.

### Scene 8 — Nadia waits for her slot (the corridor, Thursday of the first week) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: Nadia is called in for her own interview.

**Content:** She spends the wait finishing her ninth job application of the
week on her phone and is in the chair before the runner finishes with the
door.

**Possible purpose:** may exist mainly as a fast transition into Scene 9,
establishing before she even sits down that she is already deep into her
own job search independent of the school's offer.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be a small piece of information
that pays off later in the same scene, when she tells Amberg the exact
number of applications she's sent.

### Scene 9 — Nadia's interview (Amberg's office) — 25 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Nadia turns down the same offer Chloe turned down,
for a different, more concrete reason — she doesn't think the job itself
would give her anything to do — and describes the recruiting business she is
building instead.

**Content:** Amberg opens with the identical "Eleven years... Anything for
the file." Nadia says no. Asked for a reason, she says she'd be good at only
a few hours a month of the actual work; the rest is a desk with a paper at
the end that nobody calls about. Amberg points out the eighty saying no are
all going to college; she says she costed it (four years, about $110,000)
against jobs she's applying for, none of which ask for a degree. She's sent
two hundred and one applications since November for thirteen answers. She
describes her idea: a hiring service the employer pays for, not the
applicant. Amberg notes most people who say this in his office don't follow
through; she doesn't dispute it, and says if nothing comes of it by spring
she'll take whatever job is still open. Asked what her parents say, she says
she hasn't told them. He finishes writing and stands — see Feedback below
for a wrong name in this closing line.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give a second, contrasting answer to the
same offer and the same opening question — not Chloe's "I'd rather find out
what I am" but a specific, numbered case against the job on its own terms
(hours worked versus hours that matter).

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building a set of three
different refusals across the chapter (Chloe's, Nadia's, and — by contrast —
the yeses from Kavi and the language-block boy), none of which the narration
ranks against each other.

### Scene 10 — That night, the tally (unspecified, that night) — 1 paragraph

What appears to happen: Nadia checks her own numbers against what she told
Amberg and keeps working.

**Content:** She recounts the lines in her notebook (two hundred and one,
matching the figure she gave him), counts what five years of Saturday work
have left in an account her mother opened for her, writes the first sixty
lines of "the thing," and is up at five filling in more forms.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show her checking her own claims the same
way she'd expect anyone else's numbers to be checked — a private version of
the verification she just performed out loud in Amberg's office.

**What I think it tells the reader:** I'm flagging, at high confidence, that
this paragraph and the one before it may not both be about the same
character — see Feedback. If it is Nadia's paragraph (which the pronoun
chain up to this point supports), it tells the reader she keeps working
immediately rather than waiting to see how the interview outcome is treated
by anyone else.

### Scene 11 — Finding Kavi (the network room, that evening) — 7 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe seeks out Kavi after hearing secondhand that
he said yes to the same kind of offer, and he explains why the work no
longer feels like school to him.

**Content:** She hears his decision from the common room and goes to ask him
directly rather than take the secondhand version. She finds him coiling
stripped cable, working while he talks. She asks why, naming his other
options (MIT, Caltech). He says what he wants to do is already happening in
one building and he's in it. She presses — same building, same people, how
would he know it's him and not the building. He says it stopped being
school when the problems stopped having a known answer to check him
against; he describes bringing an unchecked false-positive rate that the
whole building had to wait on. He delivers the last line — "It's a lab that
also has children in it" — in what the narration calls the register he'd
use to correct a wrong exponent. She watches him go back to work and leaves
it there.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to give a third answer to the same
underlying question (why stay, why leave) — not Chloe's need to be measured
elsewhere, not Nadia's cost-benefit case, but a claim that the work itself
has already stopped being school.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be positioned deliberately against
Chloe's own reasoning in Scene 6 — she leaves because staying keeps the
question of her ability open; Kavi stays because staying is where the
unanswered questions already are. I'm not confident the text means the
contrast to be noticed rather than simply presenting two people who chose
differently.

### Scene 12 — Iyad's report, then leaving (the dining hall, that evening) — 2 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Iyad reports Chloe's interview at dinner in the same
tone he gives everyone else's, and she leaves before dessert.

**Content:** Iyad places her result in the middle of the list, in the same
voice as everything else; a boy asks if she's sure. Iyad asks if she's told
anyone at home; she answers, and he moves on. She's up the stairs before the
pudding goes round.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show her result being absorbed into the
same flat, undifferentiated reporting Iyad gives everyone else's, immediately
before the scene (the phone call home) where it very much isn't
undifferentiated.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be building toward the contrast
with Scene 13 — the version of the news that gets a single sentence at
dinner versus the version that takes an entire phone call.

### Scene 13 — The phone call home (Chloe's room, then her parents' kitchen, that night) — 42 paragraphs

What appears to happen: Chloe tells her parents she turned the job down, and
they work through what that means for close to the length of the whole
rest of the chapter.

**Content:** She calls that night, sitting on her floor. She tells her
mother she said no to something big; gives the offer's terms in the order
Amberg gave them; her mother repeats the salary figure aloud and calls in
her father. Chloe gives the same list again, word for word, when he asks to
hear it properly. He calls it a mistake and lays out what the guaranteed
money would have meant against a lifetime of paying rent; she says she did
the math herself in February and kept the page. He asks her to explain it to
him the way she explained it to Amberg; she gives the same room-and-ranking
reasoning from Scene 6, now in more detail (two buildings, since she was
seven). Her mother reframes it as choosing to find out she isn't as good as
she thinks; Chloe agrees that's the uncertainty, not a claim to being good.
Her father asks if the offer stays open later; she admits she never asked.
Her mother raises the cost of Georgetown if this is the path instead and
makes Chloe say the acknowledgment back. They ask about the others — Ruth
to MIT, Sam to the Army program, Kavi took the offer she didn't. Both
parents restate that they think it's a mistake, and her mother connects the
letter on this offer to "the first one" (presumably her original acceptance
letter) years ago. They both say okay and hang up. Chloe sits a while, then
gets up to eat.

**Possible purpose:** may exist as the chapter's actual emotional center —
the interview scene establishes what she decided and why; this scene is
where that decision is tested against two people who disagree with it and
still don't get her to change her mind.

**What I think it tells the reader:** may be that her reasoning survives
being repeated twice, word for word, under pressure from both parents in
turn — the repetition itself (not a narrator's comment on it) may be doing
that work. See Feedback for a place where I think the narrator does comment
on it directly, and for a small inconsistency in how many buildings are at
stake across this scene and Scenes 6 and 11.

## 4. The chapter whole

**Scene count and average length.** I found 13 scenes across 103
paragraphs, an average of about 7.9 paragraphs per scene. That average
hides a very lopsided distribution: seven of the thirteen scenes are a
single paragraph, and three scenes (13, 6, 9 — 42, 13, and 25 paragraphs)
account for 80 of the 103 paragraphs between them.

**Is this the vignette problem?** I think the front of the chapter (Scenes
1 through 4, all one paragraph each, covering the interview period's
schedule, the rest of April, dinner-table talk, and Iyad's habit) is the
part of this chapter that comes closest to what the brief describes — four
short, disconnected-feeling pieces of scene-setting before the chapter
narrows to Chloe's own slot in Scene 5. The rest of the chapter does not
read as vignettes to me: Scenes 6, 9, and 13 are long because each is one
continuous, unbroken conversation, not because a longer scene has been cut
into short pieces.

**Concrete merges, with what carries the seam:**

1. **Scenes 1–2** (the interview period's schedule; the rest of April
   staying unaffected) are already bridged by an explicit pronoun — Scene
   2 opens "The rest of April carries on exactly as scheduled around it,"
   where "it" is the interview process Scene 1 just described. Recommend
   one scene: the interview period's schedule, and what does and doesn't
   change around it.

2. **Scenes 3–4** (dinner talk turning to the interviews; Iyad's roll
   call) share the same location (the dining hall) and the same underlying
   subject (the interviews as a topic at the table). What carries across:
   the table itself, and Iyad specifically, who is mentioned generically at
   the end of Scene 3's kind of report and by name at the start of Scene 4.
   Recommend one scene: dinner during the interview weeks.

Applying both would take the chapter from 13 scenes to 11. I did not find
further merge candidates elsewhere in the chapter: Scenes 5 and 6 (corridor,
