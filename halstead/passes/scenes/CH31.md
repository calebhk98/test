# Scene audit — Chapter 31: Ruth

## Method note

A scene is one continuous stretch of one time, one place, and one action.
A section break (`________________`) is not a scene boundary by itself; I
looked for where the clock jumps or the place changes instead. This chapter
also has narration that is neither — long stretches summarizing weeks or
months of habitual action (a semester's shape, a year of library evenings).
I have not counted those as scenes. They get a paragraph pass too, under
"Connective narration," but they are not numbered as scenes because there is
no single time or place to give them.

Two stretches of the chapter are chat transcripts (lines 107–133 and
137–145). My rule: each name-prefixed line is one character speaking, same as
a line of dialogue, and gets the same new-speaker-new-paragraph treatment.
What decides where one chat *scene* ends and another begins is not the
transcript format but the same rule as everywhere else — one continuous
occasion of one subject. The two transcripts here are on visibly different
subjects (the hiring-pool assessment vs. "who did this") with a section
break and an unstated gap between them, so I have counted them as two scenes,
not one long one. I could not tell from the file whether they are the same
sitting continued after a pause or two separate nights — I am guessing they
are separate, because the second one opens cold on a new question rather than
continuing the first.

## Scene 1 — The registrar's office

**Location/time:** the registrar's office, several flights up, October.

What appears to happen: Ruth, believing her placement is a mistake, brings a
folder of evidence to the registrar's counter and is told the placement
stands.

**Content:** Ruth lays out the diagnostic, the placement, and the syllabus.
The woman behind the counter hears her out, stays unmoved, tells her a
petition needs a faculty signature she does not have, and slides across a
form.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it exists to open the "wrong track" belief
concretely, and to give the reader the first no before the department gives
the real one.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that Ruth's method (evidence,
argument, precision) is about to run into an institution that does not
grade on that method — though the department scene two paragraphs later
appears to make the same point with more specificity, which is part of what
I flag below.

### Paragraph walkthrough

3. (l.9) "The registrar's office is several flights up...It asks for a
   faculty signature Ruth hasn't gotten yet." No issue. Good staging (the
   dead elevator sign, the form sliding across the counter).

**Paragraph count:** 3. No thin paragraphs. No merge needed inside the

## Scene 2 — The department office

**Location/time:** the department office, late October, after weeks of
being bounced between offices.

What appears to happen: a different administrator actually reads Ruth's
folder in full and tells her, at more length and with more specificity than
the registrar did, that there is no other track.

**Content:** the back-and-forth between advising and the department over
three weeks; Ruth learns the building's geography and starts carrying a
folder everywhere; a woman reads the whole folder in order and tells her the
placement is final and always has been; Ruth argues the diagnostic must have
meant something; the woman repeats herself and offers a tutoring pamphlet
Ruth doesn't take.

**Possible purpose:** may be doing the same job as scene 1 with a sharper,
more specific voice — the woman's line about "not in this department and not
upstairs either" reads like the fuller version of the registrar's more
generic no.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that the mistake, in Ruth's
own account, has become "bigger than one office can fix" — the closing line
of the scene reframes it as systemic rather than clerical, which may be
setting up the numbers she goes looking for later in the chapter.

## Scene 6 — The insurance phone call

**Location/time:** a Sunday, undated, over the phone, sometime in the
"most of a year" span that opens section 2.

What appears to happen: Ruth's father tells her the health plan covers a
specialist visit without a referral; she corrects him with the actual
clause and the actual deadline, and he checks and agrees.

**Content:** the call happens on a Sunday; he states the plan wrong; she
gives the real rule (thirty days, already passed); he looks it up while
still on the line and agrees.

**Possible purpose:** may exist to show the same precision that fails her
at MIT working perfectly on a problem that isn't about her — she can win
an argument with a clause and a number when it isn't the placement.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly the paragraph's own last
line does this work already — "Neither of them mentions that she hasn't
once, in months of Sunday calls, said anything about the placement" — which
is why I'm not sure this needs separate scene status at all rather than
standing as one illustrative beat inside the montage it's currently buried
in. It is very short for a scene: three sentences and one line of dialogue.

### Paragraph walkthrough

This scene has no numbered paragraph of its own — it is embedded, with no
paragraph break, inside paragraph 22 (l.49), which I address as a whole
below because it mixes three different jobs.

22. (l.49) "The months underneath that sentence look, from outside, like
    everything is fine. Class happens. Problem sets go in early. Dinner is
    in the hall nearest her building, most nights, because a fixed routine
    is one less thing to decide. When her mother calls on Sundays, Ruth
    answers on the second ring and gives her the actual week, in order. Her
    father tells her, over speakerphone, that the university health plan
    covers a specialist visit without a referral, but she corrects him.
    'That's not what the policy says, Dad...' He reads it back to her off
    his laptop while she's still on the line, finds the clause, and agrees
    she's right. Neither of them mentions that she hasn't once, in months
    of Sunday calls, said anything about the placement."

    Two things flagged here.

    First: "because a fixed routine is one less thing to decide" is the
    same construction as paragraph 4's — a visible fact ("Dinner is in the
    hall nearest her building, most nights") followed by the narrator's
    reason for it. Struck, nothing I can find is lost.

    Second, a paragraph-shape issue rather than a sentence one: this single
    paragraph opens as general montage (three short sentences of habitual
    fact), then without any break becomes a specific, dated, dialogued
    scene (the phone call), then closes on a reflective sentence about what
    isn't said. That's three different registers in one paragraph with no
    seam marked between any of them. I think the general montage sentences
    and the phone-call scene read as two different things and might sit
    better as two paragraphs, breaking after "gives her the actual week, in
    order."

**Paragraph count:** the scene occupies part of 1 paragraph as it stands.
Recommended: split into 2 (see Feedback).

## Scene 8 — The professor's office, and after

**Content:** his office described; she states the result conclusion-first;

makes both lines true at once.

## Chapter as a whole

Ten scenes found, several of them very short (2, 3, and part of one
paragraph for scenes 5, 3-after-merge, and 6), one very long (16 paragraphs
for scene 8). At roughly 2,610 words this chapter sits at the low end of the
book's word-count band, so it isn't a nine-page chapter wanting four to six
scenes by the brief's own math — but ten scenes, several of them under two
paragraphs, is still on the vignette side of the line, and I think the
chapter would read as a tighter escalation with something closer to seven.

Where I'd merge, concretely:

**Scenes 1 and 2 (registrar, then department).** Both dramatize the same
event — Ruth brings evidence, an administrator says no — twice in a row,
with the department scene doing it with more specificity (a named
character, real dialogue, a reason). I think the registrar's office could
come down to a sentence or two of connective narration ("the registrar's
office sends her to a petition she can't file yet; advising sends her to
the department") folded into what is now paragraph 4, and the department
scene, which already has the dialogue and the concrete stakes, carries the
whole beat. What crosses the seam: the folder itself, already established
as accumulating paper — it can simply be heavier by the time she reaches
the department office, without a second full scene showing how.

**Scenes 4 and 5 (the fifth-grade question, and writing it down).** As
noted above, I think these are already one scene interrupted by a paragraph
break rather than two — the bridge (the walk home, "that night") is already
in the prose. Nothing needs to be added; I'd just stop treating the
paragraph break as a scene break.

**Scene 6 (the phone call).** At three sentences and one line of dialogue,
this is the thinnest thing in the chapter I'd still call a scene. I don't
think it needs to be cut — the last line does real work — but I'd also not
object to it staying exactly what it currently reads as: one illustrative
beat inside the surrounding montage, not a scene that needs its own
paragraph break to announce itself.

I don't think scenes 7, 8, 9, or 10 should merge with anything. Scene 7 and
scene 8 look similar on the surface (a proof, an error) but are doing
opposite work — one is Ruth being fast, the other is Ruth being slow — and
collapsing them would remove the contrast the chapter appears to be built
on. Scenes 9 and 10 are both chat scenes but are on different subjects with
what reads like a real gap between them.

No grammar or syntax defects of the kind the brief describes chapter 1
having (missing verbs, dangling modifiers, pronouns without antecedents)
turned up in this chapter as I read it — I'm flagging that as a possible
gap in my own reading rather than a clean bill of health, since I was
looking hardest for the narrator-explanation category and may have read
past something else.
