## Scene 1 — Timed writing, then the bridge (paragraphs 1–28, lines 5–59)

**Content:** First week. Writing exercise: the class works against a shared
fifteen-minute timer; Chloe finishes with time to spare, Ruth is fastest,
Owen slowest. A rule exchange about stopping mid-sentence vs mid-paragraph.
Later that week: a bridge-building project. Ruth argues triangles beat
squares for rigidity, demonstrates it with dowels, is told to build her own.
Chloe's table builds flat and wide anyway. On test day the load test proves
Ruth right — her table's bridge folds at the seam the same way her demo
square did; Ruth's own bridge holds nearly twice the weight before snapping.
Chloe writes down why her corner failed.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be establishing the camp's pace
(everyone moving at their own speed, nobody waiting) as the chapter's frame,
and using the bridge as a first plot payoff of that frame — Ruth is fast
*and* right, which appears to set up the fractions scene where being fast is
not the same as being right.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that Chloe notices and can
follow an argument (the diagonal) even when she isn't the one making it, and
that being overruled by the group doesn't stop Ruth from being correct.
Hedged — I don't know if that's the intended takeaway.

## Scene 2 — Fractions, then the homework that night (paragraphs 1–26, lines 63–113)

**Content:** Second week. Mr. Baptiste teaches dividing fractions by
flipping and multiplying; Chloe can execute it faster than most but can't
see why it works, which appears to be the source of the "furious" line at
paragraph 2. Owen loses the thread and is told they'll redo it tomorrow.
Ruth pushes the rule to its edge case (zero) and the bell cuts her off
mid-argument. That night, alone with her packet, Chloe re-derives the "why"
herself by drawing her own numbers and counting.

**Possible purpose:** looks like the chapter's intended emotional center —
the gap between being fast and understanding, and Chloe closing that gap
alone rather than being told the answer.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that her way of resolving not
understanding something is to keep working it by hand until it gives,
rather than to ask again or to accept the trick. Hedged.

## Scene 5 — The moon at dinner (paragraphs 1–22, lines 181–223)

**Content:** Third week. Chloe calls the moon a liar because it always
shows the same face; Sam calls that being stuck, not lying; Ruth corrects
both of them with "tidally locked" and argues that how something got that
way is the same as what it's doing now. The argument runs through dinner
and the walk back. Chloe checks her own sentences afterward out of what the
text calls a habit she's run "since November," and finds nothing wrong.

**Possible purpose:** looks like a lighter, banter-driven scene after the
Owen material, and a vehicle for Ruth's precision (the same trait shown in
the bridge scene) applied to language instead of engineering.

**What I think it tells the reader:** hedged — possibly that Chloe's
self-monitoring habit is now automatic enough to run without her noticing
it start, and that this time it comes back clean.

### Paragraph walk

1–19. The moon argument itself, including "You can't lie if you don't have
   a mouth." No issue in any of these — the physics/semantics is argued by
   the characters, not explained by the narrator, and small beats like
   "Ruth has stopped eating for this" (12) and "Sam points at her with his
   fork before any of it gets out" (16) are camera-visible behavior doing
   the work of showing engagement or interruption without naming it.
20. "They go at it for the rest of dinner and most of the walk back across
   the grass... in a voice worn down mostly because Sam keeps saying
   stuck." Issue — the "because" clause explaining why Kavi's voice is worn
   down looks like the trailing explanatory construction house rule 1
   flags; the wear is audible on its own without the cause being spelled
   out.
21–22. "Partway into the walk back it catches up with her: Kavi, the moon,
   her own mouth already moving ahead of the check she has run on every
   sentence since November." / "She goes looking for the damage anyway, out
   of habit, and the sentence comes back clean, as do the ones after it."
   Calibration issue — see the section below. The behavior itself (an

   kind of computational description the calibration note says reads wrong
   for her age ("Chloe files that" being the example already cut). Flagged
   as a register concern on top of an otherwise correctly calibrated beat.

## The chapter whole

Running total: 106 paragraphs before any merge, **104** after the two
proposed in-scene merges (Scene 2's 24→25, Scene 4's 16→17).

Does each scene need to exist? Scenes 1, 2, and 4 look load-bearing — pace
and Ruth's competence, Chloe's own understanding, and the first departure.
Scene 5 looks like it's doing something distinct from the others (banter and
the masking check-in) rather than repeating a job already done, so I'm not
proposing to fold it into anything.

Scene 3 (swimming, 6 paragraphs) is the one candidate for a structural
merge rather than an internal one. It's markedly shorter than the other
four, and both it and Scene 4 land in the same window: Scene 3 says "by the
end of the second week," and Scene 4 opens "this is Wednesday of the second
week." That's a time cue already sitting in both paragraphs, which is the
kind of seam the brief asks a merge to use rather than just removing the
divider. Concretely: the swim group's material (paragraphs 1–6) could run
straight into Owen's door standing open, letting the "second week" language
already present in both do the joining work, rather than a clean break
announcing a new scene. That would take the chapter from five sections to
four. I'm not proposing to cut any content from Scene 3 in this merge —
Ruth's swim and Sam watching her do it may be seeding something later in
the book that isn't visible from this chapter alone.

I don't see a case for merging Scene 1 with Scene 2, or Scene 4 with Scene
5 — the subject changes (writing/engineering vs. math, departure vs.
banter) and the existing breaks look like they're doing real work keeping
those tonally apart.

## Feedback

**"and that meant the whole class listening to it read out on a Friday afternoon and then guessing, correctly, whose it was"**
The superlative just before this ("the longest answer... by half a page")
already makes the point; this tail spells out the consequence and confirms
the guess was right, which nobody in the scene confirms. Looks like the
trailing-explanatory-clause pattern.
Action: cut from "and that meant" to the end of the sentence.
Replace with: end the sentence at "...by half a page."

**"in a voice worn down mostly because Sam keeps saying stuck"**
The wear in Kavi's voice is audible on its own; explaining that it's
because of Sam's repetition tells the reader something the scene has
already shown (Sam has been saying "stuck" for several exchanges by this
point).
Action: cut the "because" clause.
Replace with: "...in a voice worn down by the end of it" or simply "...in a
voice worn down," with the cause left to what's already on the page.

**"the check she has run on every sentence since November" / "She goes looking for the damage anyway, out of habit, and the sentence comes back clean"**
The behavior (a habit that didn't catch her mouth this time, then runs
after the fact and clears) looks consistent with the brief's calibration
note that masking should sometimes fail and cost her something small. The
vocabulary describing it — "the check," "goes looking for the damage,"
"comes back clean" — reads as a systematic audit rather than a felt
process, which is closer to the "Chloe files that" pattern the calibration
note says was cut for reading too computational.
Action: keep the beat (mouth ahead of the check, then checking after) but
consider replacing the audit vocabulary with something more physical or
sensory.
Replace with (alternatives, not sure which is closer to what's wanted):
"her own mouth already moving before she'd caught up to it" / "her own
mouth already past whatever she usually stops it for" — and for the second
sentence, "She checks anyway, out of habit, and doesn't find anything" or
"She goes back over it anyway, and it holds."

**"Ruth is at the end of the table with her chin on her hand, pushing one piece of food in slow circles around her plate." / "He was crying in the stairwell, on Thursday..."**
These are currently two paragraphs — staging, then Ruth's line — where the

**"Priya's light goes off."**

non-dialogue paragraphs this close together risks the isolation technique
reading as a tic rather than a choice, per the "cut back, not to zero"
