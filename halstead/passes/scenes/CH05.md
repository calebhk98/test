# Scene audit — Chapter 5: Behind

Method: `passes/SCENE_AUDIT_BRIEF.md`. House rules and `DO_NOT_FLAG.md` read
first. Chapter not edited; this is report only.

**Revision note:** an earlier version of this file used the chapter's five
`________________` breaks as scene boundaries. That was wrong. A scene is a
continuous stretch of one time and one place with one action running through
it, not a typographic section — a section break can hold several scenes, and
a scene can run across one. This version re-identifies the scenes on that
basis. All sentence- and paragraph-level notes from the first pass are
unchanged in substance; only their scene grouping, the per-scene purpose
write-ups, and the chapter-whole section have been redone.

**Scene count: 10. Total paragraphs: 106. Average scene length: 10.6
paragraphs** — but that average hides a lot: scene lengths run from 1
paragraph to 21, and the two shortest (5 and 1 paragraphs) sit right next to
each other and cover the same small group of characters. See below.

Three short passages don't fit the one-time/one-place test themselves —
they're summaries spanning several days (the camp's first-week pace, the
two-week swim-progress line, the "more leave before the end of the month"
close) — and are noted as montage/lead-in material attached to the scene
they open or close, not as scenes of their own.

A note before anything else: "furious" at chapter line 65 (Scene 4) is
flagged in `DO_NOT_FLAG.md` as still open and reserved to the author. It
appears below where it falls in the paragraph walk, with no proposed fix.

## Scene 1 — The timed writing exercise

**Location:** the camp classroom. **Time:** a specific lesson ("today," per
paragraph 4) within the first week, preceded by three paragraphs summarizing
the pace of the whole week (Tuesday through Friday) rather than one moment.

**Content:** The class works a fifteen-minute timed writing exercise against
a shared clock. Chloe finishes with time to spare; Ruth is fastest, Owen
slowest. A short exchange about the mid-sentence/mid-paragraph stopping rule.
Sam and Marisol compare page counts; Chloe's own answer is set against what
it would have meant at her old school.

**Possible purpose:** looks like it may be establishing the camp's pace
(everyone moving at their own speed, the timer belonging to everybody) as
something Chloe is still adjusting to, and setting up Ruth and Owen as the
fast and slow poles she'll be measured against for the rest of the chapter.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that Chloe is comfortable
enough with the mechanics of this exercise (six sentences, ninety seconds to
spare) that the harder version of the same problem — not just being fast but
understanding why — is what Scene 4 needs to raise separately. Hedged.

### Paragraph walk

1. "Everything here moves at the speed of whoever finishes first, and in the
   first week that person is Ruth." No issue — reads as a thesis-style
   opener for the week, not a restatement of anything shown yet. Part of the
   multi-day lead-in rather than the dated scene itself.
2. "...so that Tuesday goes past without any of the empty stretches she had
   budgeted for, the kind she used to spend with a paperback wedged into her
   desk, its corner against her palm." Possible issue — the "so that" clause
   after a camera-observable pacing description is close to the trailing-
   construction house rule 1 flags, though what follows it (the paperback
   detail) is concrete and earns its place. Lower-confidence flag; see
   Feedback. Also part of the lead-in.
3. "She had expected the speed to feel like a punishment, but it feels like
   being let out..." No issue — a comparison, not a named state. Also
   lead-in.
4. "Where in the chapter that change happens, and how a reader is to know."
   Possible grammar issue — this sentence has no main verb of its own; it
   reads as a fragment (rendering the board question in compressed form).
   May be deliberate compression matching another spot in this chapter (see
   Scene 4, paragraph 5), but it matches the "list/clause with no main verb"
   defect category from chapter 1 closely enough to flag. This is where the
   dated scene ("today") actually begins.
5. "In that time Ruth produces a page and a half, most of a page comes from
   Kavi, who is still going..." Possible grammar issue — reads as a comma
   splice: "Ruth produces a page and a half" and "most of a page comes from
   Kavi" are each independent clauses joined only by a comma.
6–10. The mid-sentence/mid-paragraph exchange. No issue in any of the five —
   natural, already spoken, no narrator gloss on it.
11. "...while Chloe sits with hers, which would have been the longest answer
   in Mrs. Aldana's room by half a page, and that meant the whole class
   listening to it read out on a Friday afternoon and then guessing,
   correctly, whose it was." Issue — the superlative ("longest... by half a
   page") plus the "and that meant" tail spelling out the consequence looks
   like the trailing explanatory clause house rule 1 names as the commonest
   defect. The word "correctly" is also the narrator confirming an inference
   nobody in the scene confirms. See Feedback.

### Paragraph count

11 paragraphs (3 of them the multi-day lead-in, 8 the dated scene proper). No
single-sentence non-dialogue paragraph reads as underweight for its beat.
No merges proposed. Resulting count: **11**.

## Scene 2 — Building the bridge

## The chapter whole

**10 scenes, 106 paragraphs, average 10.6 paragraphs per scene** — 8, 9, 8,
21, 5, 5, 1, 15, 8, 22 across Scenes 1–10 (1 additional montage paragraph
sits outside all ten, after Scene 9). The chapter is not evenly built: one
scene (Scene 7) is a single paragraph, one (Scene 4) is 21, and the
variance itself is a finding — this reads as a scene-length pattern rather
than a defect, but it's worth naming because it's exactly the kind of thing
the old section-based count hid.

**Is this a string of sub-page vignettes?** Partly. Scenes 6 and 7 —
the dinner exchange about racing and Ruth's Thursday swim — are both short
(5 and 1 paragraphs), share the same two characters (Sam and Ruth, with
Chloe as the pushback voice in the first), and do what looks like the same
job: establishing that swim-group competence is being tracked whether or
not anyone calls it a race. As written they're two separate anecdotes
rather than one pattern. I think these are the clearest case in the chapter
of two scenes that should merge into one, per the brief's "two scenes doing
the same job with the same character merge into one pattern" guidance.
Concretely: Scene 7 already opens with "Ruth is in that group," referring
back to the beginner group named in Scene 6's lead-in — that's the seam
already sitting in the text. Rather than a fresh, separately felt beat, the
