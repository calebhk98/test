## Scene 4 — The entry (lines 43-77)

**Location:** his desk, the next morning.
**Time:** continuous — and this is the scene where I think a section break (line 67) does not
mark a real boundary. Nothing changes place, time, or the action running through it between
"Theo sets the page down and squares it against the others" (65) and "Theo sits back" (69);
the man in the next cubicle who checks on him (77) is still reacting to the same folder, at
the same desk, the same morning. I've walked it as one scene.

**Content:** An eight-year-old report: four operators go over a fence near a loading dock
before dawn. They're inside the first building under two minutes before a child sees them in
a corridor. The next entry, written from memory after the fact, says all four operators were
found unconscious. Blood drawn at their debrief showed a dose of a fast-acting sedative to the
milligram; the report doesn't say how it got into them (this paragraph is protected, see
above). They come round tied up, and find four children on the floor with them, arguing not
about what happened but about how they'd score the operators' performance — whether an
eight-minute response time was a fail, whether the exercise itself was fair. One of the
children, or someone directing them, asks the operator-author of the report what score he'd
have given. An adult arrives, sends the children to bed, tells the operators an attack on a
school in the United States won't be accepted, and that they're free to go. There's no reply
on file. Theo sets the page down; a coworker checks on him; he says he's fine and picks the
folder back up. In the same continuous stretch, he places himself in the memory: twelve that
year, a fire drill overnight that the whole school stood through, a grade sheet posted
afterward that he'd thought at the time was just harsh grading.

**Possible purpose:** may be the chapter's central document, converting something the book has
referenced elsewhere as a defended-against event into a report Theo now has to sit with
personally, with the children's own competitive, unbothered reaction doing more to convey its
scale than anything the narrator says about it directly.

**What I think it tells the reader:** possibly that the severity registers for the reader
through what the children treat as ordinary (grading a response time) rather than through
anything named as extraordinary, which I'd read as consistent with house rule 1's preference
for showing over naming.

## The chapter whole

**Scenes found: 6** (the box handoff; reading the state file; the teacher's memo and the
photo; the entry; the two quiet days; the chat), plus 8 paragraphs of connective/background
material across five spots.

This chapter doesn't read to me as the vignette problem the brief describes. Its longest scene
(the entry, 17 paragraphs) carries a section break inside it without actually splitting, which
is the same lesson as the vignette warning but running in the other direction: a break mark
here does not make a new scene. I'm not proposing a merge for that scene since there's nothing
to merge — the fix, if any, is simply not to read the typographic break as a boundary.

**The structural finding I'd weight highest in this chapter is the reverse of a merge: two
paragraphs (83 and 85) each compress two separate scenes into one paragraph with no break at
the change of place.** Paragraph 83 runs a generic work day directly into the specific
supervisor exchange and then directly into the specific website check at home, crossing from
office to apartment without a reset. Paragraph 85 runs a generic second work day directly into
the specific call with his grandmother. Concretely: split paragraph 83 after "moves on down
the row" (closing the supervisor beat) and again before "At home the first night" (opening the
website-check beat); split paragraph 85 before "That second night is the night he always calls
his grandmother." What carries across each new seam is already in the prose — the shift from
office pronoun ("she," "the row") to the private one ("he," "his laptop," "his grandmother")
does the work a paragraph break would otherwise have to do alone.

I also want to flag, at the level of the whole chapter rather than any one line, that three
separate sentences in this chapter end on a because-clause that explains a character's
psychological reasoning immediately after showing the action it explains (lines 77, 85, and
89, all listed individually in Feedback). Individually each is a small, defensible instance;
together, three in one chapter this short is close to the rate house rule 1 warns against for
an otherwise-legitimate technique. I'd treat the three as one finding to weigh rather than
three independent ones.

## Feedback

**"The folder comes back up off the desk, because leaving it open there is worse than
finishing it."**
A camera-visible action (the folder coming back up) followed by a clause explaining the
psychological reasoning behind it, the shape house rule 1 names as the commonest way the rule
gets broken, though this isn't one of the six retired examples.
Action: cut at the comma.
Replace with two options: (1) "The folder comes back up off the desk." (2) "The folder comes
back up off the desk. He doesn't set it down again until it's finished."

**"The second day he clears his queue faster than usual, the cables done by mid-morning,
because working through something else keeps the loading-dock report from filling every part
of his head at once."**
Same shape: the fast pace is shown, then explained.
Action: cut the because-clause.
Replace with two options: (1) "The second day he clears his queue faster than usual, the
cables done by mid-morning." (2) "The second day he clears his queue faster than usual, the
cables done by mid-morning, and doesn't look up much."

**"Several versions of the question get typed before he sends any of it, each deleted and
started over, because even the version that says the least is still a version that says
something."**
Same shape a third time in the same chapter; see "The chapter whole" for the rate concern
across all three instances together.
Action: cut the because-clause, or replace it with something shown.
Replace with two options: (1) "Several versions of the question get typed before he sends any
of it, each deleted and started over." (2) "Several versions of the question get typed before
he sends any of it, each one shorter than the last, and deleted anyway."

**Paragraphs 83 and 85 each run a generic work day directly into a specific, differently-
located scene with no paragraph break at the change.**
See "The chapter whole" for the exact split points and what carries across each new seam.
Action: split each paragraph in two, at the location change. Not a deletion — all the content
stays; only the paragraph break changes.

**"The folder goes home with him that first night, more habit than plan..."**
