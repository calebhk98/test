# Rudeness pass, chapter 34 (The Files)

Scope: manners only, in the group thread and the narration around it. Subject
matter of the document itself is out of scope. Checked against
`passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` and `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md`; nothing below repeats an
item ruled deliberate there.

## Instances, sorted

### 1. Theo's disclosure gets no reply from anyone, ever — UNCERTAIN

Line 9: *"He opens the group thread first and types "mine has a name in it I
already know," and sends it before he can talk himself out of it. Nobody
answers before he's back at the laptop, and he keeps reading from the line he
left off on. That's the whole account anyone else gets of it."*

Who does it: all six other friends, collectively, by omission.
Who receives it: Theo.

This is the strongest candidate in the chapter. Theo names something
frightening in his own file and immediately puts himself out there in the
thread. Not one of the other six ever mentions it, asks about it, or brings it
up for the rest of the chapter, including at the very end (line 125, Theo
finishes his walk and goes back inside "the file exactly where he left it,
closed") where the chapter could have closed the loop and does not. Compare
this with how the group treats Ruth's disclosure a few paragraphs later:
immediate, sustained, multi-message engagement (lines 57-89). Theo gets zero.

Why UNCERTAIN rather than a clean FLAG: the line "That's the whole account
anyone else gets of it" reads as the narrator noticing the gap on purpose,
which argues for DELIBERATE (a chapter about a surveillance file on the whole
friend group choosing, as its one piece of irony, to have them fail to
surveil each other). But nothing in the text marks it as a character failing
another character — no one is shown feeling guilty, no one is shown as having
seen it and chosen not to answer, and it is never raised again. That could
equally be an oversight the pass introduced rather than a device.

What would settle it: whether a later chapter (35, or an earlier one
establishing Theo's relationship to the group) circles back to "a name I
already know," and whether the author intended chapter 34 to show the group's
attention getting monopolized by Ruth's story at Theo's expense. If nothing
ever returns to it anywhere in the book, this is very likely UNINTENDED and
should be flagged for a line giving Theo's message a reply, even a late one.

### 2. Ruth pressing Eli for an answer he has already given — DELIBERATE, KEEP

Lines 109-115:
```
eli: i dont know
ruth: eli how good are we
eli: ruth i dont know
eli: i have never once been at the top of a room in my life
```

Who does it: Ruth. Who receives it: Eli.

Ruth re-asks a question Eli already answered honestly, naming him directly the
second time the way Chloe named Ruth earlier when annoyed (compare line 45,
*"chloe: ruth thats the waffle house"*). Eli's reply mirrors that same
irritated-address pattern back at her (*"ruth i dont know"*), which is the
text's own established shorthand for "I already told you." The book is aware
of the pressure here and lets Eli register it in kind rather than letting it
pass silently. This is the author's device working as intended, not an
oversight — noted so it is visible, not treated as a defect.

### 3. Chloe/Ruth naming each other when annoyed — DELIBERATE, KEEP

Lines 41-47:
```
chloe: thats us
ruth: i know
chloe: ruth thats the waffle house
ruth: chloe i KNOW
```

Mutual, mild, mutually matched (both get a turn, both are heard), and marked
by the capitalization on "KNOW." Ordinary friend friction the book is plainly
in control of. Listed for completeness, not as a problem.

### 4. Kavi telling Ruth twice to "read it back" — UNCERTAIN

Lines 69-75:
```
kavi: ruth
kavi: read that back
ruth: i know what it says
kavi: no. read it back
```

Who does it: Kavi. Who receives it: Ruth.

This is the clearest match to the author's own example of the tell ("someone
asks them the same question multiple times"), except it is a command, not a
question, and Ruth is not ignored — she is pushed, and she complies (lines
77-81), staying in the exchange the whole time. Read one way this is a friend
refusing to let another friend minimize what she just wrote, which is
sympathetic rather than rude. Read another way, cutting off "i know what it
says" with "no. read it back" is a flat override of what she just told him
about her own reaction, which a real person could take as not being listened
to. I could not settle which reading the chapter intends from the text alone.

What would settle it: whether Ruth's later lines (95-99, "i think i knew the
sentence") are meant to show Kavi's push worked and was welcome, which would
argue DELIBERATE, or whether nothing in the surrounding chapters ever revisits
whether Ruth resented being cut off.

## What I would fix first

Item 1, Theo's line. Of everything here it is the one a reader is most likely
to notice and hold against the group: he is the only one of the seven who
puts himself out there in the thread and gets total silence, and the chapter
never closes the loop even when it returns to him at the very end. If the
author confirms this is not paid off elsewhere in the book, the cheapest fix
is a single line, late in the thread, where someone finally asks Theo about
it — it does not need to be resolved, only acknowledged, the way Ruth's line
was.

Item 4 is worth a second look only if the author did not intend Kavi's
insistence to read as slightly overriding; otherwise it is fine as written.
