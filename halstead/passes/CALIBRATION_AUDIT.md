# The calibration audit

A read-only check on one thing: **do these children read as very bright
children, or as something else?** Everything here is in the author's words or
directly derived from them.

This is an audit. The book is believed to be largely right already. Report what
you find; fix only unambiguous violations.

## 1. Bright, and their actual age

Every Halstead student is around 160 IQ. That has to show. What it must never
turn into:

- **Spock or a Vulcan.** Emotion suppressed or absent, everything reasoned.
- **Sheldon.** Brilliance worn as a personality, social failure played as a
  quirk, the character aware of being the clever one.
- **An AI.** Perfect recall, no wrong turns, no boredom, no wanting.
- **A miniature adult.** A nine-year-old with a thirty-year-old's priorities,
  patience, and register.

A twelve-year-old who can pass the bar is still twelve about her friends, her
parents, food, fairness, being left out, and being tired. Intelligence changes
what she can do. It does not change what she wants or how much things hurt.

## 2. They think they are normal. This is the load-bearing one.

The children have been around geniuses for their entire formative years. Their
sample is Halstead. So:

> **They think their work is average.** Passing the bar is like passing a
> driving test, and nearly everybody has one. Nearly everybody does ten on one.
> Everybody writes ten pages a week.

There is no looking down on anyone, no comparing themselves favourably, no
private knowledge of being exceptional. A character who thinks "most people
couldn't do this" is out of calibration, and so is a character who is modest
about it, because modesty requires knowing there is something to be modest
about.

**Contrary evidence does not fix this, because they rationalise it.** Years of
a consistent sample do not fall over in one afternoon. The rationalisation is
always that the outside case was defective, easy, or ordinary:

- Beating seven adults in a parking lot is possible because those seven were
  stupid, not because the four of them are unusual.
- MIT is easy because they put her in remedial classes.
- Having to repeat something twice before a colleague follows it is just basic
  social skill, the kind anybody uses.

The realisation arrives late in the book. **For most of it they have no idea
they do anything unusual**, and any scene that gives a character that knowledge
early is a defect.

## 3. Hide the scale from the reader while showing it

The model is the camp arc, in the author's words:

> *The reader sees a few scenes where she is social, the main points of the
> scenes is showing sociability, emotions, her failing, etc. Then at the end we
> have a quick little section where she rambles to her dad about a lot of other
> classes we never see a scene for, and then Dad comments on 2 of them. This
> makes it so readers skimming will think over a month, she only did about 4 or
> 5 things. A careful reader sees she did a lot more, and her list says that
> wasn't even all of it, with her just remembering nerf at the end.*

So the dramatised scenes carry feeling, failure and relationships. The scale
lives in lists, asides, and things mentioned once and dropped. A skimming
reader should undercount. A careful reader should find the rest and see that
even the list is incomplete.

**This is subtle by design, and House Rule 1 governs it absolutely.** The
narrator never points at how much she has done, never totals it, never invites
the reader to be impressed. If a passage explains the scale, that is the defect,
not the cure.

## What to report

For your chapters, per character who appears:

1. **Anything that reads as Spock, Sheldon, an AI, or a mini-adult.** Quote it.
2. **Anywhere a child knows they are exceptional**, compares themselves
   favourably, is modest about their ability, or treats a normal-for-them feat
   as a feat. Quote it.
3. **Any encounter with contrary evidence that goes unrationalised**, where the
   character should have explained it away and did not.
4. **Anywhere the scale is announced rather than buried** — a narrator or
   character totalling the achievement, or a list that flags itself as
   impressive.
5. **Anywhere the age has gone missing**: a child with no childish want, no
   boredom, no unfairness felt at full size, in a scene that had room for it.

Also report the opposite where you see it working well, briefly, so the author
knows the audit could tell the difference.
