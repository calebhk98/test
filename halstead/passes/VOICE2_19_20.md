## 1. chapters/19_sixteen.md, line 181: the negotiation board (the assigned case)

**Current:** "...and then about the negotiation board in the hallway, where
every pairing in adversarial negotiation is posted the week it happens and
stays up all year, nothing changing hands but the score."

**Verdict: CONFIRMED. Correct passive, not a defect. Left exactly as written.**

Running the three-part test from `HOUSE_RULES.md` rule 8:

1. **Topic continuity.** The paragraph is tracking "the negotiation board" as
   a fixture, the same way the bar-exam corkboard is tracked earlier in this
   same chapter (line 25, "When the list is posted on the corkboard days
   later..."). "Every pairing... is posted" and "[it] stays up all year" both
   keep the board's contents in subject position across the sentence rather
   than jumping to whoever administers the exercise.
2. **The agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious.** This is Celce-Murcia's
   function 2 by name: nobody needs telling that a school posts the results
   of its own drills. The missing doer is whichever member of staff runs
   adversarial negotiation and pins the sheet up, a detail with zero
   narrative weight and no reason to occupy the subject slot.
3. **The receiver is what the sentence is about.** The sentence exists to
   tell the reader that Nadia-versus-Priya has been sitting on public display
   since February, which is about the posting, not about the poster.

This is the same shape as two examples the rule itself already blesses: "The
boy she is paired with says, 'Lucky'" (ch. 13) and "in the order he is
required to take them" (ch. 26). Both are institutional, obvious-agent
passives; so is this one. Applying the old flat ban (any person or
institution in the subject of a passive is a fault) would have nothing to
grab here anyway, since the missing agent is not even named as an
institution the way "the Army" is elsewhere in the book; it is a plain
"somebody at the school," which is exactly the case the rule says to leave
alone.

**No change made.** Confirming a correct sentence is not a call-out in the
"wrong, but protected" sense; there is no active/passive pair to offer
because there is nothing wrong to fix.

One adjacent, unrelated observation, flagged since I was already looking
directly at this line: the sentence immediately after the target ("Nadia's
has been sitting there since February with Priya's name beside it, which is
the whole of what the board says and the whole of what anybody remembers")
is what `style_report.py`'s tic scan flags on this chapter, as both
*narrator evaluation* and *trailing explanatory clause*. That is a House Rule
1 finding, not a voice finding, and outside this pass's brief, so I am
naming it rather than touching it or writing it up in the voice format.

## 2. chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md, lines 27 and 37: the coffee-cup pairing

**Line 27:** "The coffee cup goes a quarter turn on the table under her hand,
and then another."

**Line 37:** "Ruth turns the cup again, a quarter turn at a time, always
clockwise."

**Verdict: the pairing is real. It is a deliberate use of information
structure and I am leaving both sentences untouched.**

Context matters for the read. Line 27 sits between "I'd have a whole story
ready for anyone who noticed it" (Sam and Ruth still bantering about
Amberg's car) and line 29, "My brother's in, the letter came Thursday": the
first line of the thing Ruth has actually come to this diner to say and has
not yet said. Line 37 sits after she has said it and explained the letter,
directly following "He already knows that, but he's been practicing looking
unimpressed about it since he could talk," her own line about her brother's
composure.

Neither sentence is a passive. Both are active-voice, so this pair does not
answer to the passive/active test in `PROSE_PRINCIPLES_STRUCTURE.md` directly.
It answers to the adjacent one: Williams's characters-as-subjects test
(`PROSE_PRINCIPLES_STRUCTURE.md` §6), and to the book's own named recurring
fault, "active voice with nobody in it, an inanimate subject taking a verb of
motion" (the third case this brief asks both directions to be checked
against).

- At line 27, the grammatical subject is "the coffee cup," not Ruth. Ruth is
  present only as "her hand," a possessive fragment, at the exact moment she
  is withholding the hardest thing she has to say. The cup is given the verb
  of motion ("goes"); the person actually moving it is grammatically
  demoted to a prepositional aside. This is the shape of the book's known
  fake-agency fault, deployed once, in exactly the sentence where a reader
  would expect a person to disappear from her own gesture, because in this
  scene she has not yet let herself say the thing out loud either.
- At line 37, once she has said it, Ruth is restored as full grammatical
  subject and agent of the identical physical action: "Ruth turns the cup,"
  a transitive verb with Ruth doing it to a stated object, no demotion, no
  hedge.

This tracks the same instinct the house rule already names for the "tone
construction" fix in chapter 7: give the physical fact, let the listener's
(here, the reader's) own noticing carry the meaning, and never have the
narrator name the state. Nothing in either sentence says Ruth is anxious or
that she regains composure by saying the news; the two sentences never
comment on each other, so Rule 1 (never talk to the reader) is not at risk
either. A reader who notices the parallel gets it for free from two plain
physical descriptions, ten lines apart, with the only difference between
them being exactly the one grammatical fact this pass exists to check: who
is named as doing the moving.

I looked for a competing, duller explanation (that line 27 is simply loose
prose the same way "half an hour brings them to the diner sign," six lines
earlier in the same chapter, might be) and rejected it, for three reasons:
the gap between the two sentences is the precise ten lines the brief
describes, which means whoever flagged this pairing for this pass counted
the same thing I did; the two sentences are the same gesture (a quarter-turn
of the same cup) rather than two different incidental motions, which eight
lines of comparable "loose" prose elsewhere in this chapter (the shopping
cart, the fence vault) do not repeat; and the swap from object-subject to
person-subject lands on exactly the line where the plot fact she was
withholding has just been said. Coincidence would have to produce all three
conditions at once.

**No change made, on purpose.** There is no active/passive pair to offer
here either, for the same reason as item 1: this is not a defect I am
withholding a fix for, it is a working sentence pair I am confirming rather
than second-guessing. Forcing line 27 into "Ruth's hand turns the coffee cup
a quarter turn" would flatten the exact effect the brief asked me to check
for, and would make line 37 redundant instead of a payoff.

## Call-outs: judgment calls I did not touch

Two more sentences in chapter 20 fit the same "inanimate subject, active verb
of motion, book's own recurring fault" shape as the coffee cup, without the
same clear narrative justification. Reported per the brief's instruction to
surface what is being left alone and why.

### chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md, line 13

**Current:** "...and they cut through the vacant lot behind a tire place and
out onto the road on the far side of it, so half an hour brings them to the
diner sign, waffles lit up yellow against the dark, the last stretch quiet
except for boots on gravel."

**Why the current voice is wrong:** "half an hour brings them" gives an
interval of time the transitive verb "brings," with an object ("them") and a
destination, when the actual walkers (Sam, Nadia, Chloe, Ruth) are doing the
bringing. This is the book's recurring fake-agency fault as the brief
describes it: an inanimate, non-agentive noun phrase carrying a verb only a
person (or their own legs) can really perform.

**Why it was not auto changed:** genuine judgment call, not a rule block.
"An hour brought them to X" is common enough as a period idiom in narrated
walking-journey prose that it may be earning its place as compression rather
than as an error, and it also sits inside a comma-linked "so" clause that
`HOUSE_RULES.md`'s trailing-explanatory-clause pattern would want looked at
for an unrelated reason (a camera fact plus a "so" consequence). I was not
confident enough either way to call it an obvious fix, so it is reported
rather than changed.

**Active:** "and they cut through the vacant lot behind a tire place and out
onto the road on the far side of it; half an hour of walking puts the diner
sign in front of them, waffles lit up yellow against the dark, the last
stretch quiet except for boots on gravel."

**Active:** "and they cut through the vacant lot behind a tire place and out
onto the road on the far side of it, and half an hour later they are at the
diner sign, waffles lit up yellow against the dark, the last stretch quiet
except for boots on gravel."

**Passive:** "and they cut through the vacant lot behind a tire place and out
onto the road on the far side of it; half an hour is what it takes them to
reach the diner sign, waffles lit up yellow against the dark, the last
stretch quiet except for boots on gravel." (Weak: "is what it takes" is a
cleft standing in for a passive proper, offered because a clean passive does
not really exist for this sentence; walking to a place is not something
done *to* the walkers by an external agent, so there is no natural receiver
to promote. Given to satisfy the pairing requirement, not recommended.)

### chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md, line 97 and line 99: the fight-scene cluster

**Current (line 97):** "...and the gun that was waving is waving at
something much too close."

**Current (line 99, three instances in one sentence-group):** "...and the
swing goes past his ear. The second gun comes half out of a jacket and Sam
has the wrist before the barrel clears the pocket... Chloe's weight goes onto
her hands once, when the fourth man gets round behind Sam's shoulder, and
comes off them again before she's committed to the drop."

**Why the current voice is wrong, on its face:** all four bolded verbs of
motion ("is waving," "goes past," "comes... out," "goes onto... comes off")
belong grammatically to an inanimate or bodiless noun (a gun, a swing, a
weight) rather than to the man doing the waving, swinging, or drawing, or to
Chloe herself. This is the exact "neither, but still wrong" category the
brief names: not a passive problem, an active verb with nobody real behind
it.

**Why it was not auto changed:** judgment call, and I ended up believing
this one is deliberate rather than a slip, for reasons close to the coffee-
cup case. All four instances sit inside eleven lines of one choreographed
fight, nowhere else in either chapter; the technique is scene-local, which
is what `HOUSE_RULES.md`'s "an overused technique is cut back, not to zero"
principle protects, not what it bans. The camera-permission language in Rule
1 also covers it directly: a camera can see a gun's muzzle swing, a fist's
arc miss an ear, a shoulder rise off the ground, without knowing or caring
whose intent moved them, and this is a scene where Chloe is explicitly shown
reading the fight the same object-first way, a few lines earlier and a few
lines later in this same chapter: "Every one of them is standing somewhere
that makes sense" (line 81) and, after it is over, "None of the three had an
arm set for the gun. The one at the front had it up and his elbow was locked
out; he was holding it for show" (line 135). That is a reading that tracks
weapons and trajectories as objects in motion, not as extensions of named
men, and the fight's own verbs are doing the same thing her narration
explicitly does around them. Naming "the mugger" as the subject four times in
one paragraph would also fight the "sentence opens She/He + verb" ceiling
problem the brief specifically warns against manufacturing a workaround for.
I did not find this pattern anywhere else in the book's fight or action
writing to compare it against, so I am reporting it as a considered
leave-alone rather than a confirmed deliberate device the way the coffee cup
is.

**Active:** "and the gun the man was waving comes up level, too close now."
(Restores the man as the one waving, keeps the object doing the final
motion.)

**Active:** "and the fourth man's fist goes past his ear. The man reaching
for the second gun gets it half out of his jacket before Sam has the wrist,
the barrel still in the pocket... Chloe puts her weight onto her hands once,
when the fourth man gets round behind Sam's shoulder, and takes it off them
again before she's committed to the drop." (Restores the men, and Chloe
herself, as the ones doing the moving throughout.)

**Passive:** "and the gun is waved at something much too close by the man
holding it." Rejected as a direction: it is more awkward than either active
option and does not fix the underlying problem, since the object is still
what carries the sentence's real information (how close the muzzle is), and
naming "the man holding it" adds nothing the scene needs. Given to show
passive is not the right axis for this defect, the same conclusion
`PROSE_PRINCIPLES_STRUCTURE.md` reached about the "bracket rule" example: not
every wrong sentence is a voice problem to begin with.

**Passive:** "Chloe's weight is put onto her hands once, when the fourth man
gets round behind Sam's shoulder, and is taken off them again before she's
committed to the drop." Also rejected: it buries Chloe, the point-of-view
character and the one actually deciding not to drop, behind two passive
verbs with no named agent at all, which is a straightforward instance of the
real defect (a specific, present person erased from a sentence about her own
choice) rather than a fix for one.

## What I checked and found clean

For completeness, since the brief wants both directions checked: I grepped
both chapters for every `is/are/was/were/been/being/get/gets/got + past
participle` shape and read each hit in context. Every other passive in both
chapters (chapter 19: "the list is posted," "the marker has to be given that
reasoning," "there is left to talk about," "couldn't be trusted"; chapter 20:
"laces... from being kicked off," "the pallets are stacked," "the fence
drill has been run," "most of which were spent at school," "his elbow was
locked out") is either dialogue (protected by Rule 1's ban on rewriting what
a character says) or passes the three-part test the same way the negotiation
board does: an obvious/irrelevant institutional or already-named agent, with
topic continuity intact. None of them erase a specific person already
standing in the scene from a sentence about what that person did to another
person, which is the actual fault the rule exists to catch. I found no
active-should-be-passive case beyond the fight-scene cluster above, and no
"broken sentence made grammatical" or lost-referent case in either chapter.
