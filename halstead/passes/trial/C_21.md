# Findings — chapters/21_the_applications.md

Cleared paragraph by paragraph against BRIEF_C.md. Cross-checked against
SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md, chronology/BOOK.md, and the character sheets
for Chloe, Ruth, Sam, Kavi, Odile, Fen, and Hearn. All line numbers verified
against `chapters/21_the_applications.md` with `grep -n` immediately before
writing.

1. Line 5: "the only number any of them has ever been handed is a standing in a year of ninety." The cohort is ninety-**one**, not ninety, per `chapters/23_the_first_one.md:8` ("the ninety-one of them stand in a line") and `chapters/34_the_files.md:5` ("There is a file on all ninety-one of them"). Every ranking-count reference in this chapter says "ninety."

2. Line 97: "Ninety applicants from one school, and each one argues its own way." Same ninety-vs-ninety-one discrepancy as #1.

3. Line 247: "'Ninety for ninety. That's not four percent, that's a hundred percent...'" Same discrepancy a third time within the chapter; the actual count that "got in everywhere" would be ninety-one for ninety-one.

4. Line 11: "a board game abandoned since dinner still spread across the table between them" against line 14: "Sam has a card in one hand" and line 26: "He sets the card down." A card he is actively holding and then sets down mid-conversation doesn't square with a game already called "abandoned."

5. Line 35: "and eats one of Ruth's chips anyway." Chips are never established in the scene — the room's objects are a laptop, a board game, and (later) a printed deadline list. Where did the chips come from, and why is it "anyway" (anyway to what)?

6. Line 47: "She writes about the floor of her room at seven, the pencil, refusing to move until the division comes out right on her own terms." This is the fraction-division floor scene, which is `chapters/05_behind.md` (dated *July 2012*, the camp chapter), where Chloe is six, not seven — confirmed by the chapter's own dialogue ("'Six.'" established for that stretch) and by CHLOE.md, which cites this exact scene without giving her age as seven. The essay misdates her own formative memory by a year.

7. Line 50 against line 47: Ruth says "'That's four thousand words over a six-hundred-word limit,'" implying a total near forty-six hundred words, but the narration just said the draft "runs past four thousand words" — imprecise/unsynced arithmetic between the two lines. *(unsure)*

8. Line 50: "one sentence about why the answer mattering more than the grade had was the whole point." The clause "the grade had" does not resolve into readable English; the sentence resists being said in my own words.

9. Line 68: "Odile's own essay runs four clean pages" against line 100: "Odile's essay runs three pages." Same essay, same chapter, two different page counts.

10. Line 71: "the reservoir instead, eleven miles from her parents' house." The reservoir itself is established as nine miles from the house (`chapters/12_nine.md:133`, "nine miles on the road"); eleven miles is a *further* road found past the reservoir at sixteen (`chapters/19_sixteen.md:119`, "a road past it that adds eleven miles"; `chapters/18_fifteen.md:157`, "the eleven-mile mark" on the road past the reservoir). The essay conflates the extension with the distance to the reservoir itself.

11. Line 71: "then submits it anyway." Nothing in the sentence sets up a reason she might not submit it — the "anyway" has no visible other side.

12. Line 74: The prompt "asks why this school specifically," and Chloe's answer is entirely about her mother's old sweatshirt and assumption, not about Chloe's own reasons for wanting to attend. Given how deliberately she engineers her other essays (three cutting passes to land exactly on point), answering a different question than the one asked reads oddly for this character. *(unsure)*

13. Line 77: "The essays take an evening each." This flattens everything just shown for Chloe alone — a four-thousand-word first draft, three separate cutting passes, plus at least two more essays for other schools (the reservoir piece, the mother's-college piece) — into "an evening," which the preceding paragraphs don't support.

14. Line 120: "an eleven-year-old's cryptography elective." Cryptography is established to start at twelve, not eleven: `chapters/16_thirteen.md:77` — "Ruth and Kavi took cryptography and networks last year," when they were twelve, putting them ahead of Chloe at thirteen. Other chapters consistently use "twelve-year-old" for this stage of the curriculum (`chapters/20_the_parking_lot.md:37`; `chapters/28_nadia.md:70`).

15. Line 129: "watches him hand back an argument on the Federalist Papers with a single note in the margin. His note reads, in full: *This hedges. Commit to the claim or drop it.*" Per HEARN.md, a hedged paper is exactly the case that gets extensive markup ("a paper that hedges on page one gets read line by line the rest of the way, which is where the four-line notes come from"); a single note is his treatment for the *opposite* case. A hedged paper getting only one note doesn't match his established economy. *(unsure)*

16. Line 132: "the version she's given clinicians and investigators before." The only clearance investigator in the manuscript, Whitaker, appears in `chapters/30_cleared.md`, years after this application-season chapter (Whitaker's investigation begins around age twenty). At this point in the story Chloe has not yet spoken to any "investigators," clinicians aside.

17. Line 135: "there are two people below him on it who would take him apart in a room with no clock running." This directly undercuts SAM.md's flat claim that he is "the best fighter in his year by a wide margin" (`chapters/23_the_first_one.md:65`). If two people ranked below him on the board would beat him unrestricted, "by a wide margin" doesn't hold. *(unsure — may be intentional nuance about the drill vs. real fighting, but it reads as a direct contradiction of the character sheet's flat claim)*

18. Line 41: "one in Michigan because Fen once said it was nice." FEN.md characterizes the post-childhood Chloe/Fen relationship as distant and largely transactional ("structurally adjacent to Chloe's life for a decade but never folded into her circle the way Priya is. No shared meals, no shared plans"). Building a college choice on an offhand remark from Fen implies more ongoing contact than is otherwise shown. *(unsure)*

19. Line 223 immediately follows line 217 ("The acceptances arrive in March") in the same beat, yet places Odile telling Léo she got in everywhere at "Christmas" — which, read as part of the same admissions cycle just described, would precede the March decisions it's reporting. *(unsure — could be read as the following Christmas, but the narrative flow argues against that)*

20. Line 235: "in case an office somewhere mixed up two files that both happen to say Kessler." This implies a second applicant surnamed Kessler exists somewhere in the pool. Chloe is established as an only child with no sibling anywhere in the book (CHLOE.md); no other "Kessler" is ever introduced, named, or explained anywhere else. *(unsure)*

21. Line 8: "Then the college your parents went to, then one weird one, and then, quietly and first, the place you'll actually attend." Calling the item that's named *last* in the list "first" resists being said plainly — either the ordering or the word "first" is doing something I can't parse cleanly. *(unsure — may be intentional irony about priority vs. sequence)*

22. Line 8: "You apply to the Ivies because you're supposed to try, the way you buy a ticket." This is a sudden shift into second-person address ("you") in a paragraph otherwise narrated in third person about "Chloe" and "most of them" — a voice break with no other instance like it in the chapter.

23. Line 5: "Halstead measures everyone against everyone else and stops there." This reads as the narrator explaining the school's ranking philosophy to the reader rather than dramatizing it — telling us what to conclude about the system before any scene has shown it. *(unsure)*

24. Line 247: "'It's marketing. Same thing the watch companies do.'" This restates, almost beat for beat, the case Sam already made in full at lines 26–27 ("A school wants to look exclusive, it puts a small number on the website. That's the whole trick."). The "watch companies" comparison itself is introduced here with no earlier setup and isn't used again. *(unsure — could be intentional bookending of his unchanged view)*

25. Line 153: "The Duke rep, three days into the same one-word answers, keeps his own guesses to himself." This is stated as part of the "second week" dinner scene, before the actual sequence of his three questions (first afternoon / two days later / "his last morning") is narrated in the paragraphs that follow (lines 159–168). The "three days into" figure doesn't clearly reconcile with a sequence whose last beat is "his last morning," with no stated length for his visit. *(unsure)*

26. Lines 185–191: the Caltech rep reads from "nine in the morning" to "just before four" — close to seven hours — while SYNOPSIS_CHARACTERS_TIMELINE.md describes the same scene as him reading "for six hours." Minor numeric mismatch against the reference document. *(unsure, weak)*

Noted here only to confirm #9 isn't resolvable by appeal to the character sheet; the sheet doesn't pick a single number either. *(unsure, supporting note rather than a new independent finding)*
