## Findings

- **Structural — chapter ends on a dangling section-break marker.** The file's last content line is "Then there's an exam on Thursday, and a language report due the Monday after that, and the letters go back in the drawer and stay there." (line 250), followed immediately by a bare `________________` (line 253) with no further text — confirmed with `tail -c` that this is genuinely the end of the file, not a truncated read. Chapters 20 and 22 (checked for comparison) both end on actual prose, not a divider. This reads as either a missing final section or a leftover formatting artifact that should be removed. *Not unsure — verified against the raw file bytes.*

- **Possible chronology snag (soft/unsure) — Christmas appears to follow "acceptances arrive in March" in the narration.** Line 217: "The acceptances arrive in March." Line 223, immediately after: "Odile gets in everywhere she applied and keeps it to herself until Léo asks her directly at Christmas." Christmas precedes March in the same admissions cycle. On a plain read this looks backward. It likely resolves cleanly, though: Odile's character sheet (`characters/ODILE.md:41`) lists "'How many schools?' / 'Twelve.'" as one of her two signature lines — a bare-number answer to a question about count, not necessarily about results — and since she got into all twelve schools she applied to, "twelve" is true whether Léo is asking how many she applied to or how many she got into, so the ambiguity is probably deliberate. Flagging anyway because the paragraph reads as though the Christmas conversation reveals her *results*, which shouldn't yet exist in December if the school's decisions arrive in March. *Marked unsure — plausibly intentional, but worth the author's eye.*

- **Character-sheet mismatch (unsure) — Odile's own bible entry says chapter 21 has no dialogue for her.** `characters/ODILE.md:9`: "Appears in: ... chapters/21_the_applications.md (two sentences of narration, no scene, no dialogue)." But lines 223–229 give her an actual two-line exchange with Léo ("How many schools?" / "Twelve," Odile says, already back inside her book"), which the same sheet later quotes as one of her two signature lines (`characters/ODILE.md:41`). The "appears in" note and the "signature lines" section of the same document contradict each other about whether this chapter contains dialogue for her. This is a documentation inconsistency rather than a chapter-text problem, but it's a direct contradiction I found while doing the required character-sheet cross-check, so including it.

- **Checked and found NOT to be an error (recording so it isn't re-flagged): Odile's essay page count.** Line 68 says her essay "runs four clean pages"; line 100 (a different reader, Penn) says "Odile's essay runs three pages." This looks like a numeric inconsistency at first pass, but `characters/ODILE.md:74` establishes her ten-page-report output as routinely "three or four clean pages" — the variance is inside her established range, not a contradiction.

- **Checked and found NOT to be an error: "the third author... sixteen."** Line 197's "She's sixteen" initially reads as conflicting with Chloe's age in this chapter (17, per the book's own chronology check), if the third author were Chloe. `chronology/BOOK.md:135-142` records that this was already raised and resolved: the author confirmed the third author is a different, unnamed student, and the chapter itself never identifies which of the three research-listing applicants the Caltech rep means. No finding here.

- **What would a real one do (unsure) — the registrar's phone call.** Line 91: a college caller says a transcript "must be" wrong; "The registrar reads the accreditation number back to her, slowly, twice, and asks whether she'd like the same document mailed again. She declines and hangs up first." A real registrar fielding a call that could jeopardize a student's application would more plausibly follow up in writing, loop in a counselor, or offer to have someone call back — hanging up on an admissions office that's questioning your student's paperwork is a curt move that risks the applicant, not just the school's secrecy. It's consistent with the school's established culture of terse stonewalling (the "Sport."/"Athletics."/"Recreation." escort a few paragraphs later plays the identical beat), so it may be intentional characterization of Halstead's institutional paranoia rather than a slip — flagging as unsure rather than a clear fault.

- **What's missing (unsure) — no visible safeguarding protocol for the youngest classes during the visits.** Lines 111–169 describe eleven adult college representatives, largely unsupervised in the text, sitting in on classes down to age seven ("A rep who asks to see 'the youngest ones' sits in the back of a room where a class of seven-year-olds is dividing fractions"). The chapter does show escorts for some scenes (the woman managing the Duke rep, the escort in the paper-reading room), and states classroom visits require 48 hours' written notice, which implies some coordination — but no chaperone, badge, or background-check process is ever mentioned for the youngest-classroom visits specifically, which is the one place a real school would be most exacting about outside adults. Given the book elsewhere treats Halstead as extremely security-conscious (locked research floors, camera-guarded doors, an escort who won't answer a direct question three separate times), the absence of any visible protocol here reads as a gap rather than an oversight the world would actually allow.
