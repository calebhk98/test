### Two cross-cutting notes from this pass

**The object-handling tic the author named.** It shows up in this chapter
more than once: Chloe holds her dad's sleeve (#1), Ben's papers are "squared
up" (#12, the author's own named example, verbatim), the marked sheet gets
turned face down (#48), the seatbelt gets pulled and snapped against her
collarbone (#119) and then, thirteen lines later, her thumbnail gets pressed
into the same seatbelt (#127). The last two are the same object, the same
scene, and the same coping gesture twice in quick succession. Nothing here is
broken on its own, house rules allow the device at some rate, but two
seatbelt beats in one car ride is worth a second look regardless of whether
the object-handling tic in general is over budget for the book.

**The repeated "Then he/Then Ben..." transition.** Paragraphs #30, #40 (via
"Ben props"), #62, #68, #71, #74, #84 all use a nearly identical bare
transition shape to move between subtests. Individually each is fine and
necessary; as a set it is the most mechanical, least-scened writing in the
chapter, though it is arguably the right economy for a chapter that has
eleven subtests to get through inside a normal word count.

## 2. Does it make sense

**"the pencil trick" (L273) has no antecedent anywhere in this chapter.**
Nothing earlier is ever called a trick, and nothing earlier involving a
pencil is framed as one: the pencil appears as a testing tool during Coding
(gripped hard, taken away, line still being finished), never as a named
strategy. A reader hits "bigger than the pencil trick" and has nothing to
attach it to; if it is meant to gesture at the checking-and-neatness habit
she shows during Coding (#46/#47/#53), the chapter needs to actually call
that habit a trick somewhere before this line asks the reader to remember
it as one.

**"Both times she has the picture finished before he has done setting the
card down" (L59) cannot be literally true.** Block Design requires seeing
the completed model before you can copy it; nobody, however fast, finishes
assembling a copy of a picture before the picture has finished being set
down in front of them. Read as hyperbole ("she's basically done by the time
he lets go of the card") the intent is clear, but as written it asserts a
physical impossibility rather than an exaggeration, and a reader who
pictures the scene literally will stop on it.

**"Stop, he says, and takes the pencil out of her hand" (L99), a smaller
note.** A trained clinician stopping a timed subtest would ordinarily say so
and let the child put the pencil down, rather than physically remove it from
a six-year-old's hand. This is not implausible (an examiner can be brisk and
still be good at the job), but it is worth a second look given the chapter's
own ruling that Ben "must be good at his job."

**"where the sticky note used to be" (L281), flagged softly.** No sticky
note, or anything it could refer back to, appears anywhere earlier in this
chapter. This may be a deliberate callback to something established in an
earlier chapter (this report did not read one to check), in which case it is
fine; read with only this chapter in hand, it is a definite noun phrase with
no antecedent, the same shape of problem as "the pencil trick" above.

**The date question is open and out of scope per the brief** (March 4, 2012
was a Sunday; the chapter's school and appointment context implies a
weekday). Not analyzed further here.

**Everything else checked out.** The subtest order, the item structures
(4-block then 9-block Block Design, Coding A with no numerals, untimed

## 3. Active and passive

Narration in this chapter is, on the whole, already active and already
keeps people as subjects; a search of every passive-shaped construction
(is/are/was/were/been/being + past participle) in the narration turned up
exactly one clean case, and a search for a person-shaped job given to a
non-person turned up one more. Both are reported below in the VOICE2
call-out format. Dialogue was read but not checked against rule 8; the
house convention (see `VOICE2_03_04.md`, `VOICE2_25_26.md`) treats attributed
speech as outside a line-level voice pass.

### chapters/02_march_7th.md, line 47, passive drops the person standing there

**Current:** "Her hands go after it, and the top row is done in a moment."

**Why the voice is wrong:** run the three-part test.
1. *Topic continuity.* The clause right before this one has "her hands" as
   subject. The passive swaps in "the top row" mid-thought, trading the
   person doing the work for the thing being worked on, one comma later.
2. *Agent unknown/irrelevant/obvious?* The agent is obvious, Chloe, but that
   is exactly the case rule 8 warns about: an obvious agent one comma away,
   still in the room, is not the "nobody needs telling the Army posts
   soldiers" case. Chloe is standing right there and this sentence exists
   to credit her.
3. *Receiver as topic?* "The top row" never returns; the next clause moves
   straight to "the cut ones" as a new subject. Nothing about the paragraph
   is actually tracking the row as an ongoing topic.
   All three point away from the passive.

**Why not changed here:** looks obvious rather than a judgment call. This is
the sentence meant to show her nonverbal reasoning succeeding fast, right
before her hands cost her on the harder row; putting her back as the doer
costs nothing and matches the sentence's own job.

**Replacement, active:** "Her hands go after it, and she has the top row down
in a moment."

**Replacement, active:** "Her hands go after it and finish the top row in a
moment." (keeps "her hands" itself as the one continuous subject through the
whole sentence)

**Replacement, passive, agent restored:** "Her hands go after it, and the top
row is done by her in a moment." (grammatical, but the tacked-on "by her" is
not the natural shape of this sentence; given to show that fixing the
passive in place, rather than switching voice, does not actually solve the
problem)

### chapters/02_march_7th.md, line 273, fake agency where a passive belongs

**Current:** "...finish first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the
room leaves you alone, the teacher included."

**Why the voice is wrong:** "the room" is a personified stand-in for the
actual doers (the teacher, the other kids), given a verb only people can
choose to do (leave someone alone). Run the test in the other direction:
1. *Topic continuity.* The whole sentence, and the whole closing paragraph,
   tracks Chloe (or the "you" standing in for her) as the one carrying out a
   strategy and receiving its result. "The room" is introduced only to hold
   this one verb and is dropped immediately after.
2. *Agent unknown/irrelevant/obvious?* Yes, cleanly: which specific person
   leaves her alone does not matter, it is everyone, diffusely. This is
   exactly the "a school pairs students" case rule 8 says is fine to leave
   unnamed.
3. *Receiver as topic?* Chloe is what this sentence, and this whole
   paragraph, is about. She is the receiver of being left alone, and that is
   the actual point.
   All three favor keeping her as subject of a passive over inventing a
   personified location as the actor.

**Why not changed here:** judgment call, not obvious. "The room leaves you
alone" may be doing deliberate work matching the brisk, listy rhythm of the
three items before it ("finish first, sit still, keep your answers short,
and..."), and this sentence is also the chapter's stated thesis, so a change
here is more consequential than an ordinary line fix.

**Replacement, passive:** "...finish first, sit still, keep your answers
short, and you are left alone, the teacher included."

**Replacement, passive:** "...finish first, sit still, keep your answers
short, and she is left alone, the teacher included." (keeps the surrounding
"she" instead of switching to "you" mid-thesis, if that second-person shift
is judged a separate problem worth fixing at the same time)

**Replacement, active, real agent named:** "...finish first, sit still, keep
your answers short, and the teacher, like everyone else, leaves her alone."

**Replacement, third option:** "...finish first, sit still, keep your
answers short: nobody, the teacher included, bothers her again." (keeps the
list's rhythm, replaces the personified room with a generalized but real
human subject)

## 4. The best sentence in the chapter

**Candidate to rule out first:** "But a word like that always comes with a
number stuck to it" (line 261) and "There is a version of this where she
finds out and a version where she doesn't" (line 245) are both doing real
thematic work, but both lean on an abstract, essayistic construction (a
general claim stated as if self-evident) rather than a concrete image. That
is close to the exact complaint on file about this manuscript reading like
it was written for college students: these are the two sentences in the
chapter that sound most like an adult narrator's observation and least like
anything anchored to what a camera in the room could show. Ruled out for
that reason, not picked as the best sentence even though both are strong.

**Also ruled out:** "A good one, and that's the trouble with it, because a
bad number is the kind that gets a school to do something, and hers is going
to read as good" (line 219). This states the chapter's central irony
outright and is genuinely the chapter's thesis, but it is a character
explaining the mechanism in dialogue, not a sentence earning its effect
through what is shown; it is good writing, but it is doing the work of an
essay's topic sentence rather than a novel's best line.

**The best sentence: "She still knows all twenty-eight of their names, but
not one of them has needed to learn hers." (line 277)**

Tested against the two kinds of good: this sentence has no inversion, no
wordplay, no unusual rhythm, and no construction a competent ninth grader
could not produce on their own. "Not one of them has needed to learn hers"
is ordinary quantifier syntax, not a trick. What makes it land is entirely
the fact it states: a specific, quantified, verifiable asymmetry (she knows
all twenty-eight names, zero of twenty-eight know hers) that says everything
the chapter has been building toward, a bright, invisible kid, in one plain
comparison. Strike every clever line in the chapter and this one still works
exactly as well, because nothing about its power depends on how it is built.
This is the right kind of good, and it is the sentence the chapter should
end on being judged by.
