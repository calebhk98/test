# Voice pass: chapters/09_february.md, chapters/10_april.md

Sentence-by-sentence pass for passive-where-should-be-active, active-where-should-be-passive,
and fake agency (active voice with nobody in it), per `passes/HOUSE_RULES.md` and the voice
brief. Dialogue and chat lines were not touched, and the reported findings of
earlier passes in `passes/DO_NOT_FLAG.md` were left alone.

Six sentences changed, all in the active direction: three removed a person from
the subject of a passive (the highest-priority fix), and three removed fake
agency (an inanimate or abstract subject given the action while the real actor
stood nearby in the same sentence or paragraph). No case called for the
reverse, and no case was uncertain enough to write up instead of fix — see the
note on the flagged near-miss below for the one genuinely close call.

Chapter 9 is built on things being done to Chloe, and most of that turned out
to already be handled correctly: body reactions, sound, and institutional
passives (the school takes her, the rooms have always been planets) were left
alone because no specific adult was hidden behind them. The three fixes below
are the cases where a specific adult (Dr. Ammons, Mrs. Okonkwo, her parents)
had been dropped out of the sentence by making Chloe the subject of a passive
instead.

## Changed

### chapters/09_february.md, line 5

**Before:** "There have been a handful of visits to Dr. Ammons since October; at the last of them, a fortnight ago, she was asked whether any of it had got easier and said some of it had."

**After:** "There have been a handful of visits to Dr. Ammons since October; at the last of them, a fortnight ago, Dr. Ammons asked whether any of it had got easier, and she said some of it had."

**Why:** "she was asked" is a person in the subject of a passive, matching the brief's own banned example almost word for word. Dr. Ammons is named two clauses earlier as the one she is visiting, so naming him as the one asking invents nothing.

### chapters/09_february.md, line 119 (the flagged near-miss)

**Before:** "The house comes up again in April, on a Saturday, with Chloe up the road at the Petersons' for the afternoon."

**After:** "Her dad brings up the house again in April, on a Saturday, with Chloe up the road at the Petersons' for the afternoon."

**Why:** This is the near-miss an earlier pass flagged for a human to judge. "The house comes up again" is grammatically active but nobody is doing anything: an inanimate subject (the house, meaning the offer of it) gets the verb, the same shape as the brief's own examples ("the sheet goes up on the wall," "the name of it goes across the table"). I judged this a fix rather than a leave-alone or write-up because the doer is not actually unclear: the very next two sentences show her dad with the letter open in front of him and speaking first ("'They're offering us a house,' her dad says"), so he is who raises it, and putting him in the subject costs nothing and invents nothing. I considered leaving it as an idiom close to "the bell goes" or "winter comes," but those are fixed, closed idioms for a recurring event with no actor to name; "a topic comes up" still has a real person behind it here, on the page, one sentence later, which tipped it toward fixing rather than exempting.

### chapters/09_february.md, line 143

**Before:** "Chloe asks whether the last room down that hall is still called Pluto and is told that the rooms down there have always been planets and that the names have stayed put since the building went up."

**After:** "Chloe asks whether the last room down that hall is still called Pluto, and Mrs. Okonkwo tells her that the rooms down there have always been planets and that the names have stayed put since the building went up."

**Why:** "is told" puts Chloe in the subject of a passive. Mrs. Okonkwo is running the tour and answering every other question in the same scene, so she is the doer already on the page, not an invented one.

### chapters/09_february.md, line 169

**Before:** "Her dad carries her bag in and puts it at the bottom of the stairs, and she goes through to the kitchen and pours herself a glass of water at the sink, standing on the edge of the cupboard door to reach the tap the way she has been told not to."

**After:** "Her dad carries her bag in and puts it at the bottom of the stairs, and she goes through to the kitchen and pours herself a glass of water at the sink, standing on the edge of the cupboard door to reach the tap the way her parents have told her not to."

**Why:** "she has been told" is a person in the subject of a passive. No single parent is identified as the source of this particular house rule, so "her parents," both established as the household authority in this chapter, is the doer that invents the least while still removing Chloe from the passive subject.

### chapters/10_april.md, line 105

**Before:** "Reading happens wherever the day leaves room: the hour before dinner, after lights out with the corridor light coming under the door, and the Saturdays when the others are doing something she got out of."

**After:** "Chloe reads wherever the day leaves room: the hour before dinner, after lights out with the corridor light coming under the door, and the Saturdays when the others are doing something she got out of."

**Why:** "Reading happens" is fake agency, an abstraction given the active verb while Chloe, the subject of the entire paragraph and the one actually doing the reading, goes unnamed. Used "Chloe" rather than "She" for the new subject so the fix does not add to a sentence-opener count already sitting at its target.

### chapters/10_april.md, line 171

**Before:** "Then Fen's rocks come off the sill into a shoebox with newspaper folded between them."

**After:** "Then Fen takes her rocks off the sill into a shoebox with newspaper folded between them."

**Why:** "Fen's rocks come off the sill" is fake agency: the rocks do not pack themselves, and Fen, established across the chapter as the only one who ever touches them ("nobody touches them but me"), is right there in the possessive already. Making her the subject costs nothing.

## Called out, not changed

None. Every place the pass found nobody acting, or a person on the receiving
end of a passive, had a specific, already-on-the-page doer to give it back to,
so nothing was left uncertain enough to write up instead of fix. Cases
considered and left alone as correctly passive or correctly agent-free
narration, none of which are reported here as findings:

- Body reactions and involuntary physical states: "The hand stays where it
  is." (09, line 29), "Her voice comes harder now." (09, line 61), her dad's
  hand lifting off his knee (09, line 99).
- Sound and physical/mechanical process with no meaningful actor to name: "A
  chair scrapes on the kitchen tiles" (09, line 63), "The wood gives a little
  under Ruth going up ahead of her" (10, line 15), "The bag has skidded into
  the wall" (10, line 7).
- Scheduled systems on a clock or timer: "The corridor lights go out" (10,
  line 55), "the radiator does what Fen said it would" (10, line 69), "The
  bell rings a little past the hour" (10, line 73).
- Legitimate passives naming an institution with no face, or where the doer
  is obvious and irrelevant to name: "the rooms all get reassigned in August"
  (10, line 17), "nobody is allowed more than ten" (10, line 169), "There is a
  form, already filled in, which he sent off days ago" (09, line 75).
- Generic-"you" instructional narration describing how the school's teaching
  method works in general, not Chloe specifically as a passive recipient:
  "Here you get given the thing the formula is about" (10, line 91).

## Report

- Sentences changed: 6, all active-for-passive/fake-agency direction (3
  removed a person from the subject of a passive; 3 removed fake agency).
  None went the other way (active made passive).
- Sentences called out without changing: 0.
- Strongest finding: the flagged near-miss at chapters/09_february.md, line
  119, "The house comes up again." It is the clearest match in either chapter
  to the book's own listed fake-agency examples ("the sheet goes up on the
  wall," "the name of it goes across the table"), and the fix costs nothing:
  the doer, her dad, is on the page one sentence later with the letter already
  in his hand.
