## Diagnosis

I pulled every sentence of 16+ words and checked its comma count by hand. The chapter is
already writing long sentences — the 20-35 word band beats the benchmark — but roughly a
dozen of them, all narration, run 17 to 27 words with zero internal commas: one clause
stretched out, not two clauses joined. That is the direct cause of the low CV (a long
sentence with no internal break reads at the same flatness as a short one) and the low comma
rate (one comma every two sentences).

Separately, a number of sentences carry an elided relative clause that the prose already
implies but doesn't spell out — "a lane they have never been in," "the toffees Priya's
mother sends," "a ring her aunt has brought over." Making the pronoun explicit ("that they
have never been in," "that Priya's mother sends," "that her aunt has brought over") adds no
new fact and changes no event; it just states the subordination the sentence was already
carrying implicitly. That is the lever for the relative-clause measure, and it overlaps very
little with the comma fix, which is why both need separate passes through the chapter.

All edits below are narration. No dialogue line is touched, so no character-sheet "when they
talk at length" question arises.

## What I am deliberately not touching

- Sentences already carrying 2-3 commas and a relative or subordinate clause at real length
  (the range-card list at line 37, "Eleven people have stopped moving..."; the toffee-lending
  paragraph at line 111; "Her grandmother turns the hand over..." at line 181). These are the
  chapter's own working models of the mechanism RAISING_THE_LEVEL describes — I'm matching
  the rest of the chapter to them, not editing them further.
- The three short bursts of dialogue-adjacent narration governed by Rule 13 (the "Off the
  point" exchange, the Sandoval NDA scene, the Kowalczyk ten-on-one exchange) — I only touch
  the plain narration sentences around them, never a quoted line.
- "Odile gets the first one..." and "There is a flat crack about eight metres out..." (lines
  13, 15) — both already comma-joined correctly and read as good models; forcing a relative
  clause into either felt like padding for its own sake, so I left them.
- I did not touch the 20-35-word band's overall share on purpose — it's already winning, so
  the fix is internal punctuation and clause structure within the existing long sentences,
  not manufacturing more of them.

### Comma / CV fixes (long, zero-comma sentences rebuilt with an internal joint)

1. L5 — "The whole year is breaking clays at somewhere between ninety and a hundred by the
   end of September." → "The whole year is breaking clays at somewhere between ninety and a
   hundred, a spread that holds through the end of September." (commas, CV, relative clause —
   "that holds")

2. L5 — "In the first week of October, Voss walks them down to a lane they have never been
   in and shows them the turret." → "...down to a lane that they have never been in and shows
   them the turret." (relative clause; makes the existing elided clause explicit)

3. L7 — "A machine at one end of sixty metres of open ground fires a live round down the lane
   on a fixed cycle." → "...fires a live round down the lane, on a cycle that never varies."
   (commas, CV, relative clause)

4. L7 — "They shoot across its path on a count they take off the machine." → "...on a count
   that they take off the machine." (relative clause)

5. L11 — "The range in October is the sound of a great many people missing, four sessions a
   week, ninety of them at it." — left as is; considered adding "who are missing" but it
   slows a fast list that Rule 7 wants kept quick, so dropped from the plan.

6. L21 — "On a Wednesday in the middle of February she shoots a series and scores sixty-one,
   then shoots another and scores fifty-nine." → "On a Wednesday in the middle of February,
   she shoots a series..." (comma from fronting; CV)

7. L31 — "She goes to dinner and clears the plate, but it could have been anything on it." →
   "...but it could have been anything that was on it." (relative clause)

8. L33 — "By the second week of March she has six weeks of cards fanned out across the floor

9. L33 — "She finds a pattern on the Tuesday and spends two days on it, but it turns out to

11. L49 — "Ruth comes down on a Saturday in April with a clipboard and a bag of the toffees

12. L89 — "Kowalczyk is up on her toes putting a box of wraps on a high shelf and stays there

13. L93 — "She comes down off her toes and pushes the door the rest of the way open with her

## What this does not touch

No event, fact, or dialogue line changes. No sentence is deleted or merged with its neighbour
— the brief for this chapter is specifically to add a joint inside a sentence the chapter
already writes long, not to combine short ones (that lever is for choppier chapters). Word
count grows by roughly one clause per edit, all of it real subordination, none of it filler.
