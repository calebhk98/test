## Method

The chapter's problem is mostly single-clause sentences standing next to
the next fact about the same thing, instead of one sentence carrying both
(the finding named in the task). The fix is almost all "name a thing, then
say something about it" -> one sentence with a relative clause, or two
adjacent facts on a timeline -> one sentence with a subordinate clause.
Every edit below stays inside a single existing paragraph (no blank line
removed) and touches narration only — no dialogue line licensed by a
character sheet is altered in wording. Two proposed touches were dropped
after checking against the character sheets and the paragraph-break rule;
see "Considered and cut" at the end.

## Edits

### E1 — line 17, essay-return paragraph
Now: "It comes back on the Monday with a mark, and four lines under the
mark. The four lines say she has spent ten pages on a position she already
held, that the other side does not appear anywhere on the page in a form
its own supporters would recognise, and that until it does he has no way
of telling whether she is right or only comfortable."
Becomes: "...and four lines under the mark, which say she has spent ten
pages on a position she already held, that the other side does not appear
anywhere on the page in a form its own supporters would recognise, and
that until it does he has no way of telling whether she is right or only
comfortable."
Moves: relcl (new "which", genuine fact carried, not a verdict), commas/
sentence, simple% (was two sentences, one plain), CV (removes a mid-length
sentence, lengthens the long one), top100 (drops a repeated "The").

### E2 — line 178, second Hearn return

Becomes: "Hearn gives it back on the Monday with four lines at the bottom,
two of which are about a thing she got wrong on page six and the other two

### E3 — line 89, Kavi gets the shot

### E4 — line 87, paintball rumor spreads
Now: "People start asking on the Friday. Ruth says she has been
practising. Chloe says she does not know."
Becomes: "People start asking on the Friday; Ruth says she has been

Moves: subord (new "while"), shortruns (this is a flagged run of three
sub-8-word sentences — this collapses it to one), simple%, commas/
sentence, CV. This is indirect reported speech, not quoted dialogue, so no
character's actual words change.

### E5 — line 112, the Watch sheet, stairwell finding
Now: "Number six says the north stairwell was uncovered for six minutes,

Moves: relcl (new "who"), and-rate (cuts two of three "and"s in one

### E10 — line 15, first Federalist 10 reading

Becomes: "She reads Federalist 10 that night in the chair by the window,
which takes her an hour and a half."

### E11 — line 61, Sam's February time
Now: "Sam gets fourteen seconds in February, but nobody else is close to

Becomes: "Sam gets fourteen seconds in February, a mark that nobody else
gets close to."

### E12 — line 220, the last essay before the break
Now: "The last one is on whether a promise made under a threat counts.
Hearn's note says the question is a good one and asks where the answer to
it went."
Becomes: "The last one is on whether a promise made under a threat
counts, a question that Hearn's note calls good before asking where the
answer to it went."
Moves: relcl (new "that"), subord (new "before"), simple%, CV, commas/
sentence. Drops the repeated "the question... is... asks" construction
for one continuous sentence; the facts (Hearn calls it good, then asks
where the answer went) are unchanged.

Applied after the first re-measurement showed top100 essentially
unmoved (50.42 -> 50.45): the relative pronouns this pass adds — who,
which, that — are themselves inside the commonest-100-words list, so
piling on more relative clauses trades one common word for another and
nets close to zero on that measure. This matches RAISING_THE_LEVEL's
point that vocabulary-level measures are a drafting property a line edit
should not chase; E10-E12 were chosen for the relcl and CV gain they
still deliver, not in continued pursuit of top100.

Becomes: "Some of it is in clothes and some of it is in cold water, and

## Considered and cut

- Merging "In December Hearn hands one back with no note on it at all."
  into the paragraph after it (which starts "She turns it over twice...").
  These are two separate paragraphs in the source (blank line between
  them), and the one-sentence paragraph reads as a device — isolating "no
  note on it at all" as its own beat before her reaction. Crossing that
  break would also cost a hard line break, which is a hard limit. Dropped;
  replaced by E2, a same-paragraph relative clause elsewhere in the same
  scene.
- Adding "that" inside "a position she disagrees with" to manufacture a
  relative-clause hit. This is the metric responding to a reduced relative
  clause being spelled out rather than any real combination — the kind of
  cheap move the brief warns against. Cut.
- Merging the short topic-opener sentences ("Self defense starts in
  September...", "The 10v1 starts in January.", "Paintball joins the
  afternoon block in October.", "Music runs both ways at once.") into the
  sentence that follows. These are a consistent structural device across
  the whole chapter — a flat topic sentence opening each activity's
  paragraph — and sanding them down would flatten a pattern that is
  working, not fix a defect.
- Touching any run of short sentences that is actually a dialogue
  exchange (Kowalczyk's "A minute's a long time." / "Because you'll be
  tired before they are."; Chloe's "Wait. That's it. We're reacting.";
  the Thanksgiving back-and-forth; the grandmother scene). These belong to
  characters whose sheets do not license longer turns here, or are
  Hearn's one licensed long turn, quoted verbatim in HEARN.md — his
  wording, including "He caps the pen." mid-quote, is left untouched.
- Any word-level swap toward a "fancier" synonym to move lexical
  diversity or top100 directly. Not attempted; the brief and
  RAISING_THE_LEVEL both call this a drafting property a line edit
  shouldn't chase. Whatever movement top100 gets here is a side effect of
  cutting repeated function words in the merges above.

## Net effect on paragraph measures

Every edit merges sentences that were already inside one paragraph; no
paragraph gains or loses a sentence relative to another paragraph's blank
line, and no hard line break is removed. Sentence count drops by roughly
6 across 246, so sentences/paragraph and words/paragraph should move
only slightly, and both start well clear of the benchmark.
