## The three targets

1. **Commas per sentence** (0.34 vs 1.17 bench, 0th pct). The chapter almost
   never puts two parts in one sentence. Fix: join adjacent short, simple
   sentences that share one beat, using subordination, a participial phrase,
   or a comma-join where a conjunction isn't load-bearing (STYLE_RULES 10).
2. **Sentence length CV** (70.80 vs 82.54, 4th pct). Fix: make a handful of
   the already-present sentences genuinely long (25-45 words) by combining,
   while leaving the deliberate short beats — payoff lines, dialogue-tag
   action beats, the chronological list rhythms (Oct/Nov/Jan/March;
   May/August) — alone. Spread, not uniform lengthening.
3. **Words from the commonest 100 %** (50.70 vs 49.35, lower is better). Not
   chased directly (RAISING_THE_LEVEL warns this is a drafting property).
   Expected to move as a side effect: each merge below removes a repeated
   subject pronoun, article, or "is/was" and replaces it with denser content,
   which is the same mechanism that lowers this measure elsewhere in the book.

Also in view, not a target but tracked: subordination rate (13.56 vs 22.24)
and the 78% of sentences with no subordinate/relative clause — both move
directly from the same edits.

## Constraints applied while planning

- **Dialogue is largely off-limits.** Sam (no subordinate clause, ever),
  Pruitt (two lines total in the whole book, none of them this chapter's to
  extend), Kavi (clipped by default, lengthens only for a mechanism, already
  at that length here), and Amberg (enumerated parallel clauses is his
  signature shape, not a defect to fix) are all voice-locked by their
  sheets. Chloe's father-scene dialogue is already the licensed "talks at
  length" circumstance for both of them — it doesn't need lengthening, and
  her own sentence shape (concrete, declarative, not floridly subordinated)
  stays as written. **All edits below are narration only.**
- **Devices are not habits.** Left alone: one-sentence paragraphs that set up
  or close a dialogue block (Rule 18/14 pattern, used consistently
  throughout this chapter), the short payoff lines ("She passes by
  twenty-two," "She takes the tongs"), and the two chronological fast-lists
  (the driving progression Oct-Nov-Jan-March; the practice-paper progression
  May-August) which are Rule 7 devices, not runs to break.
- **No new "and" chains.** Where the source used "and" to link two clauses,
  the fix subordinates or comma-joins instead, per STYLE_RULES 6 and 10, to
  avoid pushing the and-rate (already at 3.34%, near the tighter 3.5%
  ceiling) any higher. Net effect across the plan is and-count down, not up.
- **Front-loading stays under cap.** Only two edits (2 and 4 below) open a
  sentence on a subordinate clause; the rest that add a "when" clause put it
  after a fronted prepositional phrase ("In October, when...") so the
  sentence doesn't open on the subordinator itself. Two front-loaded
  sentences in ~164 is well inside the 3.3% ceiling.
- **No new events, no changed facts.** Every edit is a punctuation/
  conjunction/subordination change to sentences already in the text.

## The edits

1. **Opening, combine the two scene-setting sentences.**
   Now: "They take the bar in April, over two days, in the long room on the second floor. Six hours on Tuesday and six on Wednesday, at desks set a meter apart, with the windows open because the room holds heat."
   Becomes: "They take the bar in April, over two days, in the long room on the second floor, six hours on Tuesday and six on Wednesday, at desks set a meter apart, with the windows open because the room holds heat."
   Moves: commas/sentence, CV (creates one ~40-word sentence at the very top of the chapter).

2. **Same paragraph, fold the exam-marking fact into a relative clause.**
   Now: "Eleven teachers sit along the front and watch for twelve hours. They wrote the paper and they will mark it."
   Becomes: "Eleven teachers, who wrote the paper and will mark it, sit along the front and watch for twelve hours."
   Moves: commas/sentence, sentences-with-a-relative-clause, sentences-with-no-subordinate-clause.

3. **Sam/Chloe corridor scene: "and" to "as."**
   Now: "He is walking backward in front of her, and she steers him round the corner by the elbow."
   Becomes: "As he walks backward in front of her, she steers him round the corner by the elbow."
   Moves: sentences-with-a-subordinate-clause. (Front-loaded; counts toward the 3.3% cap.)

4. **The corkboard list.**
   Now: "The list goes up on the corkboard in the second floor hall eleven days later. The names run down the left in a bare institutional typeface."
   Becomes: "When the list goes up on the corkboard in the second floor hall eleven days later, the names run down the left in a bare institutional typeface."
   Moves: commas/sentence, subordination rate. (Front-loaded; counts toward the 3.3% cap.)

5. **Pass/fail aggregate, comma-join instead of a new sentence break.**
   Now: "Fifty-one in the year pass. Thirty-nine do not, and by dinner those thirty-nine have found each other by sight, because that board hangs in a hall everybody walks past four times a day."
   Becomes: "Fifty-one in the year pass, thirty-nine do not, and by dinner those thirty-nine have found each other by sight, because that board hangs in a hall everybody walks past four times a day."
   Moves: commas/sentence, CV (one long sentence out of two short/medium ones).

6. **Sam vs. Chloe's fight-class runs: contrast plus comma-join.**
   Now: "Sam is up in half a second and talks for four minutes without stopping for air. Chloe goes up the following week and stops twice inside the first thirty seconds. Both times she can hear the scene three lines out. She wants to be at the good line already instead of walking through the ordinary one to reach it."
   Becomes: "Sam is up in half a second and talks for four minutes without stopping for air, but Chloe goes up the following week and stops twice inside the first thirty seconds. Both times she can hear the scene three lines out, she wants to be at the good line already instead of walking through the ordinary one to reach it."
   Moves: commas/sentence, CV, sentences-with-no-subordinate-clause (down); also nudges the "but" rate, which the chapter currently fails at 0%.

7. **First practice paper: fold the timing/self-restatement fact into a participial phrase.**
   Now: "She writes her first practice paper in the second week of May, at a table in the library with the mark scheme face down beside her. It takes her an hour and nine minutes, restating what the shed already made obvious."
   Becomes: "She writes her first practice paper in the second week of May, at a table in the library with the mark scheme face down beside her, taking an hour and nine minutes and restating what the shed already made obvious."
   Moves: commas/sentence, CV (one ~40-word sentence).

8. **Who marks the thirty-one papers, absorb the "and" chain.**
   Now: "She writes thirty-one of those between May and September. Kavi marks some and Nadia marks some, and the rest she marks herself with her own answer folded under the page."
   Becomes: "She writes thirty-one of those between May and September, Kavi marking some and Nadia marking some, while she marks the rest herself with her own answer folded under the page."
   Moves: commas/sentence, subordination rate; and-count down by one.

9. **October driving lesson, "and" chain to a "when" clause.**
   Now: "In October they cut the engine at sixty and she brings the car in on the gearbox alone and overshoots her mark by forty meters."
   Becomes: "In October, when they cut the engine at sixty, she brings the car in on the gearbox alone and overshoots her mark by forty meters."
   Moves: commas/sentence, subordination rate; and-count down by one. (Not front-loaded — opens on "In October.")

10. **November brake failure and its fix, joined as cause and answer.**
    Now: "In November the brake pedal sinks under her foot and the car keeps its speed. The answer is the gearbox, the handbrake, and a great deal more road than she thought she would need."
    Becomes: "In November, when the brake pedal sinks under her foot and the car keeps its speed, the answer is the gearbox, the handbrake, and a great deal more road than she thought she would need."
    Moves: commas/sentence, subordination rate, CV (one ~30-word sentence). (Not front-loaded — opens on "In November.")

11. **Delacroix's running commentary, participial merge.**
    Now: "Delacroix runs the handling weeks. He talks the entire time she drives, in one flat voice, about his brother-in-law's boat and the state of the tires and whatever else occurs to him."
    Becomes: "Delacroix runs the handling weeks, talking the entire time she drives, in one flat voice, about his brother-in-law's boat and the state of the tires and whatever else occurs to him."
    Moves: commas/sentence (same comma count, one fewer sentence).

12. **Second bar sitting, "Question six": comma-join into the enumerated list.**
    Now: "She has the answer in her second line. Then she writes the rule, where the rule comes from, why a forklift is inside it, and what happens if somebody takes it off the site on a Sunday."
    Becomes: "She has the answer in her second line, then writes the rule, where the rule comes from, why a forklift is inside it, and what happens if somebody takes it off the site on a Sunday."
    Moves: commas/sentence.

13. **November-February library scene, "and" chain to participial.**
    Now: "In November she is in the library with something else open in front of her and she is thinking about April instead."
    Becomes: "In November she sits in the library with something else open in front of her, thinking about April instead."
    Moves: commas/sentence, subordination rate; and-count down by one.

14. **The forge routine, comma-join.**
    Now: "The work down there stays between her and Pruitt. In four years the whole of his commentary has been telling her which end to hold."
    Becomes: "The work down there stays between her and Pruitt, in four years the whole of his commentary has been telling her which end to hold."
    Moves: commas/sentence.

15. **The hook and bag, participial merge.**
    Now: "There is a hook on the back of her door with a bag on it. She made it in her second year."
    Becomes: "There is a hook on the back of her door with a bag on it, made in her second year."
    Moves: commas/sentence.

16. **Summer opening, "and" chain to participial, comma-join for the lead sentence.**
    Now: "After that the nights settle at nine hours. She rides out to the reservoir most days, and in July she finds a road past it that adds eleven miles and a hill she has to stand up on."
    Becomes: "After that the nights settle at nine hours, and she rides out to the reservoir most days, in July finding a road past it that adds eleven miles and a hill she has to stand up on."
    Moves: commas/sentence, CV.

17. **Negotiation board, absolute construction.**
    Now: "Nadia's eighty-one percent has been on it since February. Priya's name is next to it."
    Becomes: "Nadia's eighty-one percent has been on it since February, Priya's name next to it."
    Moves: commas/sentence.

## Round two, after the first measurement

The first round moved the chapter from the 9th to the 13th percentile and
took CV from 70.80 to 80.16 (nearly at the 82.54 benchmark), but left
sentences-with-a-relative-clause the single largest remaining gap (13.58 vs
22.24, 1.8 sd). Three more narration-only merges, chosen to add a relative
pronoun specifically:

18. **Hook and bag, swap the participial for a relative clause.**
    Now: "There is a hook on the back of her door with a bag on it, made in her second year."
    Becomes: "There is a hook on the back of her door with a bag on it, which she made in her second year."
    Moves: sentences-with-a-relative-clause, commas/sentence (comma count unchanged, but "which"-count rises from 3 to 4, still under the 0.4% ceiling).

19. **Amberg's office, "and" chain to relative.**
    Now: "She takes her paper to Amberg two days later. He has the mark scheme out on the desk before she gets the door shut."
    Becomes: "She takes her paper to Amberg two days later, who has the mark scheme out on the desk before she gets the door shut."
    Moves: commas/sentence, sentences-with-a-relative-clause, sentences-with-no-subordinate-clause.

20. **Closing beat, fold "That one stops her" into an appositive relative clause.**
    Now: "Her father turns his glass a quarter round on the step, and then asks her what the two of them are like at a table together now. That one stops her with the glass halfway up."
    Becomes: "Her father turns his glass a quarter round on the step, and then asks her what the two of them are like at a table together now, a question that stops her with the glass halfway up."
    Moves: commas/sentence, sentences-with-a-relative-clause, CV (one long sentence absorbing the short beat). The physical image (glass halfway up) stays, just embedded rather than standalone — still ends the chapter on the image, not a narrator gloss on it.

## Round three: words per paragraph held flat, fix with two real joins

After round two, words per paragraph was essentially unchanged (23.93 to
23.85) because it depends only on total words and paragraph count, neither
of which sentence-combining touches. Two genuine paragraph joins, both
Rule-14 cases (an action and the beat it leads into, no dialogue between,
no time or scene shift at the break):

21. Join "She writes her first practice paper..." with "She gives it to
    Kavi." — same continuous action, writes then hands off, immediately
    followed by Kavi's dialogue starting in the next paragraph. The original
    two-paragraph break wasn't doing the "before dialogue" work themselves;
    it was between two narration sentences, with the actual dialogue setup
    one paragraph later.
22. Join "She takes the tongs." with "There is a hook on the back of her
    door with a bag on it, which she made in her second year." — both pure
    narration, same idea (what stays of four years at the forge), directly
    before the section break. No dialogue or scene shift between them.

Left everything else alone: every other one-sentence paragraph in the
chapter sits immediately before or after a dialogue block, which is the
consistent, chapter-wide device this book uses (see "Cut from
consideration" below) and not habit.

## Cut from consideration

- Merging any of Sam's, Pruitt's, or Kavi's dialogue lines — voice-locked.
- Breaking the Oct/Nov/Jan/March driving list or the May/August paper-count
  list into fewer, longer sentences — these are Rule 7 fast-list devices,
  not habit-driven short runs.
- Joining the one-sentence paragraphs that introduce or close a dialogue
  block ("She gives it to Kavi," "She takes the tongs," "Sam catches her at
  the bottom of the stairs...") — each break is doing the same job
  consistently across the chapter, which makes it a pattern, not habit.
- Lengthening Chloe's or Dave's dialogue in the closing scene, even though
  it is the licensed "at length" circumstance for both — the content is
  already at the length their sheets license; padding it further would be
  padding, not a real combination.
- Chasing lexical diversity or word length directly — out of scope per
  RAISING_THE_LEVEL, a drafting property a line edit won't move.
