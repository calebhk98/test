## Three targets and the levers used

1. **Sentences per paragraph (1.80 -> target well above 1.96).** Lever 2 is
   the main work: join one-sentence and two-sentence paragraphs that are the
   same beat as the paragraph next to them, per Rule 14. 14 join-groups,
   reducing 60 paragraphs to 44 without dropping a single sentence's worth
   of content.
2. **Words per paragraph (17.12 -> target well above the current floor).**
   The same 14 joins move this automatically: fixed word count over fewer
   paragraphs.
3. **Reading grade (3.06 -> raise words/sentence and clause density without
   padding).** Lever 1: combine 11 short-sentence pairs, almost all
   narration, several straddling a run flagged by the run-of-3+ scan
   (`"I'll get a little." Nadia shrugs and goes over the fence. Chloe and
   Ruth go after her. They lie on the loading dock roof. Sam walks at the
   seven of them.` and `Sam doesn't answer. He looks at the sidewalk. "Why
   are they so bad at it."`). Two use a fronted subordinator ("When...",
   "While..."), which brings front-loading from 1 sentence to 3 (about 3.0%
   of a smaller sentence total) — under the 3.3% cap, and no more of that
   construction is used elsewhere in this plan to keep clear of it.

## Constraint: this chapter is dialogue-heavy and the fight scene earns its
short sentences

38 of 108 sentences are spoken; per the character sheets none of Sam, Ruth,
Nadia, or Chloe is in a "talk at length" circumstance here — this is banter,
commentary, and flat statement, exactly their default register, so no
dialogue line is lengthened or two dialogue turns merged into one paragraph.
The short clipped sentences in the fight itself ("He doesn't." / "Five get
there." / short medical-check fragments) are also left short deliberately:
Rule 13 wants short, hard sentences for action and this chapter's fight
sequence is the place that rule is describing, not a defect to sand down.
Reading grade will move less than the other two targets for this reason;
closing the full gap to the benchmark would mean lengthening dialogue no
character sheet licenses, or slowing the fight scene down, both banned by
the brief.

## One-sentence paragraphs kept standalone, and why

- "Nobody has ever snuck out." — chapter-opening device: a categorical claim
  the rest of section 1 immediately earns/undercuts. Not moved.
- "They're over the fence at twenty past eleven." — closes section 1 with a
  precise time detail after the eleven-day buildup; the button-line device.
- "There's a shopping cart in the lot behind a hardware store on the way
  home. Ruth films Sam taking it into a curb at speed. Nadia goes after him
  and is better at it." — already three sentences (kept as one paragraph);
  new beat (walking home) and section-closer, not a habit break.
- `"Phones. Watches. Come on."` — the threat delivered in clipped fragments;
  an isolated dialogue paragraph is the device.
- "Chloe checks the fence, the gap under it, the alley, the loading dock she
  could be on top of in four seconds." — the one line of Chloe's private
  tactical read, bracketed by the men's spoken threat before it and Sam's
  dialogue after; a real scene-focus shift on both sides, not habit.
- `"He's going to lose the gun," Ruth says.` followed immediately by "He
  doesn't." — kept as two separate paragraphs on purpose: the break is the
  joke, Ruth's prediction and its instant, flat contradiction land harder
  split than merged (Rule 10 territory — comedy placed right against the
  claim it undercuts).
- "Of the six who come at once, five get there. The sixth stands behind
  them with nowhere to be." — marks the escalation from one man disarmed to
  the group rushing in; a genuine content shift from the paragraph before
  it, not a habit break.
- `"There it is," Ruth says.` and the spectator lines `"He's slow on the
  transitions," Nadia says, chin on her hands.` / `"He's slow on
  everything, look at his feet."` / `"Why is he doing that with his left."`
  — running commentary from the roof, each its own speaker turn; standard
  dialogue formatting, not a joinable habit.
- `"Fifty-one seconds," Ruth says. "One of them never even got there."` —
  closes the fight with the count; a device, and it sits right before a
  section break.
- The remaining single-line dialogue turns in the two argument scenes
  (`"Why?"`, `"I'll get a little."` — no, this one is joined, see below —
  `"I got nothing out of that."`, `"You got fifty-one seconds against
  seven."`, `"Three of them had guns, Ruth..."`, `"So if that's not it,
  what do I fight."`) are kept as their own paragraphs: standard one turn
  per speaker, and none is licensed to lengthen.
