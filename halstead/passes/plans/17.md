maturity percentile: 28th (median of 16). Three worst:

- sentence length variation (CV %): 74.18 vs benchmark 82.54, 9th percentile
- sentences with no subordinate/relative clause %: 72.52 vs benchmark 62.75, 13th percentile
  (same fact seen from the other side: sentences with a subordinate clause % 16.22 vs 22.24,
  13th percentile; sentences with a relative clause % 16.67 vs 22.81, 26th percentile)
- commas per sentence: 0.61 vs benchmark 1.17, 13th percentile

Also live: 50.0% of sentences are under 10 words (bench 42.67, losing) and 19.37% sit inside
a run of 3+ short (<8-word) sentences (bench 15.08, losing). These are the same underlying
problem as the three targets — most sentences are one flat clause, so there's nothing for a
comma to mark and nothing to make one sentence longer than its neighbour.

Protected (already winning, must not cost):

- sentences of 20-35 words %: 19.37 vs benchmark 18.00, 74th percentile
- sentences per paragraph: 2.29 vs benchmark 1.96, winning
- words per paragraph: 30.54 vs benchmark 28.64, winning

## Diagnosis

I ran the run-detector from prose_grade.py sentence-by-sentence and found nine actual runs
of 3+ consecutive sentences under 8 words. Most of them are dialogue exchanges between Sam,
Kavi, Odile, and Ruth, and their character sheets license exactly this: Sam 31% of lines
three words or fewer, Odile's whole voice is "short and observational by default," Kavi
"clipped by default, three words or fewer." Padding their lines would violate the sheets and
the brief both. Two runs (the "It's just school" / "clays" argument at lines 173-183, and the
"She said something" / "I thought you knew" argument at lines 185-191) are the chapter's one
scene of open parental conflict, and PROSE_RULES Rule 13 asks explicitly for short, fragmented
sentences at genuine distress — this is the deliberate case the rule describes, not a defect.

What every one of the nine runs *does* have, without exception, is at least one plain
narration sentence sitting inside the dialogue — a dialogue tag doing nothing but naming who's
talking, or an action beat separated from the line it belongs to by a full stop where
STYLE_RULES section 7 asks for a comma ("joining speech and body... a period reads as two
separate events; a comma reads as one continuous moment"). That narration sentence is the
lever: fold it into the surrounding line with a comma or a subordinator, leave every quoted
word exactly as written, and the run breaks without touching a single character's voice.

Two further narration-only sentences (the clamp/Dov paragraph, the Priya/Sam breakup summary)
get the same treatment even though they weren't inside a flagged run, because they are flat
"and"-chains or back-to-back short facts that read exactly like the runs, just under the
metric's radius, and are pure narration with no dialogue-register constraint on them at all.

## What I am deliberately not touching

- The Sam/Kavi/Odile banter run at lines 45-47 ("They might leave it at clays." / "Kavi
  shakes his head. 'Nah. It escalates every year.'") — no narration to fold, and Kavi's
  one-word "Nah" is his signature register, not padding material.
- The core of the two argument runs at lines 173-183 and 185-191 — "It's just school," "It is
  not just school," "It's like clays but harder," "What in God's name is clays?," "Meg. Leave
  it," "And you didn't think to tell me?" all stay exactly as written. "It's like clays but
  harder" is Chloe's cited signature deflection (CHLOE.md), and the surrounding lines are Meg's
  and Dave's established flat, unhedged registers (MEG.md, DAVE.md) at the one moment the
  chapter licenses distress to show at the sentence level. I only fold the two pure-narration
  beats that sit inside those runs ("Her father is looking at his plate," "He keeps his eyes
  down") into the dialogue next to them, which thins both runs without shortening the argument.

## Planned changes

1. **Line 13** (Sam/Kavi rifle banter opener).
   Now: `Sam keeps the rifle up. "How long till this bit's boring?"`
   Becomes: `Keeping the rifle up, Sam asks, "How long till this bit's boring?"`
   Moves: shortruns (removes the narration sentence that starts this run), commas per
   sentence, CV (folds a 5-word sentence into a 12-word one). Sam's question is unchanged.

2. **Line 29** (Aymar/Voss stopwatch dispute).
   Now: `"The card says nine, Aymar." Voss keeps his eyes on the range.`
   Becomes: `"The card says nine, Aymar," Voss says, keeping his eyes on the range.`
   Moves: shortruns, commas per sentence, CV. Voss has no character sheet; his line is
   unchanged, only the bare action-tag is folded in.

3. **Line 59** (Kowalczyk's ruling).
   Now: `"That's the last round." Kowalczyk keeps her eyes on the board. "Pairs for the rest
   of the hour."`
   Becomes: `"That's the last round," Kowalczyk says, keeping her eyes on the board. "Pairs
   for the rest of the hour."`
   Moves: shortruns, commas per sentence, CV. KOWALCZYK.md names this exact habit — "keeps
   doing an unrelated physical task while giving a serious answer real weight" — so this
   folds the beat into her voice instead of inventing anything.

4. **Line 75** (Halide's test question).
   Now: `"No." Chloe is wiping the board down with her back to them, and she stops halfway
   across it and turns round.`
   Becomes: `"No," Chloe says, wiping the board down with her back to them, and she stops
   halfway across it and turns round.`
   Moves: shortruns, commas per sentence, CV. Chloe's one-word answer is untouched — still the
   shortest, flattest response available, per CHLOE.md.

5. **Line 39** (the clamp that closes wrong).
   Now: `She writes a two-page specification for a clamp in November, and a boy called Dov
   builds it off the paper. It closes on the wrong axis.`
   Becomes: `She writes a two-page specification for a clamp in November, and when a boy
   called Dov builds it off the paper, it closes on the wrong axis.`
   Moves: subordinate clause % (+1, "when"), commas per sentence, CV, simple-sentence %.
   Not inside a flagged run, but a flat two-sentence report that the "when" clause turns into
   one causal chain with no new content.

6. **Line 44** (text renderer, opening line of the fast list).
   Now: `She writes a text renderer. It reads the font file when it loads and works the
   letter shapes out of the curves stored in it.`
   Becomes: `She writes a text renderer that reads the font file when it loads and works the
   letter shapes out of the curves stored in it.`
   Moves: relative clause % (+1, "that"), CV, sentences under 10 words %. The list stays fast
   (PROSE_RULES Rule 7) — this only removes the topic sentence's own redundant full stop, the
   itemized "It adjusts... It takes... Then it lays... It runs to..." rhythm after it is
   untouched.

7. **Line 58** (Sam's growth, Priya teasing).
   Now: `"You've been eating like a man with a farm." She goes back to her tray. "You'll be
   a nightmare on the mats."`
   Becomes: `"You've been eating like a man with a farm," she says, going back to her tray.
   "You'll be a nightmare on the mats."`
   Moves: commas per sentence, CV, sentences under 10 words %. This is the exact
   narration-straddling-dialogue pattern RAISING_THE_LEVEL names directly. Priya's line is
   unchanged.

8. **Line 143** (Priya/Sam breakup, who said what).
   Now: `The two of them last until March. Priya says the girl ended it. Sam says they
   agreed. Odile was actually in the room, and what Odile says is that both of them have got
   the day wrong.`
   Becomes: `The two of them last until March, when Priya says the girl ended it and Sam
   says they agreed. Odile was actually in the room, and what Odile says is that both of them
   have got the day wrong.`
   Moves: subordinate clause % (+1, "when"), shortruns, commas per sentence, CV. Pure
   narration reporting three separate accounts; no dialogue, no character constraint.

9. **Lines 153-155** (rifle/beans, mother's hands stop).
   Now: `"On the rifle. The bit the shell comes out of." Chloe reaches past her for the
   beans.

   Her mother's hands stop on the dish. "You're shooting rifles?"`
   Becomes: `"On the rifle. The bit the shell comes out of."

   Her mother's hands stop on the dish while Chloe reaches past her for the beans. "You're
   shooting rifles?"`
   Moves: subordinate clause % (+1, "while"), shortruns (this was the longest run in the
   chapter, six sentences), commas per sentence, CV. Both quoted lines are untouched; the two
   narration beats become one sentence that keeps both images (Chloe's hand, her mother's
   stillness) in the same breath instead of two flat facts in a row.

10. **Line 181** (father's plate, "You knew?").
    Now: `Her father is looking at his plate. "You knew?" her mother says.`
    Becomes: `Her father is looking at his plate when her mother says, "You knew?"`
    Moves: subordinate clause % (+1, "when"), shortruns (thins this run from 8 to 4), commas
    per sentence, CV. Meg's line is verbatim.

11. **Line 191** ("I thought you knew," eyes down).
    Now: `"I thought you knew." He keeps his eyes down. "I thought it was fine."`
    Becomes: `"I thought you knew," he says, keeping his eyes down. "I thought it was fine."`
    Moves: shortruns (thins this run from 7 to 4), commas per sentence, CV. Dave's two lines

## Correction made mid-pass

After editing changes 1-11, I re-measured before writing anything further, per the brief's
own order of work. Median percentile had risen (28 -> 41) but sentence-length CV had gone
*down* (74.18 -> 69.65, 9th -> 4th percentile). Diagnosis: most of edits 1-4, 7, 10, 11 pair a
short sentence with an adjacent short one and land in the 10-14 word band — that pulls the
mean up without adding variance, since a value near the new mean contributes little to spread.
RAISING_THE_LEVEL is explicit that CV "improves by making the long ones longer and leaving the
short ones alone," not by converting short into medium.

Fix: five more edits (12-16 below), all pure narration, all joining an already-substantial
sentence to its neighbour with a real subordinator so the result lands in the 30-48 word range
instead of the 10-20 word range. This is the same lever as 1-11, aimed at the other end of the
distribution. After these, CV recovered past its starting point (74.18 -> 76.0) alongside the
other two targets. Five further small edits (17-24, folded into the numbered list below) round
out the subordinate/relative-clause and comma gains once CV was back on track.
