pass only. No chapter file was edited.

Category 3 essentially does not recur in this range. The chapter-1 failure was a
lonely six-year-old studying a peer group from outside; by chapter 11 Chloe has a
fixed cohort (Sam, Ruth, Kavi, later Nadia, Odile, Priya) and is never shown

carried past chapter 10, at least in these 25 chapters.

Category 5 also mostly does not recur in the chapter-1 sense (no body at all, for
pages). These chapters are physically dense — burns, calluses, a torn book
cover, a thumb that swells for a week, legs that give out. The failure that does
recur is narrower and more dangerous: at the exact moments of highest stakes,
the character's own body goes analytical instead of felt — hers is present only
as an instrument doing triage on somebody else's ribs, never as a thing with a
pulse of its own. That is really category 1 wearing category 5's clothes, which
is why it's counted once here and folded into category 4's total elsewhere.

## Does it get better or worse with age?

Neither, cleanly. The distribution is bimodal, not a slope. Damage clusters in
two acute spikes — **chapter 15 (age twelve)** and **chapter 20 (age sixteen)**
— that sit inside an otherwise sparse range, and it drops away almost entirely
from chapter 21 (age seventeen/eighteen) onward. Reading the counts as a trend
line would say "improves after eighteen," which is true but misleading about
the mechanism.

What actually predicts an instance is not age, it's stakes: every one of the
category 4 findings sits inside a violence or threat scene (the fence intrusion
in chapter 15, the parking-lot robbery in chapter 20). Ordinary chapters where
nothing dangerous happens — 12, 13, 16, 22, 23 — are clean, regardless of the
character's age in them. The book has, in effect, solved the everyday version of
the chapter-1 problem for this age range (the constant friend-group dialogue,
the forge, the archery, the mother on the phone are almost all handled with
real feeling shown through behavior) and then loses that ground specifically
whenever a scene calls for physical danger, which is exactly backward: a scared
child is the easiest, most legible feeling there is, and it's the one place this
manuscript keeps refusing to render it.

The worse finding is that chapter 15 is not an aftershock of chapter 20's
problem — it's the same failure, running four years earlier, on a
**twelve**-year-old rather than a sixteen-year-old. If chapter 20 is "not
defensible even in a sixteen-year-old," chapter 15 is that same register in a
child four years younger, which the diagnosis's own logic makes strictly worse.
So the honest answer is: the failure does not track age within childhood at all.
It tracks whichever scene puts a body in danger, and it happens to disappear
once the cast actually becomes adults with adult jobs — at which point the same
flatness reads as earned professional affect rather than as a diagnosis.

Two additional structural notes worth flagging to whoever owns chapters 1–10:
chapters 21 onward are told partly through a group chat, which gives clipped,
unfeeling text messages a genre excuse that third-person narration doesn't
have — several borderline findings below (24, 25, 32) exist only because the
format legitimately compresses feeling, and a fixer should not "solve" them by
adding interiority the chat medium can't carry. And chapter 15 pre-dates
chapter 20 by four years in-story; if chapter 20 gets rewritten in isolation,
chapter 15 will still be sitting there as an uncorrected, younger-and-therefore-
worse version of the identical failure.

## The five worst instances, in order

**1. Chapter 20, lines 141–151 — the incident-report/concussion calculus.**
This is the passage the brief names directly. "We should talk about whether
this is a thing that gets reported." / "Reported to who." / "I mean us. Four
minors, no adult inside four miles, three firearms taken apart on a public
street." / "He's breathing on his own and his pupils are even. He wakes up in
twenty minutes with a headache and six friends to take him home. ... That's not
a call-it-in problem. That's a walk-away problem." Four sixteen-year-olds who
just fought seven armed men run a reporting-risk calculation and a clinical
concussion triage with no fear anywhere in it. It should instead be: *we can't
tell anyone because we snuck out and we'll get detention/expelled*, with actual
fear about the guns underneath that.

**2. Chapter 15, lines 165–169 — a twelve-year-old volunteers to be grabbed.**
"That's the point," she says, already moving toward the door. "I'm twelve and
I'm small, and I'm a girl in pyjamas. Nobody hits that. They'll grab me and
they'll try to keep me quiet and then they'll spend at least four seconds
deciding what to do with me, and while they're deciding, they're standing still
and they're all facing the same way." This is the exact professional-operative
reasoning the brief flags in chapter 20, delivered by a child four years
younger, with the same total absence of named fear. If chapter 20 is
indefensible at sixteen, this is worse.

**3. Chapter 20, lines 165–189 — "I got nothing out of that."** The entire
walk home after fighting seven armed men, several of them holding guns, is Sam
complaining he didn't get enough of a workout and the group grading the
robbers' technique ("You could have done that at thirteen," "Go back and fight
the waitress"). Twenty-five lines of aftermath and not one line of adrenaline,
shakiness, or relief. It should be four teenagers walking home too keyed up to
be quiet, one of them still shaking, cracking jokes precisely *because* they're
scared, not instead of being scared.

**4. Chapter 15, lines 265–269 — analysis instead of any feeling about
restraining four grown men.** After Sam jokes "That's a C" about a drugged,
tied-up adult, the narration gives: "Chloe doesn't say anything for the
interrogation. She's sitting against the wall with her arms around her knees,
watching the four of them on the floor, turning it over without landing on
what's wrong. ... She's twelve, and it's four in the morning, and she lets it
go." The line "She's twelve, and it's four in the morning" reads like the
narrator noticing the problem and then doing nothing about it — the age is
named but no age-appropriate feeling follows it.

**5. Chapter 11, lines 89–99 — the earliest instance in the range.** Chloe, age
eight, overhears her grandmother tell her mother "She's put on weight" through
a door left open a few inches — a version of the exact overheard-hurt structure
chapter 1 is being fixed for — and the very next sentence is: "Chloe gets the
water and goes back through and finishes about the bridge, which held eleven
pounds, second in the room." No reaction, then an exact number standing in
where one should be. This is the chapter-1 mechanism, present on page one of
the range this survey covers, meaning it was never actually contained to the
first ten chapters.

## Full instance list

### Category 1 — Analysis where a feeling should be

- **11_eight.md:89–99.** `"She's put on weight." ... Chloe gets the water and goes back through and finishes about the bridge, which held eleven pounds, second in the room.` Chloe overhears a comment about her own body and returns to a number instead of any registered feeling. *Should be: some physical tell — going quiet, eating less, refusing the mirror for a week — before or instead of the bridge report.*
- **11_eight.md:205.** `She had stopped checking for it in January, but the notebook says January is when it turned. She adds a line at the bottom with the date on it, and puts the notebook in the drawer, and goes down to dinner.` An eight-year-old discovers she's caught up academically and the discovery is logged like an audit, with no pride, relief, or excitement rendered. *Should be: a small physical beat of satisfaction — she reads the line twice, or grins at the drawer before shutting it — before the ledger-keeping.*
- **15_twelve.md:265–269.** `Chloe doesn't say anything for the interrogation. She's sitting against the wall with her arms around her knees, watching the four of them on the floor, turning it over without landing on what's wrong. ... She's twelve, and it's four in the morning, and she lets it go.` See worst-instances #4 above.
- **17_fourteen.md:125.** `She holds one up to the window and turns it, then drops all three in the scrap bin on her way past, while Pruitt is at the far bench with two others and does not see her do it.` Three weeks of work discarded with zero named reaction; the secrecy ("does not see her do it") implies shame or perfectionism but nothing precedes the act itself. *Should be: a beat of the disappointment that made her judge the pieces unworthy, before the discard.*
- **18_fifteen.md:145–148.** `She takes a week over it, and she builds the case against first. ... Then she builds the other one, from Sandoval's side, and from her own, and from the side of the four names on the form.` A fifteen-year-old asked to keep a real secret processes it as a legal brief with two sides, entirely off-page from any anxiety about it, with "she is quiet at dinner" the only outward sign. *Should be: the anxiety itself — trouble sleeping, snapping at Sam, something bodily — shown before the case-building starts, not standing in for it.*
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:89.** `Chloe checks the fence, the gap under it, the alley, the loading dock she could be on top of in four seconds. ... That's the whole read.` The instant of first seeing armed strangers renders as a tactical scan with no fear registered at all, immediately. *Should be: a beat of fear or a specific physical tell (dry mouth, a missed breath) before the tactical read, not replacing it.*

### Category 2 — Sentence reporting an absent reaction

- **14_sixty_degrees.md:187–197.** Chloe corrects her grandmother's dismissive "Once in a whole year isn't very much, is it" with a raw count ("I've done it a dozen times since then. More than a dozen") and the scene ends there — no frustration, no deflation shown, on either side of the exchange. *Should be: some visible flicker of being let down by the correction not landing, even a small one.*
- **19_sixteen.md:25–31.** `"Four points," she says. ... "It's fine." She takes the list in one more time and steps back from the board to let somebody else in.` Missing the bar exam pass line by four points, after months of work, gets exactly "It's fine" with no narration undercutting it — a textbook version of the banned "nobody"/absence construction, self-applied. *Should be: let the flatness be visibly effortful, a held breath, hands staying too still, something that tells the reader "It's fine" is a lie she's telling herself.*
- **24_the_chat.md:549–555.** `Kavi doesn't come back with anything for four days. ... Whatever internal means, it isn't a word any of them knows how to get past. The chat moves on within about ten minutes.` A genuinely unsettling discovery (their own medicine doesn't exist in any public record) gets ten minutes of group silence, then nothing. *Should be: at least one character naming the unease out loud, even briefly, before the thread drops.*
- **25_nineteen.md:104.** `She doesn't think much of it. She has told him the truth. It isn't interesting.` More defensible at nineteen than at eight, but still a flat denial of a reaction to a stranger's odd, faintly ominous email about her output. *Should be: name what she does feel (irritation, mild unease) rather than deny feeling at all.*
- **25_nineteen.md:278–284.** `Nobody in the chat treats it as anything but ordinary; people go quiet for a month at a time and come back with a screenshot and a joke. ... Six months.` A friend going silent for six months is played entirely as a non-event via the banned "nobody" construction, evidently as a deliberate dread-building device for later chapters. Flagged because it is the construction Rule 3 bans, regardless of intent; a fixer should decide whether the dramatic-irony effect survives without leaning on it.
- **32_the_money.md:135–138.** `chloe: theo that was a drill / theo: it wasnt a drill. i read the file.` Learning that the "training exercise" from chapter 15/20 was a real hostile incursion against children gets a flat factual correction from Chloe, no reaction. Partly a function of the chat format (see structural note above), but worth a look — even a text-message register allows for `chloe: wait what` or similar.

### Category 4 — Cold-operative register in a child/teenager

- **15_twelve.md:165–169.** See worst-instances #2 above.
- **15_twelve.md:175–187.** `"They're actors," Ruth says. "If they get a work physical in three weeks they're going to fail it and they're going to have to explain why." ... "So, it's rude."` Twelve-year-olds discuss whether to use traceable vs. untraceable sedatives on adult intruders in terms of professional courtesy ("it's rude"), with zero fear present anywhere in the debate. *Should be: this exact tactical content is fine as school-taught procedure recited under stress, but a child's fear or excitement about actually doing it for the first time needs to be in the scene too.*
- **15_twelve.md:209.** `Ruth has the antidote out as she counts respirations on each of the four every two minutes and writes the numbers on her arm in pen because nobody brought paper.` Clinical post-incident vitals monitoring by a twelve-year-old, narrated with the same flatness as a nurse's chart. *Should be at minimum acknowledge this is strange/adult work for a kid to be doing calmly — a joke, a shudder, something marking the incongruity the reader is meant to feel.*
- **15_twelve.md:283.** See worst-instances list note above; Chloe's only sustained emotional register after the whole incident is being "annoyed about the C for about a week." *Should be: the grade-anxiety is fine as characterization, but it should not be the *only* feeling in evidence after drugging and restraining four adults.*
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:121–127.** `"He's slow on the transitions," Nadia says, chin on her hands. ... "Why is he doing that with his left."` Live sports-commentary from the roof while Sam fights armed men below — the register of coaches reviewing game film, not four teenagers watching a friend risk his life. *Should be: at least a beat of the tension of watching it, not just the technical critique.*
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:141–151.** See worst-instances #1 above; the passage the brief names directly.
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:165–189.** See worst-instances #3 above.
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:201.** `Nadia doesn't call her father before she leaves for the summer. ... She doesn't mention a fence, a diner, or seven men behind a hardware store.` The reason for staying quiet is left entirely implicit and reads, in context, as operational discretion consistent with the tactical training, not "I snuck out past curfew and would be in huge trouble." The brief's prescribed fix is specific here: *she should not mention it because she broke a school rule getting out, not because she's running a need-to-know assessment.*

### Category 5 — Bodies missing

- **20_the_parking_lot.md:137.** `Chloe does two of them, working down each ribcage with both hands flat, counting under her breath the same way she'd count her own, ribs, nothing floating, and one who's bitten through his lip.` Chloe's body appears in this scene only as an instrument checking someone else's ribs; her own physical state (heart rate, hands, breath) during and after a violent encounter with armed men is never rendered. *Should be: at least one line of what her own body is doing while she works, the way "her legs have decided to" sit her down does earlier in the same chapter, at line 199 (a good instance already in the text, useful as the model to extend).*

### Category 6 — Exact-number tic as narration default

- **14_sixty_degrees.md:225.** `She stands in front of that one, because she spent the whole two hours on a landing counting paint.` A moment of feeling judged/defensive gets resolved by restating a count rather than naming the discomfort.
- **20_the_parking_lot.md:129.** `"Fifty-one seconds," Ruth says. "One of them never even got there."` The immediate response to just having fought seven armed men is a stopwatch reading, functioning as the chapter's only "reaction" to the violence. Ruth's counting habit is established characterization elsewhere in the book, which argues for keeping the line, but it should not be the *only* thing said in the beat right after real danger.
- **21_the_applications.md:235.** `She reads the twelfth acceptance a second time before she believes the count, then a third, checking the name on the envelope against the name on the letter inside it ... Then she turns the top one over and over without opening the mail underneath it, working out what to do with a number that doesn't match anything the school has ever told her about herself.` Getting into twelve of fourteen colleges — a huge, joyful moment — is rendered entirely as recounting and cross-checking, with no pleasure, disbelief-as-relief, or excitement anywhere in the passage. *Should be: let the counting be underneath a first, unguarded physical reaction (grinning at the floor, calling someone), not instead of one.*

## Positive counterexamples worth protecting

Not part of the count above, but useful for whoever fixes these chapters, since
the brief notes the dinner scene in chapter 1 as the register the rest of the
book is missing — that register does recur here and should not be sanded down
to match the broken instances:

- **20_the_parking_lot.md:193, 199.** `Chloe sets her shoes down by the door, laces still knotted from being kicked off rather than untied, and lies on top of the covers for a while before she gets under them` and, mid-fight, `Chloe steps back out of the way and sits down on the floor because her legs have decided to.` Both are the physical-correlate technique working correctly, in the same chapter that fails at it elsewhere.
- **17_fourteen.md:145–199.** The whole mother-confrontation scene over the rifle range ("And Chloe has nothing at all") is genuinely good register-appropriate writing for a fourteen-year-old caught out, with real escalation and silence doing the work.
- **19_sixteen.md:103.** `She still shuts books harder than she means to in November, and she could not tell anybody why she is doing it.` A delayed physical tell standing in for feeling about the failed exam, months after the flat "It's fine" moment flagged above — the right technique, just missing from the scene where the news actually lands.
- **22_the_offer.md:94.** `He stands at the window with both hands flat on the sill until they stop shaking, then leaves without answering when someone asks if he's all right.` A background classmate's reaction to a life-changing decision, rendered entirely physically. Good model for what's missing from several Chloe instances above.
- **27_the_file.md:89.** `He puts his hand over his mouth and sits like that.` Theo, as an adult reading the classified file on his own childhood, has the emotional reaction the twelve-year-olds in chapter 15 were never given at the time. Worth noting to whoever fixes chapter 15: the book already knows how to write this feeling, four chapters and nine years later.

## Method note

Every chapter from 11 to 35 was read in full. Instances above were selected for
being unambiguous and load-bearing; borderline cases that could plausibly be
read as intentional restraint (the trailing-clause style this book uses
throughout, chat-format terseness, adult characters being genuinely composed
professionals) were left out rather than padded in. All line numbers and
quotations were re-verified against the source files with `sed -n` immediately
before this report was written.
