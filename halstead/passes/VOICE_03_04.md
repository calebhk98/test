# Voice pass: chapters 3-4

Scope: `chapters/03_the_letter.md` and `chapters/04_pluto.md`. Dialogue and
chat lines were not touched. Ten sentences changed (all narration), two
findings called out and not changed (same paragraph, same construction).

Chloe is six and seven across these two chapters. Where an institution (the
school, a receptionist, a house rule) acts on her, the fix below generally
keeps that institution as the actor rather than inventing a named person the
text does not support. Where a specific parent is clearly the actor from
context, the parent is named.

## Changed

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 73

**Before:** "Then the phone drops onto the counter, and her dad says something from the other side of the room that stays too quiet to reach the stairs."

**After:** "Then her mom drops the phone onto the counter, and her dad says something from the other side of the room that stays too quiet to reach the stairs."

**Why:** Fake agency. The phone did not drop itself; her mom, having just finished the district-office call the sentence before, put it down hard. Naming her keeps the actor and loses nothing, and it is not a put-down/set-down beat (different verb, not near that cap).

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 111

**Before:** "...whether Chloe can be pulled out on any day, for any reason, or whether there is a form, or a notice period, or a fee..."

**After:** "...whether she can pull Chloe out on any day, for any reason, or whether there is a form, or a notice period, or a fee..."

**Why:** A person (Chloe) in the subject of a passive, the highest-priority fault. This is narrated paraphrase of the mother's phone questions, not a direct quotation, so it is in scope. The doer is unambiguous: her mom is the one asking whether she herself can remove her daughter for a day.

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 145

**Before:** "Her dad looks up the website that night on the laptop at the kitchen table, and Chloe is allowed to sit next to him because it's Friday, her feet swinging free under the chair while he talks through the first part of it..."

**After:** "Her dad looks up the website that night on the laptop at the kitchen table, and because it's Friday, Chloe gets to sit next to him, her feet swinging free under the chair while he talks through the first part of it..."

**Why:** Person in the subject of a passive ("Chloe is allowed"). This is a household rule tied to the day of the week, not a specific person's one-off decision, so the fix keeps "it's Friday" as the reason rather than inventing which parent set the rule, and moves Chloe into an active "gets to" construction instead of a passive one.

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 147

**Before:** "A photograph of a field with goals on it draws a look, and a pool draws a question about whether she would swim, to which she says yes."

**After:** "Chloe stops at a photograph of a field with goals on it, and when a pool comes up her dad asks whether she would swim, to which she says yes."

**Why:** Fake agency twice in one sentence (photograph "draws a look," pool "draws a question"). The scene is Chloe and her dad going through the school website together; the look is hers, and since her dad is the one narrating the page and the question is put to her ("she says yes" answers it), he is the one asking it.

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 159

**Before:** "Searches from every direction she can think of turn up the school's own website, then stop: no news coverage, no parent forum mention..."

**After:** "She tries every direction she can think of and turns up nothing but the school's own website: no news coverage, no parent forum mention..."

**Why:** Fake agency ("searches" turning things up and stopping on their own). The paragraph already has her mom as the established subject two sentences earlier; restoring her as the one searching is a direct, uninvented fix.

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 205

**Before:** "On the ninth night Chloe is supposed to be asleep, having been sent back once, but comes out a second time in her socks, skipping the fourth stair."

**After:** "On the ninth night Chloe is supposed to be asleep, her parents having sent her back once already, but she comes out a second time in her socks, skipping the fourth stair."

**Why:** Person in the subject of a passive ("Chloe... having been sent back"). Both parents are present and arguing about her that same night, and no line specifies which one sent her back earlier, so the doer is given as "her parents" rather than guessing between the two. ("Chloe is supposed to be asleep" itself was left alone: it is a modal expectation, not an action a specific person did to her.)

### chapters/04_pluto.md, line 33

**Before:** "Lights out is earlier for the sixes than for the sevens, which puts Chloe in the first group until August."

**After:** "Lights out is earlier for the sixes than for the sevens, so the school puts Chloe in the first group until August."

**Why:** Fake agency ("which puts Chloe"), and the straightforward active fix ("Chloe is put in the first group") would have created a new person-passive, which is worse. The age-banded grouping is plainly the school's doing (this whole passage is orientation), so the school is named as the faceless institutional actor the rules call for.

### chapters/04_pluto.md, line 83

**Before:** "That evening she goes down to the phone by the front desk, which you are allowed to use whenever you want, but because there is a boy in front of her who is crying..."

**After:** "That evening she goes down to the phone by the front desk, which anyone can use whenever they want, but because there is a boy in front of her who is crying..."

**Why:** Person in the subject of a passive ("you are allowed"). Rewording to an active generic subject ("anyone can use") removes the passive entirely without needing to name the school as an actor in its own relative clause.

### chapters/04_pluto.md, line 109

**Before:** "Then her mom brings up a thing that happened at her work in June, which Chloe has been told twice, word for word both times..."

**After:** "Then her mom brings up a thing that happened at her work in June, which she has already told Chloe twice, word for word both times..."

**Why:** Person in the subject of a passive ("Chloe has been told"), the exact pattern named in the brief. The doer is the subject of the main clause one comma earlier: her mom.

### chapters/04_pluto.md, line 221

**Before:** "There's a fourth in Pluto who stays silent day after day, answering when he's called on, in as few words as he can..."

**After:** "There's a fourth in Pluto who stays silent day after day, answering when the teacher calls on him, in as few words as he can..."

**Why:** Person in the subject of a passive ("he's called on"). This is a classroom, and the teacher (Mr. Doyle, on the page later in the same chapter) is the only person who calls on children, so naming "the teacher" invents nothing. Compare line 277 in chapter 3, where the book already does this correctly ("answers only when Mr. Doyle calls on her direct") — this fix brings line 221 in line with the book's own working pattern.

## Called out, not changed

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 165

**Current:** "There is a man who was a SEAL, but the paragraph puts it in the middle of a sentence and goes straight on to something else."

**Why the voice is wrong:** Fake agency. The paragraph did not compose or arrange itself; a person wrote the teacher bios on the school website that way.

**Why I did not change it:** I could not tell who does it. It could be the school, a copywriter, or the teacher supplying his own bio, and this is the uncanny-website scene that is already building toward something being wrong about Halstead, so picking a wrong actor risks planting a fact the author has not put there. (The stray ",." after "something else" that the agent found here has since been fixed; it was a typo, not a voice question.)

**Replacement 1:** "There is a man who was a SEAL, but whoever wrote it buries that in the middle of a sentence and goes straight on to something else."

**Replacement 2:** "There is a man who was a SEAL, but it comes in the middle of a sentence, with the next one already moving on to something else."

### chapters/03_the_letter.md, line 165

**Current:** "One of them wrote a paper, and the paragraph gives the name of the paper without saying what a paper is, as though everybody has read it."

**Why the voice is wrong:** Same fake agency as the SEAL sentence two sentences earlier in the same paragraph: "the paragraph" is doing something only a writer can do.

**Why I did not change it:** Same reason as above, and the two sentences read as a matched pair (both about how oddly the bios are written); fixing one and not the other, or fixing them with different invented actors, would break that pairing worse than leaving both.

**Replacement 1:** "One of them wrote a paper, and whoever wrote the bio gives its name without saying what a paper is, as though everybody has read it."
**Replacement 2:** "One of them wrote a paper, and its name is given without saying what a paper is, as though everybody has read it."

## Report

- **Changed:** 10 sentences, all in the same direction — active voice with nobody in it (fake agency, 4 instances: ch3 lines 73, 147 (x2), 159; ch4 line 33) and a person wrongly placed in the subject of a passive (6 instances: ch3 lines 111, 145, 205; ch4 lines 83, 109, 221). No sentence needed the opposite fix (active turned passive) in either chapter.
- **Called out, not changed:** 2, both the same construction ("the paragraph puts/gives") in the same paragraph at chapter 3, line 165.
- **Strongest call-out:** the paired "the paragraph puts it... the paragraph gives the name..." sentences in chapter 3, line 165. Both are textbook fake agency, but the scene is the family's first real unease about Halstead's suspiciously polished website, and inventing a specific writer for the bios (the school? a copywriter? the teacher himself?) risks manufacturing a fact about who is behind Halstead that the book has not decided yet. This felt like exactly the case the brief means by "a scene doing something you do not fully understand" — worth the author's eye rather than a guess.
