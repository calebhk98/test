# Chapter 2: the testing scene

Two outside reviews of this scene are in `passes/review/IQ_Test.md`. They
disagree with each other in places; where they do, this file rules.

## The target

**Her official Full Scale IQ is about 128. Her true ability is about 160.**

The reader must be able to see both: the number on the page, and the child the
number fails to describe. The author's instruction is that we show her
interiority and why she fails.

## Why she scores 128, in the author's words

> *"What she is bad at is writing things down. Note that she is 6 years old, and
> is trying to be neat about it. Her hands have not physically developed, that
> portion of the test is measuring her biological development of her hands, not
> her IQ at all. She is also not trying to be as fast as she can be, she is
> trying to be neat. She then stops for a portion to see if she is allowed to go
> faster.*
>
> *Her top of the line question maxes out the test, and goes past it.*
>
> *When she answers questions, she doesn't give the full answer, such as
> stubborn. She is trying to determine what answer he wants."*

Three mechanisms, then, and nothing else:

1. **Her hands are six.** This is biological maturation and has no cognitive
   content whatsoever. It is the honest part of the low score.
2. **She optimises for neat, not fast**, and stops mid-task to work out whether
   speed is permitted. Nobody has told her the rules, and she is a child who has
   already learned that getting it wrong in front of an adult costs something.
3. **She under-answers because she is solving the wrong problem** — not "what is
   the answer" but "what answer does this man want". Every short answer in the
   scene is her calibrating to him.

**Her failures must never be cognitive.** If a single item in the scene reads as
her genuinely being unable to reason, the whole chapter inverts: she becomes a
verbally gifted child with average nonverbal ability, which is a real and common
profile and is not this character. A reader who knows the instrument will read
it that way, and the reviewers both did.

## The instrument

**WISC-IV. March 2012. That is the correct test and the only one in the room.**

Errors currently on the page:

- **Mazes must go.** Dropped with the WISC-III in 2003. It cannot coexist with
  Cancellation, which is WISC-IV only.
- **Story retelling must go**, or be moved outside the battery. It is not a WISC
  subtest; it belongs to the Stanford-Binet or NEPSY.
- **Coding uses the wrong form.** A six-year-old takes **Coding A**, which pairs
  shapes with simple marks and contains **no numerals anywhere**. The scene has
  numerals in the key, which is Coding B, for ages eight and up.
- **The mother's questionnaire** should be an examiner's Record Form.
- Picture Completion is supplementary, not core.

**Two core subtests are missing and their absence is what breaks the scene.**
Perceptual Reasoning needs Block Design, **Matrix Reasoning** and **Picture
Concepts**. The last two are untimed, answered by pointing, and carry zero motor
demand, which makes them the only place in the battery where her nonverbal
reasoning can appear uncontaminated by her hands. Without them the sole
nonverbal evidence on the page is a block failure, so the chapter currently
argues the opposite of what it intends. **Putting Matrix Reasoning in and
letting her go straight through it is the cheapest fix in the chapter.**

## Block Design: the item that has to change

Currently she cannot find where the shape starts and says she cannot do it.
That is a **visual analysis failure**, which is exactly what the subtest exists
to measure, and it is cognitive.

Rewrite it as **correct and too late**. She sees the decomposition, the reader
watches her see it, and then her hands cost her: blocks placed right and rotated
wrong, re-rotated, a design finished accurately just past the limit, which
scores nothing. **A stopwatch has to be visible during the blocks**, or the
reader has no way to attribute the loss to speed. At present the timer only
appears at Coding.

## Where the under-answering actually costs her

Not Vocabulary. Vocabulary runs to adult-level items regardless of the child's
age, so every examinee hits a wall eventually and hers comes long after the
scaled ceiling. The stubborn moment is thematically right and financially free.

**Comprehension is the subtest where it costs.** Several items require two
distinct reasons for full credit. She gives one, every time, because one answer
is what she has decided he wants. That is the mechanism, on the correct subtest,
repeatedly, by design.

Keep the stubborn beat for what it shows the reader. Put the scoring damage on
Comprehension.

## The ceiling problem, which is the best thing available here

At age six the scaled scores are normed against six-year-olds in three-month
bands, so the raw score needed to hit the maximum of 19 is low. **A 145 child
and a 175 child produce identical scaled scores at that age. The instrument has
no resolution above roughly 145 to 150.**

So no performance in that room can express 160. What it can express is that she
pinned the ceiling and kept going, which is a floor and not a value. **The
evidence for what she actually is has to sit outside the scored items**, and the
scene already has the two places it can live: the extra Similarities booklet
Prentice takes out of the drawer, and the hour-and-year answer that goes
somewhere the manual has no column for.

Use those two. Add nothing else that tries to number her.

## What is already right and must not be touched

- **Digit span at eight forward and six backward.** The typical six-year-old
  manages four and two or three; adult average is about seven and four to five.
  This is the highest single datum in the chapter and it is correctly placed.
- **The hour and year answer.** Both reviewers name it as the best evidence in
  the scene.
- **Coding being slow because her hands are six.** She is fresh when she takes
  it, roughly twenty-five minutes in, so fatigue cannot explain it and must not
  be offered as an explanation. Fresh, trying hard, and still unable to make her
  fingers do it is the strongest version.
- **Cancellation must be visibly better than Coding.** Crossing out duplicates
  is one slash per mark; Coding is about a hundred controlled novel strokes. If
  she is equally bad at both, the deficit reads as scanning or decision speed,
  which is cognitive, and the motor argument dies.

## Prentice

He is a good psychologist who makes a reasonable mistake. He believes the low
score is the processing speed dragging the composite down, and he is partly
right. **What he misses is that she under-answered the verbal and reasoning
subtests as well**, because he trusted her performance and assumed she was
trying her hardest.

He also tests limits off-protocol twice: one more digit span after she has
failed a length, and the extra Similarities booklet. Both are out of standard
administration and both are correct as characterisation. Leave them, and let a
reader who knows the instrument notice he is doing it.

## The numbers to land on

| Index | Official | True |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal Comprehension | about 130 | about 160 |
| Perceptual Reasoning | about 135 | about 160 |
| Working Memory | about 120 | about 160 |
| Processing Speed | about 100 | about 100 |
| **General Ability Index** | **about 133** | about 160 |
| **Full Scale IQ** | **about 128** | about 152 |

One point under the gifted cutoff is the cruellest available integer and it is
inside the plausible range. The chapter does not have to print any of these
figures; it has to be consistent with them.
