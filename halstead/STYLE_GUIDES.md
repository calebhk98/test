# Halstead — Style Spec and Pass/Fail Checklist

Written for someone drafting or revising a chapter. Audience for the book is 9th graders. The voice should read like a competent 12th grader wrote it: restrained, plain, no spelling errors, no stylist moves.

---

## 1\. POV and tense

Third person limited, present tense, close on Chloe.

Passes: Chloe is physically present, or overhearing from somewhere the text establishes. Working devices already in the book: the stair where the carpet stops, a door open a few inches, the thin part of the bedroom floor, a call on the car speaker, the landing with a drawer open, the kitchen window.

Fails:

* Any other character's interiority. "It occurs to him that…" / "Her parents are so relieved they don't examine it."  
* Any scene Chloe cannot perceive. If it matters, she overhears it.  
* Future POV of any kind: "she gets it out again when she's about fourteen," "he thinks about pushing it for a long time afterward," "where it lives from then on," "she doesn't have a way to say what it is yet."  
* Telling the reader what to notice.

It is fine, and intended, for readers to miss things.

---

## 2\. Register

The core rule: keep the restraint, cut the invented image.

Fails — reification. An abstract thing handled as a physical object. Grep for an unanchored *it* plus a physical verb: put, keep, carry, hold, fold, place.

* "She just puts it somewhere and keeps it."  
* "Chloe carries it around in her chest for weeks."  
* "in a way Chloe doesn't have a use for"

Fails — invented simile. Conventional, inherited figures are fine. Built ones are not.

* Fails: "her mom says it carefully, the way you'd put something down" / "it comes out of her all at once, like something tearing" / "the room detonates"  
* Passes: "the way you'd tell somebody their shoe was untied"

Passes — Chloe's own conceits. Icarus, clouds as the ocean going somewhere else, the moon as a liar, the room-shaped object. These belong to a character, get said out loud, and get argued with by other characters. Keep them. The distinction is speaker: a child's invented image in dialogue passes, the narrator's invented image does not.

Also fails: em dashes

---

## 3\. The trailing clause

This is the single most common defect. It accounted for eighteen cuts in one 2,200-word chapter.

Test: is the sentence complete and correct before the last comma? Does what follows tell the reader how to take what was just said? If both, cut it.

Real examples, all cut:

| Kept | Cut |
| ----- | ----- |
| Ruth lets go and goes | and that's the whole thing |
| Somebody picks up on the second ring | the way somebody there always does |
| She learns all nine names and uses them | which nobody at that school does |
| Mrs. Prahl makes a real thing of it | which he deserves |
| "I've just got a lot of school stuff," Chloe says | which is not true and which nobody checks |
| waits in the car with the engine running | because it's a return |
| checks them | which takes four minutes and turns up nothing |
| said some of it had | which is the answer that ends the question fastest |
| Her mom looks at her | for a second longer than the question needed |

Note the third column effect: cutting these also fixes the sentence-length burstiness, because they're what pushes sentences past 35 words. The literariness and the statistics are the same defect measured two ways.

---

## 4\. Ands

Target: 34–42 per 1,000 words. Above 50 is too many. Measure it.

Cut filler ands. Keep load-bearing accumulation — rage speech, lists meant to drag ("Then it's Friday, and Saturday, and Sunday, and Monday"), a mother's four failed questions in a row.

Replace coordination with subordination: *as, before, because, while, which, so that, where, until, after which, of which, except that*, and participials (*listening for the stairs*, *leaving Chloe the whole couch*).

* "the mail, and Chloe comes in" → "the mail, as Chloe comes in"  
* "and then a family walks between them and he isn't there" → "before he isn't there"  
* "worked in April, and all she has to do" → "worked in April, all she has to do"

---

## 5\. Joining speech and body

Attach the physical beat to the line with a comma, not a period. A period makes them two events. The comma makes them one.

* Fails: "No." Her eyes start to get wet.  
* Passes: "I don't know Kavi's either", her eyes start to get wet.  
* Passes: Her mom looks at her for a second, "It's OK, we can just ask the school", and then she closes the phone book.  
* Passes: "No," Chloe says, shaking her head against the pillow.

The splice is deliberate. Don't correct it.

Urgent speech uses comma runs, not periods. "Hey. Hey. What's going on?" is somebody composing themselves between words. "Hey, Hey, What's going on?" is somebody talking fast at a crying child.

---

## 6\. Numbers

Two columns. The baseline is the aim; it's derived from the genre corpus (mean 17.3, median 14, sd 12.4). The working range is what chapters have actually shipped at and is acceptable.

### Sentence length

|  | Baseline | Working range |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| under 10 words | 30% | 30–36% |
| 10–20 words | 41% | 35–40% |
| 20–35 words | 21% | 20–23% |
| over 35 words | 8% | 4–6% |
| mean | 17.3 | 15–17 |
| median | 14 | 13–14 |
| CV | \~72% | 65–78% |

Mode \< median \< mean must hold. Mode is unstable at chapter length — a twelve-versus-ten count gap flips it — so don't chase it, but check the ordering.

### Measure spoken and narration separately

This is the most useful diagnostic in the whole spec. The combined number hides which half is broken.

|  | Target |
| ----- | ----- |
| spoken sentences, mean | 8–10 words |
| narration sentences, mean | 14–17 words |
| narration sentences under 10 words | under 40% |

Under 6 words per spoken sentence means characters are trading fragments and the chapter will read childish no matter what the combined number says.

### Paragraphs

|  | Baseline | Current drafts |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| sentences/paragraph, mean | 2.9–3.5 | 2.0–2.3 |
| sentences/paragraph, median | 2 | 2 |
| one-sentence paragraphs | \~36% | 38–43% |
| CV | 101–107% | 66–80% |

Paragraph CV is the one metric still well off baseline across every chapter. The book's paragraphs are too uniform in length.

### Dialogue and length

* Words inside quotation marks: \~30%  
* Chapter length: 2,500–3,000+ words. A 1,000-word chapter reads like a children's book.

---

## 7\. Method

Do not write to the profile by feel. It doesn't work — a chapter drafted fresh with all these rules in mind still came out at 56% under ten words and CV 98%. Draft, measure, correct, measure again.

Three things that will happen every time:

1. Every corrective cut splits sentences. Removing trailing clauses and filler ands both drain the 20–35 band and drop the mean. Rebuild that band with subordinate clauses carrying real information, never with restored filler.  
2. Corrections over-apply. Lengthening every short sentence flattened one chapter to CV 56% with the mode above the median. Lengthen selectively; some short lines are the content.  
3. Adding words tempts padding. See below.

---

## 8\. Failure modes, ranked by how much damage they do

1. Padding to hit word count. Inventing subplots, arithmetic filler, characters reasoning at length about nothing. The tell: passages that don't parse on a read-through.  
2. Putting the author's framing in a character's mouth. An eight-year-old at a curb doesn't have a theory of the admissions process. A mother doesn't compliment a receptionist's privacy policy at her own dinner table. Both are the narrator in costume.  
3. Restaging what a previous chapter already did. Check the chapter before yours for the same beat.  
4. Softening a recurring character. The librarian is friendly and does not bend the four-books rule, in ch1, ch3, and ch7. Making her a generically warm adult who says encouraging things breaks her.  
5. Losing established facts between drafts. Keep the canon list in front of you.

---

## 9\. Quick pass/fail

Before submitting a chapter, check:

* \[ \] Every scene is something Chloe can see or hear  
* \[ \] No other character's thoughts appear  
* \[ \] No sentence points forward in time  
* \[ \] Zero invented similes; zero abstract nouns handled as objects  
* \[ \] No em dashes  
* \[ \] Grep the last clause of every sentence: does it explain rather than add?  
* \[ \] Ands per 1,000 words is 34–42  
* \[ \] Spoken sentence mean is 8–10 words; narration mean is 14–17  
* \[ \] Under-10 band is 30–36%; over-35 band is 4–6%  
* \[ \] Mode \< median \< mean  
* \[ \] Words in quotes ≈ 30%  
* \[ \] Length is 2,500+  
* \[ \] Nothing in the chapter was invented to reach that length  
* \[ \] Canon list checked

# Halstead — Style Guide and Prose Targets

Reference document for anyone editing or drafting chapters. Everything here was derived from working through Chapters 1–6.

---

## 1\. The core rule

Figurative language belongs in mouths and heads. Narration reports.

Chloe can say "he's Icarus" or "clouds are the ocean going somewhere else," because those are facts about her. A narrator who says "she just puts it somewhere and keeps it" is a writer showing you a sentence.

Everything below is a consequence of this rule.

---

## 2\. Automatic FAIL — narrator constructions

These get cut on sight. Each one is the narration doing the reader's work.

| Construction | Example (all cut from drafts) | Fix |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Trailing explanatory clause | "...doesn't look up, which is how Chloe knows the answer is a real one" | State the fact. Stop. |
| Narrator evaluation / ranking | "which is the good part", "because Gary is the best part", "with some relief" | Delete. Let the detail stand. |
| Symmetry / mirrored halves | "That part is work, and it does not stop being work"; "the second time she has said it and the second time it has been true" | Rewrite so the halves don't match. |
| Not-X-but-Y (epanorthosis) | "It's not worse, it's what happened" *(in narration)* | State the precise point directly. OK in dialogue. |
| Obvious causal clause | "because Priya will be awake for another ten minutes" | Cut the because. |
| Future POV / foreshadowing | "she will remember this hallway"; "that's the part Chloe would tell you about later" | Cut entirely. |
| Addressing the reader | "Here is what Chloe works out over the year"; "Here is what math is" | Start with the content. |
| Interiority of non-POV characters | "and means it"; "Sam thinks about it longer than he wants to"; "which Sam enjoys enormously" | Give a physical action instead. |
| Chloe knowing what others don't have | "Plenty of them don't have it" | Only observable behavior. |
| Superlatives in narration | "the only time", "never once", "for the first time" | Cut. Fine in dialogue — kids talk in superlatives. |
| Anaphora / three-beat lists | "Nobody puts her on hold. Nobody says let me check. Nobody says..." | Collapse to one sentence. |
| Stacked negatives | "Nobody makes an announcement, nobody says anything at breakfast" | One positive statement. |
| Repeated frame verb | 15× "She tells him X" | Drop the frame after the first line. |
| Pointing at the scene | Anything signalling *pay attention to this* | Delete. Readers are allowed to miss things. |
| Em dashes (—) | — | Use commas or normal dashes. |

Litmus test: if a sentence has two halves that mirror each other, or a clause that explains the clause before it, it's a writer's sentence.

---

## 3\. Quantitative targets

Measure narration and dialogue together, on the full chapter, with an abbreviation-aware sentence splitter (Mrs., Mr., Dr., initials like Aiden R. will otherwise split wrongly and corrupt every number).

### Words per sentence

* Ordering: mode \< median \< mean  
* Mean: 11–18  
* CV (sd/mean): 68–72%  
* Under 10 words: 35–45%  
* 10–20 words: 30–35%  
* 20–35 words: 15–20%  
* Over 35 words: ≤5%  
* Sentences of ≤3 words should be rare in narration (dialogue exempt)

### Dialogue

* \~30% of total words inside quotes

### Paragraphs

* Sentences per paragraph: mean 2.9–3.5, median 2  
* One-sentence paragraphs: \~37% (not 55%+)  
* CV: 100%+

### Conjunctions (% of all words)

* and: \~2.5–3.0% *(the single most common failure; drafts hit 5.0%)*  
* but: 0.5–0.7%  
* which: 0.2–0.3% *(spikes when you fix and by converting clauses to trailing "which")*  
* Sentences containing 2+ "and": under \~10%

### Word length

* Mean \~4.0 characters  
* Words ≥7 characters: \~10%

### Section breaks (---)

* 2–3 per chapter maximum. Not every other paragraph.

---

## 4\. Known trap: the metrics fight each other

Fixing one number reliably breaks another. Watch for these:

* Hitting sentence length by chaining clauses with "and" → and rate explodes. Use subordination (because, until, while, so that, which) or comma-joins instead.  
* Fixing "and" by adding trailing "which" clauses → creates the explanatory-tail tic in §2.  
* Adding dialogue → drops sentences/paragraph and raises the under-10 count. Both are structural, not fixable by trimming narration.  
* Folding dialogue into narration to fix paragraphs → drops quoted share.

Comma-join instead of a conjunction where possible:

* Weak: "It isn't the kids doing it. The kids are mostly standing next to a parent who is holding a folder."  
* Better: "It isn't the kids doing it, they're mostly standing next to a parent who's holding a folder."

Chapters that will legitimately miss: heavy-dialogue chapters may have trouble reaching 35–45% under-10 or 2.9 sentences/paragraph. Chapters centred on a character alone might not reach 30% quoted. Don't damage the prose chasing these.

---

## 5\. Structure

* Chronological. No reordering for effect.  
* Cause must be visible. If she stops calling home on night 4, the reader must have seen nights 1–3 and what changed on day 2\.  
* Timeline consistency. Day counts, ages, who has read what and when. (Caught error: dad reading a letter "20 minutes ago" that arrived 30 seconds earlier; 26 days stated on two dates two days apart.)  
* Mystery lives in characters' mouths, never in narration. Parents are correctly alarmed; the narrator never nudges. Cut anything that tells the reader how to feel about an ambiguous fact.  
* Don't over-explain in dialogue either. Cut the character who states the book's thesis ("either that's what a school is and everybody else isn't doing it, or it's something else"). Facts are stronger: "Four hours away, Dave. And free. And they wrote to her."

---

## 7\. Quick pass/fail checklist

Run before calling a chapter done:

1. Any sentence with mirrored halves? → FAIL  
2. Any clause explaining the clause before it? → FAIL  
3. Any "which is the/what/how..." evaluation? → FAIL  
4. Any narrator superlative or ranking? → FAIL  
5. Any interiority of a non-POV character? → FAIL  
6. Any "because Y" where Y is obvious? → FAIL  
7. and above 3.5%? → FAIL  
8. which above 0.4%? → FAIL  
9. More than 3 section breaks? → FAIL  
10. Same frame verb 3+ times in a passage? → FAIL  
11. Does Chloe explain her own behavior to the reader? → FAIL  
12. Is she last/worst at everything in the chapter? → FAIL  
13. Mean words/sentence under 11? → check whether dialogue density justifies it  
14. Quoted share under 20%? → check whether the chapter is legitimately interior

import re, numpy as np, sys  
from collections import Counter

ABBR \= r'(?:Mrs|Mr|Ms|Dr|St|Jr|Sr|vs|etc|\[A-Z\])'

def load(p): return open(p, encoding='utf-8').read()

def paragraphs(t):  
    t \= re.sub(r'^\#.\*$','',t,flags=re.M)  
    out=\[\]  
    for blk in re.split(r'\\n\\s\*\\n', t):  
        blk \= blk.strip()  
        if not blk or blk=='---': continue  
        blk \= blk.replace('---',' ').strip()  
        if blk: out.append(re.sub(r'\\s+',' ',blk))  
    return out

def sents(t):  
    t \= re.sub(rf'\\b({ABBR})\\.', r'\\1\<DOT\>', t)  
    t \= re.sub(r'(\[.\!?\])(\["\\u201d\\'\]?)\\s+', r'\\1\\2|', t)  
    return \[p.strip().replace('\<DOT\>','.') for p in t.split('|') if p.strip()\]

def words(t): return re.findall(r"\[A-Za-z\]\[A-Za-z'\]\*", t)

def report(path, label):  
    t \= load(path); paras \= paragraphs(t)  
    spp=\[len(sents(p)) for p in paras\]  
    sl=\[len(words(s)) for p in paras for s in sents(p)\]  
    wl=\[len(w) for p in paras for w in words(p)\]  
    body \= re.sub(r'^\#.\*$','',t,flags=re.M).replace('---',' ')  
    tot=len(words(body)); q=sum(len(words(m)) for m in re.findall(r'"\[^"\]\*"', body))  
    qs=100\*q/tot  
    mode=Counter(sl).most\_common(1)\[0\]\[0\]  
    ok=lambda b:'PASS' if b else 'FAIL'  
    print("="\*66); print(f"{label} | {tot} words | {len(paras)} paras | {len(sl)} sentences")  
    print(f"\\nWORDS/SENTENCE  mode {mode}  median {np.median(sl):.0f}  mean {np.mean(sl):.2f}  sd {np.std(sl,ddof=1):.2f}  CV {100\*np.std(sl,ddof=1)/np.mean(sl):.1f}%  max {max(sl)}")  
    print(f"  mode\<median\<mean  {ok(mode\<np.median(sl)\<np.mean(sl))}     mean 11-18  {ok(11\<=np.mean(sl)\<=18)}     CV\~68-72%  {ok(66\<=100\*np.std(sl,ddof=1)/np.mean(sl)\<=76)}")  
    for name,f,lo,hi in \[('\<10',lambda v:v\<10,35,45),('10-20',lambda v:10\<=v\<=20,30,35),('20-35',lambda v:20\<v\<=35,15,20),('\>35',lambda v:v\>35,0,5)\]:  
        pct=100\*sum(1 for v in sl if f(v))/len(sl); print(f"  {name:\>6}: {pct:5.1f}%  target {lo}-{hi}%  {ok(lo\<=pct\<=hi)}")  
    print(f"\\nQUOTED  {qs:.1f}%  target \~30%  {ok(26\<=qs\<=34)}")  
    cv=100\*np.std(spp,ddof=1)/np.mean(spp)  
    print(f"\\nSENT/PARA  mean {np.mean(spp):.2f}  median {np.median(spp):.0f}  sd {np.std(spp,ddof=1):.2f}  CV {cv:.1f}%  max {max(spp)}")  
    print(f"  mean 2.9-3.5 {ok(2.9\<=np.mean(spp)\<=3.5)}   CV\>=100% {ok(cv\>=100)}")  
    print("  bucket  this   YA")  
    for name,f,ya in \[('1',lambda v:v==1,36.7),('2',lambda v:v==2,24.0),('3',lambda v:v==3,15.1),('4',lambda v:v==4,8.0),('5',lambda v:v==5,5.9),('6-7',lambda v:6\<=v\<=7,5.3),('8-9',lambda v:8\<=v\<=9,2.2),('10+',lambda v:v\>=10,2.8)\]:

    \# conjunction rates  
    from collections import Counter as \_C
