# Halstead Style Rules

Drawn from STYLE_GUIDES.md, which holds two style specs for the same novel plus an analysis script. This file replaces the source for anyone rewriting a character reference sheet. Read no further than this document; everything needed is here.

The source has two guides. Where they set different numbers or emphasis, this file says so instead of picking one. A dedicated section near the end lists every place the two disagree.

---

## 1. Point of view and tense

Instruction: Write third person limited, present tense, close on Chloe for nearly all of the book. Chloe is present in the scene, or overhearing it through a device the text establishes: the stair where the carpet stops, a door open a few inches, a call on the car speaker.

Fails, shortest first:
- Other characters' interiority: "It occurs to him that..."
- Future POV, breaking the tense: "where it lives from then on"
- Telling the reader what to notice.

Instead: report what Chloe can see or hear, in the present. It is fine for the reader to miss what Chloe misses.

### The camera exception

Third person carries one advantage over first, and the book is allowed to use it. When a scene has to reach the reader and Chloe has no reason to be anywhere near it, write the scene without her. A plain camera: what happened, who was in the room, what was said.

Keep it rare. Keep the other constraints. No interiority for anyone in that scene, no future POV, no pointing at what matters.

A fifth contrived overhearing is worse than one clean camera scene. When the only way to put Chloe in earshot is another door left open a few inches, the device has stopped working and the camera is the honest move.

### Viewpoint may change between chapters

The camera exception above covers a scene Chloe cannot reach. It does not cover a whole chapter belonging to somebody else, and several of the later chapters do exactly that: chapter 26 sits with Sam through basic training, chapter 28 with Nadia, chapter 29 with Sam again on the exercise. Chloe is not in them at all.

That is allowed. As the cohort separates and their lives stop running through the same building, the book follows them, and a chapter given to another character carries that character's interiority the same way Chloe's chapters carry hers.

**The constraint is one viewpoint per chapter, not one viewpoint per book.** Whoever a chapter belongs to, it stays with them: their perception, their interiority, and nothing from inside a second head. A chapter with Ruth in it that steps into Kavi's thoughts for a sentence is the failure. Chapters 1-20 are Chloe's throughout, so within them the rules above hold unchanged.

The camera exception still applies where nobody's viewpoint is the right one, and the other constraints stay in force everywhere: no future POV, no telling the reader what to notice.

## 2. Register: figurative language belongs in mouths

Instruction: Figurative and evaluative language is something a character says or thinks. Narration reports plainly and does not build images of its own. Every other rule in this file about metaphor, reification, and narrator evaluation is a consequence of this one rule.

Example, cut from narration: "the room detonates"
Example, kept, spoken by Chloe: "he's Icarus"

Instead: if a line sounds like a writer's sentence rather than a plain report, move it into a character's mouth or cut it. A narrator who says "she just puts it somewhere and keeps it" is a writer showing you a sentence, not a narrator reporting a fact.

### The flatness is the narrator's, not the cast's

The rule above is about figurative language, and it generalises. **The narrator is
extremely flat. The people are not.** Everything this file bans, evaluation,
image-building, superlatives, hedging, is banned in narration and available in a
character.

This has been getting applied the wrong way round. The character sheets were
filled in by measuring a manuscript whose narration is flat, and the measurements
came back flat, and the flatness was then written into the sheets as personality.
Figurative language is ruled out in 22 of 32 sheets, hedging sits at 0% in 25 of
them, and humour at 0 out of 10 in 16. Those are the three devices that most
naturally carry feeling and a longer sentence, and they are switched off across
most of the cast, including every teacher and every outside adult with dialogue.

These are not robots. They get angry, embarrassed, jealous, elated, and unfair to
each other. A scene where nobody feels anything is not restraint, it is an absent
scene. When a character's dial rules out a device, the question to ask is whether
that is true of the person or only true of the prose as it currently stands.

What does not change: the narration. It keeps reporting plainly, and a character
feeling something is shown by what they do and refuse to do, not by the narrator
naming the feeling. Rule 23 in PROSE_RULES.md covers that and still holds.

## 3. Reification and the grep test

Instruction: Do not hand an abstract thing (a feeling, a fact, a memory) a physical verb, as if it were an object that can be moved or stored. Search for an unanchored "it" next to a physical verb: put, keep, carry, hold, fold, place. If the search hits, reify is happening.

Example, cut: "She just puts it somewhere and keeps it."
Also cut: "Chloe carries it around in her chest for weeks."

Instead: name the physical action Chloe actually takes, or drop the sentence. Abstractions do not get carried, held, or folded.

---

## 4. Invented similes versus inherited similes

Instruction: A conventional, already-known comparison is fine in narration. A newly built, writerly comparison is not, in narration. Both are fine in a character's own dialogue or thought, where they belong to that character and can be argued with by other characters.

Fails, shortest first:
- "the room detonates"
- "it comes out of her all at once, like something tearing"
- "her mom says it carefully, the way you'd put something down"

Passes:
- Inherited figure in narration: "the way you'd tell somebody their shoe was untied"
- Character-owned conceits, always spoken or thought, never narrated: Icarus, clouds as the ocean going somewhere else and the moon as a liar, all Chloe's; the room-shaped object, Sam's.

Instead: if the comparison is new and belongs to the narrator, cut it or hand it to a character. If it is a stock comparison, it may stay in narration.

## 5. The trailing clause

Instruction: This is the single most common defect in the source drafts. It accounted for eighteen cuts in one 2,200-word chapter. Test every sentence: is it complete and correct before the last comma? Does the part after that comma tell the reader how to take what was just said? If both are true, cut the trailing part.

Real kept/cut pairs from the source, all actually applied:

| Kept | Cut |
| ----- | ----- |
| Ruth lets go and goes | and that's the whole thing |
| Somebody picks up on the second ring | the way somebody there always does |
| She learns all nine names and uses them | which nobody at that school does |
| Mrs. Prahl makes a real thing of it | which he deserves |
| "I've just got a lot of school stuff," Chloe says | which is not true and which nobody checks |
| waits in the car with the engine running | because it's a return |
| checks them | which takes four minutes and turns up nothing |
| said some of it had | which is the answer that ends the question fastest |
| Her mom looks at her | for a second longer than the question needed |

Instead: state the fact, stop the sentence there. Cutting the trailing half also fixes sentence-length statistics, since these tails are what push sentences past 35 words. The literary defect and the statistical defect are the same problem measured two different ways.

## 6. Ands: cut filler, keep load-bearing accumulation

Instruction: Do not chain clauses with "and" as a default habit. Load-bearing accumulation stays: rage speech, a dragging list ("Then it's Friday, and Saturday, and Sunday, and Monday"), a parent's run of failed questions. Everything else gets subordinated.

Example: "the mail, and Chloe comes in" becomes "the mail, as Chloe comes in"
Other real fixes: "and then a family walks between them and he isn't there" becomes "before he isn't there"; "worked in April, and all she has to do" becomes "worked in April, all she has to do"

Instead: replace filler "and" with subordination (as, before, because, while, which, so that, where, until, after which, of which, except that) or a participial phrase (listening for the stairs, leaving Chloe the whole couch), or with a comma-join where a conjunction is not load-bearing.

## 7. Joining speech and body

Instruction: Attach a physical beat to a spoken line with a comma, not a period. A period reads as two separate events; a comma reads as one continuous moment. This comma splice is deliberate craft in this book. Do not correct it into two sentences.

Fails: "No." Her eyes start to get wet.
Passes: "No," Chloe says, shaking her head against the pillow.
Also passes: Her mom looks at her for a second, "It's OK, we can just ask the school," and then she closes the phone book.

Instead: use a comma where a period would split one beat into two.

Separately, urgent speech uses comma runs rather than periods, and the two read differently. "Hey. Hey. What's going on?" (periods) reads as somebody composing themselves between words. "Hey, Hey, What's going on?" (commas) reads as somebody talking fast at a crying child. Choose the punctuation for the pacing intended, not by habit.

## 8. Automatic-fail narrator constructions

Instruction: These constructions get cut on sight whenever they appear in narration. Each one is the narration doing work that should be left to the reader.

| Construction | Real example, cut from drafts | Fix |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Trailing explanatory clause | "...doesn't look up, which is how Chloe knows the answer is a real one" | State the fact. Stop. |
| Narrator evaluation / ranking | "which is the good part"; "because Gary is the best part"; "with some relief" | Delete. Let the detail stand on its own. |
| Symmetry / mirrored halves | "That part is work, and it does not stop being work" | Rewrite so the two halves do not match. |
| Not-X-but-Y (epanorthosis) | "It's not worse, it's what happened" (in narration) | State the precise point directly. This construction is fine in dialogue. |
| Obvious causal clause | "because Priya will be awake for another ten minutes" | Cut the "because" clause. |
| Future POV / foreshadowing | "she will remember this hallway" | Cut entirely. |
| Addressing the reader | "Here is what Chloe works out over the year" | Start directly with the content. |
| Interiority of non-POV characters | "Sam thinks about it longer than he wants to" | Give a physical action instead. |
| Chloe knowing what others don't have | "Plenty of them don't have it" | Limit narration to observable behavior only. |
| Superlatives in narration | "the only time"; "never once"; "for the first time" | Cut. These are fine in dialogue; kids talk in superlatives. |
| Anaphora / three-beat lists | "Nobody puts her on hold. Nobody says let me check. Nobody says..." | Collapse to one sentence. |
| Stacked negatives | "Nobody makes an announcement, nobody says anything at breakfast" | Restate as one positive statement. |
| Repeated frame verb | "She tells him X," repeated fifteen times | Drop the frame verb after its first appearance. |
| Pointing at the scene | Any construction that signals "pay attention to this" | Delete. Readers are allowed to miss things. |
| Em dashes | The long interrupting dash, however typed | Use commas or a normal short dash instead. **This one applies everywhere, dialogue included.** Unlike the other rows in this table, there is no dialogue exception: an interrupted line of speech uses a comma or a short dash, not an em dash. |

Litmus test, stated directly in the source: if a sentence has two halves that mirror each other, or a clause that explains the clause before it, it is a writer's sentence and needs revision.

## 9. Numeric targets

**Scope note: this section describes the manuscript's prose statistics. It applies only to narrative prose that an agent drafts or quotes, for example inside a character sheet's sample lines. It is not a target for the structure or length of the reference sheets themselves.**

### Sentence length

Two tables exist and give different numbers. See the disagreement section below before applying either.

Guide 1 (baseline is the aim; derived from a genre corpus at mean 17.3, median 14, sd 12.4; working range is what shipped chapters actually hit and is accepted):

| | Baseline | Working range |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| under 10 words | 30% | 30-36% |
| 10-20 words | 41% | 35-40% |
| 20-35 words | 21% | 20-23% |
| over 35 words | 8% | 4-6% |
| mean | 17.3 | 15-17 |
| median | 14 | 13-14 |
| CV | ~72% | 65-100% (raised, see below) |

Guide 2 target:

- Mean: 11-18 words
- CV: 68-100% (raised, see below)
- Under 10 words: 35-45%
- 10-20 words: 30-35%
- 20-35 words: 15-20%
- Over 35 words: 5% or under
- Sentences of 3 words or fewer should be rare in narration (dialogue is exempt)

Both guides require the ordering mode < median < mean. Mode is unstable at chapter length (a swing of ten versus twelve occurrences flips it), so do not chase the mode itself, but check the ordering holds.

### Spoken versus narration, measured separately

Guide 1 calls this the most useful diagnostic in the whole spec, because a combined number hides which half of the prose is broken.

| | Target |
| ----- | ----- |
| spoken sentences, mean | 8-10 words |
| narration sentences, mean | 14-17 words |
| narration sentences under 10 words | under 40% |

Under 6 words per spoken sentence means the characters are trading fragments, and the chapter reads childish regardless of what the combined number shows.

### Paragraphs

Guide 1:

| | Baseline | Current drafts |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| sentences/paragraph, mean | 2.9-3.5 | 2.0-2.3 |
| sentences/paragraph, median | 2 | 2 |
| one-sentence paragraphs | ~36% | 38-43% |
| CV | 101-107% | 66-80% |

"Current drafts" is a status reading, not a target; the baseline column is the target. Paragraph CV is flagged in Guide 1 as the one metric still well off target across every chapter measured.

Guide 2 target: sentences per paragraph mean 2.9-3.5, median 2; one-sentence paragraphs about 37%, not 55% or more; CV 100% or higher.

### Dialogue share

Both guides agree: about 30% of total words should fall inside quotation marks. Guide 2's script treats 26-34% as passing.

### Chapter length

Guide 1 only: 2,500-3,000+ words. A 1,000-word chapter reads like a children's book.

### Conjunction rates (Guide 2, percent of all words)

- and: about 2.5-3.0% of all words (the single most common numeric failure; drafts have hit 5.0%)
- but: 0.5-0.7%
- so: 0.2-0.6% (script range; not otherwise discussed in the prose)
- because: 0.2-0.7% (script range; not otherwise discussed in the prose)
- which: 0.2-0.3% (spikes when "and" is fixed by converting clauses into trailing "which" clauses, which then trips Rule 8's trailing-clause fail)
- Sentences containing two or more "and": under about 10%

Guide 1's "ands" rule is stated differently: 34-42 per 1,000 words, with anything above 50 called too many. See the disagreement section; this does not match Guide 2's percentage target.

### Word length (Guide 2 only)

- Mean word length: about 4.0 characters
- Words of 7 or more characters: about 10%

### Section breaks (Guide 2 only)

2-3 per chapter, maximum. Not every other paragraph.

## 10. The metrics fight each other

Instruction: treat this as a known trap, not a sign of a bad target. Fixing one number reliably breaks another. Do not chase a single metric in isolation.

Documented interactions:
- Hitting a sentence-length target by chaining clauses with "and" makes the and-rate spike. Use subordination (because, until, while, so that, which) or a comma-join instead.
- Fixing the and-rate by adding trailing "which" clauses creates the trailing-explanatory-clause fail from Rules 5 and 8.
- Adding dialogue drops sentences-per-paragraph and raises the under-10-word count. Both are structural results of adding dialogue, not something to fix by trimming narration.
- Folding dialogue into narration to fix the paragraph numbers drops the quoted-word share.
- Every corrective cut of a trailing clause or filler "and" drains the 20-35 word band and lowers the mean; rebuild that band with subordinate clauses that carry real information, never with restored filler.
- Corrections can over-apply: lengthening every short sentence in one chapter flattened its CV to 56% and pushed the mode above the median. Lengthen selectively; some short lines are the content, not a defect.

Comma-join instead of a conjunction where possible:
- Weak: "It isn't the kids doing it. The kids are mostly standing next to a parent who is holding a folder."
- Better: "It isn't the kids doing it, they're mostly standing next to a parent who's holding a folder."

Method: do not write to the target profile by feel. A chapter drafted fresh with every rule already in mind still came out at 56% under ten words with CV 98%. Draft, measure, correct the specific numbers that missed, then measure again.

Known legitimate misses, not to be corrected by damaging the prose: a heavy-dialogue chapter may not reach 35-45% under-10 or 2.9 sentences per paragraph; a chapter centered on one character alone might not reach 30% quoted words.

## 11. Structure

Instruction: apply these four structural rules to any scene or timeline content, including material summarized on a character sheet.

- Chronological. Do not reorder events for effect.
- Cause must be visible on the page. If a character changes behavior (the source example: Chloe stops calling home on night 4), the reader must have already seen the nights before and what changed to cause it.
- Timeline consistency. Check day counts, ages, and who has read or heard what, and when. Caught errors in the source: a father reading a letter "20 minutes ago" that had arrived 30 seconds earlier; 26 days stated on two dates that were only two days apart.
- Mystery lives in characters' mouths, never in narration. Parents may be correctly alarmed in dialogue; the narrator never nudges the reader toward how to feel about an ambiguous fact.

A related rule on dialogue: do not over-explain there either. The source cuts a character who states the book's thesis outright ("either that's what a school is and everybody else isn't doing it, or it's something else"). Concrete facts land harder than a stated thesis: "Four hours away, Dave. And free. And they wrote to her."

## 12. Failure modes, ranked by damage

1. Padding to hit word count: inventing subplots, arithmetic filler, characters reasoning at length about nothing. The tell is a passage that does not parse on a plain read-through.
2. Putting the author's framing in a character's mouth. An eight-year-old at a curb does not have a theory of the admissions process. A mother does not compliment a receptionist's privacy policy at her own dinner table. Both are the narrator wearing a character as a costume.
3. Restaging a beat a previous chapter already did. Check the chapter before the one being written for the same beat.
4. Softening a recurring character. The source example: a librarian is friendly and still enforces the four-books rule in chapters 1, 3, and 7. Turning her into a generically warm adult who only says encouraging things breaks the character.
5. Losing established facts between drafts. Keep the canon list open while writing.

Ranks 1 and 2 are called out as the most damaging and are the two to check first on any new material, including a reference sheet: does it invent content to fill space, and does it put narrator-level analysis into a character's dialogue or thought.

## 13. Pass/fail checklists

The two guides give two different checklists. Both are reproduced in full since they do not fully overlap; run both.

### Guide 1 checklist

- Every scene is something Chloe can see or hear
- No other character's thoughts appear
- No sentence points forward in time
- Zero invented similes; zero abstract nouns handled as objects
- No em dashes anywhere, narration and dialogue alike
- Grep the last clause of every sentence: does it explain rather than add
- Ands per 1,000 words is 34-42
- Spoken sentence mean is 8-10 words; narration mean is 14-17
- Under-10 band is 30-36%; over-35 band is 4-6%
- Mode < median < mean
- Words in quotes is about 30%
- Length is 2,500 words or more
- Nothing in the chapter was invented to reach that length
- Canon list checked

### Guide 2 checklist

1. Any sentence with mirrored halves. Fail.
2. Any clause explaining the clause before it. Fail.
3. Any "which is the/what/how..." evaluation. Fail.
4. Any narrator superlative or ranking. Fail.
5. Any interiority of a non-POV character. Fail.
6. Any "because Y" where Y is obvious. Fail.
7. "and" above 3.5% of all words. Fail.
8. "which" above 0.4% of all words. Fail.
9. More than 3 section breaks. Fail.
10. Same frame verb used 3 or more times in a passage. Fail.
11. Does Chloe explain her own behavior to the reader. Fail.
12. Is she last or worst at everything in the chapter. Fail.
13. Mean words per sentence under 11: check whether dialogue density justifies it.
14. Quoted share under 20%: check whether the chapter is legitimately interior.

Note: item 7 and item 8 of Guide 2's own checklist use different thresholds (3.5% for "and", 0.4% for "which") than the numbers stated in Guide 2's own prose target section (2.5-3.0% for "and", 0.2-0.3% for "which"). Both are Guide 2's own numbers; the checklist is the more permissive of the two internally.

## 14. Where the two guides disagree

Listed here so an agent does not have to reconcile them alone.

- **"And" rate.** Guide 1 targets 34-42 uses of "and" per 1,000 words, which converts to about 3.4-4.2% of all words, with "above 50 per 1,000" (5.0%) called too many. Guide 2's prose target is about 2.5-3.0% of all words, tighter than Guide 1's target and not overlapping with it. Guide 2's own checklist is more permissive, allowing up to 3.5%, which does partially overlap Guide 1's floor of 3.4%. Treat 3.0-3.5% as the safest zone if a single number is needed.
- **Under-10-word sentence share.** Guide 1: baseline 30%, working range 30-36%. Guide 2: target 35-45%. These barely overlap, only at the 35-36% edge.
- **Mean words per sentence.** Guide 1: baseline 17.3, working range 15-17. Guide 2: target 11-18, a much wider band that would accept numbers Guide 1's working range would reject.
- **Over-35-word sentence share.** Guide 1: baseline 8%, working range 4-6%. Guide 2: target 5% or under. Close, but Guide 1's baseline figure exceeds Guide 2's ceiling.
- **Sentence-length CV.** Guide 1: baseline about 72%, working range 65-78%. Guide 2: target 68-72%, a narrower band nested inside Guide 1's range rather than contradicting it. **Both are superseded: the ceiling is now 100%.** See "Why the CV ceiling moved" below.
- **"Which" rate.** Within Guide 2 alone, the prose section targets 0.2-0.3% but the checklist and its scoring script allow up to 0.4%. Not a cross-guide conflict, but an internal inconsistency worth flagging.
- **Paragraph CV.** Not a true disagreement: Guide 1's "current drafts" figure of 66-80% is a status reading, not a target, and Guide 1's own baseline target of 101-107% matches Guide 2's target of 100% or higher.

Everything outside these numeric bands (POV and tense, register, reification, similes, the trailing clause, comma joins for speech and body, the automatic-fail construction list, structure, and the ranked failure modes) is consistent between the two guides. Guide 2's section 1 states the same core rule as Guide 1's section 2 in different words: figurative language belongs in mouths and heads, narration reports.

## Why the CV ceiling moved

Sentence-length CV is the standard deviation of sentence lengths over the mean.
Both guides put the ceiling near 72-78%. Measured against 23 books, only three
fall inside 65-78%; the corpus runs 64.3 to 100.6 with a median of 85.3. The old
ceiling ruled out four books in five.

What the number turns out to measure is worth knowing before anyone chases it.
Across the whole 1-45 word range this book and Tom Sawyer, at CV 100.6, have
almost the same distribution, and Tom Sawyer is the more short-sentenced of the
two: 29.7% of its sentences are under six words against this book's 28.2%. The
entire gap is in the tail.

| | 1-5 | 6-10 | 11-15 | 16-20 | 21-30 | 31-45 | 46+ | longest |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| this book, CV 79.5 | 28.2 | 24.1 | 16.1 | 11.2 | 12.9 | 6.7 | 0.9 | 71 |
| Peter Pan, CV 82.5 | 23.7 | 23.3 | 16.5 | 12.2 | 13.9 | 7.8 | 2.6 | 104 |
| Tom Sawyer, CV 100.6 | 29.7 | 25.3 | 14.9 | 9.5 | 11.0 | 6.1 | 3.6 | 174 |
| Winesburg, CV 64.3 | 10.8 | 21.2 | 19.8 | 17.6 | 19.6 | 8.9 | 2.0 | 133 |

Remove Tom Sawyer's 35 sentences longer than 71 words, 0.65% of the book, and
its CV drops from 100.6 to 90.6. Remove Peter Pan's eleven, and 82.5 becomes
79.3, below this book. Ten sentences of 110 words added to this manuscript would
take it from 79.5 to 84.0.

So CV is not a measure of rhythmic variety in any sense a reader experiences. It
is close to a measure of whether the book ever writes an enormous sentence. Note
that Winesburg, the most adult book in the corpus, has the lowest CV of the 23:
its sentences are long and consistently long. A high CV is permitted now, not
required, and it is not a target to optimise toward. What it stops being is a
reason to refuse a sentence that wants to run.
