# Prose Rules

This document distills reader feedback on the manuscript into instructions a writer can apply directly. Each rule states what to do, shows the shortest real example of the problem from the feedback, and gives a concrete replacement. Use it to write and rewrite character reference sheets, the same failures that weaken the novel's prose will weaken a reference sheet's voice notes and sample dialogue if they creep in here too.

---

## Rule 1: Banned Constructions, Assert Nothing, Show the Substance

Never write a sentence whose entire content is a verdict on correctness or ranking. A verdict tells the reader what conclusion to reach; it supplies no evidence for reaching it. Writing that does the work states the fact, the reason, or the measurement, and lets the verdict follow from that on its own.

**Banned family, do not write any of these, or a close variant:**
"that is correct" / "that is right" / "that's wrong" / "I was wrong" / "you're right" / "that is the best" / "that is the worst" / "that is a better form" / "the correct answer is" / "the right way to do this" / "this is the best approach" / "this is worse than."

**Recognize the pattern even when the words change:** any sentence whose job is to rank or validate something instead of describing it. "That's the strongest version" is the same failure as "that is the best", a label standing in for a reason.

**Do instead:** name the specific thing that makes the claim true. Replace "that is the best archer" with "she has the tightest grouping at seventy meters of anyone in her year." Replace "I was wrong about the timeline" with "the timeline assumed a four-week camp; it ran six." The reader reconstructs the verdict from the fact themselves, which is a stronger position than being handed one.

## Rule 2: Negative Framing and Minimisation, State What a Thing Is

Do not define something by what it is not, and do not undercut a statement in the breath that makes it. Writing that spends its force denying a reading is weaker than writing that commits to the reading it wants.

**Example (from the material being rewritten):** "an absence of comparison data, never as modesty", the sentence spends itself ruling out an interpretation ("never as modesty") rather than saying plainly what the absence is.

**Do instead:** say what is true, directly. Rewrite that fragment as something like: "the missing comparison data is a gap in the record, not a claim about her standing." Or simpler still, cut the denial entirely and state the positive fact the sentence was protecting: "there is no comparison data for this period." The denial ("never as X") can usually be deleted outright once the positive statement is doing its job.

**Watch for the pattern in your own drafts:** any sentence built as "not A, but B" where A was never asserted by anyone in the scene. If nobody claimed modesty, there is nothing to deny, and the denial is only smuggling in the interpretation it claims to rule out.

---

## Rule 3: The "Nobody" Construction, Don't Normalise by Reporting an Absent Reaction

A sentence that reports nobody's reaction to an event is not evidence the event is ordinary. It is a claim standing in for evidence, and repeated often enough it trains the reader to accept "nobody commented" as proof of normalcy on command.

**Example:** "Nobody turns around."

**Do instead:** either cut the line and let the silence live in the scene's actual physical detail (what people are doing instead of reacting), or give one specific character an actual small reaction so the reader is judging a response rather than an absence. If the device is meant to establish that a whole institution is unshockable, say so once, through a character, rather than reasserting it with every scene.

---

## Rule 4: Repetition as Emphasis, Say It Once and Let Placement Do the Work

Restating a point to make sure it landed tells the reader the first version wasn't trusted to land. A strong sentence at the end of a paragraph, followed by white space, carries more weight than the same sentence said twice.

**Example:** "Usually pretty good." / "Usually pretty good.", one character says it, a second repeats it back verbatim, with no new information added by the repeat.

**Do instead:** cut the second instance, or make it different enough to earn its place, a character mishearing it, repeating it in a new context, or reacting to it for the first time. Blocks of technical or procedural text reproduced verbatim across chapters (a scoring rubric given twice, word for word) should appear once; a later reference can be a single line acknowledging the reader already has it.

---

## Rule 5: Dialogue That States the Theme, Cut the Character's Gloss on Their Own Image

When a character explains what a scene's central image means, the explanation replaces the reader's own arrival at the meaning with a delivered conclusion. A careful reader gets there faster without the gloss, and trusts the writing more for not needing it.

**Example:** "That's my whole thing," Chloe says, spoken immediately after an image that already carried the point on its own.

**Do instead:** end the scene one beat earlier, on the concrete image or action, and cut the line that names its significance. If a closing line is doing the job of a thesis statement for the whole book, that is the clearest sign it should go.

---

## Rule 6: Telling After Showing, Don't Explain the Image You Just Rendered

A moment rendered well and then explained loses the explanation's audience twice over: once because the image already did the work, and once because naming it flags that the writer doubted it worked.

**Example:** "She doesn't say that part out loud.", appearing directly after a moment where the dialogue already made clear she hadn't said it.

**Do instead:** trust the image. If the scene shows restraint, showing it is enough; narrating the restraint on top of showing it is redundant. Cut the sentence that explains the sentence before it.

---

## Rule 7: Bury Scale Inside a Fast List, Deliberately

Formatting extraordinary material as a rapid list, while giving ordinary material full scene treatment, teaches a reader to skim exactly the content that would prompt scrutiny. That is the intended effect. A skimming reader should miss the details. The list stays complete, so a reader who slows down finds every item, and the delivery speed keeps most readers moving past it.

**Example:** "Then the bridge... Then the pool... Then dividing fractions... Then Spanish...", a month of curriculum delivered as one breathless inventory, followed by five pages of scene-time on a friendship.

**Do:** give the full list. Keep the pace fast enough that the items arrive as texture rather than as evidence. Give the emotionally legible material the scene-time, because that is where the reader's attention is meant to go. The scale is technically present and functionally hidden, and both halves of that are the design.

**The one thing to watch:** the list has to survive a slow reading. A reader who stops and counts should find the arithmetic holds.

---

## Rule 8: Name the Feat Once When Later Chapters Will Refer Back To It

A character stating an extreme fact in bare summary hands the reader a conclusion the scene was building toward, and invites them to stop and check whether it is plausible. That cost is worth paying when the book needs the fact on the record.

If a later chapter treats a capability as established, an earlier chapter has to have said it plainly. Implying it and then referring back to it as though it had been stated leaves the reader with nothing to point at.

**Example:** "They are shooting bullets out of the air."

**Do:** say it once, in the plainest available form, at the point where it first becomes true. Then never explain it again. Everywhere after that, describe the lane, the sound, the miss rate, the setup, and let the mechanism carry it.

**What to avoid:** the same fact restated in summary form a second and third time. One plain statement is a record. Three is the narrator making sure.

---

## Rule 9: Undercut the Achievement in the Same Breath

Pairing a striking fact with an immediate qualifier redirects a reader's attention to the qualifier before the fact registers. Use it. The book is written from inside a perception where twenty-four seconds is a middling number, and a reader who feels the deflection is feeling what the character feels.

**Example:** "she can hold twenty-four seconds in the 10v1, but she is still only ranked 30 out of 90."

**Do:** keep the two facts joined. Put the number and the rank in the same sentence and let the rank win. The reader should come away thinking twenty-four seconds is unremarkable, because that is what she thinks.

**Do not** give an achievement its own scene with the physical cost on the page and the ranking somewhere else. Separated, the reader weighs each fact independently and works out what the number means, which is what the perception depends on them failing to do.

---

## Rule 10: Put the Comedy Straight After the Violence

A joke placed directly against a violent moment controls how long a reader dwells there. That is the purpose. The reader should be moved along before the consequences settle.

**Example:** "'That's a C,' Sam says, walking back with his hands up", closing a scene in which four children have drugged and restrained four armed adults.

**Do:** when there is a joke, let the deflection arrive on the next line, in a character's voice, before any beat of stillness. The characters are not sitting with it, and the reader travels at their speed.

**This rule is about placement, not obligation.** It offers a tool and says where the tool goes. A violent scene that ends without any comedy is not breaking this rule; nothing here requires a joke. The violation is a joke that arrives late, after the scene has been allowed to settle, or one that never lands in a character's mouth at all.

**What to watch:** the joke has to belong to the character rather than to the narrator. Sam grading the operation is Sam. A wry narrating aside doing the same work is the author.

---

## Rule 11: Sentence Rhythm, Break the Subject-Verb-Object Drumbeat

A run of short declarative sentences that all share the same shape produces a flat, reportorial rhythm. Held for a page, it reads as a witness statement. Held for a whole manuscript, the reader's ear goes numb and can predict a sentence's shape before it arrives.

**Example:** "He sets the clock. Then the middle changes. Then it goes round."

**Do instead:** combine short sentences using subordination, vary sentence openings (a prepositional phrase, a participle, the object, instead of always the subject pronoun), and let some sentences run long enough to hold more than one fact at a time.

---

## Rule 12: Subordinate Instead of Chaining With "And" / "Then"

Coordinating everything with "and" or "then" gives every clause equal grammatical weight, which means the prose cannot distinguish a trivial action from a consequential one, they sit side by side in the same flat chain.

**Example:** "She does the first one, and then the second one, which is 8 − 3, and partway through the second one she gets how it works."

**Do instead:** subordinate one clause to another so the sentence has a hierarchy. "After the first one, she does the second, which is 8 minus 3, and partway through it, she sees how it works." Same information, but one clause now depends on another instead of sitting next to it.

---

## Rule 13: Let Register Escalate With the Moment, Earn Flatness by Contrast

A single sentence-shape used for joy, grief, boredom, and terror alike gives the reader no way to feel the difference between a small moment and a catastrophic one. Flatness as a deliberate voice is legitimate; flatness that never breaks is not a choice the reader can perceive as one.

**Example (the finding, not a single line):** the same declarative rhythm and the same small physical gesture (a fork put down, a hand going flat on a table) render a minor disappointment and a screaming breakdown identically.

**Do instead:** let three sentences run against the established rhythm, longer, more specific, more alive, so that when the fourth flat sentence arrives, it lands like a hammer. Break the sentence shape for genuine distress: shorter, harder, fragmented, so the prose does at the sentence level what the character cannot do at the scene level.

---

## Rule 14: Paragraph Breaks, Keep One Beat in One Paragraph

An action and the dialogue it leads into are a single unit. Splitting them into separate paragraphs treats a continuous beat as two isolated events and gives both artificial, equal weight.

**Example:** "'We'll get you a bigger one.' / 'It's fine.'", set as its own paragraph, disconnected from the rest of the same exchange that follows immediately after.

**Do instead:** merge paragraphs that share one continuous action, speaker, or moment. A new paragraph should mark an actual shift, in time, place, speaker, or focus, never just a new sentence that happens to be gesturing at the same beat.

---

## Rule 15: Talking Heads, Anchor Dialogue in Physical Space

Long runs of dialogue with no intervening action leave the reader without a room, without bodies, without any sense of where anyone is standing. The exchange reads like a transcript rather than a scene happening somewhere.

**Example:** "'We drove,' a man says. / 'Nobody put theirs on the bus.' / 'Somebody must have.'", six more lines follow with no anchor beyond two dialogue tags.

**Do instead:** drop a physical detail in every three or four lines, a hand near a coffee machine, weight shifting, a glance, enough to place bodies in the scene without slowing the exchange down.

---

## Rule 16: Homogenised Dialogue, Give Each Character a Verbal Signature

When every character speaks in the same short, declarative, unhedged register, the reader loses the ability to track who is speaking without a tag, and the characters become interchangeable.

**Example:** "'How do you know if she doesn't say it?' (Mom) / 'Because of what she does with the locker key.' (Chloe)", mother and child are syntactically indistinguishable; either line could be assigned to the other speaker.

**Do instead:** give each character one or two consistent habits, over-qualifying, dropping subjects, using corrective "actually," speaking in conditionals, trailing off, and hold those habits consistently, especially in high-tension scenes, where dialogue tends to flatten into one voice regardless of who's speaking.

---

## Rule 17: Dialogue Attributed to Furniture, Not People

Having a character speak "to the wall" or "to the papers" shows avoidance effectively the first few times. Used as a default mode of speech, it stops registering as avoidance and becomes a tic, by the tenth instance, nobody in the scene appears to be speaking to another person at all.

**Example:** "Her mom says it to the papers."

**Do instead:** use the device sparingly, for the specific moments where avoidance is the point. Everywhere else, let people speak to people, with eye contact or its absence conveyed through a beat rather than through the object a voice is aimed at.

---

## Rule 18: Dialogue Tag Clutter, Let Action Beats Carry Tone

Loading a dialogue tag with a prepositional explanation of tone or circumstance slows the exchange and tells the reader something the dialogue, or a separate action beat, should already be showing.

**Example:** "her dad says, standing in the kitchen with his keys already in his hand"

**Do instead:** split the tag. Give the physical detail its own sentence, then attribute the dialogue plainly: "Her father stands in the kitchen, keys in hand. 'Well, that's the class.'"

---

## Rule 19: The Said-Bookism Overcorrection, Vary Attribution in Long Exchanges

Using only "says" as a dialogue tag is sound advice for a short back-and-forth. Applied rigidly to a longer exchange, it strips out tone and physicality, and the reader has to count line breaks to track who's speaking.

**Example:** an unbroken four-line exchange, "It's a stupid question because it's got an answer... / Then you'll get a C and he'll be right to give you one. / Thanks very much, that's extremely helpful. / 'Devil's advocate,' Sam says, through most of a bread roll.", with only one tag across four speaker turns.

**Do instead:** in exchanges longer than two lines, attribute enough that a reader always knows who is speaking and roughly how, through an action beat, a fragment of physicality, or an adverb, not by defaulting to invisible "said" for the entire passage.

---

## Rule 20: Adjective-as-Adverb Dialogue Tags ("She Says It Tired")

A construction like "she says it tired" is effective the first few times and becomes a visible crutch by the fortieth, a way of avoiding a fuller description of how a line was actually delivered.

**Example:** "She says it tired."

**Do instead:** use it rarely, or replace it with a standard adverb ("she said, tiredly") or, stronger, a physical detail that shows the tiredness instead of labeling it.

---

## Rule 21: The Predictable Correction Pattern, Vary the Beat You've Trained the Reader to Expect

A recurring scene shape, a character states something with confidence, an authority figure says some version of "no, actually," then explains the real mechanism, is a serviceable device once or twice. Repeated across many different authority figures, the reader recognizes the setup and knows the beat before the dialogue arrives.

**Example (the pattern, drawn from many instances across the manuscript):** a librarian, a psychologist, and a string of teachers each deliver the identical "confident statement, correction, explanation" beat.

**Do instead:** vary it. Let a scene occur where the character is right and the authority is wrong, or where nobody corrects the mistake and that absence itself matters, either breaks the pattern instead of confirming it again.

---

## Rule 22: Exposition as Dialogue, With One Standing Exception

Two characters explaining mechanics to each other so the reader receives the information makes the dialogue sound like narration wearing a voice.

**Example:** "Because you can't track it, and shooting one down after it is in the air is not something you are going to manage."

**Do:** when both speakers already know the thing, let them talk like people who know it, in fragments and shorthand, and move the mechanics into action and description.

**The exception, which covers a great deal of this book:** these people are at a school. A teacher explaining something to a class is a teacher doing their job, and a student correcting another student who has it wrong is the school's whole method on the page. Neither is exposition wearing a costume. The test is whether the speaker knows something the listener does not. If they do, let them say it. If both already know it, cut it.

## Rule 23: Emotional Detachment, Render the Physical Correlate, Not the Label

**Read `THEY_ARE_CHILDREN.md` before applying this rule.** Together with rules 24, 26 and 6 it closed every route into the viewpoint character's inner life, and outside readers of chapter 1 concluded she was not human. This rule bans a cliché label. It does not license a scene in which nothing is felt, and it does not license analysis standing in for the feeling. The feeling arrives first, in the body or the behaviour; working the moment out afterwards is how a clever child copes and belongs after it, not instead of it.

Naming a feeling with a physiological cliché signals emotion without letting the reader experience it. "Heart going," "stomach drops," and "cold hands" could describe any anxious moment for any character; they carry no specificity.

**Example:** "Chloe sits there with her heart going."

**Do instead:** choose one specific, physical, and slightly unexpected detail rooted in the character and the moment, something a reader hasn't seen in every other anxious scene in the book.

---

## Rule 24: Filtering, Cut "She Sees / Hears / Notices / Knows"

Routing every observation through a verb of perception puts a pane of glass between the reader and the thing being perceived. The reader doesn't need to be told a character noticed something; they need the thing itself, rendered directly.

**Example:** "Chloe hears her switch the phone to her other ear."

**Do instead:** remove the filter and render the stimulus on its own: "The phone clicks as her mother switches it to her other ear."

---

## Rule 25: Vague Abstractions, Replace "Something," "Nothing," "The Whole Thing"

These words function as emotional shorthand. Used often, every internal beat flattens into the same gray placeholder instead of a specific, nameable thing.

**Example:** "there is nothing left in the worksheet to find out"

**Do instead:** replace the abstraction with a concrete image or action that stands in for the feeling, a physical gesture, a specific object, a precise consequence, rather than a word that could describe almost anything.

---

## Rule 26: Thought Reported vs. Thought Rendered

Stating that a character thought about something is a summary of thinking, not an instance of it. The reader is told the fact of cognition without its texture.

**Example:** "She thought about that one for a while."

**Do instead:** render the thought happening, its own rhythm, its own interruptions, a dash where the thought catches on something, so the reader is inside the reasoning rather than being told it occurred.

---

## Rule 27: Crutch Phrases That Have Stopped Meaning Anything

Certain phrases recur so often across a manuscript that they stop functioning as language and start functioning as filler. Individually harmless, they become wallpaper in volume.

**Examples:** "the whole thing," "for a second" / "for a while" as universal duration markers regardless of what kind of moment is being timed, "which she has done X times already," "and that's the end of it" as a reflexive scene-closer, "the thing where" / "the part where" as a placeholder for behavior that should be shown instead of labeled.

**Do instead:** replace each instance with something specific to that moment, a real duration, a real image, a rendered action, and delete the phrase where it isn't doing any work at all.

---

## Rule 28: Hedge Where the Character Could Not Know, Cut Filler Everywhere Else

Two different things wear the same clothes here.

A hedge attached to an estimate a person is actually making is more accurate than a bare number. "About four feet away" is what someone glancing across a room can report. "Four feet away" claims a measurement nobody took, and a reader who has ever estimated a distance feels the difference. Keep those hedges. They are precision about the limits of the observation.

**Cut the filler:** "really", "just", "kind of", "sort of", "actually", "somewhat", "a bit", wherever they attach to nothing and soften a claim the narrator is entitled to make. These advertise uncertainty that does not exist.

**The test:** would the character be able to know the exact version? If not, the hedge is the honest number. If yes, the hedge is padding.

---

## Rule 29: The "It Was" Construction

"It was" plus an adjective is a weak-verb construction that drains energy from a sentence and tells the reader how to feel rather than creating the feeling directly.

**Example:** "It was so quiet she could hear him breathe."

**Do instead:** cut the "it was" and let the description stand on its own: "So quiet she could hear him breathe."

---

## Rule 30: Inhabit the Subject, and Give the Reader One Line of It

Dropping into an academic register to define a field reads as imported, and the prose starts to sound like a syllabus rather than a person's encounter with the material.

**Example of going too far:** "Formal epistemology is the room four other subjects have been standing outside."

**Do:** show the accumulated experience through concrete moments and let the reader reach the larger idea through them.

**And give them a line.** A student saying one sentence of actual subject content is characterising and grounds the scene in something real. A student delivering a paragraph is the syllabus again. One line of the thing itself, then back to the person.

---

## Rule 31: Match Syntax to the Age, With the Ability Built In

If sentence structure never changes across a manuscript, the character's mind reads as static while the plot insists years have passed. A six-year-old and a sixteen-year-old should not build a sentence the same way.

**Example:** "You can end up somewhere by accident and still be there.", delivered by a six-year-old in the same subordinate-clause structure the same character uses at sixteen.

**Do:** give the younger version shorter clauses, less subordination, more repeated structure, and let the complexity grow with the years.

**The calibration these characters need:** they are children and they should sound like children, and a seven-year-old at 160 does not think or talk like a seven-year-old at 100. The difference shows in what she notices, how many steps she holds at once, and how fast she gets to the point, rather than in vocabulary or clause depth. Keep the grammar age-appropriate. Let the content be what marks her out. A child saying a simple sentence about something no other child in the room has noticed is the register.

---

## Rule 32: Ration a Character's Verbal Tic to the Moments It Characterises

A distinctive habit, counting things, naming exact durations, repeating a phrase, does real characterization work when it appears at moments tied to what it reveals (anxiety, control, dissociation). Used by the narration as a reflexive default for every scene regardless of viewpoint or stakes, it stops characterizing anyone and reads as authorial autopilot.

**Example:** "eleven minutes," "twenty-eight blanks," and "the phone call took ninety seconds" work early as characterization; by the point the narration itself says "the whole dinner takes nine minutes" regardless of whose scene it is, the tic has become a default rather than a signal.

**Do instead:** reserve the device for moments where it is doing something specific for that character, and let ordinary scene-setting use ordinary description the rest of the time.

---

## Rule 33: Keep Narrative Tense Consistent Unless the Break Is Signalled

An unexplained shift from past to present tense, sustained across a chapter with no other chapter doing the same thing, reads as an error rather than a choice, even where it might have been intended to mark a rupture in the story.

**Example:** "They're over the fence at twenty past eleven.", present tense, in a manuscript otherwise told consistently in the past, with no signal that the shift is deliberate.

**Do instead:** keep tense consistent throughout. If a deliberate break is wanted for a specific rupture, mark it explicitly, a typographic cue, a line that acknowledges the shift, so the reader receives it as a choice rather than a draft that was never converted back.

---

## Rule 34: Use Past Perfect for Action Before the Narrative Present

When a passage in past tense refers back to something that happened earlier still, simple past collapses the two time frames together and creates temporal confusion about what happened when.

**Example:** "Chloe read a book about Icarus at the library two weeks ago and thought about it for four days afterward."

**Do instead:** "Chloe had read a book about Icarus two weeks ago and had thought about it for four days afterward", past perfect signals clearly that this happened before the scene's own present moment.

---

## Rule 35: Dialogue Formatting and Typography

Mechanical errors in how dialogue is punctuated and tagged are small individually and cumulative in effect, each one interrupts a reader who was otherwise moving smoothly through a line.

**Examples:**
- Dangling fragments used as tags: `"I haven't said anything to you at all." smirking.`, not attached to a sentence.
- Comma splices before dialogue in place of a period or proper attribution: `Chloe looks confused for a moment, "I don't know."`
- Inconsistent numeral formatting within a single house style: "eleven," "sixty," "eighty percent" spelled out throughout, but "8%" and "at 4" appearing as numerals in a couple of spots.
- Stray capitalization following a comma: `"Nah, They have moved everything"`.

**Do instead:**
- Attach every tag to a complete sentence: "she said, smirking," not a bare participle.
- End the narrative clause before the quotation, or use a proper tag: "Chloe looked confused for a moment. 'I don't know.'"
- Pick one numeral convention (spelled out or numerals) and hold it everywhere.
- Lowercase after a comma unless the word is a proper noun or starts a genuine new sentence.

---

## Note for agents rewriting character material

A few techniques in the source material are working as intended and should not be "fixed" away: hesitant, self-aware sentences that arrive at their point in a final clause; flat, unsentimental assessments that refuse to beg for an emotional reaction; and short sentences held back until the end of a paragraph, arriving after real setup. The goal of every rule above is not uniform ornamentation, it's variation, precision, and trust in the reader, applied against a prose style that is otherwise sound. Don't sand a working flat line down to match a broken one; sand the broken ones to match the standard the working lines already meet.
