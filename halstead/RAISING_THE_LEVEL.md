# How the two best chapters do it

The book measures at the 22nd percentile for reading maturity against 23 published
books, and readers say it reads like a children's book. But two chapters already
score at the 74th and 67th: `08_the_asking.md` and `10_april.md`. Same narrator,
same rules, same author. Whatever they are doing is available to the rest of the
book, and it is not a different voice.

Two agents read them closely, separately, and arrived at the same sentence:

> **The flat chapters are flat and simple. These two are flat and dense.**
>
> One fact, one clause, one sentence, versus several facts, several clauses, one
> sentence. The register is identical. Only the packing changes.

That is the whole finding. Flat is not the same as short, and complexity does not
mean warming the voice up.

## The numbers that separate them

| measure | ch8 | ch10 | book average | benchmark |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| words per sentence | 15.9 | 15.0 | 12.5 | 14.6 |
| sentences with a subordinate clause % | 27.0 | 28.4 | 18.3 | 22.2 |
| sentences with a relative clause % | 23.6 | 23.0 | 18.2 | 22.8 |
| sentences in a run of 3+ short ones % | 1.7 | 6.4 | 18.8 | 15.1 |
| commas per sentence | 1.2 | 0.8 | 0.7 | 1.2 |

## The five mechanisms

**1. Fronted participial and prepositional openers, instead of subject-first.**
"Coming down the hall at a full run, he hits her." "Of the ninety who were at
camp, twenty-some came back." The flat failure mode is every sentence opening on
its subject, same shape, same length. This is rule 11 satisfied structurally
rather than by ornament.

**2. Subordination absorbs what the flat chapters chain with "and".**
Where a flat chapter writes "She asks a lot and she asks it carefully, and she
asks who ran it", chapter 8 nests the whole list under one governing verb:
"working down through who ran it, and how they found you, and whether Chloe
stayed overnight". One participle turns a coordinate chain into a sentence with a
spine. Rules 6 and 12 both ask for this already.

**3. Stacked indirect questions and cause-chains in one sentence.**
Chapter 8 puts six facts in one sentence with repeated "whether", eight commas,
and no change of register: still a flat inventory. Chapter 10 chains cause to
consequence rather than splitting it: "because the library is a four-minute walk
from her room and it is open at eleven at night, so there is no reason to carry
more than she can carry in one hand." A flat chapter writes that as three
sentences and earns three full stops instead of two commas.

**4. Short sentences are rationed, not scattered.**
Chapter 8's run-of-three figure is 1.7% against a book average of 18.8%, but it
does not have far fewer short sentences overall. It spends them **one at a time**,
as isolated single-sentence paragraphs after real setup: "Then she waits." "She
stops asking in January." The flat chapters produce a similar short-sentence
percentage by stringing three together. Same ingredient, different distribution,
and the distribution is what a reader feels.

**5. The trailing clause carries a fact, not a verdict.**
STYLE_RULES section 5 bans the trailing explanatory clause and it is the most-cited
defect in this manuscript. These chapters keep their comma rate anyway, because
what trails adds information: "for which her mom takes the whole afternoon off
work without saying anything about taking it" tells you something new about Meg.
It does not tell you what to think about the afternoon. That distinction is the
difference between a high comma count and an automatic fail.

## Why this does not fight the prose rules

An earlier pass found the rules and the maturity target pulling against each
other, because cutting crutch phrases and trailing clauses removes subordinate
clauses and the grade fell. These two chapters show the resolution. Emotional
beats convert to **located physical action** — "Her mother puts her forehead on
the steering wheel and stays there" — and a physical action almost always wants a
subordinate or relative clause to place it in time or space. So rule 23, which
asks for the physical correlate instead of the named feeling, produces subordination
as a side effect. The register discipline and the clause-rate numbers pull the same
way once the complexity comes from staging rather than from commentary.

## What not to do

- **Do not pad.** Filler raises words-per-sentence and makes the prose worse. Every
  gain has to come from a real combination or a real subordination.
- **Do not reach for a fancier word.** Lexical diversity improves by choosing a
  different ordinary word, not a longer one.
- **Do not lengthen dialogue that a character sheet does not license.** Each sheet
  now has a "When they talk at length" section naming the circumstance and its
  cost. Half the spoken lines in this book run to three words or fewer, and some
  of that is character rather than defect.
- **Do not sand down a short sentence that is doing work.** Rule 11 wants variety,
  not uniform length. A short sentence after three long ones is a device.
- **Do not trail an explanatory clause to get the comma.** Front-load it instead.
  Same subordination, same comma, no rule violation.
