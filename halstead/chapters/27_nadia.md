## Chapter Twenty-Seven: Nadia

*May 2024*

Somebody runs a scam through Nadia's company in the spring.

The company is eleven months old and four people besides her. It is known inside three counties, which is as far as word travels when every employer on it was signed in person, in a car, on Saturdays.

What the thing does is take the resume out. A user spends one evening on short tests, the profile builds itself out of work she can put in front of somebody, and after that the thing fills the forms and sends them out for her, to the employers on the site and to whichever outside boards are still accepting submissions from it that month.

It runs out of three rooms over a laundromat, a hundred yards up the same street as her parents' shop. The heat off the dryers comes through the floor from ten in the morning, so the windows stay open into November and the traffic noise is a fact of the job. There is one bathroom and it belongs to the laundromat. The key is on a paint stirrer behind the change machine.

The building gave her two working outlets a room. There is a run of surge strips down the baseboard now, taped flat with duct tape, and an orange extension cord stapled up over the door frame between the two front rooms. She did the tape and the staples herself on a Sunday in February and the run has not moved since.

The kitchen is a folding table with a kettle on it and a jar of instant coffee Bev brought in October. Nobody has opened the jar. Nadia buys hers at the gas station on the corner twice a day and rinses the same cup in the laundromat sink.

In April she paid ninety dollars for the company name in white vinyl and put it on the street window from the outside, standing on the second step of a ladder on a Sunday morning. The second word went on half an inch low. Lifting it again would have torn it, so it is half an inch low.

"Nobody comes up here," Bev said, from the window.

"I come up here," Nadia said.

There is no morning meeting. She says what went live yesterday from wherever she happens to be standing, and it takes under a minute, because there are four people to say it to and three of them were there when it went live.

She's still in the front room at nine on the Tuesday of the second week of May, because the new-employer queue has run long. She reads that queue herself, every name on it, the same way she reads the till tape at her parents' counter before she says hello to anyone. A good week puts four names in front of her. This week has nineteen, and eleven of the nineteen are companies she has never driven to.

She opens the eleven.

The job descriptions are one job description, four paragraphs of it, with the company name swapped at the top and again in the third line and left where it was everywhere else. One of them advertises a role in a city the same posting gives as forty miles from its own address.

All eleven accounts were created between twenty to twelve and twenty past on the same night, inside a single forty-minute window. No genuine employer in three counties has ever come close to that. The hires behind them come to zero, and the number sits in front of her without her going to look for it, because the hire count sits at the top of every employer's row.

The rest of it is in the message logs. Traffic between an employer and a user stays on her own servers until an offer gets logged, a decision she made in June to hold the support calls down. So the eleven had no way to ask for anything except in writing, in her building, in a log she owns and reads.

Nine of them ask for a Social Security number and a bank routing number in the third message, in the same four sentences, comma splice and all, reproduced without a character moved. Three people answered. One of the three gave both numbers on the Sunday, and two thousand four hundred dollars left her checking account on the Monday.

Before she sits down, she deletes the whole batch of accounts from where she's standing, which takes about ninety seconds. Then she takes the three names into the back room and telephones them one at a time, at nine at night, and tells each of them what to do in the morning before she hangs up.

The third call runs twenty minutes, because the woman on the other end wants to explain how it happened and Nadia lets her. The woman apologises four times in the twenty minutes. Twice for taking up the evening, and twice for having answered at all.

"Call the bank at nine and read them that number," Nadia says, at the end of it. "Then call me back and tell me what they said."

She reads the digits out twice, slowly, and writes them on the back of the page before she hangs up. Then she sits in the back room with the phone in her hand for a while with the light off.

The week that follows happens mostly before eight in the morning, before the laundromat opens and the phones start, at the kitchen table with a legal pad and eleven company names written down the side of it in the same small fast hand she keeps the shop's ledger in.

Every one of the eleven typed a genuine state registration number into her employer form. That is the interesting part all by itself, since anybody willing to file with the state is somebody the state now holds paper on.

She spends two mornings on the state's business filings, the same site she used to register her own company. Nine of the eleven were filed inside a single week eight months ago through one registered agent. Eight months old tells her the paperwork has been used before.

She spends a third morning cross-referencing the agent against the county's property records. It comes back a suite above a tyre shop off the county road, four miles from the laundromat. The agent's name is Hanley, and Hanley has thirty-one filings against that one address.

On the fourth morning she calls the tyre shop's front counter.

"Does the office upstairs keep regular hours, or is it just whoever happens to be up there that day?" she asks, in the flat unbothered voice of somebody scheduling a delivery. "I've got a package that needs someone up there to sign for it."

"They're in most days," the man behind the counter says. "Nine to five, four or five of them, give or take."

He answers it the same easy way he'd answer anyone.

None of it moves anything else on her list. Payroll clears on the twenty-second the way it always does. She signs two employers on the Saturday in the middle of it, one of them a machine shop with eleven people in it and a fax number on the door. On the Thursday she takes the difficulty on the test generator from point four five down to point three and lets it rebuild overnight.

She keeps the rest of it to herself. No police, no lawyer, and at the company only that she's out for the afternoon. On the seventh day she closes the shop books at home, tells her parents she'll be back before dinner, and drives out alone. She parks in front of the building where the window can see the car, and leaves the doors unlocked, because she is going to be ten minutes.

The stairs run up the outside of the building, a bare steel case bolted to brick, and she can smell the tyre shop the whole way up. Rubber and hot metal working together in the heat.

The door at the top gives when she tries it.

Four men are inside. Two at a folding table with laptops open, one behind a desk in what's meant to look like an office, one leaning in the doorway to a second room with a coffee going cold in his hand. There is paper on the folding table as well as laptops, printouts with lines highlighted in three colors, and a whiteboard behind the desk still carrying somebody else's job in half-erased marker.

All four look up at a door opening straight into the room with no knock ahead of it.

"Whoa," the nearer one at the folding table says. "Hey."

"You want the tyre place," the man behind the desk says. "That's downstairs."

She stays on her feet just inside the door, hands loose at her sides.

"You put eleven employer accounts on my site on the night of the second and asked nine people for a bank routing number," she says. "Three of them answered. One of them is out two thousand four hundred dollars."

Nobody says anything for a second. The man behind the desk sets down what he was holding.

"Get out," he says.

She doesn't. The one in the doorway comes off the frame, walks around behind her, and pushes the door shut with the flat of his hand. Then he stays against it. She hears the latch and does not turn around.

"How old are you," the man at the folding table says.

"Eighteen."

"Jesus." He looks over at the desk. "She's eighteen."

"Does your father know where you are?"

"No," Nadia says.

The man behind the desk sits back in the chair for the first time since she came in. The one with the coffee has his phone up now. He takes a picture of her, then walks to the window and takes one of the car in the lot, and reads the plate out loud, all seven of it, slowly, twice.

"You're not going to do anything to me," she says. "There are eleven filings with a real agent's name on them, and a shop full of people downstairs who watched me come up."

"It's twenty past five." The man at the window doesn't turn around. "Chuck locks the bay at five. There's nobody downstairs and nobody watched you come up."

She goes on to the next thing on her list.

"I'm not calling the police. A county detective gets eleven form submissions and a Tuesday he already had plans for."

"You can't prove any of it," the man at the folding table says. "None of that is in anybody's name."

Nadia laughs. It is one sound and it is out of her before she has decided on it.

"Nine of you typed a live state registration number into my form," she says. "It's field four. It's required."

The man at the folding table stands up. He is not fast about it and he does not need to be. He comes around the end of the table and stops close enough that she has to tilt her head to keep his face.

"Say that again," he says.

She says it again, exactly, in the same order and at the same speed.

"Nine of the eleven were filed in one week in September through the same registered agent," she says, from where she's standing. "The agent is Hanley. Hanley has thirty-one filings on that address and twenty-six of them are not yours."

Nobody in the room says anything to that.

"I'm going to send the file to the business division at the Secretary of State. Nine time-stamped copies of the same four sentences, and a dollar figure attached to them, which is the only kind of complaint anybody opens. They do not come for you. They pull Hanley."

She lets that sit for exactly as long as it takes.

"Then twenty-six other people find out who cost them their paperwork, and it will not be me who tells them. It will be the notice."

The man in front of her hasn't moved. The one behind the desk is looking past him at her.

"Or none of it goes anywhere," she says. "The eleven are already gone. A twelfth doesn't appear, nothing else of yours comes near my site, and I never drive out here again. That's the whole offer and I'm not improving it."

Nobody says yes. The man behind the desk looks at the one standing over her, and keeps looking at him until he walks back to the folding table and sits down in the chair he got out of.

"Get out of my office," the man behind the desk says. "Second time I've said that."

"That's fine," Nadia says.

The one at the door steps aside for her and says something to the back of her head on the way past, three words with sweetheart on the end of them, and one of the others laughs at it. She's down the steel stairs before either sound has finished. Somebody comes out onto the landing and stands there while she crosses the lot, and does not come down.

She gets the key in on the second try.

She drives back and counts the till her mother has already counted. It comes out four dollars under. She counts it again. Her mother asks where she was, and Nadia says out at a tyre place, which is true, and her mother lets it go. She stays behind the counter until close, which she hasn't done on a Thursday since November.

The hiring changes Nadia's mind.

She has never written a test. She wrote the thing that writes them, in the second and third week of last July, before there was anybody to test. It reads the requirement text off live listings, pulls whatever public documentation and question bank the skill has, and produces a set of short problems out of that. By May it covers eleven hundred skills and she has read maybe forty of them.

She has taken four. Python, Excel, Spanish, and the one on brake systems, which she took to see whether it would catch what her father catches by ear. The Python set ran eleven minutes. She got full marks on all four and wrote the times on the pad.

The first thing is that almost everyone fails.

She makes it easier. The generator has a difficulty figure and she moves it, and the whole eleven hundred rebuild themselves overnight while she is asleep. She moves it again in September. By November it is a version she'd have handed a twelve-year-old at Halstead, and the pass rate is under a third. She takes the November Python set herself and it runs four minutes, and she writes that on the pad too.

She writes every posting herself, the same flat one-paragraph shape she uses for a labor contract: what the job needs, what it pays, and no line about anything else. The two other outfits fishing the same short list of local engineers post in a different language entirely, doing more with fewer hands, a lean team outpacing a bigger one. She reads those the way she reads an estimate from a mechanic still guessing at what's under the hood, and puts up a fifth role the same afternoon.

In February she builds a pass over the submissions so she doesn't have to read them, tuned to put ten in front of her a week. It puts ten in front of her a week. She reads the other sixty as well, at the kitchen table before eight, because the ten it picks are not always the ten she'd have picked and she wants to know how often.

She meant to sit down with twelve people this year. She has sat down with thirty-one, all but one of them herself, in the back room with a folding table between two chairs and a laptop open to whatever the candidate submitted the night before.

She asks them to explain something they built. Most of them do that part fine, because four years of resumes have taught people how to talk about their own work in front of a stranger. Then she asks two questions about it. The first is usually fine.

The second one asks what happens at the edge of the thing they built, past the part they actually tested, and that is where most of them run out of road.

One candidate walks her carefully back through a payment system he'd built at his last job, confident the whole way. "What happens if two requests land in the same millisecond?" she asks.

"That's an edge case," he says.

She writes one line on his sheet and moves to the next person.

Another spends four minutes describing a scheduling tool with real pride in his voice. "Who checks it when the schedule it produces is simply wrong?" she asks.

"That's what the review step is for."

"Name the person."

"Whoever's on it that sprint, I guess," he says, when she asks him a second time, and he can't.

The rare one who answers the second question properly gets a longer interview than the sheet calls for, because she'd rather spend the extra twenty minutes finding out where the edge of that person's knowledge actually sits than end the day guessing.

The four she already has are the four who answered it.

A board starts rate-limiting her submission traffic on a Saturday in March. Tomas catches it off a graph nobody had asked him to watch, rewrites the back-off, puts it out himself, and tells her on the Monday, by which point it has held through a weekend. She asks him why he didn't raise the retry ceiling while he was in there. He says the ceiling was not the thing that was broken. She writes the ceiling herself that night.

Bev drives out on her own on a Saturday in April to four employers Nadia hadn't got to and comes back with two signed and one who wants to see Nadia in person. There is a thirty-day clause in both contracts that Nadia's template does not have.

"Where did the thirty days come from," Nadia says.

"I made it up in the car."

"Keep it." She rolls it into the template that evening and does not mention it again.

Manny finds the generator marking a whole category wrong, six weeks of it, and tells her before he fixes it rather than after. Nadia reads the mail standing up, says fix it, and is on the next thing inside a minute.

A vendor pitches her twice that spring on an applicant-screening tool built around exactly that gap, machine-scored off uploaded resumes, sized for the one role she could then leave unfilled. She takes both calls standing at the desk, says she'll think about it, and hires the fifth person instead before the month is out.
