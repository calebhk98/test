## Chapter Twenty-Three: The First One

*June 2023*

Nobody at Halstead has ever done a graduation, and it shows.


Backstage, before any family has even found a seat, the ninety-one of them stand in a line that gets rearranged twice. Somebody assumes the order should go by height first. Then it gets fixed properly, alphabetical, once Ruth points out that alphabetical order is the one piece of the whole afternoon they actually rehearsed, and they've already gotten it wrong. The gowns come in three sizes for ninety-one different people, so half the line is cinched at the back with a binder clip, which somebody grabbed from the front office at the last minute. Sam is one of them, sleeves stopping three inches short of his wrists. The stairs up to the stage are steeper than anyone accounted for, no handrail screwed into place yet. The fourth or fifth student across catches a heel without quite falling, grabbing hard enough at whoever's handing out the certificates to leave a mark.


They put chairs out on the field, rows of them running well past where the field itself ends. Nobody who ordered them has ever had to guess how many parents, grandparents, and driving-distance cousins ninety-one students might produce between them. Off to one side stands a stage built that week, plywood over scaffolding, still smelling faintly of the primer somebody rolled onto it on Tuesday. Halfway through the ceremony an usher starts quietly folding up the emptiest rows and carrying them off. Whoever placed the order asked for a crowd that never showed, and nobody in the building has done this before, so nobody knew how many a family needs. The programs, printed that same week, are supposed to cover the whole crowd, but they run out before the P's, and the family behind Chloe's spends the second half of the alphabet sharing one with strangers.


The whole of the school below them sits on the grass behind the last row of seats, because there is nowhere else to put eight hundred children. The twelve-year-olds nearest the aisle spend the first twenty minutes kicking a water bottle down the gap between two empty rows, working out how many times it will go end over end before somebody's mother turns around.


The head of school gives a short speech that is mostly logistics: four years, some of them longer than four; ninety-one names; a line of thanks to parents, grandparents, and guardians, delivered in that order like an item list. There's one real sentence at the end, and Chloe doesn't hear it, because Sam has spent the last two minutes trying to make her laugh and finally succeeds on the word "accomplishment." The microphone cuts out twice during it, and the second time whoever's holding it keeps talking for six more words before anyone notices, so half the field gets a graduation speech with a six-word hole in the middle of it. Whoever's running the sound board can be heard for three full seconds after the mic comes back, telling somebody named Carla that this whole thing was supposed to be tested Tuesday, before somebody realizes the levels are still up and cuts it again. Nobody laughs harder at that than the eight hundred kids on the grass, who don't often get to watch an adult at this school sound rattled about anything.


Ruth, three seats down from Chloe, counts the empty chairs still standing in the row ahead of them. "That's forty they'll need to clear before the applause even starts," she says, to nobody who asked. Nobody moves a chair. Sam laughs at the dead microphone before anyone's fixed it, loud enough that two rows turn around, and keeps laughing after everyone else has stopped. The mic still doesn't work.


Then they read out ninety-one names in alphabetical order, and the pacing is wrong from the start. Too fast through the A's, so the first few students are still finding their families in the crowd when the next name gets called. Then too slow through the back half of the alphabet, once whoever's reading catches the mistake and starts trying to correct for it. One name gets skipped clean between the D's and the E's. The girl has to be walked back up after the Z's for her turn, and gets more applause for it than anyone else all afternoon.


When Sam's name gets called, four rows of people who aren't his family stand up anyway, Chloe's mother among them. Eleven years of pickups and drop-offs means she has watched this particular boy grow six inches in a single summer, and she still isn't used to it. When Ruth's name comes, half a row does the same thing for her. Priya's parents, an electrician and a woman who works the front desk at an urgent care clinic, clap for every single name, not just their daughter's, the whole way through.


Her mother cries from the K's onward, a tissue out three names early. Her father keeps one hand on the back of her chair the whole time.

Her mother knows this campus. She's been coming for eleven years. She knows where the good parking is, knows Ruth's mother by name, and knows to bring a jacket because it's colder up here.


She finds Ruth's mother in the crowd before the ceremony even starts, and the two of them fall into the same easy back-and-forth they've had every year for over a decade. A supplier problem on one side, a form the school sent that neither of them has filled out yet on the other. "Is he in yet?" Ruth's mother asks, meaning the brother. "Any day now," Meg says. "He's already looking at dorms." Ruth's mother, who hasn't remembered her own jacket in eleven years, takes Meg's spare without asking for it. "You'll catch your death," Meg says, the same as every June before this one. Eleven years of parking in the same overflow lot, and neither of them has ever been inside the other's house.


A few rows over, Dave has already decided the stage is going to sag under the last few pairs of feet crossing it, and says so to the stranger sitting next to him. The stranger agrees with him, in real detail. It takes them both most of the conversation to work out that the stranger is Kavi's father. Somebody in a school polo comes by folding up an empty chair from the row behind them and apologizes for the state of things. "First time we've actually run one of these," she says. "It shows," Dave says, not unkindly, and goes back to the joists with Kavi's father.


Nadia watches an usher fold three more chairs and doesn't offer to help. It isn't her mess, and it isn't her problem, not today.


After, on the grass, with a paper cup of lemonade:


"You looked so tall up there."


"Mom."


"You did. Kavi's mother said the same thing." She fixes Chloe's collar. "I'm so proud of you I could be sick."


Her dad fills a memory card and gets none of her face, four hundred photographs of the backs of gowns and the tops of heads and, more than once, his own thumb. He lowers the camera long enough to ask her one real question. "So who's actually driving up in August, you or one of us?"


"Probably me," Chloe says. "I want the car for the fall anyway."


"Huh," he says, and lifts the camera again.


Her grandmother came, and sits in a folding chair at the end of a row, working through everyone who passes her like a receiving line nobody arranged. Sam is the first one caught, gown still on over yesterday's t-shirt. She asks what he's doing next, and when he tells her the Army, she pats his arm and says it'll do him good, teach him some discipline. "Yes ma'am," Sam says, and does not mention the four-minute drill or the number he's been sitting on since he was fifteen.


Kavi is next, in no hurry to get past her. She asks if he's staying on for more school. When he says he's staying at Halstead itself, she nods like that's the same thing and asks whether he'll get his own room out of it.


"Not yet," Kavi says.


"Well, that's something," she says, already looking past him for the next one.


The third one she catches, Chloe doesn't recognize, a girl from another family's cluster of chairs entirely, in a gown still cinched with somebody's binder clip. By the time the girl has extracted herself and gone to find her own people, Chloe's grandmother turns to Meg with the same flat satisfaction she'd bring to a verdict on a roast. "You all seem like very nice young people," she says, to nobody in particular, meaning all of them, the whole field.


Not everyone gets caught. Before Chloe can get away too, her grandmother takes her hand, turns it over once in both of hers the way she always does, and doesn't say anything about what she finds there this time.


By the end of the afternoon the grass by the parking lot holds a small, accidental museum of everything a family brings to something like this: folding chairs people brought of their own because they didn't trust the ones provided, two coolers, a phone charger passed hand to hand down an entire row, a dog that shouldn't be there, and, near the back, a folding table somebody's grandfather set up to sell water bottles at cost because he'd assumed, wrongly, that nobody else would have thought of it. He sells out in twenty minutes.


________________


The school does not empty out.


Everything keeps running: the labs are still going, the bell still sounds at ten past every hour. Eight hundred kids who are not graduating today are back in their seats before the folding chairs on the field have even finished coming down. By the time Chloe carries her own box past the language wing, the same twelve-year-olds who were kicking a water bottle an hour ago are already back at their desks. A substitute drills verb conjugations off a whiteboard, like nothing on that field this afternoon happened at all.


Chloe carries her own box down the ground-floor hallway, a forge mallet with a handle that's slightly the wrong shape wedged in next to a stack of notebooks. On her way out she reads the nine brass plates in the hall, the way she has every day for eleven years, and doesn't stop for them now any more than she ever did.

That evening, before anyone's started packing anything, or booking anything, or signing anything, they are all still on the same patch of grass at the same time, in gowns nobody's bothered to hang up yet. Someone has found a speaker from somewhere. Sam and Kavi are re-running an argument about volcanoes that's older than most of the certificates just handed out. Ruth is already on her third phone call about a dorm assignment. Theo takes a photograph of the group before anyone thinks to arrange themselves for one, more of them in it at once than end up in any other picture from that day.


Sam joins the Army. He's beaten every upperclassman he's sparred since ninth grade, and in July, at a recruiting office off the highway outside Pittsburgh, he signs the papers the way he laces his boots, without checking twice. The office smells like carpet cleaner and stale coffee, a flag in the corner leaning slightly off true. The recruiter slides three separate forms across the desk and starts explaining what each one means, and Sam is already signing the first one before the man finishes the sentence, but he reads the fourth form properly, twice, because it's the one about pay grade and he wants the actual number. He asks more questions about the physical assessment than about anything else on the form. "Anybody try to talk you out of this," the recruiter asks, more out of habit than curiosity. "No," Sam says, and means it exactly as flatly as it sounds. He doesn't look back at the office on his way out.


Ruth goes to MIT, with a box of her own things labeled and half packed before the ceremony even starts, the label written in her own small, upright hand.


Nadia doesn't go anywhere. She spends two months looking at jobs and comes out of it baffled. Then she goes back behind the counter of her parents' shop and checks the till before she says hello to anyone, same as she's always done.


She finds the discount in the ledger on a Tuesday, in her mother's own handwriting: ten dollars knocked off an hourly rate her mother has charged for six years. It matches a number a competitor three blocks over has been quoting all spring. Nobody at the counter thought it was worth mentioning at dinner.


Nadia pushes her sleeves up before she brings it to her father.


"My mother knocked ten dollars off her own hours last week," she says.


"It is a cost," her father says, not looking up from the invoice in front of him.


"That's not a cost. That's the one thing in the shop that gets better every year." She's got the ledger open on the counter between them, one finger flat on the entry. "She's been quoting jobs since before I could reach this counter, and she still doesn't check what the guy three blocks up is charging before she writes a number down. Nobody's built the thing that would've caught it for her. I could build that."


Her father sets the invoice down and looks at her properly. "What would it even do?"


"Watch what the competitors charge. Flag it before she quotes, not after." She says it the way she'd say a fact about the weather.


He closes the invoice and doesn't answer right away.


She brings the same complaint to the chat that week, half a joke by the time it gets there. Eli says he'll look into it and doesn't, and by June she's started something of her own, and by September she's hiring.


Eli takes a job on a security team three states from where he grew up, and tells the group chat about it before he tells his mother. She finds out from a photograph he posts a month later, and is proud in the same uncomplicated way she's always been, without needing to see a contract first.


Theo goes into the federal government as an analyst, having been interviewed four times and having answered every question completely. He keeps that photograph from the grass afterward, the one with more of them in it than any other, and never shows it to anyone.


Kavi stays.


Ten others make the same choice, for their own reasons. Only Kavi says his out loud.


Priya takes a year and goes to South America with a bag. There's no work in it, no address on file yet, no return flight booked either.


"What's in the bag?" Chloe asks her, out on the grass, the two of them working through what's left of a cup of lemonade.


"Not much," Priya says.


"What are you doing when you get there?"


"Nothing, yet." She shrugs, working the strap of the bag between two fingers. "I've never had a whole year with nothing already decided for me. I want to find out what that's like."


Sam, half listening, tells her she's insane. Priya agrees, cheerfully, and doesn't change anything.


Chloe doesn't ask anything else. She never has, with Priya, and doesn't start now.

By the time she's sitting in her own gown, Chloe already knows what the next two years look like, because she found out in May.


She reads the eligibility page for the Foreign Service exam a third time at her desk, to make sure the first two readings said what she thought they said. Twenty to sit it. Twenty-one to be sworn in, once the file behind it clears. She turns eighteen in June.


The math doesn't move.


She closes the laptop and doesn't open it again that night. Instead she goes down to the forge and works later than she has any real reason to. Past a certain point, the piece in front of her stops being about the piece in front of her.


She tells her father at the kitchen table once her mother's gone up, not because it's a secret from her mother but because this is the version of it that goes to him. Through the ceiling, water is running into the bath. Upstairs, it's still an ordinary Tuesday. The last of dinner sits between them on the table, foil pulled halfway over a plate of ham bones, which neither of them has bothered to clear yet.


She doesn't just say she can't sit the exam yet. She walks him through it the way the page laid it out: the age floor, the training year sitting behind the floor once you clear it, and the number of separate stages between passing the written part and actually being sworn in. Then she shows him where, in that stack of stages, a person her age would already have to be standing for the numbers to come out anywhere close to twenty-one.


"How many stages," her father asks.


"Six, roughly. Written test, personal narrative, oral assessment, medical, security review, the roster itself. Nobody actually tells you how long any one of them takes until you're already inside it."


"So the two years in the middle," her father says, when she's finished, "that's a long two years to spend just waiting on one date. Find something in it you'd actually want to do anyway."


Chloe doesn't say anything back. She reaches for the last piece of ham on the table and eats it instead.


Upstairs, the bath stops running. A few minutes later her mother comes down for a glass of water, sees them still at the table, and turns back upstairs without asking what they were talking about.


So she takes the state school, an hour from where she'll be living, and the plan for the next two years is: wait.
