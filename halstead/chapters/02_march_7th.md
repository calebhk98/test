## Chapter Two: March 7th

*March 2012 – June 2012*

Her dad is at work. That morning he tried to move two things and managed neither, standing in the kitchen with his keys in his hand, saying sorry until her mom told him it was fine. Chloe had held his sleeve a second longer than the door needed. She doesn't know whether this is the kind of appointment you bring a dad to, so she reads the waiting room instead.

The fish tank has no fish in it, only water, the little plastic castle, and the bubbles coming up in a line from the filter, so she puts her hands on the glass and looks along the gravel in case they are under something. "Where are the fish?"

"Beats me, honey," her mom says, and she keeps writing, which Chloe decides means she is telling the truth.

The form the office gave her mom is several pages. Chloe comes back to the arm of the chair and watches her check boxes down a column, then start a second column on the back of the sheet. Her mom shifts the clipboard half an inch to keep the light on it, then goes back partway down the page to change a box she has already checked.

"What's it about, all those boxes?"

"It's about you, mostly how you're doing at school and how you feel about stuff, so the people here get a fuller picture of you than a report card would."

"What part of me, though? Do you know how I feel about stuff?"

Her mom stops, looks at the clipboard, then at Chloe. "I only get to see the outside of you, so some of these are a guess. A better guess than anybody else in this building could make, but a guess."

There is a poster about handwashing, and Chloe gets three lines into it before the words stop being ones she knows. Then she sees the same poster underneath in English and works out that she has been reading the Spanish.

There is also a sign that says NO CELL PHONES with a picture of a phone under it and a red line through it. The picture is for the people who can't read the sign, and the people who can't read the sign are looking at a phone with a line on it. Chloe puts her chin on the arm of the chair, and she is still finding it funny a minute later, when a man with a beard opens the door and says her name.

Dr. Prentice, the psychologist, has a room with a small table in it, more like somebody's kitchen than an office: a box of tissues, a bin of blocks, and a window with the blinds halfway down over the parking structure.

There is a stack of printed sheets squared up by his elbow, the top one reading RECORD FORM upside down, and she gets that far before his forearm comes across the page. Beside the stack is a silver watch with his thumb resting on the button.

"I'm Ben. What would you like me to call you?"

Chloe tells him, and he sits down on a chair no bigger than hers. "All right. We're going to do some things that are kind of like games. Some are easy and some are hard. You're not expected to get every one."

"Okay, but is it a test with a grade at the end? Can you fail it? Say I get all of them wrong. What happens then?"

"No, and there is no passing number either. I am watching how you do things rather than whether you get them. If you get stuck on one, I want to see what you try next, not whether you get there," he says straight away, the answer already lined up.

Chloe looks at him hard. The last part sounds like the things adults say when they want you to sit still.

The blocks are red and white, and she turns them to match the picture on a card. The first pictures are so easy she keeps looking at Ben, in case she has missed something, but all he says is mm-hm.

The card goes down, his thumb presses the watch, and he says go. When she takes her hands away he presses it again and writes.

Then the cards want nine blocks instead of four, and the pattern stops showing her where one block ends and the next one starts.

The picture comes apart while she is still looking at it: a solid strip along the top, then a band through the middle where every block is cut corner to corner, then the bottom strip like the top with the colours swapped, and she can see which blocks go where.

Her hands go after it, and the top row is done in a moment. Then it's the cut ones, which have to sit in the right square with the right face up and the right way round all at once. Chloe knows which way the first one goes, but her thumb turns it further than she means to, and bringing it back she knocks the block beside it crooked and has to straighten that one too. Now she can hear a clock somewhere outside the room.

The last block is under her hand when Ben says okay and stops the watch, but she puts it in anyway, because that's where it goes.

Then she checks it against the card, corner by corner, because there is nothing else to do with her hands now. It matches, and she sits with that.

Ben writes something down, small and quick, but she can't tell from his face whether it was a good thing to write.

"Do you want to try one more, or do you want a break?"

"One more. Is there another like it?" She hooks her heels on the rung of the chair, then comes up onto her knees on it to reach the middle of the table.

There is another like it, and then another. Both times she is finished almost as soon as the card is down, and both times Ben says okay while a block is still in her fingers.

The lid goes on the bin.

After the blocks he asks what two things have in common. The pairs get further apart as they go.

"What do a boat and a car have in common?"

"They're both for taking people places. Places too far to walk to."

"Good, and what do an hour and a year have in common?"

"They're both amounts of time. And people picked them both. An hour isn't really a thing out there. It's just where we decided to cut it. Somebody decided a year too. Nobody just found it lying around."

Chloe sits up a little, waiting to find out if that counts extra. Ben stops writing for a moment, then writes.

He reaches the last pair, closes the book, and puts it away.

Then he says numbers and she says them back, and after that he says numbers she has to say back in the other direction.

They keep getting longer, and she holds eight going forwards but six going backwards before she loses the middle of the next string. "I lost the middle of that one," she says.

"That's fine," he says, and he does another anyway, and she loses the middle of that one too. Nobody has ever said a string of numbers to her before, so she doesn't know how many she was supposed to hold.

Ben props up a booklet between them with pictures in rows, and she has to pick the ones that belong together, something from the top row and something from the bottom. This time he leaves the watch alone.

She points at the boot and the glove.

"Can you say why?"

"They're the covers for the ends of you."

The things that go together get harder to spot, and she goes at the speed he turns the page. After her last answer he closes the booklet.

There is a sheet with a key along the top: a line standing up inside a star, two dots inside a circle. Below the key are rows of the same shapes with nothing in them, and she has to put the right mark in each one.

A last look at the key, then Ben says go and starts the watch with his thumb, but her hand won't go as fast as the page wants. The mark has to sit inside the shape and the shape is small, so her fingers grip up hard around the pencil to hold the line where it belongs, and by the end of the first row the web of her thumb aches with it. Bad at the easiest thing on the table, she can tell it while it's happening.

Halfway down the second row she stops and looks up, because he only said to put the right mark in. There might be a version of this where the mark can be scribbled and it still counts. Ben watches her while the watch keeps going, and she goes back to doing it neat.

Stop, he says, and takes the pencil out of her hand and turns the sheet face down before she can count the rows, though she has a rough idea how much a page holds, and her face goes hot, not the good kind.

"Was that a good one, or is that a question you skip?"

"That is a question I skip," Ben says, easy about it, then asks whether she would like juice.

After washing her hands she drinks half a paper cup of juice while Ben writes.

"On the page with the marks. You stopped in the middle of a row."

"I was checking. Does it have to be neat?"

"Neat enough that I can tell which mark you meant. What do you do when you finish your work early?" he says.

"Nothing, mostly. Anything I start, I have to put away again."

Ben doesn't ask anything else.

"I look out the window at the parking lot. There was a guy who left his coffee on the roof of his car and drove off, and it went off at the turn. Then Mrs. Aldana asked me a question and I didn't know there was one." She looks at the cup.

"How long is it, usually, between when you finish and everybody else does?"

"Ages, a long time, like a really long time."

"Longer than we've been in here?"

Chloe counts back through the things they have done. "No, less than that. Half of it, like half of it, but every day."

Next it's words, and he asks the hard ones in the same voice as the easy ones.

Ben asks what a bicycle is and she tells him about the wheels and the pedals, then what a hospital is for, then what stubborn means, which snags her, because there are two in her head, one about people and one about a jar.

"Somebody keeps doing the thing. You show them why not to. They keep doing it."

"Good, and what does nuisance mean?"

"Something not hurting you. It just won't stop."

The words get longer and then they get stranger. One of them stops her cold, and she tells him so, then another stops her too, so she says she would rather go back to the first kind. Ben says that's not how it works, puts the next word to her anyway, and that one goes past her too, and then he closes the booklet.

The numbers come back with letters mixed into them, and he gives her the rule before he starts, the way he gives every rule: the numbers first in counting order, then the letters in alphabet order, whichever way round they went in.

The first string arrives and she hands it back the way it arrived, because that is what the first number game wanted. Ben writes, and she does the same with the next. Before the one after that he says the rule again, in the voice he said it the first time. All of it lands, and she says the rule back to him before he starts, to be sure she has it.

After that she sorts them in her head, numbers and then letters, and they get long before he stops giving them to her.

Ben props the booklet up again with a different kind of page, a pattern with a square missing out of it and a row of pieces along the bottom, and she puts her finger on the piece that fills it.

She points, he turns the page, she points, he turns the page.

The pieces stop being shapes and start being rules about shapes, and she keeps pointing while Ben turns pages faster than he has turned anything all morning. Then the pages stop.

Now he asks about things people do.

"Why do people wash their hands before they eat?"

"Because of the stuff on them. It would go in you with the food."

"Tell me another reason."

Chloe goes back over what she said and can't find the part that was wrong, so she gives him the same thing with more of it: the doorknobs and the underside of the table at school. Ben writes.

"Why do people keep their money in a bank instead of at the house?"

"So it doesn't get taken."

"Tell me another reason."

Chloe explains again, this time with a burglar coming through the window.

They keep coming: wallets on the ground, smoke at night, lines painted on a road. She keeps answering and he keeps wanting something else, so she stops trusting the questions and watches the pen instead. It tells her nothing.

There is another timed sheet, little symbols in rows where she looks at the ones on the left and puts a line through a box on the right for yes or no. Her eyes are on the next row while her pencil is still finishing the line on this one, and twice she goes back to check a box she has already done.

The next page has animals, shoes and cups all over it, and she crosses out every animal, one stroke each, then gets to the bottom and looks up. For once she reaches the end before he says stop.

By then her stomach is loud enough that she covers it with her forearm, and she keeps switching which leg she is sitting on. Ben asks if she's tired and she says no. After the next page, he asks again.

"You can say yes, you know."

"I know. Do we stop, though? Or do we just do a different one?"

"Two more of these and then we're finished, and you can tell me if you want the break before or after," he says.

At the end he caps his pen and puts the last card back in its box. "You worked really hard."

Chloe nearly asks whether she did good, then presses her lips together, because that question is off limits to children. "The one with the marks, does everybody finish it?"

He doesn't answer straight away. "Some people do. Finishing that page isn't what tells me how you did," he says.

Then he talks to her mom, and Chloe waits outside with her jacket in her lap.

The door is open a few inches, and through the gap her mom's foot keeps moving, heel up, heel down, while Chloe works the zipper pull on her jacket.

Only pieces reach her, her mom's voice saying the teacher says, then Ben's voice running on underneath it. Then he turns towards the door and she can hear him.

"I'm not seeing what the school is seeing. She finishes in the first few minutes, and then she has the rest of the hour with nowhere to put it."

"So there's nothing wrong with her?"

"She's completely fine, as far as I can tell from what's going on with her."

"Her teacher keeps writing to us about her handwriting."

"There is nothing the matter with her hands. She copies about as fast as any child her age. The talking is not six. Both of those go into one average, and the average is the part that gets printed. The school did not call you in about her handwriting."

"No."

"Right, and here is the thing about the report, which is going to have one big number on the front, a composite that averages two very different pictures into a single score, and on the talking and reasoning she's at the top of it, but on the speed she's ordinary, so what comes out says bright kid, but bright kid undersells the room I sat in this morning."

"How far at the top of it?"

Ben says the number. Through the door it is one word and Chloe does not catch it. "On a couple of them she answered every item the form has for a six-year-old. There is nothing above that on the page for me to give her."

Chloe shifts on the chair, her feet nowhere near the floor.

"So what number do I get?"

"A good one, and that's the trouble with it, because a bad number is the kind that gets a school to do something, and hers is going to read as good."

"Then what am I supposed to do with that?" her mom says.

"Take the report to the school. The empty hour is theirs to fill. I can write it all up and you can walk it in there, and some of them do move on a report like that."

"And the rest of them, what do they do with a report like that?"

"The rest of them put it in the folder." He says it without any particular feeling. "Is there a gifted program in the building?"

"It starts in third grade, they told us when we asked."

"Then that's two years between now and the only lever the school has for her."

A phone rings somewhere else in the building, and her mom says okay.

Chloe looks at her shoes and holds still, in case moving is the thing that makes the door close.

In the car her mom puts her hands on the wheel, checks the mirror, and starts it. "So you're all good. Everything's great, he thinks you're doing just fine," she says, pulling out of the parking spot.

"Okay," Chloe says, holding the seatbelt away from her neck.

"Do you want to know what he said?"

Chloe pulls the seatbelt out and lets it snap back against her collarbone, against the rules, and her mom says nothing about it. There is a version of this where she finds out and a version where she doesn't, and she can't tell whether knowing would make it worse. "No," she says.

Her mom is still looking at her when the light changes.

"What's a percentile?"

"Where did you hear that?"

"In there, through the door."

The car behind them honks, and her mom pulls away. "It's a number for how you did next to other kids your age, like the tallest in your class being a high percentile for height. It's not a big deal, everybody gets one."

"So what was mine, then? Everybody gets one."

"He didn't give me a number," her mom says to the windshield.

But a word like that always comes with a number stuck to it. Chloe watches the side of her mom's face and presses her thumbnail into the seam of the seat, because they are having a nice time.

They get ice cream, unheard of on a Wednesday, and she eats it in the car, where some of it goes on the seat.

________________

After the appointment, nothing changes.

On March 8th it is the same worksheet, the problems sitting after the one that tells you how it works, and Bryce Dolan counting on his fingers. Mrs. Aldana keeps grading at her desk, and when she asks Chloe once, in April, whether everything is all right at home, Chloe says yes.

Some days she asks to play four square and gets a round in before a bad bounce puts her out and the line re-forms without her, so she sits on the wall with her library book until the bell.

What she works out that month is bigger than the pencil trick: finish first, sit still, keep your answers short, and the room leaves you alone, the teacher included. It works in reading and in math, every day until the end of the year.

________________

She still knows all twenty-eight of their names, but not one of them has needed to learn hers. By June, Chloe is quiet, she does what she's told, and she eats lunch by herself.

On the last day they clean out their desks, where she finds a handful of pencils, somebody else's bracelet, and a permission slip from October. Chloe returns her library book that afternoon, ahead of the summer due date. Kayleigh Burns hugs everybody at the door, while Chloe watches from beside her desk with both straps of her backpack already on.

Her report card comes in an envelope a week later, saying she is meeting expectations in all areas, and under comments, where the teacher writes, it says Chloe is a pleasure to have in class. Her mom holds onto it a while, then puts it on the refrigerator under the magnet shaped like a strawberry, where the sticky note used to be.
