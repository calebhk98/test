## Chapter Thirty-One: Ruth

*October 2023 – November 2025*

Ruth spends her first year at MIT believing she has been put in a remedial track.


It's the only explanation that works. The first-year sequence covers, in a semester, material she had at eleven. She assumes there's a real sequence somewhere she hasn't been given, a bureaucratic problem, and spends most of October trying to find the right office.


The registrar's office is three flights up in a building with no elevator sign that works, and the woman behind the counter listens to the whole explanation without interrupting once. Ruth lays out the timeline: the diagnostic she took in August, the placement it produced, the syllabus she's printed and highlighted in three colors to show exactly where week one repeats what she covered at eleven. The woman is patient and entirely unmoved. She says the placement stands, that nobody reviews a placement without a petition, and slides a form across the counter that asks for a faculty signature Ruth does not yet have.


Advising sends her back to the department, and the department sends her to advising, and by the third week of October she's learned the actual geography of the building better than most sophomores: which stairwell is faster, which office keeps coffee out for visitors, which one locks its door after four. She starts bringing the same folder everywhere, problem sets clipped in date order, because a claim is easier to make with paper in front of somebody than out loud, and because a claim that isn't written down is, in her experience, a claim nobody has to answer.


A woman in the department office finally reads the whole folder, not just the top page, and tells her, kindly, that the sequence she's in is the only sequence there is. There's no other track to be moved onto. Ruth says that can't be right, because the material doesn't match a first-year course at a school like this one, and the woman repeats that it's the placement anyway and offers her a pamphlet about tutoring resources that Ruth doesn't take. Ruth keeps the folder. She decides the mistake is bigger than one office can fix, something upstream of advising and the registrar both, and files it under a problem she'll solve once she works out who actually owns it.


The classes themselves settle into a shape she recognizes from the middle years at Halstead: a problem set due Friday that takes her forty minutes on a Tuesday, a lecture that spends fifty minutes proving something she could prove in three, a recitation where half the room is still copying the professor's board when she's already checked her answer against the back of the book. She sits in the third row, out of habit rather than ambition, and finishes early enough most weeks to read ahead into the following one before the hour is up. When a preceptor hands back a problem set with a perfect score and no comment, she reads the blank space where the comment should be as confirmation: nobody corrects work that doesn't need it, and nobody explains a class that's already pitched at the right level for the room. She tells herself the sequence will catch up to her eventually, the way the diagnostic she took at six eventually caught up to whatever she already knew, and keeps the reading two weeks ahead so she'll have something to do once it does.


By November the study group meets in a fourth-floor common room with a whiteboard nobody erases properly and a couch that smells faintly of the microwave popcorn someone burns most Tuesdays. Five of them are spread across the low table most weeks with problem sets open, two of them still eating, and Ruth has usually finished her own work early enough to be checking somebody else's arithmetic before anyone asks her to.


In November she makes a joke in a study group.


It's a small one. It needs two things held together and it takes about four seconds to say, and it is exactly the sort of thing that would have got a groan out of Sam and a correction out of Kavi.


Three people look at her.


One of them says, "wait, what?" and she explains it, and explaining it kills it, and the group moves on. Walking home, she runs it back: the timing, the words, where the four seconds went wrong.


By February the same group has moved to a study room in the library with a door that doesn't latch and a table scarred with other people's initials, five of them working through a problem set that's given everyone but Ruth real trouble.


In February she asks a study group when they think the course will get past fifth-grade concepts.


She has no idea it could be taken that way. A boy called Devansh laughs, because it looks like a bit. Then he watches her face two beats past the laugh and stops. The table goes quiet, and Ruth cannot work out why.


Somebody changes the subject back to the actual problem set, and the room goes back to work. Ruth goes back to work too, finishing the set in silence and saving the question for the walk home.


She goes home and writes down that MIT tracks students by ability and keeps that quiet.


She writes it on the inside cover of the same notebook she uses for problem sets, in the small upright hand she uses for everything, three lines and a date, and closes the notebook and starts the reading due Monday. It isn't a theory she says out loud to test it, the way she'd test anything else. This one gets to sit for a while before anyone else gets to argue with it.

She holds that for about ten months.


The months underneath that sentence look, from outside, like nothing is wrong. She goes to class, turns problem sets in early, eats dinner in the same dining hall at the same time most nights because a fixed routine is one less thing to decide. When her mother calls on Sundays, Ruth answers on the second ring and gives her the actual week, in order, the way she always has, and corrects her father when he tells her, over speakerphone, that the university health plan covers a specialist visit without a referral. "That's not what the policy says, Dad. It says you need one within thirty days or they can deny the claim retroactively." He reads it back to her off his laptop while she's still on the line, finds the clause, and agrees she's right, and neither of them mentions that she hasn't once, in months of Sunday calls, said anything about the placement.


She keeps the study group folded into a smaller room in her head: something she manages rather than something she shares. Devansh, who ends up in three of her classes by spring, gets the closest look at it without knowing what he's looking at. That spring he brings her a proof he's stuck on, and she finds the error in under a minute, a sign flipped two steps in, and tells him so directly. "That's not wrong because the idea's bad, Devansh. It's wrong because you dropped a negative on line four." He fixes it, thanks her, and asks, half joking, whether she's ever actually stuck on anything. She says no, and it isn't quite a lie, because being stuck and being wrong about the reason don't feel like the same thing from the inside.


She keeps a private tally instead of a public one: every problem set returned without comment, every study group she finishes ten minutes ahead of everyone else, filed as evidence for the theory rather than against it. The idea that the material could simply be beneath her, rather than mismatched to her, is available to her the whole time, and she doesn't reach for it, because reaching for it would mean the numbers she already half-suspects are the real ones.


What eventually breaks it is a professor she likes, in office hours, someone she is genuinely fond of, who cannot follow her past the third step of her reasoning and says so, cheerfully, without embarrassment, before asking her to go back.


His office is on the fourth floor, narrow, with a window that doesn't fully close and a whiteboard crowded with other students' handwriting he hasn't gotten around to erasing. She likes him the way she likes very few of her professors here: he corrects a wrong answer the same day he gives one, out loud, without softening it, and he's the only one who has ever pushed back on a proof of hers rather than simply crediting the answer. She brings him a result she's proud of, something she worked out over two nights, and states it the way she states everything, conclusion first. "It holds because the boundary term cancels once you fix the sign convention, and once that's fixed the rest follows directly."


He doesn't follow it, and says so, and asks her to start again from the top.


She starts again from the top, the way she would for anyone. Step one, step two — and at step three he stops her again, admits he still doesn't have it, and asks whether she'd mind slowing down further than that. She slows down. She takes the assumption she'd folded into a single line and splits it into the two separate claims underneath, states each on its own, and watches him nod at the first and hesitate at the second. It's the second one, she realizes watching him hesitate, that she has never had to defend out loud before, only ever checked against her own head, where it always came out fine.


He gets there eventually, the whole way through, and tells her the result holds. He also tells her, setting his pen down, that it took her four tries to explain something most of his graduate students would get in two, and says it as an observation rather than a complaint, already moving on to the next question on his list. Ruth thanks him, packs the two loose pages into her folder in the wrong order, and leaves before office hours are officially over.


Ruth walks out and sits on a wall until the light changes twice.


It isn't only what he said. It's that somebody her own age, sharper than most of the room and thoroughly ordinary by the standard she's always used, would have needed two tries at what took her four. Once she's up off the wall she doesn't go back to the dorm and doesn't call anyone. She walks to the library instead of to dinner, finds a terminal, and starts looking for the number instead of the excuse.


She goes and finds the numbers herself, over the following months, one at a time, and every one of them says the same thing, each worse than the one before.


The first is the easiest to find and the hardest to sit with. A government survey breaks down what percentage of adults report speaking a second language fluently, and she reads the methodology twice before she believes the figure, because it's so far under what she'd assumed that she suspects, at first, the study of undercounting people like the ones she grew up around. It isn't undercounting. It's describing the country she's living in now rather than the one she came from.


She works through the rest of them the same way, at a carrel on the fourth floor with her laptop propped on two other books because the desk lamp doesn't clear the screen otherwise: adult reading levels, checked against grade-level texts she remembers assigning to the twelves back at Halstead; a fractions benchmark from a testing organization, checked against a worksheet she still has saved from when she was nine. Each search takes an evening, and each one ends the same way, closing the laptop a few minutes before the library asks everyone to leave and walking back across an empty campus running the number over instead of anything else that happened that day.


Second language. Reading level. Fractions. She keeps posting them in the chat and watching people make a joke inside a minute and move on.


She types out fuller versions than what actually goes up, drafts that spell out the methodology and the sample size and why this isn't the same as being slow at Portuguese, and deletes most of them before sending, because a paragraph like that commits her to defending it in a way three numbers on their own don't. What she posts instead reads, to everyone reading it, like Ruth being Ruth: a fact, stated flat, no setup. Nobody in the chat has any way of knowing it's the fourth draft of something, or that the first three said the actual thing underneath the numbers and got deleted before she could talk herself into pressing send.


In April she stops posting, because she has understood what the numbers add up to and would rather somebody else say it first.


After that the chat stays open on her phone same as always, the unread count climbing the way it does for everyone during finals. She reads every message and answers almost none of them, because reading costs nothing and answering commits her to a version of herself she isn't ready to defend out loud yet, not even to people who've known her twelve years and would, she suspects, take it better than a room of strangers ever did.
