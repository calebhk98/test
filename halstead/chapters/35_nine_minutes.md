## Chapter Thirty-Five: Nine Minutes

*June 2026 – July 2026*

The worm goes quiet on a Thursday in June.


Eli is at the second desk in the corner of the apartment when it happens, the one that isn't for the job he's paid for, with a terminal tuned low in one window while he does something else entirely, a habit left over from every all-nighter the last four years have cost him. The tool has been running fourteen months without once needing him to look at it directly; it checks in on a schedule he tuned himself to sit inside the noise floor of an ordinary reconciliation job, and for fourteen months the report has said the same three lines in less time than it takes to read them. He doesn't reach for it the moment the eleven-forty check-in fails to land. A missed one isn't new — a router resets somewhere on the far side of the planet, a scheduler stutters, the thing catches itself a minute later without anyone needing to know it happened — and he finishes the sentence he's typing, the two-finger tap against the desk edge running ahead of it the way it always does, before he looks back at the corner where the report should already be sitting.


A check-in doesn't arrive. Nine minutes later, everything is precisely where it should be. Same state, same position, exactly as it was.


He doesn't call the gap a coincidence yet, and doesn't call it anything else either. Nine minutes is long enough to matter and short enough that a hundred boring explanations could still cover it, and he wants at least one of them ruled out before he says a word to anyone. He opens three logs instead of one — the process's own record, the host underneath it, and the outbound trace Kavi built to sit beneath both without either one knowing the other exists — on the theory that a single log is a story somebody wrote and three logs that agree on the same story are closer to a fact.


Eli pulls the logs. No error, no restart, no gap. There is no nine minutes anywhere in them, and the process reports itself running the whole time.


Page four of the nine pages defines what counts as being noticed, in language exact enough that Eli argued two of its clauses into their final shape himself: an external party observing, recording, or interacting with the tool in any manner inconsistent with its intended dormancy. He has the document open in the next window before he's finished reading the logs a second time, and he reads the clause the way he'd read a spec he was trying to break, once for what it says and once for what it would take to make it not apply. Nobody has observed anything he can point to on a screen. Nothing has been recorded, because there is nothing on any of his three logs to record. On the only reading he can defend to himself, nothing that happened tonight meets the definition on the page, and he closes the window and opens the one where Kavi already is.


Kavi's watcher, whose entire job is to see anything that anybody else might see, reports a healthy process across the whole window.


It's three in the morning where Kavi actually is, an hour that has never registered to him as unusual, and he's awake anyway when Eli's message lands, on the same steady schedule he's kept since he was a student in the building he still hasn't left. He built the watcher specifically to distrust a log with nothing wrong in it — that shape of silence was the whole design brief — and pulling its own report now feels less like checking a tool than rereading his own handwriting for a lie he'd have to have told himself first. There isn't one to find. The watcher logged a continuous, unremarkable process for the entire window, the same report it would generate if nothing at all had happened, and Kavi turns the pen on his desk over twice and sets it back down exactly on its mark before he starts reading his own code instead of the log, because if the report is wrong the reason will be in there and nowhere else.

It happens twice more in six weeks.


The second time is the second Tuesday of July, and Kavi has it flagged inside four minutes, because he's stopped waiting for the watcher to tell him on its own schedule and started polling it on his instead. Nine minutes again, to the second this time, which is worse than a different number would have been: different would say clumsy, and the same number twice says measured. He lines the three timestamps up in a document nobody else can reach, June against the first July date against the second, and the gap between the first pair is five weeks and a day and the gap between the second pair is eleven days, and there is no pattern in either interval he can make argue for anything. He writes that down too. A negative result is still a result.

She has the group chat open on her phone and the nine pages open on the laptop beside it, the document she didn't write but has read often enough that she can find any clause in it without the search bar, and her hand goes to page seven without her deciding to, the way it finds a light switch in her own house in the dark. The paragraph there is four lines long. Any one of the seven can end it, no vote required and no explanation owed to the other six, all of it stopped inside the hour, and the four lines have sat on that page since Chloe wrote them not because anyone expected to need them but because Chloe doesn't build a system with a limit in it without also building the door out. Ruth reads the four lines twice, the same clinical pass she'd give a proof she was checking rather than one she'd written herself, looking for the place it breaks. It doesn't break. It says exactly what it has always said. She closes the laptop, picks the phone back up, and types into the chat instead of the clause.

Chloe reads that at one in the morning with work in six hours.


She's five months into the job, long enough that her badge scans without her checking twice, in a building that is the last physical place in her life that would recognize the name sitting in that chat thread — which is rather the entire point of the building. She doesn't reread the messages. She sets the phone face down on the nightstand, the way she's set a phone down a hundred times before a piece of news that changes nothing about what tomorrow requires of her, and closes her eyes on the version of tonight that ends here rather than the one that keeps going somewhere she'd have to stay awake to follow.


She goes in the next day and does her job, which is the job of a government that has been trying to find this man since before she could read, and which has two wrong answers on file and no idea that seven people in their twenties settled it in sixteen weeks.
