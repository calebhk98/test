\# Critique: Prose and Sentence-Level Analysis

\#\# Overall Assessment

This is a \*technically competent\* piece of writing—clean, controlled, and consistent in its register. The author demonstrates a clear understanding of how to construct a narrative voice. However, there are patterns in the prose that become \*\*repetitive and flattening\*\* over the course of twenty chapters, and some sentence-level choices actively undermine the emotional weight the narrative seems to want.

\---

\#\# Pattern 1: The "Declarative Catalog" (Overuse)

The author has a tic: \*\*short, declarative sentences in sequence\*\*, often starting with "She" or "He" or a character's name, describing actions in a flat register.

\*\*Examples:\*\*  
\> "Chloe gets water. She goes back through and finishes about the bridge."

\> "Kavi listens to the whole thing. Then he puts his own working up."

\> "She reads it in the corridor. Then she starts to yell in Russian."

\*\*Why this is a problem:\*\*  
\- It creates a \*monotone effect\* across dozens of pages.  
\- Emotional moments (like her breakdown on the floor) are rendered in the \*same register\* as her describing the weather.  
\- The reader loses texture. Every action carries the same weight—none is elevated above another.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "Chloe got water. Then she went back through and finished about the bridge."

Even that small change (changing "gets" to "got") gives the sentence a \*past-tense narrative flow\* rather than a \*present-tense screenplay beat\*. But more importantly, the issue is not tense—it's \*\*rhythm\*\*. These beats need \*variation\*.

\---

\#\# Pattern 2: The "And Then" Structure (Unacknowledged)

The prose frequently relies on a \*\*chronological list\*\* construction where events are simply \*stated in order\* without connective tissue.

\> "She runs upstairs, then runs back down twenty seconds later and stops on the third step from the bottom with both hands on the rail."

\> "She takes the book down and reads it in the kitchen instead of her room. She gets it on the second ring twice, and on the first ring once..."

\*\*Why this is a problem:\*\*  
\- It's \*passive storytelling\*—things happen, but the reader isn't experiencing them.  
\- There's no \*subordination\* of information. Everything is equally prominent, so nothing is prominent.  
\- The prose reads like a \*summary of events\* rather than a \*rendering of experience\*.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "She ran upstairs, then back down twenty seconds later, stopping on the third step with both hands on the rail."

Notice: one "ran" covers both directions, and "stopping" is a subordinate action that gains emphasis through its grammatical position.

\---

\#\# Pattern 3: The "Glass Wall" (Emotional Distance)

The narrator is \*\*extremely close to Chloe's perspective\*\* but refuses to enter her interiority except through reported action. We \*see\* her do things. We rarely \*feel\* her feeling them.

\> "Chloe sits there with her heart going."

\> "Something goes wrong with her. It happens before anybody has had time to answer. Her stomach drops, her hands go cold, her ears do what they did in October..."

\*\*Why this is a problem:\*\*  
\- The author \*signals emotion\* ("heart going," "stomach drops") but doesn't \*render it\*.  
\- These are \*\*physiological clichés\*\*—the "cold hands," the "dropping stomach"—that could describe \*any\* anxious moment for \*any\* character.  
\- We don't know what Chloe \*thinks\* about her feelings, only that she \*has\* them.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
Consider how a child's mind might \*process\* fear:  
\> "Her stomach dropped, but she didn't understand why—nothing had happened yet. She'd been fine a second ago. The hallway was the same hallway. But her hands were cold, and her ears were doing that thing they did in October, and she could see the desk and the wood chips and Peanut's cage, even though she was standing in a hallway with a brass plate that said PLUTO."

This gives us: the \*experience\* of dissociation, the \*child's attempt to understand\* it, and the \*specificity\* of the memory (not just "October" but "the wood chips and Peanut's cage").

\---

\#\# Pattern 4: The "Said Bookism" Avoidance (Overcorrection)

The author almost \*never\* uses dialogue tags other than "says." This is a common advice response ("just use 'said'"), but it's been applied so rigidly that the prose becomes \*\*flat and difficult to follow\*\*.

\> "Why do you want to go?"  
\> "It's got my name on it."

This is fine for two lines. But in a longer exchange:

\> "It's a stupid question because it's got an answer, and they knew what was under them..."  
\> "Then you'll get a C and he'll be right to give you one."  
\> "Thanks very much, that's extremely helpful."  
\> "Devil's advocate," Sam says, through most of a bread roll.

\*\*Why this is a problem:\*\*  
\- We lose \*who is speaking\* in longer exchanges.  
\- We lose \*how\* they're speaking—the emotional tone, the physicality.  
\- The "says" tag becomes invisible in a way that actually makes reading \*harder\*, because the reader has to constantly check the line breaks to track speakers.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "It's a stupid question because it's got an answer, and they knew what was under them—"  
\> "Then you'll get a C and he'll be right to give you one." Sam said it flatly, not looking up from his food.  
\> "Thanks very much," Chloe said. "That's extremely helpful."  
\> "Devil's advocate." He shrugged through a mouthful of bread roll.

We get: attribution, \*tone\*, \*physical action\*, and the dialogue still moves.

\---

\#\# Pattern 5: The "Just the Facts" Description (Lack of Sensory Specificity)

The narrative tends toward \*\*generic description\*\* of places and objects:

\> "The waiting room has a fish tank in it with no fish. Just the water, the little plastic castle, the bubbles coming up in a line from the filter in the corner."

This is \*fine\*. It tells us what's there. But it doesn't tell us:  
\- What it \*feels like\* to be in that room  
\- What \*Chloe notices\* (as opposed to what the narrator reports)  
\- What the room \*smells\* like, \*sounds\* like, \*feels\* like under her hands

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "The waiting room had a fish tank with no fish. Just water, a plastic castle, and a line of bubbles from the filter. Chloe pressed her palms against the glass, looking for something under the gravel. The bubbles made a soft hum she could feel through her fingers."

Now we have: \*tactile engagement\*, \*specificity\* about what she's actually doing, and a \*sound\* that creates atmosphere.

\---

\#\# Pattern 6: The "It Was" Construction (Passive and Weak)

The author frequently uses \*\*"it was" \+ adjective\*\* constructions that drain energy from the sentence:

\> "It was so quiet she could hear him breathe."

\> "It was the easiest thing she had done all morning and she was bad at it."

\> "It was the second week that gets her..."

\*\*Why this is a problem:\*\*  
\- It's \*\*weak verbs\*\* disguised as description.  
\- It tells the reader how to feel rather than creating the feeling.  
\- It's \*lazy\* in a way that's cumulative—one "it was" doesn't matter, but forty of them do.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "So quiet she could hear him breathe."

\> "The easiest thing she'd done all morning—and she was bad at it."

\> "The second week got her."

\---

\#\# Pattern 7: The "Thought Report" (Overuse of Internal Statement)

The narrator frequently \*states what Chloe thinks\*, rather than rendering it:

\> "She thought about that one for a while. The real answer is that she knows what the teacher is talking about almost all of the time."

This is \*telling\*, not \*showing\*. It's the narrator \*summarizing\* thought rather than \*inhabiting\* it.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "She knew what the teacher was talking about. Almost always. But sometimes—and this was the part that didn't fit into one word—sometimes she was entirely somewhere else and didn't know a question had happened at all."

The second version uses \*sentence rhythm\* and \*dash interruption\* to mimic the \*experience\* of thinking through a problem. It's thought \*rendered\*, not thought \*reported\*.

\---

\#\# Pattern 8: The "Educational Jargon" Fetish

The prose occasionally \*\*drops into an academic register\*\* that feels imported rather than absorbed:

\> "Formal epistemology is the room four other subjects have been standing outside."

This is cute, but it's also \*explaining\* the curriculum rather than \*inhabiting\* Chloe's experience of it. The prose begins to read like a \*syllabus\* rather than a \*novel\*.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "She'd been circling this room for three years. Logic gave her the question. Statistics gave her the odds. Rhetoric gave her the person behind it. Cryptography gave her what it took to be sure. Now someone was going to give all of it a name."

\---

\#\# Pattern 9: Dialogue Rhythm Problems

The dialogue is \*\*extremely naturalistic\*\*—which is both a strength and a weakness. It sounds \*realistic\*, but it lacks the \*compression\* and \*musicality\* of good literary dialogue.

\> "No, I'm actually asking, you don’t have to act like the fake attackers, somebody looked at those and went, yeah, that's the ones."

This is how people \*actually talk\*. But fiction isn't a transcript. The "um"s and "you know"s and false starts are \*realistic\* but they \*\*slow the reader down\*\* and add no dramatic value.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "No, I'm actually asking. Somebody looked at those and said, yeah, that's the ones."

We lose the false start and the "you don't have to act" (which is \*explaining the joke\*), and the line moves faster.

\---

\#\# Pattern 10: The "Understated" Emotional Climax

The breakdown scene on the floor (Chapter Nine) is meant to be a major emotional turning point:

\> "Then it comes out of her all at once and she can't get one word of it out whole, so what comes out is please, over and over..."

The prose \*describes\* the breakdown but doesn't \*render\* it. We get the \*content\* of what she says ("she said she'd be good... she said they could have her birthday...") but we don't get the \*experience\* of it.

\*\*Better:\*\*  
\> "Then it came out—not words, not yet, just sound, just a noise from somewhere she didn't know she had. 'Please,' she said, and then 'please' again, and then she was saying it over and over and couldn't stop, couldn't find the space between one please and the next to get air—"  
\> "You can have my birthday. I don't want it. You can have every birthday, I don't need them, I don't need anything, I'll never ask for anything again, I'll do everything—"  
\> But she was already talking over herself, the words coming in layers now, each one trying to outrun the one before it."

The second version: \*shows\* the acceleration, the panic, the \*desperation\*, rather than \*reporting\* that she said desperate things.

\---

\#\# Positive Observations (Things That Work)

\- \*\*The child's logic\*\* in early chapters is rendered beautifully. The library argument is \*perfectly\* child-brained.  
\- \*\*The refusal to sentimentalize\*\* is a genuine strength. The prose never begs for tears.  
\- \*\*The anti-climax\*\* structure of many scenes (e.g., the psychologist's report not changing anything) is \*bold\* and \*effective\*.  
\- \*\*The "Pluto" scene\*\* (where she names the room and Sam's laughter releases her tension) is the best piece of writing in the entire text. It renders an emotion \*through action\* rather than through description.  
\- \*\*The detail work\*\* (the coffee cup on the roof of the car, the library stamp) is \*specific\* and \*memorable\*.  
\- \*\*The long-term accumulation\*\* (how skills build over years) is structurally impressive and feels earned.

\---

\#\# Structural Prose Patterns Worth Examining

\#\#\# The "Skip" Construction  
\> "She does not have the second half of that sentence, and she is fairly sure the first half sounds like she thinks she's better than everybody, which she has learned not to sound like."

This is a \*characteristic\* construction—long, clause-heavy, with the actual point arriving at the very end. It's \*Chloe's voice\*—hesitant, self-aware, working through things as she goes. \*\*This works.\*\*

\#\#\# The "Negative Assessment" Construction  
\> "It isn't complicated, or interesting, so there is nothing to be done about it except get better at sitting there."

This is another \*voice marker\*—the refusal to sentimentalize, the flat assessment. \*\*This works.\*\*

\#\#\# The "Late Arrival" Construction  
\> "Nothing comes, all day."

Short. Flat. Arrives at the end of a paragraph after being set up. \*\*This works.\*\*

\---

\#\# Summary of Recommendations

1\. \*\*Vary sentence rhythm\*\*—short sentences are effective, but a page of them is monotonous.  
2\. \*\*Add sensory detail\*\*—not more of it, but \*different kinds\* of it (smell, texture, sound, temperature).  
3\. \*\*Deepen interiority\*\*—show Chloe \*experiencing\* emotions, not just having them.  
4\. \*\*Vary dialogue tags\*\*—not with "exclaimed" or "declared," but with \*action\* and \*tone indicators\*.  
5\. \*\*Cut "it was" constructions\*\*—find stronger verbs.  
6\. \*\*Reduce the "and then" structure\*\*—use subordination to give events weight.  
7\. \*\*Add variation to the "declarative catalog"\*\*—mix in longer sentences, fragments, and inversions.  
8\. \*\*Compress dialogue\*\*—naturalism is good, but \*fiction\* needs compression.  
9\. \*\*Render emotional climaxes\*\*—don't just describe what was said, \*show the experience\*.  
10\. \*\*Trust the reader more\*\*—you don't need to explain every school subject; the accumulation is enough.

This is a \*\*70-75th percentile\*\* manuscript in terms of prose. It's clean, controlled, and consistent. The problems are not \*errors\* but \*patterns\*—habits of construction that become visible over 20 chapters. With a serious line edit focusing on rhythm, sensory detail, and interiority, this could be a \*\*90th percentile\*\* piece.

The author is clearly talented. The work needs a reader who will say: "This is good. Now make it \*feel\* like something."

\# Prose-Level Critique

You're right that the plot mechanics work. What follows is sentence-level, paragraph-level, and voice-level. I'll be thorough because you asked me to be.

\---

\#\# 1\. The Monotony Problem: Sentence Rhythm

The dominant sentence shape across all twenty chapters is:

\> Subject \+ verb \+ object. Subject \+ verb \+ object. Subject \+ verb \+ object.

\> \*She reads it three times standing in the driveway with the rest of the mail going warm against her arm. Then she puts the mail on the counter, takes the letter up to her room, and says nothing about it for four hours.\*

\> \*She does problems four through thirty in the time that's left and gets every one of them right, and by then it doesn't count for anything.\*

This is clearly a deliberate register — flat, declarative, paratactic. And it works in bursts. But across roughly 30,000 words it never modulates. Joy gets the same rhythm as grief. Terror gets the same rhythm as boredom. The parking-lot man with the coffee cup gets the same cadence as Chloe screaming on the floor begging to go to Halstead. There is no sentence in the entire text that \*surprises\* you rhythmically. No long subordinating sentence that pulls you forward. No fragment that stops you cold. No inversion. Nothing. The prose has one gear, and it stays in it for twenty chapters.

The effect is that by chapter five or six, the reader's ear goes numb. You can feel the next sentence's shape before it arrives. That's fatal over this length.

\---

\#\# 2\. The Physical-Gesture Tic

Almost every emotional beat is rendered through a small physical action:

\- \*Her mom puts her forehead on the steering wheel and stays there.\*  
\- \*Her dad puts the fork down to hold it with both hands.\*  
\- \*Chloe has both hands flat on the table.\*  
\- \*Her mom sets the serving spoon down on the edge of the dish.\*  
\- \*She puts her fork down.\*  
\- \*Her dad turns his glass a quarter round on the step.\*  
\- \*She puts the pen down flat on top of the pad.\*  
\- \*Her mom pulls out a chair and doesn't sit in it.\*

I stopped counting at around eighty instances. Forks are put down. Hands go flat on surfaces. Foreheads meet steering wheels. Characters stand in doorways. People sit on the ends of beds. This is the text's primary method of conveying feeling, and because it's the \*only\* method, it flattens. The mom putting her forehead on the wheel after the evaluation is the same gesture-class as the mom putting her forehead on the wheel after Chloe says "you hate me." The reader can't distinguish between a small sadness and a catastrophic one because both get a forehead and a flat hand.

\---

\#\# 3\. "The Whole Thing" and Other Verbal Tics

Certain phrases recur so often they stop functioning as language and start functioning as filler:

\- \*\*"the whole thing"\*\* — appears dozens of times. "That's the whole thing." "The whole thing takes forty minutes." "She can't get past the whole thing." "The whole thing is still in there."  
\- \*\*"for a second" / "for a while" / "for longer than"\*\* — used as universal duration markers. Nobody waits "a moment" or "a beat." It's always "a second" or "a while."  
\- \*\*"She says it \[adjective\]"\*\* — "She says it tired." "He says it interested." "She says it careful." This construction appears maybe forty times. It's effective the first three. After that it reads as a crutch for avoiding actual dialogue description.  
\- \*\*"which she has done X times already"\*\* — "which she has done twice already this morning." "which she has now read three separate times." "which she has now argued about with the same librarian on three separate occasions."  
\- \*\*"and that's the end of it"\*\* — used as a scene-closer repeatedly.  
\- \*\*"the way she does" / "the way you do"\*\* — a hedge that avoids specificity.  
\- \*\*"Nobody says anything"\*\* — appears in some form in nearly every chapter. Sometimes it's powerful. By chapter twelve it's wallpaper.

\---

\#\# 4\. Over-Explanation / Not Trusting the Reader

The text repeatedly shows something, then explains what it means:

\> \*Which is the whole thing. That's the whole book, right there, in the bowl. Because at the start the key is the most important thing either of them has, and at the end it's just in with the batteries and the rubber bands, and nobody put it there on purpose, it just ended up there. And that's how you know they're okay.\*

This is a six-year-old explaining a literary metaphor at dinner. But even setting aside the characterization problem, the \*prose\* problem is that it explains the metaphor three times. "That's the whole book, right there, in the bowl." Then "because at the start..." Then "And that's how you know they're okay." One of those sentences is enough. The other two are the text not trusting the reader.

It happens elsewhere:

\> \*She doesn't say that part out loud.\*

\> \*which is funny, because if you can read the sign you don't need the picture, and if you can't read the sign the picture isn't going to help you.\*

The sign joke is funny. The explanation of why it's funny is not. The text keeps doing this — landing a moment, then narrating the landing.

\---

\#\# 5\. The Interiority Problem

Chloe's inner life is almost entirely rendered as:

\- \*She thinks about it for a while.\*  
\- \*She can't work out...\*  
\- \*She can't get past...\*  
\- \*She works on it through lunch and gets nowhere with it.\*  
\- \*She doesn't know what to do with it.\*

We almost never get actual thought content. We get the \*fact\* of thinking without the \*texture\* of thought. The one exception is the fractions scene on the bedroom floor, where we get genuine cognition:

\> \*Dividing by an eighth is asking how many eighths fit inside it. That's the entire question, that's all it ever was.\*

That's the only time in twenty chapters the prose actually \*thinks\* on the page. Everything else is "she thought about it" or "she worked on it" or "she couldn't get it down to one." For a character whose defining trait is her mind, the prose keeps us outside the mind almost constantly.

\---

\#\# 6\. Dialogue That Doesn't Sound Like Children

Six-year-old Chloe says:

\> \*"It's moving the whole time. It's going the whole way around us, and it's turning while it does it, at exactly the speed that keeps the same side pointed here."\*

And:

\> \*"You can end up somewhere by accident and still be there."\*

And the locker-key speech at dinner. And the "mailing something to a stranger" speech at seven. These are adult observations in a child's mouth. The text never adjusts the \*sentence structure\* for age. A six-year-old's syntax is different from a ten-year-old's is different from a fourteen-year-old's. Here, Chloe speaks in complex subordinate clauses at six and in complex subordinate clauses at sixteen. The vocabulary grows, but the grammar doesn't.

Similarly, the adults all sound identical. Mom, Dad, the librarian, Mrs. Aldana, Dr. Prentice, the woman at the district office — they all speak in the same flat declarative register. Nobody has a verbal tic, a vocabulary quirk, a rhythm that's theirs alone.

\---

\#\# 7\. The "And Then" Problem / Flat Causality

Nearly everything is connected by "and" or "then":

\> \*She reads it three times... Then she puts the mail on the counter, takes the letter up to her room, and says nothing about it for four hours, because she is working out how to ask.\*

\> \*Then it's words. He asks them the same way every time...\*

\> \*Then he says numbers and she says them back, which is easy. Then he says numbers she has to say back the other way round...\*

There is almost no subordination. No "because" that isn't tacked on at the end. No "although." No "while." No sentence where one clause is grammatically \*dependent\* on another in a way that creates hierarchy. Everything is coordinate. Everything gets equal weight. This means the prose can't distinguish between a trivial action and a devastating one. They sit side by side in the same flat chain.

\---

\#\# 8\. Over-Narration of Silence and Non-Action

The text spends enormous energy telling you what \*doesn't\* happen:

\- \*She doesn't say that part out loud.\*  
\- \*Nobody comes over.\*  
\- \*She doesn't cry.\*  
\- \*Nothing about it is big enough to cry about.\*  
\- \*Nobody says anything.\*  
\- \*She doesn't say anything about it.\*  
\- \*She lets it go.\*  
\- \*The whole thing takes about two seconds, after which it's over.\*

This is effective for showing Chloe's suppression and the school's indifference. But it's used so relentlessly that by chapter eight, "nobody says anything" has lost all force. The absence of response is only powerful if response is the expected default. Here, non-response is the default from page one, so narrating its absence stops registering.

\---

\#\# 9\. Scene Structure Repetition

Nearly every scene follows the same shape:

1\. Setup (a worksheet, a dinner, a phone call, a class).  
2\. Chloe observes something specific.  
3\. Chloe says something or doesn't.  
4\. A small physical gesture (fork down, hand flat, forehead on wheel).  
5\. Silence or a non-answer.  
6\. Scene ends.

This repeats hundreds of times. The dinner scenes in particular are all structurally identical: Chloe talks, parents respond with short sentences, a gesture, the scene ends in nine minutes or someone clears plates. By the fifth dinner scene you can predict every beat.

\---

\#\# 10\. The "In \[Month\]" Chapter Transitions

\> \*In October, Peanut climbs the bars...\*

## **1\. The "nobody" construction as a normalizing device**

This shows up constantly, and it's doing more work than it looks like it's doing:

* "Nobody turns around." (after Icarus)  
* "Nothing comes, all day." (after Ruth corrects the teacher)  
* "Nobody in the dining hall asks her where she is from, or what she is good at."  
* "Nobody at the table asks a follow-up question." (Olympic swimmer)  
* "Nobody in the room seems surprised by any of this." (fluent Spanish in a week)  
* "Nobody gives her a test. She keeps waiting for the part where somebody gives her a test."  
* "nobody in that building knows it happened"  
* "Nobody says one word about any of it, then or afterward."

**Why it's a problem, not just a tic:** each instance is doing the same rhetorical job — it tells the reader how to feel about an event by reporting the absence of a reaction to it, rather than earning that feeling through a real one. "Nobody was surprised she's fluent in a week" isn't evidence the achievement is ordinary; it's a sentence standing in for evidence, and it's used so often that the reader is trained to treat the absence of comment as proof of normalcy every single time it appears. That's the exact mechanism you flagged: instead of showing why something is or isn't remarkable, the text tells you nobody remarked on it, and asks you to conclude that means it isn't.

**Fix:** for at least some of these, either cut the line entirely and let the silence be implicit in the scene, or replace it with an actual character reaction (even a small one) so the reader is judging a response instead of an absence. If the device is meant to characterize the *school* (nothing fazes anyone there), that idea should be established explicitly once, by a character, rather than reasserted eleven times by narration.

## **2\. Repetition used to flag importance instead of trusting the reader**

The fraction insight (why you flip the second number) gets explained in full at least three separate times: alone at night with the rectangle, to Ben off-page reference, and again on a napkin to her father — the second telling explicitly restaged ("she does the whole thing a second time on the clean side, and he doesn't interrupt any of it"). The scene where she gets it the first time is genuinely strong. Restaging it twice more doesn't deepen it, it just underlines it, the same move as a character saying "let me repeat that" — the text is telling you this matters instead of letting one well-built scene matter on its own.

The DEFENSIVE WATCH scoring block is reproduced verbatim, word for word, twice (chapters 14 and 15), including the same unbolded final line about response time. That's not thematic reinforcement, that's the manuscript reminding the reader of a rule it already gave them, as if it didn't trust the first instance to land.

**Fix:** cut the second and third fraction re-explanations down to a line of consequence ("she'd had it since the floor of her room in second grade, and it still worked") rather than restaging the mechanism. Cut the second DEFENSIVE WATCH block entirely, or replace it with a single line acknowledging the reader already knows the rules.

## **3\. Dialogue that states the theme out loud**

* "That's the whole book, right there, in the bowl." — the six-year-old explains her own metaphor's meaning to us in case we missed it.  
* "That's my whole thing," Chloe says. — after the moon argument, in case the parallel (stuck vs. choosing to stay facing something) wasn't clear.  
* "Because they're criminals... That's what's left after everything else didn't work." — closing line of the book, spoken by a sixteen-year-old, functioning as the author's thesis statement about the criminals and, by extension, about what her own school has made of her.

**Why it's a problem:** each of these takes a moment that was already legible from the scene and has a character narrate its meaning directly at the reader. It's the prose equivalent of a laugh track. A careful reader doesn't need Chloe to tell us the locker key is a symbol; they'll get there faster and trust the book more if she doesn't.

**Fix:** cut the explanatory tag lines and end the scene one beat earlier, on the concrete image or action, not on the character's gloss of it.

## **4\. The camp recap: burying scale inside pacing**

Across roughly twenty pages of the camp chapters, the narration gives real scene-time (dialogue, conflict, named characters, sensory detail) to: making friends with Priya, the Pluto joke, homesickness and the phone calls, Ruth correcting the teacher, the bridge, fractions. The text is lying by volume: giving you so much that you retain almost nothing, then handing you a small curated subset and saying "here, this is the important part."

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Cut the list. If the father picks two items, let those two be the only ones the reader sees. The other ten don't need to exist. If they must exist, they should exist as \*problems\* — Chloe should struggle with one, fail at another, spend three days stuck on a third. Right now they're a parade, and a parade is skippable.  
\- If the full list stays, the father's phone call should not reduce it. Let him say "she talked for twenty minutes and I got maybe a third of it." That's honest. Selecting two items and presenting them as the summary is the text editorializing.  
\- Alternatively: break the list across multiple scenes. One activity per scene, with friction, with failure, with time. Then the reader \*earns\* each item and can't skim them.

\*\*The yearly school dump (Chapters 11-12 and beyond):\*\*

The same trick runs every year. The text gives you three or four classes in full scene form — the fractions scene, the bridge scene, the Pluto scene. These are slow, emotional, character-driven. Then, in the middle of the chapter, it gives you a rapid-fire paragraph listing every other subject:

\> "Mandarin is with a woman called Mrs. Sun, who grew up in Harbin... The reading comes fast, because the characters go in and stay in. In November she gets through a page without stopping. By February she is doing novels. By March she is turning in the ten-page end-of-term reports in Mandarin..."

\> "Statistics turns out to be an hour of somebody taking a number apart in front of you..."

\> "Chemistry is Vasquez, who does set things on fire on purpose..."

\> "First aid is Mondays and it is on a clock from the first week."

\> "Textiles is Tuesdays, and textiles turns out to be geometry with cloth in it."

Each of these is two to four sentences. They sit between scene-based passages. The reader treats them as transition, as connective tissue between the "real" scenes. But they contain the actual curriculum: a nine-year-old is doing Mandarin (reading novels by February, writing ten-page reports by March), statistics, chemistry with actual fire, first aid under timed pressure, and textiles with drafting. Five subjects, each in two sentences, buried in the middle of a chapter about a bread argument and a boy climbing a wall.

\*\*Why this is bad:\*\* The text is structuring the reader's attention to skip exactly the information that would reveal what this school actually is. The scene-based content is emotional: friendship, frustration, belonging. The list-based content is curricular: what is being taught, at what level, at what age. By making the emotional content slow and the curricular content fast, the text ensures the reader absorbs the feelings and skims the facts.

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Give each subject a scene. Even a short one. Even four lines. If a nine-year-old is doing chemistry with actual fire, that deserves a moment where the reader sits in the room and watches it happen. Two sentences is a footnote, and footnotes get skipped.  
\- If you must summarize, summarize honestly. "She had nine subjects that year" is more honest than listing them in two-sentence bursts that read like transitions.  
\- Remove the scene-based content from the same chapter. If Chapter 12 is the curriculum chapter, let it be the curriculum chapter. Don't sandwich it between the bread test and the climbing wall. That's hiding vegetables in a sandwich.

\#\# 2\. Normalization of Absurd Capabilities

The text presents extraordinary feats through a lens of boredom, frustration, or social concern, so the reader absorbs the \*emotion\* rather than the \*fact\*.

\*\*The 10v1:\*\*

\> "By Christmas she can hold twenty-four seconds in the 10v1, but she is still only ranked 30 out of 90."

A twelve-year-old holds off ten attackers for twenty-four seconds. The text immediately follows this with her \*ranking\*, which redirects the reader's attention to the social hierarchy rather than the physical fact. The reader thinks "oh, she's not the best." The reader does not think "a child just held off ten people for twenty-four seconds and this is presented as mediocre."

\*\*The arrow scene (Chapter 14):\*\*

Children are shooting arrows at each other. Not at targets. At \*each other\*. The arrows meet in mid-air at the top of a sixty-degree arc, seventy metres apart. The text renders this through the lens of counting and timing, of Chloe being frustrated that her release is early. The reader is meant to feel the \*frustration of practice\*, not the \*impossibility of the task\*. Children are doing something that would be extraordinary for an Olympic archer, and the text treats it as a Tuesday afternoon problem with a metronome.

\*\*The Watch drills:\*\*

Children defend a school against adult intruders. The text renders this as routine: "Most of them are announced. There is a board and it says Thursday." The reader is meant to absorb the scheduling, not the content. Children are engaging in tactical defense against grown men, and the text's register is "this happens on Thursdays."

\*\*The language list:\*\*

By sixteen, Chloe has studied: Spanish, French, Mandarin, Russian, Latin, German, Japanese, Hindi. Ruth is doing Arabic. Kavi is doing a third. This is nine languages across a student body of roughly ninety children. The text never presents this as a list. It presents each language as it arrives, in its chapter, in two or three sentences, surrounded by scenes about bread or climbing walls or Sam's girlfriend. The reader never gets the full count because the text never gives the full count.

\*\*Why this is bad:\*\* The text is asking the reader to accept that these are "smart but normal" children at a "normal" school by never letting the reader see the full picture. Every extraordinary fact is delivered in isolation, surrounded by ordinary emotional content, so the reader normalizes each one individually. The text never says "and here is everything this child can do at age twelve." It says "here is a scene about her being frustrated about a bridge, and oh, also, she is learning Mandarin."

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Let the reader be shocked. If a ten-year-old is doing chemistry with fire, write the scene so the reader feels the wrongness of it. Don't smooth it over with "Vasquez is fun." Let the reader sit in the room and think "this child should not be here."  
\- Give the reader the list. All of it. In one place. Let them count. If the text is honest about what these children are doing, the reader will feel the weight of it. If the text hides the list, the reader feels manipulated, even if they can't say why.  
\- Stop using ranking and social comparison to deflect from capability. "She can hold twenty-four seconds but she's only thirtieth" is the text saying "don't be impressed, there are twenty-nine better." That is not a fact about the character. That is the text managing the reader's reaction.

\#\# 3\. Negative Wording: Narrating Absence

The text constantly narrates what does \*not\* happen. This is the issue your supervisor flagged: "Nobody comments on it" is only meaningful if commenting was expected. Otherwise you're narrating the absence of the sun, or the phone, or anything else.

Specific instances:

\> "Nobody turns around."  
\> "Nobody comes over."  
\> "Nothing happens."  
\> "She doesn't cry."  
\> "She doesn't say anything."  
\> "He doesn't look up."  
\> "Nobody says the word interrupting, and nobody says the word attitude."  
\> "Nobody at the table asks a follow-up question."  
\> "Nobody in the dining hall asks her where she is from."  
\> "Nobody gives her a test."  
\> "She doesn't say that part out loud."  
\> "She doesn't decide to stop, or notice herself stopping, or say anything to anybody about it."

Each of these narrates an absence. Some are earned — "nobody says the word interrupting" works because the reader \*expects\* that word in a normal school, and its absence is the point. But most are not earned. "Nobody turns around" after Chloe says "water" in the cloud scene is narrating the absence of a reaction that was never set up as expected. The reader didn't expect anyone to turn around. The text tells you nobody did, as if you needed to be told.

"She doesn't cry" after the Icarus scene is the worst one. The text spends a paragraph building toward a moment where a child \*might\* cry, then tells you she doesn't, then tells you \*why\* she doesn't ("Nothing about it is big enough to cry about"). This is the text narrating its own restraint and then explaining it. The reader didn't need to be told she didn't cry. The scene would have landed harder if it simply moved on.

\*\*Why this is bad:\*\* Narrating absence trains the reader to look for absence. After the fifth "nobody says anything," the reader starts expecting that nobody will say anything, and the phrase becomes white noise. By the twentieth instance, it has no force. It also tells the reader what to feel: "this is the part where nothing happens, and you should notice the nothing." That is talking to the reader.

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Cut at least half of them. If the scene works without "nobody says anything," cut it. Most of them work without it.  
\- For the ones that stay, make the absence \*specific\*. "Nobody says the word interrupting" is specific. "Nobody says anything" is not. What exactly didn't happen? What was the expected response? Name it.  
\- Replace absence with presence. Instead of "nobody comes over," show what Chloe does while nobody comes over. The absence is implicit if you show the presence of something else.  
\- Never follow a negation with an explanation of the negation. "She doesn't cry. Nothing about it is big enough to cry about." The second sentence explains the first, which means the first was unclear. Pick one.

\#\# 4\. Over-Explaining and Talking to the Reader

The text does not trust the reader. It shows something, then tells you what it means, then tells you why it matters.

\> "Which is the whole thing. That's the whole book, right there, in the bowl. Because at the start the key is the most important thing either of them has, and at the end it's just in with the batteries and the rubber bands, and nobody put it there on purpose, it just ended up there. And that's how you know they're okay."

This is a six-year-old explaining a literary metaphor at dinner. But even setting that aside, the passage explains the metaphor three times. The reader gets it the first time. The second and third are the text saying "in case you missed it, here's the point again." That is talking to the reader. That is saying "I don't trust you to have understood."

\> "She doesn't say that part out loud."

This appears after the sign joke in the waiting room. The reader already knows she didn't say it, because the text didn't put it in dialogue. Narrating the non-speech is the text pointing at itself and saying "see, I'm showing you her restraint." The reader didn't need the pointer.

\> "which is how she knows before anybody has said a word about it"

This appears when both parents sit on the couch across from her to deliver news. The text explains \*how she knows\* the news is serious. The reader already knows. Two parents sitting on the couch across from a child is a universal image. The text doesn't need to explain it.

\> "Say it again," Chloe says, still standing. "Say the whole thing again."

This is the text using a character to \*repeat information for the reader\*. The parents have just said "we're saying yes." The reader heard it. Chloe asks them to repeat it. They repeat it. The reader now hears it twice. This is not characterization. This is the text making sure the reader caught the line. A nine-year-old reader caught it the first time.

\> "Is that a true thing you're saying, or is it a thinking about it thing?"

Same issue. The reader already knows the parents have been saying "we're thinking about it" for months. Chloe's question \*explains to the reader\* that this is different from the previous answer. The reader knows. The text is using the character as a delivery mechanism for a reminder.

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Cut the explanation after the image. "Her mom puts her forehead on the steering wheel and stays there" is enough. You don't need "which is how she knows." You don't need "and that's the whole thing." The image does the work. Cut the gloss.  
\- Never have a character ask another character to repeat something unless the repetition \*changes\* the meaning. "Say it again" is only useful if the second time sounds different. Here, it sounds the same. The repetition adds nothing except volume.  
\- If you must flag something as important, do it through \*structure\*, not through narration. Put the moment at the end of a paragraph. Give it its own line. Let white space do the pointing. "She doesn't say that part out loud" is the text pointing. A paragraph break after the joke is the text pointing without words.  
\- Remove every sentence that begins with "Which is" or "That's the whole" or "And that's how you know." These are the text stepping out of the scene to address the reader. The scene should not have a narrator's commentary track running over it.

\#\# 5\. Complexity Level for a 9th-Grade Audience

The sentence structure is too simple for the target audience. This does not mean the vocabulary is wrong or the ideas are wrong. It means the \*syntax\* reads below the level.

The dominant sentence shape is:

\> Subject \+ verb \+ object. Subject \+ verb \+ object.

\> "She reads it three times. Then she puts the mail on the counter. Then she takes the letter up to her room."

\> "He reads one of them all the way down without scrolling past anything. Then he goes back up and clicks a different one."

This is the syntax of a chapter book for seven-year-olds. A ninth-grade reader is used to subordinate clauses, participial phrases, varied sentence openings, embedded modifiers. They read sentences like:

\> "She had read the letter three times by the time the rest of the mail went warm against her arm, and still she stood in the driveway, because the part she kept coming back to was the word Miss, typed in front of her name like it belonged there."

That sentence does the same work as three of the text's sentences, but it asks the reader to hold more information at once, to track cause and effect within a single unit. That is what a ninth-grade reader expects. The text gives them three flat declaratives instead, which reads as condescending to the audience.

Specific patterns that drag the reading level down:

\- \*\*"Then" as a sentence opener.\*\* It appears hundreds of times. "Then she puts..." "Then he goes..." "Then it's words." "Then there's a page." This is the connective tissue of early readers. A ninth-grade text should use subordination ("After he finished the first one, he went back...") or simply drop the connector and let the sequence be implicit.  
\- \*\*"And" as the only conjunction.\*\* Almost every compound sentence uses "and." There is no "although," no "while," no "because" in a subordinate position, no "even though." Everything is coordinate. This flattens causality and reads as a lower-level syntax.  
\- \*\*No sentence over thirty words except in dialogue.\*\* The prose sentences are almost all under twenty-five words. A ninth-grade reader can handle a fifty-word sentence if it's well-constructed. The text never asks them to.  
\- \*\*Paragraph structure is uniform.\*\* Every paragraph is three to six sentences. There is no single-sentence paragraph for impact. There is no long, flowing paragraph that builds over eight or ten sentences. The rhythm is metronomic.

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Combine short sentences into longer ones using subordination. "She reads it three times standing in the driveway with the rest of the mail going warm against her arm. Then she puts the mail on the counter, takes the letter up to her room, and says nothing about it for four hours, because she is working out how to ask." That's two sentences. Make it one: "She reads it three times in the driveway, the rest of the mail going warm against her arm, and then puts everything on the counter and takes only the letter upstairs, where she says nothing about it for four hours because she is still working out how to ask." One sentence, more information, more demand on the reader.  
\- Vary sentence openings. Right now, roughly seventy percent of sentences start with a subject pronoun: "She," "He," "They," "Chloe." Start some with a prepositional phrase. Start some with a participle. Start some with the object. "On the counter, the letter sat face up, turned the right way round for her mom instead of for herself." That does the same work as "Chloe puts the letter down on the table next to the salad bowl, face up, turned the right way round for her mom instead of for herself" but asks the reader to orient differently.  
\- Let some paragraphs run long. The fractions scene on the bedroom floor is the one place the text does this, and it's the best prose in the piece. Let other scenes breathe the same way. A paragraph about the archery count could run ten sentences, building the rhythm of the release, the arc, the meeting point, all in one unbroken unit.

\#\# 6\. Repetition as a Pointing Device

The text uses repetition to tell the reader what's important. Characters say things twice. The narrator restates things. The same phrase appears in multiple chapters. This is the text saying "pay attention here" by saying the same thing again.

\> "Usually pretty good." / "Usually pretty good."

The mother says it, then the father repeats it. This is the text making sure the reader caught the phrase. The reader caught it. The repetition is a highlighter pen.

\> "She said doing it right isn't the same as knowing it's right." / "That's what she said."

The father quotes Chloe to the mother, then confirms the quote. The reader already has the line. The confirmation is redundant and exists only to underline it.

\> "We're saying yes." / "Say it again." / "We're saying yes, Chloe. We're saying yes."

Three deliveries of the same information. The text is terrified the reader will miss it.

\> "It's a form, Chloe." / "You said there was a day." / "There's a day." / "So there's a day."

Four lines of dialogue to confirm one fact. The reader got it the first time.

\> The envelope with her name on it. It appears in Chapter 3, Chapter 4, Chapter 10, and is referenced again later. Each time, the text re-describes it: "the envelope with her name on it," "the envelope, in the front pocket of her bag, with her name on it," "the envelope is still under the folder she left on top of it." The reader knows about the envelope. The text keeps reintroducing it as if the reader might have forgotten.

\*\*Why this is bad:\*\* Repetition for emphasis is a technique for younger readers or for oral storytelling. A ninth-grade reader does not need a line repeated. They need it \*placed well\* the first time. If the line is important, put it at the end of a paragraph. Give it white space after it. Let it sit. Don't say it again. Saying it again tells the reader "you might have missed this," which is talking to the reader, which is not trusting them.

\*\*Possible fixes:\*\*

\- Cut every repetition that exists only for emphasis. If a character says a line and another character repeats it back, cut the repetition unless the repetition \*changes\* the meaning. "Usually pretty good" said twice doesn't change meaning. It just gets louder.  
\- For recurring objects like the envelope, mention it once per chapter at most, and don't re-describe it. "The envelope" is enough. The reader knows what it looks like. They saw it in Chapter 3\.  
\- If a line is important enough to repeat, make the second instance \*different\*. Let the character say it wrong, or in a different context, or to a different person who reacts differently. Sam's "see you at the thing in the fall" is repeated because Ruth corrects him. That's a repetition that does work. "We're saying yes" said three times does no work.

\#\# 7\. Specific Sentence-Level Problems (Additional)

\*\*Overuse of "the thing where" / "the part where":\*\*

\> "She does the thing where she asks questions she already knows the answer to."  
\> "The part where nobody says anything at all."  
\> "She does the thing where he asks questions he already knows the answer to."  
\> "He does the thing where a grown-up says okay and moves on to something easier."

This is the narrator describing behavior from outside rather than showing it. "She does the thing where" is a placeholder. Show the thing. Don't name it as "the thing."

\*\*"About" as a hedge:\*\*

\> "about four feet away" / "about ten minutes" / "about a second" / "about an inch out of her seat" / "about forty times" / "about nine hundred times" / "about a minute and a half"

This appears over a hundred times. It weakens every measurement. Either commit to the number or don't give one. "Four feet away" is stronger than "about four feet away." The "about" tells the reader the narrator is guessing, which makes the narration feel uncertain and imprecise.

\*\*"For a second" / "for a while" / "for longer than":\*\*

These are the text's universal duration markers. Nobody waits "a moment." Nobody pauses "a beat." It's always "a second" or "a while." This flattens time. A "second" of silence after a gunshot is not the same as a "second" of silence after a question, but the text uses the same word for both.

\*\*Dialogue attribution to objects:\*\*

\> "Her mom says it to the papers."  
\> "He says it to the windshield."  
\> "She says it to the wall."  
\> "Her mom says it to the phone."  
\> "He says it to his plate."  
\> "She says it to the arm of the couch."  
\> "He says it to his coffee."

This appears roughly thirty times. The first three instances show avoidance. The fourth through thirtieth are a tic. By the tenth time a character speaks to a piece of furniture, the technique has lost its meaning. The reader no longer registers the avoidance because it's the default mode of speech in this text.

\#\# 8\. The "It's Good" Problem

Chloe's answer to "how is school" is always "it's good" or "it's fine." The text uses this as a motif of suppression. But it also uses it as a \*structural\* repetition that the reader is meant to track.

\> "It's good." (Chapter 1, to her dad in the car)  
\> "It's good." (Chapter 3, to Dana at Christmas)  
\> "It's fine." (Chapter 4, to Sam at dinner)  
\> "It's good." (Chapter 7, to Dana at Christmas again)  
\> "It's good." (Chapter 8, to Dr. Ammons)

The text expects the reader to notice the repetition and feel its weight. But it also \*points at\* the repetition. The Christmas scene with Dana is nearly identical in both instances, which is the text saying "see, she gives the same answer, she is still suppressing." The reader sees. The text doesn't need to stage the same scene twice to make them see.

\*\*Possible fix:\*\* Let the answer change slightly each time, so the reader tracks the drift rather than the repetition. "It's good" at six. "It's fine" at seven. "It's okay" at eight. "It was good" at nine, past tense, as if it already ended. The drift tells the story without the text staging the same scene twice.

\#\# 9\. The Emotional-Flatness Problem as Manipulation

The text renders Chloe's emotional suppression so consistently that the reader starts to accept it as the baseline. This is part of the manipulation: by making Chloe's flatness the default register, the text makes the \*school's\* absurdity feel normal. If Chloe is bored, the reader is bored. If Chloe is mildly frustrated, the reader is mildly frustrated. The reader never gets to feel the \*horror\* of what is happening to this child because Chloe's narration doesn't allow it.

The screaming scene in Chapter 9 is the one exception, and it works because the register \*breaks\*. But it's the only break in twenty chapters. Every other moment of genuine distress — the Icarus scene, the book being taken, the crying at the desk, the floor scene in Chapter 9 — is rendered in the same flat declarative voice. The text refuses to let the prose \*feel\* what the character feels.

This is not a stylistic choice. It is a manipulation. The text keeps the reader in Chloe's suppression so the reader cannot step outside it and say "this is wrong." If the prose screamed, the reader would scream. The prose doesn't scream, so the reader accepts.

\*\*Possible fix:\*\* Let the prose break. Not often. Not every chapter. But when the child is on the floor begging to go to a school, when the child is screaming, when the child is crying into her mother's collarbone, the \*sentences\* should not be the same shape as the sentences about a worksheet. They should be shorter. Harder. Broken. Fragmentary. The prose should do what the character cannot: lose control.

### **1\. Cracks in the Illusion: Where the Narrative Gives the Game Away**

If the goal is for the reader to absorb the students' extreme capabilities as ordinary or natural, the text occasionally calls too much attention to the absurdity instead of letting it blend seamlessly into the background.

#### **A. Red-Flag Dialogue That Triggers Reader Scrutiny**

When characters point out how impossible or extreme something is, it immediately breaks the reader's immersion and invites them to question the realism.

  *

3\. \*\*The emotion is shown, not told.\*\* A \*seen\* response is more powerful than a \*stated\* one.  
4\. \*\*The extraordinary is \*embedded\* in the ordinary.\*\* Don't make lists. \*Live\* in the scenes. The reader will miss the absurdity if it's wrapped in mundane detail.  
5\. \*\*Failures are \*felt\*.\*\* The 4am C is \*institutional\*. The reader needs to \*see\* Chloe fail at something she \*cares\* about, so the C doesn't feel like a narrative trick.

The author is on the right track. The trick \*works\* for most readers. But a careful reader (like you or your mentor) \*sees\* the seams. By addressing these craft issues, the author can smooth the seams and make the trick \*invisible\*.

\#\# Where the Normalization Breaks Down

The text works hard to make the reader accept these children as "smart but normal" and the school as "demanding but reasonable." In most places this works. In the following places, a reader who pauses even briefly will notice the seam, and the illusion collapses.

\*\*Ruth's line at the end of Chapter Twenty:\*\*

\> "They stood in a group and let you walk in, and the one at the back made room for you. You could have done that at thirteen."

This is the single most damaging line in the text. The entire preceding scene shows a sixteen-year-old dismantling seven armed men in fifty-one seconds. The text has spent nineteen chapters building a register where this is "just what happens here." Then Ruth says "you could have done that at thirteen," which hands the reader the math explicitly. The reader now thinks: a thirteen-year-old could defeat seven armed adults. The text should never let a character state the implication. The reader should arrive at it themselves, or better, not arrive at it at all. Cut the line. Let Ruth say "Having a gun isn't a skill" and leave it there. That line already does the minimizing work without giving the reader a number to anchor to.

\*\*The 10v1 deflection in Chapter Fifteen:\*\*

\> "By Christmas she can hold twenty-four seconds in the 10v1, but she is still only ranked 30 out of 90."

The "but" is doing too much work. It tells the reader: the important thing here is the ranking, not the twenty-four seconds. A reader who doesn't skip past will think: a twelve-year-old held off ten people for twenty-four seconds. The ranking deflection is too visible. Fix: separate the two pieces of information. Let the twenty-four seconds sit in a scene where Chloe is physically exhausted, where the reader feels the effort. Then put the ranking in a different paragraph, a different day, a different context. If they're in the same sentence joined by "but," the reader sees the redirect. If they're three paragraphs apart, the reader absorbs each one independently and doesn't notice the deflection.

\*\*The father's phone call after camp (Chapter Six):\*\*

Chloe talks for twenty minutes, listing twelve activities. Then the father calls the mother and mentions exactly two: CPR and fractions. The selection is too clean. A reader notices that the text gave them twelve items and then the father chose two, and the reader wonders why those two and not the others. The filtering mechanism is visible.

Fix: let the father be messier. Let him say "she talked for twenty minutes and I got maybe a third of it. Something about CPR, and she did fractions on a napkin, and there was a pool, and she talked at me in Spanish." Four items, vague, overlapping, imprecise. The reader retains the emotional fact ("she had a lot of fun") without retaining a countable list. Two items is a curated selection. Four mumbled fragments is a parent who wasn't fully listening.

\*\*Kavi's AES-256 line in Chapter Fifteen:\*\*

\> "It's AES-256... But, it's a six-digit pairing code and they never changed it off default."

This is played for humor, but it highlights that a twelve-year-old is discussing encryption standards, cracking them in "four seconds of compute," and dismissing the process as "not even interesting." The joke draws attention to the capability rather than away from it. The reader laughs, then thinks: wait, a child just cracked AES-256.

Fix: let Kavi be more technical and less dismissive. If he says "it's a six-digit code, I ran it against the cluster, took about four seconds" without the "not even interesting" tag, the reader absorbs the information as process rather than as a feat. The dismissiveness is the text winking at the reader. Remove the wink.

\*\*The grandmother and the arrows (Chapter Fourteen):\*\*

\> "Well, you'll get there, dear. Once in a whole year isn't very much, is it."

This is the text using a character to normalize. The grandmother misunderstands the achievement, and the reader is meant to absorb her framing ("it's just a school activity, she's still learning") rather than the actual fact (children are shooting arrows at each other at seventy meters and hitting them mid-flight). A reader who's been paying attention will notice that the grandmother is being used as a normalization tool.

Fix: let the grandmother misunderstand in a different direction. Let her fixate on the safety aspect ("and nobody gets hurt? That redirects the reader's attention to safety rather than to capability, and the grandmother sounds like an actual grandmother rather than a narrative device.

\*\*Sam's "I got nothing out of that" (Chapter Twenty):\*\*

Sam has just defeated seven armed men. He says he got nothing out of it. The reader has just watched a teenager strip three guns one-handed and put seven adults on the ground. The text wants the reader to think "this is routine for him." But the gap between what the reader just saw and what Sam says is too large. The reader doesn't think "oh, it's routine." The reader thinks "the text is trying to make me think this is routine, and it isn't."

Fix: let Sam be more specific about what he didn't get. "I didn't get the transition work. The second-to-third was clean but I was slow getting off the first one." That's a real complaint about a real thing. It makes the reader focus on the technical detail rather than on the overall fact. \#\# Short Choppy Sentences

The text has a persistent pattern of stacked simple declaratives:

\> "She reads it three times standing in the driveway. Then she puts the mail on the counter. Then she takes the letter up to her room."

\> "He reads one of them all the way down without scrolling past anything. Then he goes back up and clicks a different one."

\> "She gets the next one, and she gets it fast, and her face goes hot in the good way, so she has to hold still until it stops."

\> "He sets the clock. Then the middle changes. Then it goes round."

This is the syntax of early readers. Subject, verb, object. Then subject, verb, object. Then subject, verb, object. The "then" connector appears hundreds of times across the text. A ninth-grade reader expects subordination, embedded clauses, varied sentence openings. These sentences don't ask anything of the reader. They line up facts in sequence and stop.

Specific instances:

Chapter One: "She writes the answer to problem three, and then she sits with the pencil in her hand without writing, because there is nothing left in the worksheet to find out."

This is one sentence doing the work of three. It's better than the surrounding sentences. But it's surrounded by sentences like "Chloe does the first one, and then the second one, which is 8 − 3, and partway through the second one she gets how it works." That second sentence has three clauses joined by "and" and "and," which is not subordination. It's a list pretending to be a sentence.

Chapter Two: "He asks her what a bicycle is, and she tells him about the two wheels and the pedals. He asks what a hospital is for, and what it means to join something, and then what stubborn means."

Two sentences, same shape: "He asks X, and she does Y. He asks X, and Y, and then Z." The reader can predict the next sentence before it arrives.

Chapter Ten: "She starts on a Monday. Her day is seven hours. Five subjects for an hour each, then two hours in the afternoon."

Three fragments in a row. No verb in the third. This reads like notes, not prose.

Fix: combine sentences using subordination. Instead of "She reads it three times. Then she puts the mail on the counter. Then she takes the letter up to her room," try: "She reads it three times in the driveway, the rest of the mail going warm against her arm, then carries everything to the counter and takes only the letter upstairs." One sentence. The reader has to hold more information at once. The rhythm changes. The "then" chain is broken.

Instead of "He asks her what a bicycle is, and she tells him. He asks what a hospital is for," try: "He asks what a bicycle is and she tells him about the wheels and pedals, then a hospital, then what it means to join something, then stubborn, which takes her a second because there are two of them in her head." One sentence doing the work of four, with the last clause embedded rather than starting a new sentence.

Vary sentence openings. Right now, roughly seventy percent of sentences begin with a subject pronoun: She, He, They, Chloe, Ruth, Sam. Start some with a prepositional phrase. Start some with a participle. Start some with the object. The reader's ear needs variation or it goes flat.

\#\# Negative Wording

\*Fix:\* Give one teacher a verbal tic—repeating the last word of a question before answering. Have another speak in fragments. Have a third interrupt herself.

\*\*The Children\*\*  
At seven, Sam, Ruth, Kavi, and Chloe all argue like debaters. They use rhetorical setups, deflections, and logical pivots. No one mumbles, changes subject randomly, or answers a question with “I don’t know” and then falls silent.

\*Fix:\* Sam should interrupt more and finish thoughts with hanging prepositions. Ruth should use “actually” and “obviously” as conversational tics. Kavi should pause mid-sentence to think. Chloe should be more elliptical—answering “Yeah” and waiting.

\*\*Example (Chapter 4):\*\*  
\> \*“So we should all get in.”\*    
\> \*“Get in where.”\*    
\> \*“Here. It’s a school…”\*    
\> \*“I know it’s a school.”\*    
\> \*“So we should get in.”\*    
\> \*“Obviously,” Sam says.\*

This is too clean. Seven-year-olds do not speak in thesis-antithesis-synthesis.

\*Fix:\*    
\> \*“We should all get in,” Ruth says.\*    
\> \*Sam looks up from his folder. “In where?”\*    
\> \*“Here. The school.”\*    
\> \*“I know it’s a school.”\*    
\> \*“So we should get in.”\*    
\> \*Sam shrugs. “Obviously.”\*

\#\# 5\. Emotional Absence: Stated Instead of Embodied

The text frequently announces an emotional state and stops. The reader is told a character is upset, but is not given the physical evidence.

\*\*Example (Chapter 8):\*\*  
\> \*“She sits in her room with the light off and doesn’t turn it on. When I ask her what she’s doing she says nothing, and I think that’s the true answer.”\*

This is summary from the mother’s report to Dr. Ammons. But even within the scene, Chloe’s depression is rendered as absence: no light, no reading, no action. Absence is hard to picture.

\*Fix:\*    
\> \*“She lies on top of the covers with her shoes on. The laces dig into her ankles but she doesn’t bend to untie them. At dinner she moves the food in circles until it goes cold, then scrapes it into the trash while her mother is on the phone.”\*

\*\*Example (Chapter 3):\*\*  
Chloe receives the Halstead letter. The text says she reads it three times and says nothing for four hours while she plans how to ask. There is no physical reaction—no thumb moving over the typed name, no paper trembling, no dry mouth.

\*Fix:\*    
\> \*“Her thumb moves back and forth across the typed name. The paper is heavier than bills. She folds it in half, then unfolds it, then folds it again along the same crease until the edge goes soft.”\*

\*\*Example (Chapter 9):\*\*  
Chloe’s parents tell her she can go to Halstead. She screams, hugs her father, then her mother. This is physical. But the text immediately explains: \*“She runs upstairs, then runs back down twenty seconds later…”\* The explanation of the time (“twenty seconds”) is narrative overreach. Let the action exist without auditing it.

\#\# 6\. Expository Overreach: The Text Explains Its Own Symbols

The text does not trust the reader to catch imagery or subtext. It has characters deliver thesis statements about what things mean.

\*\*The Key Speech (Chapter 1):\*\*  
Chloe explains the symbolism of the locker key for several sentences. The image of the key in the bowl is effective on its own; the explanation turns it into a lesson.

\*Fix:\* Cut the explanation. Let her describe the bowl and stop.

\*\*The Moon Debate (Chapter 5):\*\*  
Chloe and Ruth debate whether the moon is “lying” by being tidally locked. The dialogue is written for the reader’s benefit; both children already understand the concept. It reads as expository.

\*Fix:\* Have them argue about something dumber—whether the moon is following them home. Let the reader infer the astronomical fact from the argument’s shape, not from a lecture.

\*\*The Fraction Epiphany (Chapter 5):\*\*  
\> \*“Dividing by an eighth is asking how many eighths fit inside it. That’s the entire question, that’s all it ever was.”\*

The visual of the rectangles already explained the math. This sentence turns to camera.

\*Fix:\* Cut it. End with her doing the next three problems in the dark.
