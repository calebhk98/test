# Halstead Curriculum Grid

Working reference for the ch10–20 rewrite.

**Bold** = anchored in existing text. Plain = proposed, move freely.

---

## The model

**Day:** 10 one-hour blocks. **Five subjects, then the two afternoon hours, then three more subjects.** The afternoon sits in the middle of the day, not at the end.
**Terms:** 3 per year.
**Ramp:** 5 + 2 in the arrival term, 6 rising to 7 at age 8, full 8 + 2 from age 9.

### Six permanent academic slots

Once started they never rotate out.

| Track | Starts | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Language | 7–8 | New one every year. No continuation. |
| Mathematics | 7–8 | **The thing the formula is about and a week.** Runs to research level. |
| Writing | 7–8 | Becomes **Hearn's ten pages** at 10 and stays weekly. |
| Literature | 7–8 | No reading list, ever. She reads what she wants and is marked on the writing. |
| Politics | 8 | **Never ends. No final. Updated weekly.** |
| Political history | 7–8 | **Splits off from history in her second term** and runs continuously. The other half of that split, **drawing the world on blank paper**, runs inside it and never stops. Geography has no separate year because it never left. |

### Two rotating academic slots

Refilled each term, so up to 6 new subjects a year. These are the only academic subjects that come and go.

### Two afternoon blocks

They are not the same kind of hour.

**Block one is compulsory and the school fills it.** Assigned physical and practical work before ten, then **fighting, daily, permanent from ten**, then marksmanship sharing the block from eleven. This is why she can ask to drop dance at eight and be told no, and why **the teams are assigned and you don't get to trade.**

**Block two is hers.** She adds and drops what she likes, whenever she likes, and nobody records any of it. This is where **the forge on Thursdays** lives, which is what makes it a thing she chose rather than a thing she was given. Nobody would have noticed if she had stopped.

### Teaching, from 13

**Always the year below.** Thirteen teaches twelve, fourteen teaches thirteen, fifteen teaches fourteen, on to graduation. One session a week, and **how they do is part of her own record.**

**Her cohort is the oldest in the building.** There is no year above her, which means she was never taught by a student. Adults taught her for six years, and then at thirteen she starts teaching and it runs downward only. Her year is the only one that will never have received it, and the twelves she teaches will teach the elevens next year, and so on down. Nobody remarks on the asymmetry, including her.

*This also means her cohort is the pilot for everything. Whatever the school is doing, it is being run on them first.*

### The hidden ramp

**The nominal standard never changes. The hours available to meet it shrink every year.**

Ten pages a week at ten and ten pages a week at seventeen. A week for the maths problem at eight and a week at seventeen. Nobody ever says the standard went up, because it didn't.

What moves is taught instruction time, as teaching, lab work, and her own research take blocks out of the eight.

| Age | Taught hours | Taken by |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 9–12 | 8 | — |
| 13–14 | 7 | teaching, lab (alternating) |
| 15–16 | 6 | teaching, lab |
| 17–18 | 5 | teaching, lab, own work |

By seventeen she gets five hours of instruction where she got eight, and the essay is still due Wednesday.

*This is the grading mechanism applied to time. A visible standard increase is something the school did to her and she would notice and have a feeling about it. A shrinking denominator is something she absorbs. She experiences it as having less time, which reads to her as a scheduling problem rather than an escalation, and she solves it the way she solves everything, by doing more than was asked. The C for stopping four armed men and ten pages a week at seventeen are the same instrument.*

### Two standing rules

**No repeats.** A continuous track is not a repeat. A subject that appears, stops, and returns years later, with or without a numeral, is the pattern to kill. Nothing on this grid does that.

**No credentials.** Halstead awards nothing and enters no one for anything. The bar at sixteen is the only external exam any of them sits, and Amberg's reason is that you'll sign a lease. Everything else is coursework. By eighteen Chloe has the material of a mathematics doctorate, a philology degree, a political history degree, and a surgical training, and no piece of paper says so, and it has never occurred to her that one might. The first time any of it is counted is by a stranger, from outside, in the file at ch22.

One a year, graded by a ten-page report written in the target language: a week of newspapers, a political system, a legal document, a technical paper.

| :---- | :---- | :---- |

| 7–8 | **Spanish**, through the arrival term | I |
| 8 | French | I |
| 9 | **Mandarin** — behind all year, finishes behind, stays the one she reads and never speaks | IV |
| 10 | **Russian** — **loves it, can't explain why.** Still what she reaches for when angry, two years on | III |

| 12 | Arabic | IV |

| 15 | Hindi | III |

**Standard is reading and writing.** Speaking is uneven. At 21 Whitaker asks for three and she gives him three.

Two things have to be true and neither needs stating on the page: they use them on each other at their own table, which converts classroom hours into something else; and the eleven are not equal, with Mandarin readable and unspeakable.

## Mathematics

Calibrated against the medical track. Someone who can operate at sixteen is further along in medicine than measure theory at sixteen is in mathematics, so the tail runs past coursework and into work.

| Age | Content |
| :---- | :---- |
| 7–9 | **No formulas given. You get the thing the formula is about and a week.** |
| 10 | Algebra |
| 11 | Calculus |
| 12 | Multivariable calculus, linear algebra |
| 13 | Differential equations, real analysis — the proof year, where it stops being computation |
| 14 | Abstract algebra (groups, rings, fields), probability theory |
| 15 | Complex analysis, number theory, point-set topology |
| 16 | Measure theory, functional analysis |
| 17 | Algebraic topology or differential geometry, plus a reading course to the current literature in one area |
| 18 | An open problem. You work it for the year and you produce something. Almost nobody closes it. |

Algebra at 10 and calculus at 11 each get one clause in passing and no remark. Nothing else in the track is ever named on the page.

**The open problem produces real work and nobody is told what counts.** A year of research yields what research yields: a partial result, a special case, a counterexample, a better bound, a reformulation that makes the thing tractable for somebody else. Closing the problem is not the expected outcome and never was.

Nobody at Halstead says this. So ninety-one seventeen-year-olds each produce a genuine piece of mathematics, and each of them privately concludes they came up short, because the problem is still open and that is the only measure any of them has.

**Chloe's is real and she thinks it is nothing.** Ruth's is bigger. Neither is described as remarkable by anyone. *Same instrument as the forge (four years, one hook, which is a thing she made) and the minute against ten (she gets forty-one seconds, which is forty-one seconds against ten people).*

Number theory at 15 means she was breaking ciphers at 13 before she understood why they work, which is the right order for this school.

## Marksmanship

Four stages. Each one defeats the method that worked in the stage before, and nobody ever says that is what is happening.

| Age | Stage | Why the old method fails |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 8 | **Nerf** | Nothing yet. **A dart is fat and slow and about as hard to hit as a walking pigeon.** Reaction is enough. |
| 10 | Paintball | Roughly 90 m/s. Across a gym that is under two tenths of a second, which is at or below reaction time. Reaction stops working. You have to lead it. |
| 11 | **Arrows** | You cannot lead what you cannot see. **A recurve arrow is about three times faster than a dart and a fraction of the width, and at seventy meters it is coming at you end-on.** Nobody sees it. So you stop looking and you count. |
| 14 | **Rifles** | Behind glass, fixed mounts, known ballistics. **Same progression: paper, then moving, then thrown, then the count.** |

**Bell's number.** The draft says an arrow is fifteen times faster than a dart. It is about three. Cut the figure or fix it, because the argument does not need it: **her case is that an arrow is invisible, not that it is fast.** A paintball is a fat bright sphere crossing in front of you. An arrow is a seven-millimetre shaft pointed at your eye. *This is also what resolves the paintball drill and the arrow lecture sitting four beats apart in the same chapter. Speed was never the axis.*

### The escalation nobody announces

**Bell says nobody can do it and she means it.** Then two hundred children start doing it.

Her cohort is the oldest in the building, so **the curriculum above any given age does not exist until they reach it.** Every increase is invented in the year they arrive at it, by adults who did not expect to need one.

| Age | What changes |
| :---- | :---- |
| 11 | Metronome. Seventy metres. Fixed interval. **First intercepts in the last week of October.** |
| 12 | Metronome goes away. You hold your own count and call it. |
| 13 | The interval stops being fixed. It changes between rounds. **By thirteen most of the year can do it some of the time. Odile does it nearly always.** |
| 14 | Lanes shorten. Less hang time at the top of the arc, so the window narrows. |
| 15 | Wind. The meeting point moves and you have to agree where it went. |
| 16 | **The machine.** It fires horizontally at a target and they stand off to the side, shooting across the path. Nothing is ever pointed at a person. |
| 17 | They are above fifty percent on the machine, because it is consistent and they have read it. |

*None of this is ever framed as an increase. It is just the next unit, which is what school always is. The students cannot catch it. The reader can, from Bell's absolute language, then them doing it, then harder versions arriving with no fanfare and nobody ever saying we did not think you would get here.*

*This is the first crack in the school's competence and it is invisible from inside. Amberg is running an untested design and finding out whether it works at the same time they do.*

### Why the machine is a different subject

The apogee drill was forgiving in two ways Bell says out loud. **At the top of the arc it stops climbing and hasn't started dropping, so if one of you is late the arrow's barely gone anywhere.** And **if you're late the meeting point slides that way or this way, it doesn't slide sideways.** Late along the lane is survivable. Late sideways is the one that kills you.

Horizontal flight removes both. Full speed the whole way, no hang at the top, and they are standing perpendicular to the path, so timing error now translates straight into missing. There is no forgiving axis left.

And the part that makes it a new problem rather than a harder old one: **they were counting with a partner who was also counting.** The machine is not cooperating and will not tell them its cycle. The count survives. The agreement does not.

**The machine is consistent, and that is how they beat it.** It has a fixed rhythm and by seventeen they have read it and are hitting better than half. Nobody built a version that varies, because nobody expected them to get this far. *Same shape as apogee: an adult sets a standard believing it to be a ceiling, and the children turn it into a floor.*

## Block two: the electives

A long list, posted, and nobody guides you through it. You take what you want, you drop what you want, and nothing is recorded anywhere.

**The catalogue.** Drawing · painting · pottery · sculpture · glassblowing · bookbinding · calligraphy · the forge · woodworking · auto mechanics · electronics and radio · model building · photography · film · instruments · composition · choir · theatre · improv · fencing · climbing · sailing · riding · orienteering · chess · go · bridge · poker · gardening · beekeeping · entomology · observational astronomy · baking · weaving · whittling · juggling

*The list is longer than anyone reads and includes things nobody has taken in years. That's the point. The school offers everything and recommends nothing.*

### Chloe's four, ages 8 to 11

| Age | Choice | What happens |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 8 | Choir | She **sang in the shower all summer, badly and at volume**, so she picks singing. She is not good and drops it inside a term. The first thing she ever chose for herself and it didn't work. |
| 9 | Riding | Because Priya does it. She drops it when Priya's afternoons change. Chosen for the person, not the thing. |
| 10 | Instruments | Sticks. **A mediocre player and unusually good at writing down what she hears.** |
| 11 | Woodworking | Beyond the compulsory shop at eight. **You make a tool and then use that tool to make the next one**, and eventually the next tool has to be metal. |

Then the forge at twelve, and she never changes again.

*The list scene belongs at eight, the first time she has ever been handed a choice with no correct answer on it. She has spent her whole life being told what to do next by people who were wrong about her, and now nobody will tell her anything, and she picks badly. Nobody corrects her. Nobody notices she dropped it.*

## Fighting: the format

An hour a day from ten. Groups of eleven. One in the middle, ten on them, a minute on the clock, then rotate until everyone has taken the middle. Two or three times through, then pairs for the rest of the hour.

The scores are her standing number at each age, not an annual event: **31 at ten, 38 at twelve, 45 at fourteen, 41 at fifteen.** Odile does a minute and nine at fifteen; three others in the year do the minute.

The unstated consequence: in every group she spends ten rounds attacking and one being attacked. By fifteen she has attacked each of those ten people several hundred times and they've done the same to her. Kowalczyk's line about them getting better than nothing is much colder with that in the reader's hands, and he never says it.

## What she is actually bad at

Three different things, and the book currently uses the same words for all of them. You need to know which is which while you write.

### 1. Ordinary in absolute terms, bottom in relative terms

**Drawing. Dance.** She is decent at both by any normal measure. Most people would call her work fine. Ranked against ninety-one people who are all like her, she is last, and the school reports the relative fact because that is the only fact it reports.

She hears *I am bad at this.* A reader paying attention can see she is perfectly average, which is the thing nobody in the building would ever say out loud.

**She takes each of them for one year, at eight, and never again.** At fifteen she still describes herself as bottom of her year in drawing, in the present tense, on a single measurement taken seven years earlier that she has never once re-examined. *That is the whole book in one detail. She takes a reading and keeps it forever, and nobody ever tells her the reading expired.*

### 2. Exceptional in absolute terms, middling in relative terms

**Sixty percent on rifles. Forty-one seconds against ten. Sixth of nine on the paper. Failing the bar by four. The C on the intrusion. Fencing by sound.**

Every one of these is remarkable. Every one of them is reported to her as a shortfall, because the comparison class is ninety-one people who can also do it. This is the engine of the book and it runs on her never once seeing the other denominator.

### 3. Genuinely good, self-rated poor, and comparison has nothing to do with it

**The forge. The release count.**

She is good at the forge. Possibly better than most of her year, and told so. She rates herself low anyway, and not by measuring against anybody. She sees flaws in her own work at a resolution nobody around her can perceive, and **she is not wrong about them.** Ruth could stand next to her and not see the thing she is looking at.

Nobody grades the forge. So she grades it, and she is stricter than the school.

**This is the only place in the book where the criticism comes from her rather than from Halstead**, and it is the reverse of everything else: her judgement exceeds the standard instead of falling short of it.

The same instrument runs the count. **Her release runs early when she's keyed up and late when her hands are cold**, which nobody asked her to notice, and **she practises it walking to dinner, four fingers going against her leg.** Same person, same resolution, same two places that are hers.

*Consequence for the forge scene: "one thing in four years that she'd show anybody" is not four years of failure. She made plenty. She judged nearly all of it unfit. Identical words, opposite content. Mr. Pruitt's "your heat's wrong" and keeping walking is a real correction given to somebody competent, not a dismissal.*

*Consequence for her flatness everywhere else: she is indifferent to grades because grades belong to other people. The forge is hers, so her own standard applies. That is why the C costs her a week and the hook costs her four years.*

### Deliberately absent

**Social engineering.** Nadia clones a badge with forty seconds of small talk at sixteen using psychology, negotiation, and improv, which she assembled herself out of three legitimate classes. Naming it as a course would make the school look like it teaches manipulation on purpose, which is much less interesting.
