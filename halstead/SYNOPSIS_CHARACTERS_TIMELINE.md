# Chloe / Halstead — Synopsis, Characters, Timeline

Companion document to the issues list. Everything here is reader inference from the eight drafts supplied. Where the text is silent I've said so rather than filled in.

---

# PART ONE: SYNOPSIS

## The short version

A bored six-year-old is misdiagnosed as an attention problem, correctly identified as unchallenged, and then abandoned by a school system that has nothing to offer her for two more years. An unsolicited letter arrives from an institution nobody can trace. Eleven years later she is one of ninety-one graduates who are, without knowing it, the most capable people of their generation — and the only ones who have ever caught the man who built them.

## The full arc

**Chloe Kessler is six and there is a folder about her in the school office.** Her kindergarten teacher started it, her first-grade teacher added to it, and in December the pediatrician wrote something down. The word all three of them use is *attention*. From inside her head nothing is wrong. She solves the logic of a thirty-problem subtraction worksheet at problem two and then looks out the window for the rest of it. She reads ahead in the class novel and gets her book confiscated, which she does not understand: she got in trouble for reading, in reading. When the class hamster falls off his cage she says he's Icarus, and a girl called Kayleigh Burns tells her she talks weird, and Chloe puts it somewhere and keeps it. In November a teacher asks what clouds are made of and Chloe almost says they're the ocean going somewhere else, and says "water" instead, and it works, and it works every single time after that. She concludes the problem is her: everyone is equally bored and she is simply worse at tolerating it. By the end of first grade she is quiet, compliant, and friendless.

**On March 4th a psychologist tests her and finds nothing wrong.** She overhears him through a door left ajar: she finishes in the first two minutes and has thirty-eight minutes with nowhere to put them. Her mother asks what to do. The gifted program starts in third grade. That's two years. In the car her mother offers to tell her what he said and Chloe says no. Then nothing changes at all. Same worksheet, same twenty-eight problems, and Chloe learns that if you finish first and sit still, nobody talks to you, and that this is the whole trick.

**In July, a letter arrives addressed to her.** A school called Halstead. Four weeks, residential, everything free, nobody applied. The district has no record of it and says be careful. Her mother interrogates them for twenty-five minutes and gets every safety question answered instantly and one question deflected: where the name came from. Her father finds an expensive website and a faculty roster with an Olympic swimmer, a former Navy SEAL, and a researcher whose paper his department circulated. The school is four years old and he cannot work out who is paying for any of it. They argue for nine days and go, because she's been flat since Christmas.

**Camp is the best month of her life.** She makes a joke about being housed in the room named for the planet that isn't a planet anymore, and has a full panic response before anyone can react, and a boy called Sam laughs and says it's a room-shaped object. Ruth argues with her about Pluto's classification. Kavi is silent for six days and then never stops. She watches Ruth correct a teacher and nobody says *interrupting* and nobody says *attitude*. She is also, for the first time, the slow one: she needs a night alone on the floor with rectangles to understand why you flip the second fraction, and Kavi already knew. A boy called Owen goes home in week two and Chloe cannot construct any theory of him. At the last dinner she recites the month to her father — fractions, negative numbers, a bridge that held eleven pounds, karate, wound care, CPR, Spanish, a robot arm, a room-sized computer kept cold — and her father puts both hands flat on the table and says "cool," and later, on the phone, tells her mother he doesn't know what that is.

**Then she goes home, and second grade destroys her.** She cannot produce a single friend's surname; the school won't share family contact details; she waits by the phone for eleven days. The first math sheet is adding with carrying. She says something about time capsules that comes out before she can stop it and Bryce Dolan tells her she was weird before and now she's weirder. In September she cries at her desk and "I don't know" is the completely true answer. She asks to go back every day for four months. Her mother fights the school instead and wins her early entry to the gifted program, which turns out to be one hour on Thursdays, nine kids, a cart, and a woman covering four buildings; Chloe finishes the packet in fifteen minutes and waits forty for the part where it gets hard. A doctor diagnoses depression at seven. Asked what she liked about the summer, she says "it was hard," and the doctor stops writing and writes that down. In November she screams that she hates her mother. In January she stops asking, and her parents are so relieved they don't examine it. In February her mother finds her on the floor between the bed and the wall, offering to do everybody's chores forever, to give back the bike, to give up her birthday. "I can't do it anymore." On March 14th they say yes. In April Halstead offers the family a free house ten minutes from campus and her mother refuses, because the day she wants to pull her out is the day they'd have nowhere to live. They can have her for the year. They're not getting the roof.

**Halstead is not a school for gifted children.** Over eleven years the curriculum accumulates: a language every year; mathematics where you get the object and a week and no formula; ten pages of argued prose weekly from age ten, marked against Alexander Hamilton; the political class of every country that matters, including spouses, children, in-laws and creditors, updated weekly, forever, because "the kids are why." Cryptography taught breaking-first. Negotiation with public scoreboards. Poker at real stakes. Economics with money that is genuinely theirs to lose. Law to bar standard by sixteen. Medicine past first aid. Psychology, specifically how people come apart under load. Fighting ten men at once, daily, from age ten. Marksmanship that runs nerf at eight, paintball and arrows at eleven, rifles at fourteen — and the skill being taught throughout is intercepting a projectile in flight, because a weapon you can't stop a projectile with isn't a tool. From thirteen you teach the year below and their results go on your record. There is a rotation called Watch, a scoring sheet on a corkboard written by the ex-SEAL, and its second rule is that outsiders learn nothing about your numbers, your capability, or your positions.

**At eleven Chloe writes fourteen thousand words arguing for area bombing**, having drawn it out of a hat, and feels sick for two days, and then stops being angry and starts being interested, which frightens her more than the anger did — and gets a B, for hedging. At twelve she is one of four woken at four in the morning by an alarm nobody announced. Four men are inside the building. Kavi breaks their radios in thirty seconds. Chloe walks around a corner in her pyjamas because nobody hits a small girl and they'll all spend four seconds deciding what to do with her while facing the same direction. It takes nine seconds. They tie the men, monitor their breathing, and argue about response times. She notices the bolt cutters are wrong for the fence and cannot make the wrongness mean anything and lets it go. They get a C. She reads it in the corridor and goes to Russian and doesn't mention any of it at Thanksgiving.

**The same year, she stops being able to talk to her parents**, because the explanations would need three prior explanations. At fourteen her mother finds out at a dinner table that she has been shooting rifles since September, and Chloe has no answer, because six other things started in September and she didn't mention those either. At fifteen a researcher tells her he won't ask her to sign anything because she's fifteen and that wouldn't be right, and that if she talks about the work it lands on the people who did sign. She thinks about it for a week and can't find anything wrong with it. The reflex he is using is the one she built at six to survive a first-grade classroom.

**At sixteen or seventeen, four of them sneak out** — the first students ever to do it — clone a badge, loop the cameras, and eat waffles for two hours. Seven men with three guns try to rob them in a parking lot on the way back. Three of them go over a fence and watch from a loading dock roof, critiquing footwork, while Sam handles it in fifty-one seconds. Then they check all seven men for injuries, put a shoulder back in, and leave. They argue the whole way home about whether the waitress could take Sam.

**At eighteen, ninety-one of them apply to college and every one of them gets in everywhere.** Admissions offices assume the transcripts are formatting errors. Thirty institutions open files. A Caltech representative reads an internal paper for six hours and asks to speak with the third author, who is sixteen and in class. Sam looks at MIT's four percent admission rate and concludes it's marketing, because Halstead taught him that institutional numbers are signals. Halstead offers all of them research posts at three times market with housing and cars, and eighty of them leave anyway. There has never been a graduation, so they put out too many chairs.

**They scatter.** Chloe wants the Foreign Service and finds she can't sit the exam until twenty, so she takes a translation job and a state school and waits. Ruth goes to MIT and spends a year convinced she has been placed in a remedial track, and files a complaint, and looks for the right office. Sam joins the Army, maxes the fitness test in week two and again in week three because nobody told him a test could be over, and asks a captain when the real assessment is. Nadia starts a company, interviews four hundred people, and keeps making the take-home easier until it's what she'd have given a twelve-year-old, and the pass rate is under a third. Eli finds five holes in his employer's systems in an afternoon after his boss jokes about a bounty. Theo becomes a federal analyst. Kavi stays. Every one of them concludes that the story means something about the other person.

**Ruth breaks first.** A professor she is fond of cannot follow her past the third step of something and says so cheerfully, and she walks out and sits on a wall for a long time. Then she goes and finds population base rates — second languages, adult reading level, fractions, how many people have ever been in a fight — and posts them in the group chat with no commentary, four times, and each time somebody makes a joke inside a minute. In April she stops posting, because she has understood something and does not want to be the one who says it. Six months later she says it: we are not slightly ahead, I don't think we're the same kind of thing. Asked why she waited, she says she didn't want it to be true. Then she asks the real question of the book: somebody wrote to my mother when I was six, a person sat down and picked me, and I want to know why it was me.

**Theo has read the file.** Nineteen years of federal work on Halstead, revised twice, still wrong, with one caveat that has never moved off the bottom of the page: the funding cannot be traced. A man was placed undercover on the teaching staff and identified by children in a day and a half. Four operators went over the fence at four in the morning and were unconscious before anyone made a decision, and the report notes that the children were arguing about their own performance and that one of them asked what grade he'd have given them. An adult arrived, sent the children to bed, and told four federal officers that an attack on a school in the United States would not be accepted. There is no further entry.

**So seven of them go and find him.** Eli builds a financial worm designed to be boring, Kavi builds a watcher for it, and in eleven weeks Ruth argues it into a proof: not from absence, the way the government did, but from presence. Gaps of three to five days with no calendar logic, because committees don't get flu. A decision band that drifts forty minutes earlier across twenty years, because that's a person getting older. And a preference for a marginally worse instrument, held since 2003, for no reason at all. One man. Ninety-nine point nine nine percent, and the remaining fraction is her being humble.

**Then they burgle the government**, over Theo's objection and with full knowledge of the sentencing ranges, and find files on all ninety-one of them — and an incident report on a night four teenagers ate waffles. Seven witnesses who cannot agree on the order. None who could describe any of the four. Every camera on the block corrupted exactly where the students appear and intact everywhere else, plus the surveillance team's own recordings, gone. *No known method.* Ruth built that box in a weekend when she was thirteen so they wouldn't get detention. It wasn't even hard. It was a fun problem. She wasn't trying. Asked how good they are, Eli says he genuinely doesn't know, because he has never once been at the top of a room in his life.

**And then somebody switches their worm off for nine minutes and switches it back on**, cleaning up so completely that the cleanup isn't there either. Chloe reads it as a message. Eli says no: you kill a tool, you follow it, or you feed it garbage, and you only hand it back if you think nobody will notice it went. He is very slightly wrong about how good we are. That is the only advantage they have, so they do nothing at all. Theo asks how many mistakes he's made that they didn't catch.

Chloe reads that at one in the morning, and goes in the next day, and does her job for a government that has been hunting this man since before she could read, and tells nobody anything.

# PART TWO: CHARACTERS

**On the numbers:** IQ figures are reader inference from performance, not from anything in the text — the manuscript never uses scores. Above roughly 160 the scale stops being a measurement and becomes extrapolation, so treat these as ordinal. Ranking is within the Halstead year of ninety, which is the only ranking any of them has ever been given.

---

## The core seven

### Chloe Kessler — protagonist

**Gender:** female. **Age span:** 6 to 21\. **Est. IQ:** 160–168, spiky. **Halstead rank:** explicitly middle, and she has been told so.

**Profile.** Verbal and analytical reasoning at the top of the range; processing speed and psychomotor ability notably lower, consistently, from the symbol-copying subtest at six through to being bottom of her year in drawing at fifteen. The split is the most important thing about her and the school makes it visible constantly, because Halstead measures across so many domains that her weak axis is always on display while her strong axis is invisible — everyone around her is also strong on it.

**Good at:** argument construction, steelmanning positions she finds repugnant, languages (eleven, per her clearance interview), political analysis, translation, reading people, holding a plateau for years without quitting, threat modelling, seeing what an argument is doing.

**Bad at:** anything timed and clerical, drawing, dance, fencing by sound, the forge (four years, one hook), lying with her face, asking for things, saying no to people she's loyal to.

**Likes:** difficulty, specifically. Reading. Being argued with. Deb from work and Deb's son's entire life story. Fractions once she got them. Being one of four sitting on a floor because nobody wants to go to bed.

**Dislikes:** repetition, waiting for the class, being told not to rush, being handled, being told things are fine when they aren't.

**Wants:** at seven, to go back to Halstead, which is the first thing she has ever asked for. At twenty, the Foreign Service. Underneath both: to be somewhere the water is deeper than she can stand in.

**Fears:** at six, that something is wrong with her — never stated, load-bearing for the whole book.

**Defining trait:** she does not read outcomes as information about herself. Twelve acceptance letters, a Foreign Service pass, a readership — all filed as things that happened rather than as evidence.

**Arc:** compliance → deprivation → collapse → immersion → competence → compartmentalisation → felony. The mechanism she built at six to survive a classroom is, by twenty-one, the reason she withholds from the United States government.

---

### Ruth

**Gender:** female. **Est. IQ:** 175+, top of anyone we see. **Rank:** top tier. Ninety-something percent on the rifles.

**Profile.** Generative rather than retrieving. Given a rule she immediately probes its domain — she gets fraction division partway through the explanation at six and asks whether it holds for negatives. Built a general-purpose surveillance-defeat tool in a weekend at thirteen that the federal government still cannot identify six years later.

**Good at:** mathematics, networks, engineering, forensic reasoning, argument, holding a factual distinction against social pressure ("it's not worse, it's what happened"), interrogation by forced binary.

**Bad at:** letting things go, being wrong in public, tolerating a joke when she needs a serious answer, asking for help.

**Likes:** correctness. Being argued with. Latin, contentiously.

**Dislikes:** surprises without notice ("four's fine, four's happened, they *tell* you"), arguments from outcome rather than reasoning, being alone with a true thing.

**Wants:** to know why she was chosen. That question is the engine of the last act and she is the one who asks it.

**Family:** a mother known to Meg by name, a father who sells insurance, a brother who gets into Halstead later.

**Arc:** the most complete in the book. Denial (the remedial track theory, held ten months) → the professor who can't follow her → solitary calibration → six months of silence → the confession → the proof. She is the first person of ninety-one to find out what they are, and it nearly destroys her, and what she does with it is find the founder.

---

### Sam

**Gender:** male. **Age:** seven and a half at camp, so slightly older than Chloe. **Est. IQ:** 155–165, lowest of the named core. **Rank:** forty percent on the rifles. Best fighter in the year by a wide margin.

**Profile.** The healthiest person in the manuscript, and it isn't because he's the most able. He has opted out of the ranking without opting out of the work. "Because I'm forty and I do other stuff. Go be miserable at Ruth, she's ninety-something."

**Good at:** fighting, above every professional benchmark the book supplies — a soldier with position, timing, surprise and ground gets under two seconds. Command presence. Making plans in ninety seconds and defending them before he's finished. Being liked.

**Bad at:** shooting relative to his peers. Patience with his own limits. Recognising when an institution isn't running a test.

**Likes:** schedules, which he has had every day since he was seven. Sergeant Ruiz. Being told to do it again. Waffles. Shopping trolleys.

**Dislikes:** being at forty percent, mildly. Being laughed at, acutely — one laughing face in a group chat lands hard enough that he won't discuss it for months, which is the only instance in the book of anyone caring what someone else thinks.

**Wants:** the real assessment, which he is certain exists and is being withheld from him.

**Defining trait:** no internal ceiling. Maxes a test in week two, does it again better in week three, because nobody told him to stop and it had not occurred to him that a test could be over.

**Detail:** sleeps with his window open and told everyone four times like it was a fact about the world.

---

### Kavi

**Gender:** male. **Est. IQ:** 165–175. **Rank:** upper tier. Passed the bar first attempt at sixteen.

**Profile.** Silent for six days on arrival at camp, then corrected Sam about volcanoes and never stopped. Already knew fraction division at six, which suggests somebody at home was feeding him before Halstead ever found him.

**Good at:** cryptography, networks, systems thinking, second-order defences nobody asked for. Builds the watcher that catches the nine minutes purely because he's him.

**Bad at:** letting an unjust mark stand — disputes a sensor note in writing, at length, and gets nowhere.

**Likes:** the lab. The problem being the interesting thing.

**Dislikes:** being told something is impressive when it took four seconds of compute.

**Wants:** to stay where the work is. He is the only one of the core who takes Amberg's offer: "It stopped being school for me a while ago. It's a lab that also has children in it."

**Defining trait:** correct scepticism applied indiscriminately. He is right about the performance reviews and wrong about MIT, using the same instrument.

---

### Nadia

**Gender:** female. **Est. IQ:** 160–170. **First appears:** the badge clone in the sneak-out, with no prior mention — see issues list.

**Profile.** The only one who doesn't go anywhere and the only one who builds something. Looks at two months of job listings and comes out baffled that everyone files labour under costs when it's the only resource that improves on its own, and that nobody is building anything for a person to use.

**Good at:** social engineering (a copied badge in forty seconds of small talk), physical work, attribution, hiring, running things.

**Bad at:** calibrating a standard. Makes her take-home easier and easier until it's a twelve-year-old's and still under a third pass, and concludes the world is broken.

**Likes:** being come at properly. "If you're going to come at me, come at me. Don't insult me with a cleaner."

**Dislikes:** being underestimated, cheap attacks, wasting a police officer's afternoon.

**Wants:** to build a thing an actual person uses.

**Defining trait:** proportionality. Fires the thief, traces the buyers in a week, walks into their office alone, declines to prosecute, and gets an apology.

---

### Eli

**Gender:** male. **Est. IQ:** 160–170.

**Profile.** Security. Finds five vulnerabilities in his own employer's systems because his boss joked about a bounty and Eli didn't know it was a joke, and one of them the company didn't believe was possible. Gets a lawyer instead of a million dollars and finds the lawyer the most interesting thing that has happened to him since he started.

**Good at:** building tools that don't look like tools. The financial worm's whole design constraint is that it must be boring and patient, which he identified as the group's temperamental weakness and engineered around.

**Bad at:** risk aversion. Argues that a felony is a rule with a known enforcement mechanism and you learn where the cameras are, and that if he's wrong he goes away for fifteen years, so he isn't going to be wrong.

**Defining line:** "I have never once been at the top of a room in my life." The most quietly devastating sentence in the manuscript.

---

### Theo

**Gender:** male. **Est. IQ:** 165–175.

**Profile.** Federal analyst. Interviewed four times and answered every question completely. His supervisor keeps returning his work asking him to show the reasoning, and he did show the reasoning, and he is apparently skipping five or ten steps every time and genuinely cannot see where.

**Good at:** reading things and saying what they mean, which is literally his job description.

**Bad at:** hiding what he knows once he knows it. Says no to the second worm for nine days and then signs off last, saying again that it's insane.

**Wants:** for it to be somebody else's problem. Does the only thing available to him, which is nothing, and then posts a hypothetical in the chat.

**Defining moment:** reads a classified report of a night he personally remembers as a boring Tuesday, and puts his hand over his mouth and sits like that for a while.

## Other Halstead students

### Odile

**Female.** The physical outlier. Nearly always intercepts the arrows. A minute and nine against ten, the highest number in the book. Has a scar on her hand from an ejection port. Got into every school she applied to. Her college essay mentions a self-defence assessment involving ten people in passing, as background, which is what triggers thirty institutions to open files.

### Priya

**Female.** Chloe's camp roommate. Talks about horses for nine minutes and falls asleep instantly. Still doing the horse thing years later. Takes a year in South America with a bag after graduation, which several of the others find genuinely confusing because there's no work in it — which makes her, arguably, the sanest person in the cohort. Badly underused; see issues list.

### Fen

**Gender: unstated.** From Michigan. Rock collection. Asleep by nine. Chloe's Halstead roommate at eight. One line, never returns.

### Owen

**Male.** Leaves camp in week two. Was crying in a stairwell on the Thursday. Four more leave after him. Chloe cannot construct any model of a person who didn't like the bridge, and gets loud about it, and thinks about it all day and gets nowhere. He is the book's only evidence that the invitation isn't universally a gift, and the reading the text supports is that he wasn't less capable — he was one of the roughly eighty families out of ninety who didn't have a parent in a hotel half a mile away.

## Family

### Meg (mother)

**Female.** Bachelor's in accounting. **Est. IQ:** 110–120, with unusually high emotional perception.

**Good at:** noticing. "She stopped talking. At home. She used to talk the whole time. She'd talk through a whole dinner. And in like September, she just, I don't know how to describe it. She got polite." That is a precise, hard-won description of a child disappearing, and she'd been carrying it for six months. Also excellent at institutional due diligence: calls the district, calls the neighbours to check the sample, interrogates the school for twenty-five minutes on staffing and licensing and medical cover, researches the faculty individually, identifies the funding anomaly, drives instead of using the bus, stays four days, and rings the emergency number from the hotel car park to confirm it works.

**Bad at:** sitting with a hard thing. Jokes about the confiscated book. Lies in the car ("so you're all good, everything's great"). Hears "nobody was mean to me" as being about bullying and lands on "you'll make friends this year," which is kind and completely wrong.

**Wants:** for her daughter to be all right. Spends nine days trying to keep Halstead away from her and is proved right in April, and capitulates in the same month.

**Best moment:** refusing the free house. "The day I want to pull her out is the day we don't have anywhere to live. They can have her for the year. They're not getting the roof." She has correctly identified a capture mechanism and kept one exit.

**Arc:** advocate → sentinel → defeated by her own daughter's collapse → the woman sitting in a parked car with the engine off, unable to come inside. She earns the emotional payoff of the camp section and doesn't get it; see issues list.

### Dad (unnamed)

**Male.** A professional whose department circulates papers; a bachelor's in something he doesn't use. **Est. IQ:** 120–130, the plausible genetic source.

**Profile.** Does not exist in ch1–2 (see issues list) and is central from ch3. Recognises a researcher's name off a faculty roster. His reasoning in the kitchen is genuinely good: he never argues the school is fake, he isolates the single unfalsifiable anomaly — how does a four-year-old school get that man, what does it pay him, with what — and uses website production spend as a proxy for capital. Then he overrides all of his own analysis on emotional grounds and knows it, and Meg calls him on it.

**Good at:** research, analysis, keeping pace with his daughter longer than anyone else. The Thanksgiving tariff conversation at ten is the last good one, and it's excellent.

**Bad at:** pushing when it matters. "Her dad says yeah, and doesn't push it."

**Best moment:** listening to his daughter move furniture over his head for ten minutes and realising he is looking at what she's supposed to be like and that he will see it about nine weeks a year.

**Defining line:** after she stops being able to explain things to him — "Her dad still asks. He asks every time."

### Grandmother (unnamed)

**Female.** Elderly enough that nobody expected her at graduation. The only outside observer in the entire manuscript who accurately assesses anything, and what she assesses is weight: "She's put on weight." "I know." "She was so thin at Christmas." Then tells three separate teenagers they seem like nice young people.

### Ruth's mother, Ruth's brother, Ruth's father, Kavi's mother

Unnamed. Ruth's mother is known to Meg by name and had already asked about admission when the girls were six. Ruth's father sells insurance and, per Ruth, has better security at work than four professional operators. Ruth's brother gets in later. Kavi's mother says Chloe looked tall at graduation.

## Pre-Halstead school

### Mrs. Aldana

**Female.** First grade. Not a villain, which is the point and is harder to write. Confiscates the book gently, with two fingers on the spine. Says "that's what I thought" and doesn't say it mean — she says it *tired*. That word is the whole indictment.

### Ms. Vance

**Female.** Second grade. Everyone says she's the nice one. Crouches next to Chloe when she cries and asks what's wrong. Also never looks up during the four seconds in which Chloe is told she's got weirder, which is a more damning portrait of ordinary schooling than anything Aldana does.

### Mrs. Prahl

**Female.** The gifted pull-out. Nine kids, a cart, four buildings. Chloe finishes four logic puzzles in fifteen minutes and asks when it gets harder, and Prahl says "let's not rush." She is the control case for what a mediocre gifted teacher actually sounds like, and she is the prize Meg fought six weeks for.

### Kayleigh Burns

**Female.** Delivers "you talk weird" the way you'd tell someone their shoe is untied, like she's doing her a favour — which is exactly how six-year-olds enforce norms. Returns in second grade with "why do you talk like that."

### Bryce Dolan

**Male.** Still counting on his fingers in first grade. In second grade delivers the single most accurate observation anyone outside Halstead ever makes about Chloe: "You were weird before and now you're weirder." Not cruel. He is reporting the weather, and he is right — she came back from camp with the brakes off.

### Aiden R.

**Male.** Reads aloud and stops at every fourth word.

### Ellie Peterson

**Female.** Neighbour. In the pull-out group. Did not get a Halstead letter, which is how the family establishes the invitation wasn't a mass mailing.

### Peanut

**Male, hamster.** Climbs toward the lamp, gets his back feet off the bars, falls into the wood chips. Is Icarus. Everything that happens to Chloe socially for the next fifteen years is set up in this scene.

---

## Clinicians

### Dr. Ben Prentice

**Male.** Psychologist. Beard, a small table, a bin of blocks, no exam bed. Tells her some of it is supposed to be hard and that's how it's built. Administers what is recognisably a WISC. Correctly identifies that the school is wrong: "I'm not seeing what the school is seeing. I think she finishes in the first two minutes and then she's got thirty-eight minutes left over and nowhere to put them." Identifies the lever as the school, learns the program is two years out, and drops out of the book entirely.

He is the only person in the story holding a formal cognitive profile of Chloe, he has a stated opinion that nobody is going to help her, and he is the most obvious candidate for who put her on the list. Underused; see issues list.

### Dr. Ammons

**Female.** Diagnoses depression at seven. Her question sequence about the summer programme — who ran it, how they found you, did she stay overnight, how far, did anything happen there, did she come back different — is both correct abuse screening and exactly what someone gathering information on Halstead would ask. Stops writing at "the happiest I have ever seen her in her life." Asks Chloe what she liked about it, gets "it was hard," and writes it down.

## Halstead staff

### Sinclair

**Male.** Former Navy SEAL. Wrote the Defensive Watch scoring doctrine, which has been on the same corkboard since before Chloe arrived. Runs the paintball exercise and says "good luck" in a tone suggesting he doesn't mean it. Arrives after the night intrusion, stops at the corner, looks at four tied men and four children sitting on the floor next to them, and stands there a second longer than he needs to — the only glimpse of an adult perspective on Halstead in the entire manuscript. Asks who's monitoring, asks for numbers, doesn't comment on Ruth having written them on her arm in pen, and sends them to bed. Tells four federal officers that an attack on a school in the United States would not be accepted.

### Mr. Hearn

**Male.** Writing and history. Puts Hamilton's arithmetic on the board with no motivational language attached and sets ten pages a week at less than half the rate. Asked whether it has to be as good, says yes, and that's the hard part, not the pages. Returns a paper with no note at all, which lands worse than a bad note. Holds the library alone for forty minutes against two hundred children, and when they finally get him says "that's fine" and puts his glasses back on. Marks Chloe's fourteen thousand words a B: you hedge, you keep apologising for the argument while you're making it, make it or don't.

### Kowalczyk

**Male.** Fighting. Explains ten-on-one like a bus timetable — five can reach you at once, six if they're good, seven if you're stupid about where you stand, so ten means five on you and five waiting to swap in, and a minute because you'll be tired first. Tells Chloe she isn't getting worse, they're getting better than nothing, and predicts it'll happen again around eighteen when the boys finish growing. That forecast is the tell that he is carrying a developmental model of these children years into the future — this is not indifference, it is calibration management.

### Coach Bell

**Female** (inferred from "the woman escorting him" is a separate character; Bell's gender is not explicitly stated — flag for the author). Marksmanship. ## The founder (unnamed, never appears)

**Gender:** male, per the group's usage. **Age:** the forty-minute circadian drift across twenty years puts him plausibly in his fifties or sixties by the end. **Est. IQ:** not meaningfully assessable, and the interesting answer is that he is not necessarily smarter than Ruth.

**What he did.** Founded Halstead roughly nineteen years before the end of the book. Selected children individually — a person sat down and picked each of them — through a mechanism nobody can trace. Staffed it with an ex-SEAL, Olympians, and researchers of note. Built a curriculum that produces languages, elite political mapping, cryptography, negotiation, law, medicine, close combat and marksmanship, and requires every student to teach from thirteen. Ran it at scale: eight hundred students below the first graduating cohort. Supplies hardware and pharmaceuticals that do not exist outside the campus. Offered eighty graduates three times market to stay and let them go anyway, into the Foreign Service, the Army, federal analysis, corporate security, MIT, and a startup.

**What he beat.** Nineteen years of federal investigation, revised twice and still wrong. An undercover officer placed on his staff, removed in a day and a half by children. Four operators over the fence, unconscious in nine seconds. Six years of passive surveillance producing no content from the graduates' channel on any occasion. And the money has never been traced.

**Where he fails.** He restored their worm after switching it off, believing nobody would notice a nine-minute absence — which is true of every organisation on Earth except the one he built. He is very slightly wrong about how good they are, and he is the only person alive who ever had grounds for an accurate estimate.

**Goals: unstated.** The inferable shape is generational placement rather than a strike capability. "Because the kids are why" is the thesis: you learn the political families because these students will be the counterparts of the next generation of power. He is not building a team. He is seeding institutions with people who will be in their fifties in thirty years, and he does not want it observed until it's done.

**The open question, and the book's real one:** Ruth asks why it was her. If the answer is that he was building people capable of eventually finding him, that is a very different novel than if he was building diplomats.
