# Risk Review — Outcome-Aligned Dating App

**Scope.** This is an adversarial review of `SPEC.md` v1.0, cross-checked against what is
actually implemented in `src/services/`, `src/http/`, `src/jobs/`, and `db/migrations/`.
It intentionally does **not** repeat the 13 ambiguities already catalogued in
`docs/conformance.md`'s "Open Questions / Spec Conflicts" section (OQ-1–OQ-13) except
where a finding here is a direct downstream consequence of one of those decisions (noted
inline). Every finding below is traceable to a specific spec section/mechanic or a
specific file/line in the codebase; findings marked *speculative* are reasoned from the
spec's own mechanics but not verifiable from code alone.

**Code-status legend.** `mitigated` = the code actually closes this gap. `partial` = code
reduces but does not close it. `open` = nothing in the current codebase addresses it.
`n/a (design-level)` = this is a property of the spec's product design itself; no amount
of correct implementation fixes it.

**Totals.** 48 findings: **11 critical**, **16 high**, **16 medium**, **5 low**.

## What the spec gets right (don't get talked out of these)

- **Mutual hard filters, strictly enforced, never overridden by the algorithm** (§9, §16.1).
  This is the single best trust-building decision in the document and is implemented
  faithfully (`filter.service.ts`, `eligibility.service.ts`). Keep it non-negotiable.
- **No swipe deck, no public like counts, no boosts, no pay-to-win visibility** (§10.1,
  §3). This is a genuinely differentiated stance against a well-documented harm pattern
  in the category (engagement-maximizing swipe mechanics), and it's actually built, not
  just declared — no boost/like-count field exists anywhere in the discovery response.
- **Refundable authorization holds instead of an immediate charge, captured only once
  both sides commit** (§14.2–14.3). This is the correct payments shape for a two-sided
  commitment device, and the partial-failure handling (one side's authorization fails →
  release the other) is implemented and tested per `docs/conformance.md` C-14.5.*.
- **Immutable payment ledger** (§14.8, `payment_ledger`). Good financial hygiene,
  actually append-only in the schema.
- **Explicit "prefer not to say" on sensitive questions, treated as neutral rather than
  forced** (§8.5). Correct instinct for reducing dishonest data — see ALG-4 for a tension
  this creates elsewhere, but the instinct itself is right.
- **Structured report categories with a `minor_suspected` fast path that doesn't wait for
  score accumulation** (§18.3, §18.5). The *intent* — treat suspected-minor reports as
  maximum severity, act immediately — is exactly right for a zero-human-moderation system.
  The problem is entirely in how "immediately" was implemented (see SAF-1); the underlying
  design instinct to special-case this category is correct and should survive the fix.
- **Policy snapshots on interests and date proposals** (§21.3). Freezing the rules that
  applied when a commitment was made, rather than retroactively applying config changes,
  is exactly the right call and is implemented consistently (`policy_snapshot` used
  throughout `dateProposal.service.ts`, `interest.service.ts`).
- **No phone number, no mandatory government ID** (§5.2, §5.3) as a *friction-reduction*
  stance is defensible and well-reasoned — the problem this review raises is that the
  *substitute* trust signals (device fingerprint, payment method) are weaker than the
  spec assumes (SAF-5, ALG-3), not that avoiding ID/phone friction was the wrong instinct.

## 3. Safety and abuse

| ID | Finding | Mechanic | Sev | Likelihood | Hurts | Code | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SAF-1 | **A single `minor_suspected` report from any account, at any trust level, instantly suspends the target — no score threshold, no accumulation, no human review.** Confirmed in `moderation.service.ts#applyThresholds`: `if (minorSuspected) { targetAction = 'suspension' }` runs before and regardless of the normal score-threshold ladder. The spec's own intent (§18.3/§18.5 "maximum severity, immediate protective action") is defensible for genuine child-safety signals, but the *mechanism* has no floor at all — a brand-new, zero-trust, throwaway account can suspend anyone with one click. | §18.3, §18.5 → `moderation.service.ts` | Critical | High (this is the single easiest weaponization vector in the whole system) | User | open (implements spec literally, but the literal implementation is the vulnerability) | Require the report to clear a very low reporter-credibility bar (e.g. verified email + account age > 24h) OR require ≥2 independent, non-device-linked `minor_suspected` reports before auto-suspension — but always keep the *interim* action strong (e.g. immediate restriction + suspended date-proposal ability) so genuine signals still get fast protective action. This is exactly the kind of decision that needs the "minimum viable human escalation" path MOD-6 describes. |
| SAF-2 | **Distance leaks via inconsistent, high-precision rounding.** `discovery.service.ts#toApproximateDistanceKm` rounds to the nearest **1 km**; `profile.service.ts#approximateDistanceKm` independently buckets to the nearest **5 km**. Two different granularities for the same "approximate distance" concept is itself a latent bug (a card and its own detail page can show different numbers), and neither path adds jitter, caps repeat queries against the same target, or varies the figure over time. This is precisely the setup that has been used repeatedly by security researchers to trilaterate real dating-app users' exact locations from a handful of attacker-controlled vantage accounts — cheap to script (create 3 accounts at known coordinates, record the reported distance from each, solve). | §7.1, §28.5 → `discovery.service.ts`, `profile.service.ts` | Critical | High — trivial to automate | User (physical safety, stalking) | partial (coordinates never shown directly; the rounding meant to prevent triangulation is inconsistent and too fine-grained to actually prevent it) | Pick one shared bucketing function; widen the bucket (5–10 km with per-viewer-pair random jitter, not a clean multiple of a fixed grid); rate-limit/cap how often the same viewer can re-fetch the same candidate's distance; consider a decoy-point offset per user rather than snapping their true coordinates. |
| SAF-3 | **No safety exception in the cancellation-refund policy.** `dateProposal.service.ts#cancelDateProposal` applies `date.late_cancel_refund_percent` (default 0%) to *any* cancellation inside the 24-hour cutoff, with no distinction for a user who wants to back out because a red flag surfaced in chat. This directly creates financial pressure to attend a date the user no longer wants to attend, or to eat a real loss to protect themselves — the app's own commitment device becomes a coercion lever. | §14.7 → `dateProposal.service.ts` | Critical | Medium–High (any late-surfacing discomfort in chat triggers this) | User (physical/psychological safety) | open | Add a `safety_cancel` reason that always grants a full refund regardless of the 24h window and never applies a negative trust/no-show event to the canceling party; rate-monitor (never deny) the exception for abuse patterns instead of gating it up front. |
| SAF-4 | Venue staff see both matched users' real names, the venue, and the exact scheduled time (§15.1 ticket contents) — by design, and correctly scoped to "this venue's staff only" in `redemption.service.ts`. But scoping stops at the venue level: **any** staff account at a venue can list **all** vouchers for that venue, not just ones assigned to their shift, and nothing logs a staff member simply *viewing* the list (only redemption events are logged, §15.3/§23.21). A compromised, bribed, or malicious venue employee — or an abuser who takes a job at a partner venue specifically — is handed a live feed of who is meeting whom, where, and when. | §15.1, §15.3 → `redemption.service.ts` | High | Low–Medium (requires insider access) but high impact when it happens | User (stalking, ambush) | partial (venue-scoped, but not staff-member-scoped or view-logged) | Log every voucher-list view (not just redemptions) by staff ID for audit; consider showing only first name + thumbnail to staff, not full profile name; give users a self-serve "move this to a different venue" option so someone uneasy with a specific venue's staff doesn't have to explain why. |
| SAF-5 | **Ban evasion is close to friction-free.** With no phone (§5.2) and no ID (§5.3) by design, the only persistent identity signals are: a freely re-creatable email, a `deviceFingerprint` that is an *optional, client-supplied string* the server never independently derives or verifies (`auth.service.ts`: `deviceFingerprint: z.string().max(500).optional()`), and a payment token a suspended user can simply swap. A suspended or shadowbanned user can be back on the platform in minutes with a new email and a different (or omitted) fingerprint string. | §5.2, §5.3, §5.4 → `auth.service.ts` | Critical | High | User, Business | open | Replace the client-supplied fingerprint string with real device attestation (platform device-integrity APIs) or, short of that, a hashed payment-instrument fingerprint checked against suspended accounts' instruments at signup, applied as elevated Limited-tier friction rather than an outright block (to avoid false-positive lockouts on shared cards/family plans). |
| SAF-6 | **This directly compounds SAF-1 and SAF-5.** The anti-brigading discount ("duplicate report from same social cluster = reduced weight," §18.5) is implemented in `report.service.ts` by matching reporters who share a `device_fingerprint` via `user_auth_events` — the exact field SAF-5 shows is trivially spoofable per account. A coordinated harassment campaign (an ex-partner and friends, or an organized brigade) can file one `minor_suspected` report each from N accounts with N distinct fake fingerprints, get zero brigading discount on any of them, and trigger SAF-1's zero-threshold instant suspension with the very first one anyway. | §18.5, §19.2 → `report.service.ts` | Critical | Medium (requires coordination, but the tooling need is trivial) | User | open | Anchor the anti-brigading signal to something costlier to fabricate than a client string — payment-instrument fingerprint, IP/ASN clustering, or account-age-weighted reporter credibility — and fix SAF-1's zero-threshold behavior at the same time, since either fix alone leaves the other's gap exploitable. |
| SAF-7 | The trust system rewards a "completed date with no report and no no-show" identically whether the date reflected genuine good faith or was scam-grooming stage one. §19.1's own reasoning ("scammers generally do not want to lock real money") assumes cash-constrained, low-effort scammers; professionalized romance-scam operations routinely spend real money up front *because* the deposit or a completed low-stakes interaction is what earns victim trust for a much larger extraction later — and this design pays that exact playbook in Trusted-level visibility (§6.2's positive factors: verified payment method, completed dates, positive feedback — all attainable by a patient, well-funded bad actor). | §6.2, §19.1 | High | Medium | User (financial/emotional) | open | Do not let "completed a date" alone move trust meaningfully upward without a time-decayed, pattern-based fraud-signal check (message content across the relationship, not just the single date outcome) gating how much weight a completed-date event carries. |
| SAF-8 | Scam-pattern messages (crypto, gift cards, wire transfer, cashapp/venmo/zelle keywords, §19.3) are, by explicit policy, **never blocked** — only flagged and shown a banner. The banner does fire deterministically and immediately on first occurrence (`textscan.service.ts` — confirmed, this is better than the spec's own permissive wording might suggest), but there is no escalating friction for *repeated* scam-pattern messages within one conversation — the platform's posture stays purely advisory even after multiple high-confidence signals in the same thread. | §12.5, §19.3, §19.4 → `textscan.service.ts` | Medium | Medium | User | partial (immediate banner is a real, code-confirmed mitigation; no escalation exists beyond it) | After N scam-signal flags in one conversation within a short window, require the recipient to explicitly acknowledge a stronger interstitial warning before the next message renders, without ever hard-blocking (keeps the spec's "don't punish normal link-sharing" principle while adding real friction once the pattern repeats). |
| SAF-9 | The entire product exists to financially engineer a real-world meetup with a stranger, yet neither the spec nor the code contains any post-date safety infrastructure beyond a feedback survey (`post_date_feedback`, §15.4/§18.2) with an optional `safetyConcern` boolean collected **after the fact**. There's no pre-date check-in prompt, no "share your date's name/venue/time with a trusted contact" flow, no in-app "I don't feel safe, alert someone" action tied to the ticket. Every competitor category norm around real-world-meetup safety (and every documented incident pattern involving dating-app-arranged meetings) points to this being the highest-consequence gap in the whole spec relative to how little it would cost to add. | §15.4, §18.2 (silent elsewhere) | High | Low-probability-per-date, catastrophic-when-it-happens | User | open (not addressed anywhere in spec or code) | Add an opt-in "share this date" flow (venue, time, match's first name/photo, auto-expiring) generated from data the ticket already has (§15.1); add a simple day-of safety-check prompt. Low engineering cost given the ticket/voucher data model already exists. |
| SAF-10 | Shadowban's stated purpose is to "reduce ban evasion" by being covert (§18.4), but §6.3's *own* requirement ("users MUST be able to see why their trust level is limited") is implemented literally enough that a shadowban shows up in the user's trust-event history as `"reduced visibility action"` (`trust.service.ts`). That's enough for a moderately savvy bad actor to infer they've been shadowbanned and go create a fresh account (compounded by SAF-5's cheap ban evasion) — the spec's own two requirements are in tension and the code resolves the tension in the direction that undermines the anti-evasion goal. | §6.3, §18.4 → `trust.service.ts` | Medium | Medium | Business | open (both spec requirements implemented as written; the conflict is structural) | Explicitly decide the trade-off: either accept covert shadowbanning is incompatible with full trust-reason transparency and omit shadowban-caused deltas from the user-facing reason list, or accept that shadowban is not meaningfully covert against a sophisticated user and stop relying on covertness as its anti-evasion mechanism. |

## 4. Moderation without humans

| ID | Finding | Mechanic | Sev | Likelihood | Hurts | Code | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MOD-1 | See SAF-1/SAF-6 for the concrete mechanism. Restated at the policy level: the zero-human-moderation constraint (§18.1, "MUST assume no human moderators") combined with a maximum-severity auto-action category makes the single highest-stakes decision in the entire moderation system (is this person a predator, or is this a false accusation) fully automatic with zero review, in either direction. | §18.1, §18.3 | Critical | — | User | open | See SAF-1's recommendation; this is the policy-level restatement of the same gap. |
| MOD-2 | Automated recalculation can only **escalate** — de-escalation requires the explicit, user-initiated appeal flow (`docs/conformance.md` OQ-11, decided and implemented this way). The population most likely to be a false positive on device/behavioral heuristics (new phone, shared IP, irregular schedule) overlaps heavily with the population least able to satisfy the two "fast" appeal paths. | §18.6, OQ-11 → `appeal.service.ts` | High | Medium | User | open | Add a genuinely zero-cost appeal path (e.g., a structured self-attestation form with rate-limited resubmission) that doesn't require money or biometrics, even if it's weighted less than the other two — right now "poor and camera-shy" has no fast path at all. |
| MOD-3 | Categories of harm that rules/thresholds structurally cannot catch: low-grade, plausibly-deniable harassment that never trips a keyword/velocity rule individually; coercive-control patterns that unfold over weeks in an *established* (post-date, non-decaying) conversation with no further automated scanning described once a chat is established; a stolen photo used for the first time (perceptual-hash matching, §7.2, only catches images already seen once on-platform — it cannot catch a photo lifted from elsewhere on the internet); and any judgment call requiring context (is this banter or grooming, is this cultural difference or a red flag). None of these are code bugs — they are the necessary, structural cost of the zero-human-moderation constraint (§18.1), and the spec does not name them or budget for them anywhere. | §18.1, §18.2, §7.2.4 | Critical | High (guaranteed, not probabilistic) | User | n/a (structural — the constraint itself produces this gap) | Name this trade-off explicitly to leadership as an accepted risk rather than an oversight, and prioritize MOD-6's minimum human-escalation path for exactly these categories rather than trying to regex your way out of them. |
| MOD-4 | The false-positive cost to an innocent user is high and largely unaddressed: the exact weights are deliberately hidden (§6.3, correctly, to prevent gaming), which also means a wrongly-restricted user cannot know *which* of their normal behaviors tripped the system, cannot contest a specific fact (no evidence review ever happens — see MOD-3's structural-gap framing), and the automated appeal menu (liveness/payment/cooldown/signals) proves personhood or waits out time, none of which addresses "the accusation itself was false." | §6.3, §18.6 | High | Medium | User | open | At minimum, tell the user which *category* of signal triggered the action (e.g. "flagged for message pattern," "flagged by user report") without the exact weight — narrows the target of the appeal without enabling gaming. |
| MOD-5 | Legal exposure: jurisdictions with a right to meaningful review of automated decisions with significant effects on a person (the general shape of GDPR Art. 22 and similar emerging platform-accountability rules) plausibly reach an account suspension that (a) cuts off access to money already put into escrow trust and (b) cuts off the entire service. A zero-human-moderation architecture is a *structural*, not incidental, conflict with that class of rule — it can't be patched with better regex. | §18.1 (constraint itself) | Critical | Depends on target markets | Business (legal) | n/a (design-level) | Treat "does this market's law require meaningful human review of automated account actions with this level of consequence" as a go/no-go gate per launch market, not an engineering backlog item. |
| MOD-6 | There is no defined minimum human-escalation path anywhere in spec or code. Admin can view moderation actions read-only and "override payment disputes only where legally necessary" (§4.3, §27) — but nothing defines an SLA, a queue, or a trigger for when a human *must* look. The one category that most obviously deserves a human touchpoint — `minor_suspected`, which carries both the highest child-safety stakes and the highest false-accusation stakes simultaneously — currently has zero human involvement at any point (SAF-1/MOD-1). | §4.3, §18.1, §27 | Critical | — | User, Business (legal) | open | Define a minimal exception queue: any `minor_suspected` report and any suspension reversed by a *third* consecutive appeal attempt (signal that the automated path is repeatedly failing this account) routes to a human within a defined SLA — this can stay a tiny fraction of total moderation volume and still close the worst gap. |
| MOD-7 | See SAF-10 — the shadowban/trust-transparency contradiction is as much a moderation-design issue as a safety one: the system's covert enforcement action is not actually covert against its own transparency requirement. | §6.3, §18.4 | Medium | Medium | Business | open | Same recommendation as SAF-10. |

## 9. Precedents — documented patterns this spec should learn from

*Presented as patterns this category has repeatedly produced, not as asserted specific
incidents with confirmed dates or figures where memory isn't certain enough for that.*

- **Distance-based trilateration against dating apps.** Security researchers have
  repeatedly demonstrated, against multiple major dating apps over the years, that a
  displayed "approximate distance" figure — if precise enough, static enough, and
  queryable enough times from attacker-controlled vantage points — can be used to
  triangulate a target's real location to street-level accuracy. This is a documented,
  repeated *pattern* in the category, not a one-off. **Direct implication for this
  spec:** SAF-2's inconsistent 1 km / 5 km rounding with no jitter and no query-rate
  limiting sits squarely in the historically exploited zone; this needs to be fixed
  before launch, not treated as a nice-to-have.
- **Sensitive inferred-attribute data shared or exposed without adequate consent.**
  Location-and-lifestyle-based apps that infer or store sensitive-category attributes
  (sexual orientation, health-adjacent lifestyle data) have faced real regulatory
  scrutiny and enforcement action in multiple jurisdictions over the past several years
  for how that class of data was processed, shared with third parties, or retained.
  **Direct implication:** PRIV-1 and PRIV-2's gaps (sensitive `answers` not erased on
  deletion, no distinct consent/encryption treatment for `sensitive:true` questions)
  sit in exactly this exposure category.
- **Deceptive-engagement enforcement against dating platforms.** Consumer-protection
  regulators have taken enforcement action against at least one major dating platform
  over allegations that it used misleading signals (including automated/fake profile
  activity) to drive paid engagement. This spec avoids the specific mechanism at issue
  in that pattern (no fake profiles, no LLM-generated messages, §1/§3), which is a real
  point in its favor — but the general regulatory posture toward dating-platform growth
  tactics is not lenient, and that context matters for how conservatively PAY-1/PAY-2
  should be resolved before scaling.
- **Large-scale breach of a dating platform whose entire user base is defined by a
  sensitive, stigma-carrying attribute.** The category has at least one well-known case
  of a breach where the sensitive nature of *what the platform was for* (not just the
  data fields themselves) made the exposure far more damaging than a generic breach —
  including real-world harm to individuals named in leaked data. **Direct implication:**
  this spec's private/reciprocal tags (PRIV-3), sensitive question answers (PRIV-2), and
  the fact that religion/relationship-intention/lifestyle hard filters are themselves
  sensitive-adjacent (§9.2) mean a breach here carries category-typical elevated stakes,
  not generic-app stakes — encryption-at-rest scope and breach-response planning should
  reflect that.
- **Romance-scam and money-request pattern.** Regulatory and law-enforcement fraud
  reporting bodies have consistently reported very large annual aggregate financial
  losses (commonly cited as in the hundreds of millions to low billions of dollars range
  in recent years, figures that move year to year and should be sourced fresh rather
  than treated as fixed) attributable to romance scams originating on dating platforms.
  **Direct implication:** SAF-7/SAF-8's gaps (trust rewards a scammer's "loss-leader"
  completed date; scam-pattern messages are never blocked, only flagged) are not
  hypothetical — this is the single best-documented financial harm pattern in the whole
  category, and the escrow model's own stated anti-scam rationale (§19.1) is only a
  partial defense against it.
- **Money-holding marketplaces needing money-transmitter licensing or a licensed
  processor-of-record structure.** Across many two-sided marketplace categories (not
  specific to dating), platforms that collect funds from one party and later disburse to
  another — rather than acting purely as a biller-of-record for their own service — have
  had to either obtain money-transmitter licenses in relevant states/countries or
  restructure through a payment-facilitator product from their processor that assumes
  that regulatory position. This is a general regulatory principle rather than a single
  incident, and it maps directly onto PAY-1's escrow-plus-venue-payout structure.

## Launch blockers in a regulated market

These are not "fix eventually" items — each one is a plausible reason a regulator,
payment processor, or app-store review blocks launch outright in at least one real
target market:

1. **PAY-1** — money-transmission licensing analysis for the escrow + venue-payout
   structure, before `venueSettlement.service.ts` is enabled with real money.
2. **PAY-2** — age-assurance beyond self-declared birthdate, per-market, before launch
   in any jurisdiction with an age-assurance mandate for platforms of this kind.
3. **PRIV-1** — the account-deletion gap is a plausible GDPR Art. 17 / CCPA-erasure
   compliance failure the moment a EU/CA user requests deletion and later audits it.
4. **MOD-5 / MOD-6** — the zero-human-moderation architecture's conflict with
   automated-decision-review rights in jurisdictions that have them is structural, not
   patchable by better rules; needs a per-market legal go/no-go call, not an engineering
   fix.
5. **SAF-1** (secondary legal angle, beyond the pure safety harm in the Top 10) — an
   automated, zero-review account suspension triggered by an unverified allegation is
   itself close to the kind of "significant automated decision" some of the same
   automated-decision-review rules target; fixing it for safety reasons also reduces
   this legal exposure.

## Summary

- **48 findings total: 11 critical, 16 high, 16 medium, 5 low.**
- Top 10 spans five of the eight lenses (safety ×4, moderation ×1 folded into safety,
  payments/legal ×2, privacy ×1, marketplace ×1, operational ×1) — this is not a
  single-category problem.
- The three I would act on **this week**, in order:
  1. **SAF-1 + SAF-6 together** (the `minor_suspected` instant-suspend combined with
     the spoofable-fingerprint anti-brigading gap) — this is the single easiest way for
     a bad actor to weaponize the platform against an innocent user, it's a one-file
     fix (`moderation.service.ts#applyThresholds`) plus a stronger anti-brigading
     signal, and it's also the finding with the most direct legal-exposure angle
     (MOD-5/MOD-6) if left as-is.
  2. **SAF-2** (distance trilateration) — concrete, code-confirmed, cheaply exploitable,
     and directly threatens the physical safety of the exact users the "approximate
     distance only" design was already trying to protect. Also cheap to fix (pick one
     shared, coarser, jittered bucketing function).
  3. **PAY-1 and PAY-2** (money-transmission licensing and age assurance) — not
     engineering fixes, but they are launch-blocking legal questions that take real
     calendar time to resolve, so they need to start now, in parallel with everything
     else, rather than being discovered at the point of trying to launch in a regulated
     market.
