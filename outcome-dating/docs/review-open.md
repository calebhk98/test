# Independent Review: Outcome-Aligned Dating App Spec (v1.0)

Reviewer note: this is an independent read of `SPEC.md` only, plus a light look at the in-progress implementation (`INTERFACES.md`, `src/`, `db/migrations/`) for grounding. I did not read `docs/conformance.md` or `docs/risk-review.md`, per instructions, so I have no idea whether the points below duplicate or diverge from that other review.

## Bottom line

This is an unusually disciplined spec for what it is — a state-machine-and-schema document, not a marketing deck. The payment/escrow flow, the config-snapshot pattern, and the phased build plan are genuinely well thought through. But the document's central premise — a dating app that facilitates real-world meetups and holds real money, built on a **zero-human-moderation assumption** — is where I'd focus scrutiny before writing more code. Several of the places where the spec is vaguest (dispute money, venue payout, "minor suspected" reports, age assurance) are exactly the places where vagueness is most expensive: legal exposure, user safety, or both.

---

## What's genuinely well done

- **Payment state machine is careful about not stranding a hold.** Every failure branch (§14.5) explicitly releases the other side's hold. "Neither user should be charged unless both have committed" (§14.3) is stated once and then actually threaded through every downstream state — proposer-fails, recipient-fails, and capture-fails are all handled distinctly. This is the kind of thing that's easy to get wrong and the spec gets it right.
- **Policy snapshots (§21.3) are a good call.** Capturing the config values that were in effect at interest/proposal creation time, and never re-reading live config for that row afterward, avoids a whole class of "we changed the refund window and it silently changed the terms of a date someone already paid for" bugs. The config table (§21.4) even marks, per key, whether existing objects should track live config or freeze — that's more precision than most specs bother with.
- **The reasoning column.** Nearly every rule has a one-line "Reason:" attached. That's unusually good practice for a developer spec — it lets an implementer push back intelligently ("this conflicts with the reason you gave for X") instead of just complying mechanically. It also makes this review possible in the form it's in.
- **The anti-engagement-bait posture is coherent, not just decorative.** No swipe deck, no public like counts, no boosts, "algorithm sorts, never hides" as a stated principle, established chats that stop decaying once a date happens — these all point the same direction (optimize for a completed date, not for time-in-app) and reinforce each other rather than being a grab-bag of features.
- **Private/reciprocal tags (§8.4)** is a nice, cheap mechanism for the real problem it names (stigmatized interests suppressing honest signal) without requiring anything clever.
- **The phased build plan (§31)** has real exit criteria per phase rather than "build the chat system" hand-waving, and the module boundaries the implementers derived from it (`INTERFACES.md`'s "may call" graph) are acyclic and match the spec's own service boundaries reasonably closely — a sign the spec's structure was implementable as written, which is its own kind of validation.

## The load-bearing assumption I'd push back on hardest

**Section 4.3 and 18.1 state the system "must assume zero human moderation," full stop.** For a generic social app that's a defensible cost-control decision. For *this* app it's a much bigger bet, because this app doesn't just host chat — it actively brokers real-world, in-person meetings between strangers and holds their money in escrow to make the meeting happen. That combination is where dating-app safety incidents actually occur, and it's also where the report taxonomy in §18.3 itself admits the stakes are high: **"minor suspected"** and **"unsafe behavior"** are both listed as first-class report categories, but §18.4–18.6 route every single outcome — including those two — through the same automated restriction → shadowban → suspension → automated-appeal pipeline as "spam" or "no-show." There is no described escalation path anywhere in the document for a report category that is categorically different in kind (suspected child safety issue, suspected physical danger) from the others on the list. If the intent really is zero human involvement even for those two categories, that's worth stating and defending explicitly rather than leaving it to fall out of the general rule by omission — and if it isn't the intent, the spec needs a carve-out.

A related, more specific gap: §7.2 requires automatically blocking "known illegal imagery," which in practice means CSAM hash-matching. In the US, actually detecting that content triggers a legal reporting duty to NCMEC's CyberTipline (18 U.S.C. § 2258A) — this isn't optional policy, it's a statutory obligation once a platform has actual knowledge. The spec has nothing about a reporting/retention pipeline for that case. This isn't a product nice-to-have to defer to a later phase; it's closer to a compliance requirement that should be locked down before the photo-upload feature ships to real users, not something to reverse-engineer from "automatically blocked."

## Age assurance is thinner than the rest of the trust model

The spec is explicit and consistent that it wants to avoid mandatory ID and phone verification (§5.2, §5.3, §33), for real friction/privacy reasons it states plainly. I don't think that's wrong as a default posture. But the *only* age gate described is "user types in a birthdate, app computes age ≥ 18" (§5.1). There's no described consistency check (device signals, no re-verification, no signal from the "device reputation"/"behavioral consistency" trust factors in §6.2 being applied specifically to age plausibility). Combined with the point above — "minor suspected" reports going through the same fully-automated, no-escalation pipeline as spam reports — age is one of the few places in this spec where the "layered trust, no single mandatory gate" philosophy (§5.4) is applied to a question where the cost of being wrong is categorically higher than the cost of being wrong about, say, whether someone is a bot. I'd want to see this deliberately re-examined rather than let it inherit the general friction-reduction default by default.

## Money that gets captured has no defined destination in the failure paths

The payment *authorization* and *capture* flow (§14.2–14.6) is precise, as noted above. But once money is captured, the spec goes vague exactly where precision matters most:

- **§14.7, "after acceptance, less than 24 hours before date":** "no refund by default... `voucher = canceled or retained according to policy`, `date_proposal = canceled`." Both halves of that "or" leave real money (already captured, per §14.2 step 3) somewhere unstated. Retained by the platform? Forfeited to the venue as a no-show fee? Split? The state name `canceled` on a row that was already charged is also confusing on its face — most users read "canceled" as "undone," not "charged and non-refundable."
- **§15.4, disputed dates:** "Automated handling applies according to policy" is the entire specification for what happens when one user confirms attendance and the other doesn't. This is the single case in the whole document that most needs an actual rule (whose money moves where, and on what evidence), and it's the one place the document explicitly declines to give one.
- **Venue payout is never specified at all.** `venues.margin_percent` exists in the schema (§23.16) and is listed as a venue field (§13.2), strongly implying the venue gets some cut of the $20-per-person escrow, but no section describes when or how a venue is actually paid, on what schedule, against what accounting record, or what happens to venue payout on a disputed/no-show/refunded date. I confirmed this isn't just my misreading: the in-progress implementation has a `venueSettlement.service.ts` whose own header comment states plainly that this is "a decision-layer addition (not part of the original 5-agent parallel build) — the spec gives every venue a `margin_percent` but never defines a payout mechanism, ledger type, schema, or endpoint," and a separate `disputeResolution.service.ts` whose header similarly notes it's implementing behavior for "§15.4 ('automated handling... according to policy', never named further)." That's independent, code-level confirmation that these two gaps are real and were load-bearing enough that implementers had to invent policy to fill them rather than translate the spec directly — exactly the kind of thing a spec review should catch before build, not after.

Given the document is a two-sided marketplace (daters *and* venues) moving real money, "how does the venue get paid" and "who keeps the money in a dispute" aren't edge cases to leave implicit — they're core to whether this is legally a simple payment facilitator or something closer to a money-transmission/marketplace-facilitator arrangement, which has its own compliance surface the spec never raises (no mention of 1099s, sales-tax-on-marketplace-fees, or whether Stripe Connect vs. plain Stripe changes any of this).

## "The algorithm sorts, it never hides" is true in the score dimension and false in practice

This principle is stated repeatedly and clearly — §1 rule 3, §9.1, §10.3, §16.1, and again in the decisions table at §33 ("No threshold hiding — Users see eligible pool sorted, not censored"). It's a good principle and I think the spec means it sincerely. But §10.2's visibility rules 5 and 6 make a profile invisible in discovery once their **incoming interest queue or active conversation count hits its cap** — which in practice means the *most* compatible / most popular profiles (the ones scoring well against many viewers) are the ones most likely to saturate their caps and disappear from view, while a mediocre-match profile with an empty inbox stays visible indefinitely. That's a real hiding mechanism, just implemented via capacity rather than via score. It's a defensible tradeoff (§30.3's stated reason is inbox-overload prevention, which is legitimate), but it sits in tension with the "we never hide, only sort" narrative the spec repeats as a trust-building claim in three separate places. If a user notices their top matches keep vanishing from the grid, "the algorithm never hides anyone" won't hold up as an explanation, and the spec's own reasoning about *why* hard filters matter — "users need to trust that filters are real" (§9.1) — cuts the same way against an unacknowledged de facto hiding mechanism elsewhere in the same screen. I'd suggest either naming this plainly as a capacity-based visibility rule (distinct from algorithmic hiding) in user-facing copy, or building in queue rotation so popularity-driven saturation doesn't become permanent invisibility for the people the compatibility algorithm considers best matches.

## Automated moderation has a false-positive/appeal-fairness gap

Section 6.3 is admirably committed to transparency for the *trust* system specifically ("Users MUST be able to see why their trust level is limited," with a worked example). That same standard of transparency doesn't carry over to the *moderation* system in §18: a shadowbanned user, by the definition given ("can still use the app but is not shown to others," §18.4), may not even know they've been shadowbanned, and the automated appeal steps offered (§18.6) — liveness check, verify payment method, cooldown, "confirm identity through existing account signals" — all either cost money/effort/a working camera or just require waiting, with no path that involves someone actually looking at what happened. Report scoring (§18.5) explicitly tries to guard against brigading via "reporter/report relationship" and "duplicate report from same social cluster," which is the right instinct, but coordinated-harassment detection via social-cluster analysis is a genuinely hard problem the spec gestures at in one sentence and never returns to. Given the zero-human-moderation constraint is explicit and non-negotiable per §18.1, I'd want the false-positive path (a real user, wrongly reported, with no camera or spare card) worked out in more detail before this ships, since there's no human backstop to catch what the automated appeal misses.

## The escrow-as-scam-deterrent reasoning (§19.1) is probably too optimistic

"Date escrow is a major scam deterrent... scammers generally do not want to lock real money" is stated as the entire justification. Two things worth weighing against that: first, the escrow is refundable up to 24 hours before the date (§14.7), so a scammer who never intends to show up loses nothing by proposing and then canceling in time — the deterrent only bites *after* a real commitment is made, which is late in a romance-scam timeline. Second, and more importantly, the well-documented pattern in romance-scam reporting (this is a general pattern I'm describing, not a specific figure from this document or a dataset I've checked) is that the scam typically stays entirely in-chat, moves off-platform quickly, and extracts money directly from the victim over an extended period — it rarely proceeds to the "let's book a real date through the app" stage at all, because the scammer's whole model depends on never actually meeting. A $20 in-app hold that only activates once a real date is being booked mostly deters low-effort spam/bot accounts trying to farm interests, which is a real and worth-solving problem, but it's a different problem from the organized-romance-scam threat the section's own framing invokes. I'd suggest either narrowing the claimed benefit or pairing it with the off-app-link nudges in §12.5/§19.4 as the actual first line of defense against the slower-moving scam pattern, since that's the mechanism that's actually positioned to intervene before money leaves the app entirely.

## Smaller items worth a second look

- **§16.2, "if there are too few shared answered questions, score defaults to 0 or neutral"** — this reads as an unresolved either/or left in the spec text itself, not just an implementation detail. Zero and "neutral" (presumably ~0.5) produce very different discovery-grid orderings for new users with few answers, which is exactly the population (new signups) this choice affects most.
- **§7.2's "first photo must contain a visible face, detected by computer vision"** combined with §18.1's zero-human-moderation stance means an automated face detector is the sole, non-appealable-to-a-person gate on whether a new user is visible in discovery at all. Automated face/photo classifiers have a well-documented history of uneven accuracy across presentation styles and image conditions (this is a general pattern from computer-vision fairness research — e.g. Buolamwini & Gebru's "Gender Shades" work on accuracy disparities in commercial face-analysis systems — not a specific claim about whatever vendor or model this app would use). Worth confirming the chosen face-detection approach has been evaluated for that kind of disparity before it's made a hard gate with no human fallback.
- **Discovery, cold start, and geography aren't addressed at all.** The spec is thorough on mechanics but says nothing about minimum viable density (users, and separately, venues, per city/category) below which the discovery grid, the interest caps, and the venue-category list all become theoretical. That may be intentionally out of scope for a *developer* spec, but it's worth flagging since several of the "reasons" given (e.g., "if the pool becomes too small, show the result count instead of preventing filters," §9.2) assume the product still works at low density rather than address what "too small" actually looks like operationally.
- **Photo A/B testing (§7.3)** silently varies what a *viewer* sees, not just what the profile owner controls — different viewers of the same profile see different primary photos, and the accepted-interests metric that decides the "winner" is being measured on real people's real matching decisions, not a UI cosmetic. That's a live experiment run on third parties without their knowledge as a matter of course, which is a different ethical category than typical product A/B testing (where the two sides of the test both just see a different UI, not a different set of actual human introductions). The spec doesn't mention a minimum sample size or significance bar before "the app recommends or automatically reorders photos" (§7.3.5) acts on the result, which matters given how sparse per-photo interest-accept counts will be for most users.
- **No data-retention periods.** §29 lists the right checkbox items (deletion, export, consent, marketing opt-out) but never says how long moderation evidence, device/location signals, or deleted-account financial records (which §29 itself says must be retained "as legally required") are actually kept.
- **No accessibility or localization mention anywhere in 34 sections** — plausibly out of scope for MVP, but worth an explicit call rather than silence, especially since currency is treated as effectively USD-only in every example (`$20`, `2000 cents`) despite `currency` being a schema field.

## What I'd want resolved before more implementation work

In rough priority order:

1. What actually happens to captured money in a dispute, and in a captured-but-late-cancel (§14.7's "or retained")? This is the one gap I'd block on — it's both a UX-trust issue and plausibly a compliance issue.
2. Is "minor suspected" really meant to be fully automated end-to-end, same as "spam"? If not, what's the carve-out?
3. Who pays the venue, on what schedule, and what happens to that payout when the underlying date is disputed or refunded?
4. Is the zero-human-moderation constraint absolute, or does it admit a narrow, rare, high-severity escalation lane? The document currently reads as absolute, which is a stronger claim than most of its individual "Reason:" justifications (cost, availability) actually require.

None of the above changes the overall shape of the app — they're gaps in an otherwise coherent document, not signs the core design is wrong. The parts of this spec that got the most attention (payment state machine, config snapshots, filter/sort separation) are the parts that are hardest to get right and they're in good shape. The parts above are, not coincidentally, the parts that touch money-in-dispute and safety-in-person — exactly where "we'll figure it out with automation later" is the riskiest place to leave a gap.
