# Playtest notes — naive15 — Roman Empire under Trajan

Playing blind: never read source, never read other playtest notes, never read the repo's design docs. Everything below is worked out from the game's own text.

## Setup (Year 100, before turn 1)

The game opens with a well-written framing screen: "ONE PERSON, AND EVERYTHING THEY KNOW". You're a modern person dropped into a past society with knowledge but no industrial base. Good hook, sets expectations correctly (this is a tech-tree game, not combat/4X).

Choices at setup, in order:
1. Which civilization/era (5 choices). Picked "The Roman Empire under Trajan, 100" per instructions.
2. Fog of war on/off — **irreversible once chosen** (can go off→can't go back on if you start with it off; on stays on). Chose ON, since that's clearly the "real" way to play (off is described as a planning/spoiler mode). This means I cannot see the tech tree, only what's buildable now and rumors of things I've "heard of."
3. Starting wealth tier: destitute / poor_scholar (default, 400 den) / artisan / merchant / rich_merchant / equestrian / absurd. Took the default, poor_scholar — "a knife, a lens, a codex of notes."
4. Mortality: age and die, or not. Default is NOT aging — explicitly said to be the way "to measure the tree rather than a lifespan lottery." Left it off, since the immediate goal (transistor) is stated to take centuries.
5. The GOAL. This is the actual win condition, chosen once at setup, from a list of 17 with wildly different scope: goals range from "closure 1, floor 2y" (Establish experimental science) up to "closure 168, floor 142y" (the transistor — the default and the game's named endpoint). Chose the default: **Grown and alloy junction transistors**. This was a deliberate choice to see the real shape of the game rather than a quick lifetime-sized goal — but in hindsight, given limited session time, a shorter goal might have let me see an actual ending. Noted as a thing to flag: the game front-loads a huge, mostly irreversible commitment (goal + fog + mortality) before you've seen a single turn of actual play. There's no "try a short goal first" onramp.
6. Horizon: how many in-game years before the run force-ends. Options 400/500/650/endless. Picked Standard (500). This one IS changeable later via `options`.

First confusion: right after choosing "1" for the goal (transistor) via **empty-Enter-for-default**, the game process crashed and killed the whole tmux session twice in a row, with no traceback visible in the captured output. The THIRD attempt, this time typing the digit "1" explicitly instead of pressing Enter on the default, worked fine and proceeded normally. I did not chase this further (told not to read source), but it's worth flagging: something about accepting the bare-Enter default at the goal-selection prompt looked fragile in my environment. Might be an artifact of my terminal/tmux setup rather than the game — noting it rather than concluding it's a bug.

## Turn 1, Year 100 AD

Game state is shown as a prompt-bar `[100 AD | 400 den | you:2000 hr | sch 1 art 1 | rep 5] >` plus a fuller `state` dump on request. Core resources, as best I can tell:
- **Money** (denarii). Starts 400.
- **Founder-hours**: 2000/year, does not carry over. This is YOUR personal labor budget for the year.
- **Staff**: counted as "scholars" and "artisans" — starts at 1 and 1 (i.e., just you, counted as both, apparently, since you're a "poor scholar" with "a knife" — a hybrid).
- **Reputation**, **protection%**, **scandal**, **eminence** — a cluster of social/political stats. Scandal >25 apparently ends the run (denunciation). Eminence >26 also dangerous somehow. Both were explained in the `state` output, which is nice — the game surfaces its own danger thresholds without me having to guess.
- Every game starts with **139 technologies "granted for free"** — i.e., baseline tech this civilization already has (roads, concrete, etc. from the intro text). Progress is measured in techs "built by you" on top of that base.

The starting command vocabulary the game itself recommends: `state`, `available`, `why <name>`, `start <name>`, `step`. That's a good minimal onramp — I used exactly these first.

### `available` — my first real look at the tree

209 things startable immediately, bucketed by subject (textiles 54, finance 26, roads/bridges 20, "the remaining arts" 20, power and precision 16, farming 10, household 10, metallurgy 9, transport 9, mathematics 7, medicine 7, etc.) This is a LOT of surface area on turn 1. Slightly overwhelming — the subject-summary table is the right instinct for taming it, and the "cheapest six" / "most staff-rests" call-outs at the bottom were genuinely useful shortcuts. Still, 209 options with only "why" as a way to interrogate them means turn 1 is a lot of reading.

Two commands stood out as load-bearing: `why <id>` gives full cost/risk/prereq/effect detail, and its "HOW MUCH RESTS ON THIS" line (none / a few things / almost everything) is basically the only compass available under fog. I leaned on it heavily to find "foundation" nodes.

### First real decisions: scientific_method and units_standards

Checked `why scientific_method` — flagged "tier 0, foundation... HOW MUCH RESTS ON THIS: almost everything". Cost 230 den, 350 of my hours, 2-year floor, 20% failure risk. Flavor text is good: the risk is explicitly *cultural* ("displacing Aristotelian authority"), not technical — a nice bit of texture that ties the number to the fiction.

`why units_standards` (define length/mass/time/temperature standards) — also "tier 0, foundation... almost everything" rests on it, cost 444 den.

Both looked like unavoidable foundations, so I started both on turn 1 (`start scientific_method`, `start units_standards`), spending my hour budget and taking on debt against my 400 den. This turned out to be **too aggressive for a poor_scholar start** — see the debt spiral below.

### Debt spiral, years 101–107 — the single most important thing I learned this run

Mechanic, pieced together the hard way:
- Starting a project doesn't charge you the money up front; it "owes" the cost, drawn down over time as your founder-hours and available cash let it, subject to a calendar floor.
- If you overcommit and go into **arrears** (negative cash), the interest on arrears (~11%/yr) starts eating your income, and — this is the part that isn't obvious from any single screen — **being in arrears also stalls the founder-HOURS going into your projects**, not just the money. The game does say this explicitly if you read `help economy`'s debt entry, but I only found that after several turns of watching a project sit at "13% of your hours spent... in arrears: the hours offered this year did almost nothing" without understanding why my own free hours weren't moving it forward. That was a real "wait, what?" moment — I initially assumed hours and money progressed independently once a project had "enough" money queued.
- Meanwhile I had `open`ed scientific_method and units_standards, which do NOT earn anything but DO cost upkeep (100/yr and 30/yr respectively) once opened — "open" just means "actually running," separate from "known/built." I opened them reflexively, without realizing opening a pure-knowledge tech with 0 revenue is pure downside unless something else needs it running (see below). That pushed net income to -220/yr on top of the debt-interest problem, and my capital went from +400 to -835 den over about 4 in-game years while a project inched forward at a trickle.

How I dug out:
1. `mothball <id>` on all three open-but-earning-nothing concerns (scientific_method, units_standards, cap_measure_len). The game confirmed explicitly: mothballing stops the upkeep AND stops the (zero) earnings, but "you still know how to do it" — i.e., **the built/finished status is permanent and separate from the open/running status**. I verified this really matters by checking `why corpus_written` afterward, which listed "PREREQUISITES (all met, and finished counts for ever): scientific_method, units_standards" and STATUS: CAN START NOW even with both mothballed. So: opening a "concern" is a pure economic decision about revenue vs upkeep, not a precondition for using it as a tech-tree prerequisite. This was NOT obvious up front and cost me real playtime to work out — the flavor text for scientific_method ("Multiplier on every research node thereafter") reads like it should matter whether it's kept running, but as far as I can tell that's just narrative color describing why the technology itself is important, not a live "must stay open" bonus. I never got a crisp confirmation either way and I'm flagging that ambiguity rather than claiming a bug.
2. Discovered the `work <trade> <hours>` command — explicitly sell your own founder-hours for ordinary wages instead of spending them on your own projects. This was hugely effective: `work scholar 2000` (all my free hours) earned ~650 den in a single year, net ~+400-500 after accounting for what it took away from my existing "practice" income (I earn passive money from practicing medicine on the side — cataract surgery and trepanation, apparently part of the poor_scholar's existing skillset, which the game explicitly said pays "about a third of what the tree quotes" because it's informal, one-person work). Using `work` for 1-2 years while nothing else was running dug me out of debt (-835 → -190 by year 107) and then to a healthy surplus (net +205/yr) once the debt principal was down and a revenue concern (horizontal loom) came online.

**Lesson for the designers, if I could say one thing**: the debt-and-arrears mechanic is well-designed and well-explained *in isolated help text*, but the consequence (hours wasted, not just money) is not shown prominently on the turn-to-turn status screen — it shows up as a terse one-line annotation under the affected project ("in arrears: ... did almost nothing") that reads more like flavor than a mechanical warning. A new player doing exactly what the game's own opening suggests (start two "foundation, almost everything rests on this" techs on turn 1) walks straight into this trap. It might be intentional — early overreach being punished is thematically appropriate for "you arrive with nothing" — but it wasn't at all obvious that opening a completed, zero-revenue technology has ongoing cost with no compensating benefit; I'd have expected either (a) opening to be free for pure-knowledge techs, or (b) the game to warn "this only exists to be later prerequisite-checked; you may prefer not to open it" instead of me discovering that by trial, error, and cross-referencing `why` on a downstream tech.

### Recovery and progress, years 107–109

- Opened `tex_horizontal_loom` (a textile technology, cost 225.8 den, cheap) once it finished — this one DOES earn (300-500 den/yr, ramping up over ~3 years). This was my first actual revenue-producing "concern," and it flipped my net income positive.
- Started and finished `arithmetic_positional` (decimal positional notation, zero, negative numbers) — flavored as "recovered from Indian sources" and "highest return on personal hours in the entire tree," another "almost everything rests on this" foundation. Cost 1032 den, financed on credit (credit limit had grown to 2163 den by this point — apparently it scales with reputation/standing over time, though I haven't confirmed the mechanism). Confirmed it has NO open/close mechanic at all — `ventures` doesn't list it, `why` shows no UPKEEP/REVENUE line. So not every tech is a "concern" — some are pure permanent knowledge with zero ongoing cost. Good, that's the sane default; I wish I'd realized on turn 1 that scientific_method/units_standards were the unusual, upkeep-bearing kind rather than assuming (wrongly) all techs behave like arithmetic_positional.

Currently (year 109) sitting at -245 den, net income positive again, one artisan/scholar short of building `atomic_theory` (needs 2 scholars; I have 1, hiring one costs 625 den/yr in wages — a real recurring commitment I'm about to make).

## Running impression so far

The writing quality is very high — every `why` entry has real, specific, era-appropriate flavor text, and the framing (you know how modern tech works, but "knowing is free, building is not") is sustained consistently rather than being setup-screen-only. The economic model (founder-hours vs money vs staff, practice income vs concerns, credit and arrears) is legible once you've been burned by it once, and the help system is comprehensive if you go looking (`help commands`, `help money`, `help economy`, `help labour` all had exactly what I needed). The single biggest friction point so far is that the game front-loads you into being able to start big "foundation" projects immediately, with all the framing suggesting you should ("almost everything rests on this"), and the debt/arrears trap that follows from doing so is easy to fall into and non-obvious to climb back out of without reading `help economy` closely — a status-screen line more clearly saying "you are locked out of paying down debt / your own hours are being wasted here" instead of the drier "in arrears" annotation would have saved me a few turns of confusion.

## Year 109-127 AD: correcting myself, and the `identity_cover` discovery

Two things from the notes above turned out to be wrong or incomplete once I kept playing, and I want to correct them rather than leave a tidy-but-inaccurate record:

**Correction 1 — the "KEEP THIS OPEN" ambiguity is actually resolved, cleanly, by the game itself.** I had flagged not knowing whether mothballing scientific_method lost some kind of live "multiplier" bonus. It turns out the game answers this directly and consistently: some `why` entries carry an explicit line, `KEEP THIS OPEN: yes - it is knowledge (that part is permanent), but scholars it supports, household places it adds, credit or standing it lends you, or a future start it clears all stop the moment you close it`. scientific_method and units_standards do NOT have this line — so they really are safe to mothball with zero downside beyond the (already-accounted-for) loss of their 0 revenue. But `identity_cover` and `corpus_written` DO have it. I opened `identity_cover` (1580 den, "establish a respectable cover identity") expecting nothing since its own EARNS/YR is 0, and was surprised: my **protection** stat jumped from 5% to 17% immediately, and later to 32-38% once `patron_local` was also open. So "protection" (the stat that presumably reduces confiscation/denunciation severity) is a live effect of specific open concerns, not something you get once and keep. Lesson: read for the `KEEP THIS OPEN` line specifically — its presence or absence is the actual answer to "does this need to stay open," not the flavor text.

**Correction 2 — `patron_local` was not a bug, just reputation-gated and unhelpfully worded.** Earlier I flagged as a likely bug that dozens of items said "get at least a local patron first: 'start patron_local'" while `start patron_local` and `why patron_local` both refused with "you have never heard of any such thing." I revisited this at reputation ~14 (it had been ~7-9 when I first hit it) and `start patron_local` worked immediately, no different phrasing, no warning it had become available. So this gates on some hidden threshold (reputation, or possibly built-tech-count or eminence) that the game never surfaces to the player. The two failure states — "this literally doesn't exist for you yet" vs "you haven't reached the threshold that reveals it yet" — produce byte-for-byte identical refusal text, which is a real design smell even though the underlying mechanic (grow your reputation, then the state stops being "wary" of you) makes good narrative sense once you know it's there. **This is what I'd most want the designers to fix**: either say something different for "known name, not yet unlocked" vs "genuinely never heard of," or don't print a command name in blocker text if the game won't tell you why it refuses that literal command.

Once `patron_local` unlocked, it opened a **huge** batch of previously-invisible techs across agriculture, mathematics, textiles and metallurgy — everything that had been quietly sitting behind "the state is wary of this." This was one of the best moments of the run so far: a single unlock made 15+ items appear in the "heard of" list at once, several of them very lucrative (tl_horseshoe, sc2_geometry_trigonometry, several ag2_ items). It's a well-designed gate, just badly communicated in the moment.

**A repeated pattern I want to flag as possibly a genuine inconsistency, not just my misunderstanding**: several times, `why <id>` reports "HOW MUCH RESTS ON THIS: nothing else; this is worth having for itself" for a technology that I can then find, moments later, explicitly named as a blocking prerequisite for something else already visible in the "HEARD OF, CANNOT BEGIN YET" list. Two concrete instances: `met_ore_crushing_sorting` says "nothing else rests on this," but `met_jigging_gravity`'s blocker text reads "missing prerequisites: met_ore_crushing_sorting." Same pattern with `in2_tape_measure_steel` ("nothing else... worth having for itself") vs `in2_baseline_measurement_apparatus`'s "missing prerequisites: in2_tape_measure_steel." I did not go and read the source to find out whether "RESTS ON" is deliberately only counting some category of downstream node (maybe only ones not yet "heard of," or only ones past some value threshold) and simply phrasing that badly — but as a player it reads as a flat contradiction: the game telling me in the same breath that "nothing rests on this" and "this other thing you can already see needs it."

## Year 127-166 AD: the debt-recovery loop, staff mechanics, and building a real economy

Once past the early debt trap, the back half of this session was a fairly clean loop: `available` (or `why` on a specific named target) → `start` the highest-leverage "almost everything rests on this" item(s) → `step` → `open` anything that finished and earns money → repeat, with `work scholar <hours>` used opportunistically whenever nothing was running to convert idle founder-hours into cash rather than let them go to waste (a year's unused founder-hours simply evaporate — "Hours do not carry to next year"). This loop scaled well: starting capital of 400 den grew to over 127,000 den by year 166, with a real production chain built up (horizontal loom → tape measure → world map → horse collar → identity_cover/patron_local → workshop_first → case_hardening → refractory_fireclay → cementation_steel → balance_analytical → glass_clear → glass_labware → lens_grinding → telescope → mirror_amalgam → freedman_staff → cap_vac_1torr), 30 technologies built by year 166 (of a stated closure of 168 for the full transistor goal — so roughly a sixth of the way there by node count, though node count is clearly not linear in difficulty; the early "tier 0" foundations were cheap and the later ones cost thousands of denarii and multiple years each).

Mechanics learned in this stretch, in the order I ran into them:

- **`STAFF NEEDED` (to start) vs `STAFF TO KEEP IT OPEN` (to run) are genuinely different and BOTH enforced, but at different times.** Firing a scholar right after starting `atomic_theory` (which needed 2 scholars to start) did NOT stop it running — the start-gate is checked once, at `start`. But firing my artisans later caused an already-open concern (`in2_tape_measure_steel`) to auto-close with the message "nobody left to keep an eye on 1 concern... You still know how; reopen with 'open' once you have the people." That was a genuine surprise — I had assumed (wrongly, and this cost me a small amount of lost ramp-up progress on that concern) that once a concern was open it stayed open regardless of later staffing changes. The `open`-time "STAFF TO KEEP IT OPEN" number is a live, continuously-checked constraint, not a one-time gate. The game DOES warn about this ("Often smaller than staff_needed... Checked when you 'open' it, not when you 'start' it") but I hadn't understood "checked when you open it" to mean "checked every subsequent year," which in hindsight it clearly must, given what happened.

- **`train <trade> <n>` creates the trade and starts teaching specific people, but does NOT put them on your payroll.** I trained 2 machinists (900 of my own founder-hours, 675 den, ready "in 141") expecting that when ready they'd simply be available; instead at year 141 `labour machinist` showed "you employ: 0, the town can supply: 0 hours" until I separately ran `hire machinist 2` (688 den/yr each). Training unlocks the trade's *existence* in the society and produces trained individuals; hiring is a separate, additional, ongoing-wage step to actually employ them. This wasn't clearly signposted — the confirmation message after `train` says "training: 2 machinists will be ready in 141" which reads like they'll just show up working.

- **Household capacity is a hard cap, separate from money.** At various points `open` and `hire` were refused with "you can supervise, house and teach 0.67 more people, not 3 - you have no room for even one," naming specific techs that raise the cap (`freedman_staff` +10, `school_founded` +12, `crucible_steel` +12). This is a real, binding constraint independent of cash — I had 48,000 den in hand and still could not hire past the household ceiling until I built `freedman_staff`.

- **Staff attrition is a persistent background drain, separate from named historical hazards.** The log repeatedly shows lines like "you lose 1 artisan to death and to better offers" with no further detail, at a rate of roughly one every several years even outside the Antonine plague window. When the plague did arrive (year 165, flagged clearly on the turn summary as "HAPPENING NOW: Antonine plague"), I could not find any log line distinguishing plague deaths from ordinary attrition — both are folded into the same generic "you lose N to death and to better offers" message. I wanted to check exactly how many of the losses in 165-166 were plague-attributable versus background churn and could not tell from anything the game showed me. This might be intentional (the fog of war framing suggests you're not supposed to get a clean scientific breakdown of your own losses either) but it made the arrival of a named, dated historical catastrophe feel anticlimactic — I knew a plague was scheduled and it eventually got announced, but its actual bite was indistinguishable from ordinary bad luck.

- **`quote mine <material> <amount>` is a genuinely useful "look before you leap" command** I hadn't tried until late — it shows standing cost, years to first production, and what you can actually afford, without committing you to anything. I used it on nickel (needed as a hard materials-gate for `gp_glass_metal_seal`, not a tech-gate) and found a nickel mine would cost 44,000 den/year to stand up with a 3-year lag before output — steep enough that I decided not to pursue it this session. Worth flagging as a different *kind* of blocker than everything else I'd hit: most blockers are "build tech X first," but a few are "this raw material simply is not available to you without a large separate capital commitment," and the game is careful to distinguish these two cases in its blocker text (e.g. `gp_glass_metal_seal`: "this needs a nickel supply and you have none of the things that would serve, and none of them is anything you have heard of yet" — genuinely clear once you see it, distinct from a tech-prerequisite message).

- **Literacy/scholar headcount has a hard society-wide ceiling that money cannot buy past.** `quantum_solidstate_theory` (visible as early as year ~114, one of the very few glimpses of "endgame" content this run got under fog) wants 8 scholars; at 2 scholars employed the game told me flatly "8.0 wanted; you have 2.0 and literacy here will never let you HIRE more than 6.0. Printing, paper, schools and academies raise both [the ceiling and literacy itself]." This is a nice piece of design — it forces a detour through literacy/schooling infrastructure rather than just "throw more denarii at hiring," and ties back to the `score` command's own literacy component (see below). I did not get far enough to build `school_founded` (still blocked on 2 unknown prerequisites as of year 166) so I haven't seen how the ceiling actually lifts in practice.

- **`score` is genuinely useful mid-run, not just an end-of-game tally.** Running it at year 166 (goal not reached) still printed a full provisional breakdown: literacy, workforce, economy, institutions, resilience, standing, each with a raw value, a normalized 0-1 score and a weight, everything except the withheld "technology coverage" share (explicitly noted as withheld because "the tree's total size is the one thing fog keeps from you"). This is a good design choice — it lets a fog-of-war player still see roughly where they stand on axes other than "did I reach the goal," which otherwise would be completely opaque under fog.

## What I still wanted to find, and deliberately did not look up

I never found the second, unnamed prerequisite for `thermometer` (only ever showed "this needs 1 other thing you have not heard of yet"), despite building glass_clear, glass_labware, lens_grinding, telescope, and cap_vac_1torr — all things that felt adjacent. I was tempted more than once to go and grep the source for `thermometer`'s prerequisite list rather than keep guessing from flavor text and category-browsing `available`, and did not do that; I kept discovering new "almost everything" nodes by category-scanning (`available glass and optics`, `available find <keyword>`) instead. I'm noting this as a real, felt friction of playing honestly under fog: sometimes there is no in-game signal at all pointing toward the missing piece, and the only way to find it is trial-and-error browsing across unrelated subjects, hoping the right thing surfaces once its OWN prerequisite chain resolves.

## Final summary — how far I got, what stopped me, what I would change

**How far I got.** Played from the very first "New game" prompt through to year 166 AD in-game (66 of 500 available years), stopping mid-Antonine-plague with the run in a healthy, stable state rather than because I was stuck or failing. Set up as: Roman Empire under Trajan/100 AD, fog of war ON, poor_scholar start (400 den), mortality OFF (founder does not age), goal = the default and largest goal, "Grown and alloy junction transistors" (closure 168, floor 142 years), horizon = Standard (500 years). By the stopping point: 30 technologies built by me on top of the 139 the civilization starts with, against the goal's stated closure of 168 nodes (so roughly a sixth of the way through the tree by node count, though not linearly — the early tier-0 foundations were far cheaper than the later instrumentation chain). Capital grew from 400 denarii to 127,476 denarii, with a self-sustaining economy: ~37,630 den/year gross revenue against ~11,330 den/year upkeep across 20 open revenue concerns, and a payroll of 12 employees besides myself. Standing was healthy throughout: reputation 24, protection 37%, scandal 0.99 (well below the 25 denunciation line and falling), eminence 1.9 (well below its 26 danger line). Never reached the goal or any sub-goal — the transistor is a genuine multi-century project, and 66 years in I was still building 17th/18th-century-equivalent scientific instruments (thermometers, balances, vacuum pumps), centuries of tech distance from a 1951 transistor.

**What stopped me.** Nothing external — no failure state, no denunciation, no bankruptcy. I stopped by choice at a natural checkpoint (saved the game) having: recovered fully from an early self-inflicted debt spiral and built a genuinely stable, growing economy, proving the core loop works and scales; exercised essentially the whole visible command surface (`state`, `available` with all its sort/filter options, `why`, `start`/`stop`, `step`, `open`/`mothball`/`restore`, `ventures`, `money`, `log`, `labour`/`hire`/`fire`/`train`/`commission`, `work`, `risk`, `score`, `rush`, `quote`, `save`) — though I never used `bribe`, got `bounty` to succeed, `buy forest/nitre/slaves/manumit`, `close <material>`, `policy`, or the mid-game `options` toggles, so real surface remains unexplored; and reached a natural point of diminishing returns for one sitting, since continuing meant many more hours of essentially the same loop (find the next "almost everything" node under fog, fund it, staff it, open it) against a 168-node tree.

**What I would tell the people who made it.**

What's working very well: the prose is doing real work, not just flavor — every `why` entry ties cost, risk and staffing to something specific and period-accurate, and the "you know how, but knowledge is free and building is not" framing stays consistent from the opening screen through the driest cost breakdown sixty turns later. The economic model (founder-hours as a hard personal budget, staff with separate start-gate vs keep-open-gate, real credit with real interest and a real ceiling, "your practice" as an always-available income floor) is coherent once understood, and the `help` topics genuinely answer the questions they should. Fog of war does what it says — hunting for case_hardening's hidden prerequisite, or getting `patron_local` to finally unlock, felt like real discovery, never like looking at a known tree through a keyhole. `score` giving a live, honest partial breakdown mid-run, goal unreached, is a small but real kindness under fog.

What I'd change, in priority order:
1. Being "in arrears" silently wastes founder-hours, not just money, and this is the easiest trap for a new player to fall into — the game's own opening advice points straight at two "almost everything rests on this" foundations that, started together on turn one with a poor_scholar's 400 denarii, produce exactly this trap. The consequence is explained well in `help economy` but not surfaced prominently on the turn summary itself; a plainer warning there, not just a terse "in arrears" tag, would save real playtime.
2. Two structurally different "you can't do that yet" states print identical refusal text. `patron_local` was genuinely unlocked, just gated behind a hidden threshold (reputation, most likely) I hadn't crossed yet — but `start`/`why` on it said "you have never heard of any such thing," the exact wording a truly-never-revealed tech gets, even though other techs' own blocker text was actively telling me to `start patron_local` by name. This reads as a bug or a dead reference; it should read as "not yet, but close."
3. "HOW MUCH RESTS ON THIS: nothing else; this is worth having for itself" contradicted visible information at least twice (`met_ore_crushing_sorting`, `in2_tape_measure_steel`), where another already-visible tech's own blocker text named the "nothing rests on it" tech as a missing prerequisite. Whether this is a real bug or an intentional narrower definition of "rests on," it undermines trust in that line as a planning tool.
4. `train` produces taught people who then still need a separate, further `hire` before they do anything or cost anything — the training confirmation ("2 machinists will be ready in 141") reads like they'll simply be at work by then. A one-line addendum would have saved a confused `labour machinist` check two years later.
5. Smaller and not fully attributable to the game: the process crashed twice, silently, at the identical goal-selection prompt on my first two attempts to accept the bare-Enter default, both times recovered cleanly by typing the digit instead. Never chased further per instructions, so this is flagged rather than diagnosed.

What surprised me, in a good way: the scheduled historical hazards (Antonine plague, currency debasement, the sack of Rome, all the way out to the Carolingian renaissance at 774-800) being visible from turn one, with real percentages tied to specific things I've built, is genuinely well-designed dramatic irony — the game tells you in its opening screen that you have sixty-five good years, then lets you watch the calendar walk toward it. The depth of the foundation layer (scientific_method, units_standards, arithmetic_positional, identity_cover, patron_local, workshop_first) forming a real "unlock the rest of the tree" tier that has to be earned economically before the interesting instrumentation chain becomes reachable at all was a satisfying structure once its shape became visible. And the sheer breadth of the tree even in subjects with nothing to do with the stated goal (54 textile items visible turn one) makes this feel like a genuine simulation of "everything a civilization could build," not a thin wrapper around one linear path to a transistor.
