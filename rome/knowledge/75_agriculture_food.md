# Module 75: Agriculture, Food and Surplus

Rome in 100 AD is not agriculturally ignorant. Columella, Varro, Cato and Pliny
between them describe crop rotation of a sort, manuring by dung type, marling,
liming, grafting, layering, controlled viticulture, and animal selection by
conformation and pedigree of a kind. A Roman *vilicus* (farm manager) running
a well-run estate already does most of what a competent 18th-century English
farmer does, by instinct, trial and inherited lore rather than by theory. Do
not walk into this expecting to teach farmers how to farm. You will look like
a fool, because they will out-argue you from Cato's actual text.

What you have that they do not is three things: the germ theory of soil
fertility (you know *why* legumes work, they only know *that* they work),
the habit of quantitative measurement and controlled comparison (Module 60),
and a shopping list of specific techniques and crops that exist somewhere in
the world in 100 AD but have not yet reached, or taken root in, Roman
agriculture. You also carry the honest bad news: the crops that actually
transformed Old World farming after 1500 (potato, maize, tomato, chilli
pepper, cassava) come from the Americas and are permanently unavailable to
you. There is no substitute for them. Your gains here are real but modest by
comparison, and they are gains of systematisation and cross-pollination
between regional practices, not miracles.

### crop_rotation — Three-course rotation with a legume break (*ordo satorum*, no exact Latin term for the system as such)

**What it is / why you want it.** The standard Roman arable regime is a
two-field alternation: half the land is cropped with cereal, half rests as
bare fallow (*ager vervactus*), ploughed two or three times over the season
to kill weeds and let it "recover," then the fields swap the following year.
At any given moment only about half the arable land is producing food. Add a
third course of legumes (beans, vetch, lentils, chickpeas) in place of some
of the bare fallow, and you (a) put more of the land into production every
year and (b) leave the soil measurably richer for the cereal course that
follows, instead of merely rested.

**Why you would never guess this.** A Roman farmer sees that fallowing
restores fertility, sees that a bean crop grown where fallow used to be does
not exhaust the following wheat crop the way a second wheat crop would, and
concludes, correctly by observation, that legumes are "gentle" on land. He
does not know why, and without knowing why he cannot optimise it: how much
legume land is enough, which legume, ploughed in or grazed off, on which
soils. You can, because you know the mechanism.

**Prerequisites.** None beyond ordinary ploughing and seed grain. Legume seed
stock (faba beans, vetch, lentils, chickpeas) already exists on every farm.
Benefits from Module 60 (measurement) to actually verify the gain on your own
land rather than taking anyone's word, mine included.

**Roman-available inputs.** Broad bean (*faba*, Vicia faba), vetch (*vicia*,
Vicia sativa), lentil (*lens*), chickpea (*cicer*), lupin (*lupinus*, useful
even on poor sandy soil), all already cultivated across Italy and the
provinces. Wheat (*triticum*) and barley (*hordeum*) as the cereal course.

**Procedure.**
1. Divide the arable land into three roughly equal blocks instead of two.
2. Year 1: Block A wheat/barley, Block B legume, Block C true bare fallow.
3. Year 2: rotate one position, so last year's legume block now takes cereal,
   last year's fallow takes legume, last year's cereal rests or takes
   legume again if soil is poor.
4. After the legume harvest (beans, lentils, chickpeas for food; vetch can be
   cut green for fodder or grazed by sheep), either plough the stubble and
   root residue directly into the soil, or graze livestock on the legume
   aftermath so their dung returns nutrients along with the root nitrogen.
   Either works; grazing is the lower-labour option and Roman farms already
   do it.
5. Keep true fallow only on the poorest third of the land, or drop it
   entirely once you have several years of measured yields showing the
   legume course does not need a bare-fallow year behind it as often as
   cereal does.
6. The mechanism, stated in terms you can act on without any chemistry: bean,
   vetch, lentil and chickpea roots carry small swellings (root nodules,
   visible if you pull a healthy legume plant and wash the roots) that pull
   a fertilising vapour out of the air itself and fix it in the soil. Cereal
   roots cannot do this. That is why a field that grew beans grows better
   wheat afterward than a field that grew wheat twice running, and why it
   beats plain bare fallow too, since bare fallow only rests the soil, it
   does not add anything to it.

**How you know it worked.** Mark out two adjoining strips of visibly similar
soil, same aspect, same seed, same sowing date. Run one on old two-field
fallow, one on the new three-course rotation, for at least three full cycles
(so at least six years, since a fair test needs the cereal course on both
strips to have followed the different treatments more than once). Weigh or
measure the grain harvest from each strip by the same method (Module 60).
The three-course strip should show both a higher average yield per iugerum
in the years it carries cereal, and, because a smaller fraction of it sits
idle, a higher total harvest across the full multi-year cycle.

**Failure modes.** Legume disease and pests (bruchid weevils in stored
beans, vetch rust) can wipe a legume course in a bad year, same as any
Roman crop; keep some seed reserve. On very poor or very dry soil, legumes
still need reasonable moisture to nodulate well; on genuinely marginal land
a full legume course may simply fail rather than help, in which case keep
part of the old fallow. Grazing the aftermath too hard, so animals eat down
to bare earth and compact wet soil, undoes some of the gain.

**Cost & labour.** No new capital: same plough, same oxen, same seed types
already on the farm. The only new "cost" is administrative: keeping the
three-block pattern straight over years, which a literate manager with
Module 60's measurement habit can do with a wax tablet. ESTIMATED (basis:
comparable to the historically attested medieval European shift from
two-field to three-field systems): moving roughly a sixth of total arable
land from bare fallow into legume production, plus the direct fertility
transfer to the following cereal crop, plausibly raises average cereal yield
per unit area sown by something on the order of 10 to 30 percent, and raises
total food output per unit of total farmland by considerably more than that
because a much larger fraction of the land is productive in any given year.
Treat both numbers as an informed range, not a promise; your own strip trial
(above) is the only number that matters for your own land.

**Danger.** None physical. Socially, do not present this as "your"
discovery to a Roman agronomist; present it as a variant worth testing
against the standard practice, on his own terms, with his own measuring
methods. Claiming to have invented crop rotation to a man who owns a copy of
Columella will cost you credibility you need elsewhere.

**Confidence: HIGH** on the mechanism (nitrogen fixation by root-nodule
bacteria in legumes is settled modern biology) and on the fact that Rome
already runs a two-field alternate fallow system (Columella, Varro, Cato all
describe it). MEDIUM on the exact magnitude of yield gain, which is an
estimate extrapolated from later historical transitions, not a Roman-era
measurement.

### heavy_plough — Mouldboard plough with coulter and wheeled forecarriage (*plaumoratum*, attested regional term)

**What it is / why you want it.** A wheeled plough with an iron coulter (a
vertical knife ahead of the share, to cut roots and turf), a share that
slices horizontally, and a mouldboard that turns the cut slice completely
over, burying weeds and surface trash and creating a proper furrow with
drainage. This is a genuinely different tool from the Mediterranean ard
(*aratrum*), which merely scratches a groove and is usually cross-ploughed
in two directions to break the soil, without turning it.

**Why you would never guess this.** The mistake a modern person makes here
is assuming "bigger, heavier plough" is a universal upgrade. It is not. On
the thin, dry, stony soils of central Italy, the ard is the *correct* tool:
it disturbs the soil minimally, does not invert and expose what little
moisture and organic matter there is to the sun, and is light enough to be
pulled by a single pair of oxen across ground where a heavy mouldboard
plough would drag, clog, or simply be the wrong answer to a problem Italy
does not have. The heavy plough earns its keep specifically on the deep,
heavy, wet clay soils of northern Gaul, Britain and Germania, ground that
the ard cannot break properly and that badly needs the ridging and drainage
a mouldboard furrow provides. Sell this as a universal replacement for the
ard and you will be laughed off an Italian estate, correctly.

**Prerequisites.** Good iron for the share and coulter (Noric iron from the
eastern Alps is already reputed for quality; Spanish iron also good),
blacksmithing to shape and periodically resharpen the edges, a wheelwright's
skill for the forecarriage, oak or similar hardwood for beam and mouldboard.
A team of oxen (or, on the heaviest clay, more than the normal pair) able to
pull the greater draught load; this interacts directly with
`horse_collar_harness` below if you intend to use horses instead of oxen.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron (Noricum, Spain, Britain's own Weald and
Forest of Dean ore already worked locally by the time you introduce this,
which is convenient since that is exactly where you want the tool). Oak for
the frame. The general concept of a wheeled plough with small forecarriage
wheels is not entirely foreign to the Roman world: Pliny (Natural History
18, attributed, unverified as to exact chapter) records a plough with a pair
of small wheels recently in use among the Raeti of the Alpine provinces,
called by a local name. You are not introducing an alien concept from
nothing, you are taking a marginal regional device and deliberately routing
it to the frontier provinces where it solves a real problem, and pairing it
with a true mouldboard, which the Raetian wheeled ard may not have had.

**Procedure.**
1. Forge a coulter: a straight iron blade roughly knee-height, mounted
   vertically ahead of the share, edge forward, to slice through turf and
   roots before the share reaches them.
2. Forge or fit a share (*vomer*, already a normal plough part) shaped to
   cut a horizontal slice of a consistent width, commonly reckoned in
   Roman practice by the ploughman's eye and the furrow width he is used
   to; do not invent a precise modern dimension, set it by trial on your
   own soil and widen or narrow the share until the mouldboard turns a
   clean, unbroken furrow slice.
3. Fit a curved mouldboard, wood is workable and was likely the original
   material historically, iron-faced wood lasts longer, mounted behind and
   above the share so the cut slice rides up and over, flipping upside
   down into the previous furrow.
4. Mount the whole assembly on a two-wheeled forecarriage ahead of the
   beam. The wheels carry the weight of the front of the plough and let the
   ploughman set ploughing depth by adjusting the axle, rather than fighting
   the plough by hand the whole furrow, which is the real labour-saving
   point as much as the turning action.
5. Test on the heaviest, wettest field available. Plough in autumn so the
   turned clods can weather and break down over winter frost, which the
   Mediterranean ard-and-cross-plough method does not need because it does
   not produce large clods in the first place.
6. Lay the furrows with a slight camber (ridge and furrow), so that low
   furrows act as informal open drains carrying surface water off the
   ridged crop rows. This is the single biggest reason the heavy plough
   matters on wet ground: it is not just tillage, it is drainage.

**How you know it worked.** Compare a ridge-and-furrow heavy-ploughed field
against an ard-ploughed (cross-ploughed) control strip on the same heavy
clay soil, same crop, same season. Look first at standing water after rain:
the heavy-ploughed field should shed water off the ridges within a day or
two where the ard field puddles. Then compare yield the way you did for
`crop_rotation`.

**Failure modes.** Used on thin dry Mediterranean soil, it overturns and
exposes topsoil to sun and wind, drying it out and losing what organic
matter it has; do not deploy it there. On very heavy clay when too wet, the
mouldboard clogs solid and the whole team stalls; wait for the soil to dry
to a workable state (a handful should crumble, not smear, when squeezed).
The coulter and share need frequent resharpening on stony ground, more
smith-hours than the simple ard.

**Cost & labour.** More iron than an ard (coulter plus share versus share
alone), a wheelwright's forecarriage, and a larger draught team on the
heaviest ground. ESTIMATED: several artisan-days of blacksmith and carpenter
time to build one, against an ard that a farm carpenter can largely make
himself; ongoing smith time for resharpening is the recurring cost. No
denarii figure is given here because it depends entirely on local iron and
labour prices, which you should look up locally rather than trust an
invented number.

**Danger.** None beyond the ordinary risks of working oxen and sharp iron
tools. Socially: do not present this to an Italian landowner as an
improvement on his ard, he is right and you are wrong there. Present it,
correctly, as a specialised tool for specific provinces.

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.** The physics of mouldboard turning versus ard
scratching, and the soil-type distinction (heavy wet soils benefit, thin dry
Mediterranean soils do not), is well established agricultural history.
The Pliny reference to a wheeled plough among the Raeti is a genuine ancient
attestation but the passage is brief and the device's exact form
(true mouldboard versus wheeled ard) is debated among historians; treat that
specific point as attributed, unverified.

### horse_collar_harness — see Module 40 for construction; agronomic case here

**What it is / why you want it.** A rigid padded collar that rests on the
horse's shoulders and lets it push its whole body weight into the load,
instead of the Roman throat-and-girth harness (a strap across the throat and
a girth strap round the belly) that Roman carts and light ploughs generally
use for horses. Full construction detail is in Module 40; this entry covers
only the agronomic case for bothering, because the case is weaker and more
contested than popular accounts claim.

**Why you would never guess this.** Everyone who has read a popular history
of technology "knows" the Roman throat-and-girth harness choked horses and
that the medieval collar harness gave a fourfold increase in useful pulling
power. That specific claim, and the figure, comes from Lynn White's 1962
book on medieval technology, and it has been substantially challenged since
by historians working directly from harness iconography and from
physical reconstructions (notably Georges Raepsaet's work on ancient
traction, and John Langdon's study of horses and oxen in medieval English
agriculture). The honest modern picture is more like this: throat-and-girth
harness genuinely does restrict a horse's breathing and reduce useful
tractive effort under sustained heavy load, most severely on things like
deep ploughing heavy clay or hauling very heavy freight; but ancient
harness was not uniformly throat-and-girth either, breast-strap
(breast-collar) harnesses attested from the Roman period distribute load
across the chest rather than the throat and perform considerably better;
and Rome's primary draught animal for heavy work was the ox, yoked at the
withers by a method the throat-choke criticism barely applies to at all.
So the honest range for the true horse-specific efficiency gain from a
proper rigid collar, for sustained heavy draught work, is something like a
few tens of percent up to perhaps a doubling of sustained tractive effort,
not the discredited "four times" figure, and the gain is essentially
irrelevant for ox traction, which most Roman heavy field work already uses.

**Prerequisites.** Module 40 (harness and traction) for the actual build.
Leatherworking, padding materials (wool felt, straw stuffing), and either
wood or iron for the rigid collar frame.

**Roman-available inputs.** Tanned leather (already a mature Roman craft),
wool felt, straw. No exotic material is needed; the missing element is
purely the design, specifically the rigid load-bearing frame that keeps
pressure off the windpipe and jugular and puts it on the shoulder bones.

**Procedure.** See Module 40.

**How you know it worked.** Harness two comparable horses to identical
loads on a level road or in a comparable ploughing task, one on
throat-and-girth, one on the new collar. On the collar horse you should see
less visible distress (head not thrown back, less audible wheezing) at
loads that make the throat-and-girth horse struggle, and the collar horse
should sustain a heavier load or a longer working period before tiring.
This is a comparative field test, not a laboratory measurement; do not
expect precise numbers.

**Failure modes.** A badly fitted rigid collar, too tight or poorly padded,
causes shoulder sores worse than the harness it replaces; fit it to the
individual animal, as you would a saddle.

**Cost & labour.** As Module 40.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary animal handling.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The existence and rough shape of the historiographic
dispute is well attested (this is a live debate in medieval and Roman
technology history, not something I am inventing), but the precise
magnitude of the real-world efficiency gain is genuinely unsettled in the
scholarship, and any single number, including the range given above, should
be treated as an informed estimate rather than a fact.

### manuring_and_lime — Marl, lime, compost, bone meal, and the road to N-P-K

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already manures, marls, and limes.
The genuine addition here is threefold: systematising what is already
scattered practice into estate-wide routine backed by measurement, adding
bone meal as a phosphorus source (a genuine gap in Roman practice as far as
the sources show), and flagging superphosphate (bones treated with strong
sulfuric acid) as a large, real, but distant win that depends on chemistry
you do not yet have.

**Why you would never guess this.** Cato and Columella already rank manure
types by potency (pigeon and poultry dung rated highest, human waste next,
then the dung of sheep, goats, and larger stock in descending order,
attributed to their texts, exact ranking order unverified in detail) and
already recommend marling on land that needs it, a practice they in turn
attribute to Gaul and Britain (Pliny, Natural History 17, on *marga*, a term
he treats as a Gallic loanword; attributed, exact chapter unverified). What
Roman practice does not have is any concept of the soil needing specific
chemical elements in specific ratios, so a Roman farmer manures by "this
land looks tired, apply dung," not by "this land is short of phosphorus,"
and has no route to bone meal or mineral-derived fertilisers as distinct
from organic waste.

**Prerequisites.** None for marling, liming, and composting; these are
already within Roman practice, your contribution is measurement and
consistency (Module 60). Bone meal needs only a mill or mortar to crush
dried bone. Superphosphate needs concentrated sulfuric acid, which is a
separate and much harder prerequisite (see below).

**Roman-available inputs.** Farmyard and pigeon-house dung (already
collected on any working estate). Marl (clay-lime deposits, found in chalky
regions of Gaul and Britain, and in parts of Italy too). Limestone, burned
in the same kilns already used to make quicklime for mortar and concrete
(Vitruvius, De Architectura, book 2, attributed) to spread on acid soils.
Butchery bone waste, abundant around any town with meat markets. Sulfur
(Sicily). Iron or copper vitriol (green and blue vitriol, both already
known Roman substances per the standing inventory of Roman-available
materials).

**Procedure.**
1. Composting and manure: collect and rot dung in a covered pit or heap
   rather than leaving it exposed to rain, which leaches away much of its
   value before it ever reaches the field; this single change, keeping
   manure covered and letting it rot properly before spreading, is a real
   and immediately actionable improvement over casual practice on many
   estates, even though the underlying idea (rotted manure works better
   than fresh) is already known to Columella.
2. Marling: dig marl from a local pit (test by taste and by its effect when
   worked into a handful of acid, sticky, unproductive soil; marl improves
   texture and reduces acidity on heavy clay in particular) and spread it
   thickly, then plough in. This is a slow, heavy-labour operation with
   effects that last years, not a seasonal input; Roman and Gallic sources
   agree marled land stays improved for a long time.
3. Liming acid soils: burn limestone to quicklime (bright white, caustic,
   already routine for mortar), slake it with water (it will hiss and heat,
   handle with the same care as mortar lime, it burns skin), let it weather
   in the open for some weeks until it crumbles to a fine powder, then
   spread on soil that shows signs of acidity (sorrel, rushes and certain
   weeds thriving where cereal struggles are the traditional signs a Roman
   farmer would already recognise even without knowing the word "acid").
4. Bone meal: dry butchery bone waste thoroughly, then crush or pound it as
   fine as you can manage (a stone mortar or an animal-powered mill
   normally used for grain works). Spread on land intended for a
   demanding crop. This adds phosphorus, which supports root growth and
   seed formation in a way that manure and marl alone do not reliably
   supply; a Roman farmer will have no existing framework for why this
   helps, only that fields treated with crushed bone show stronger, more
   vigorous growth, and that is enough to justify the practice on its own
   evidence.
5. Superphosphate (the distant goal): treating crushed bone with strong
   sulfuric acid converts the phosphorus into a much more soluble, faster-
   acting form. This is real and worth building toward, but it requires
   concentrated sulfuric acid, which the Roman world of 100 AD does not
   possess (they have vitriol salts and sulfur, not the acid itself); making
   it is a chemistry problem outside the scope of this module. Treat this
   as a flagged future win once your chemistry program can deliver oil of
   vitriol, not a near-term recipe.

**How you know it worked.** Split-plot comparison again (Module 60):
adjacent strips, same seed, one treated, one not, harvested and measured
the same way. For bone meal specifically, look for stronger stem and root
development and better seed set rather than just taller leaf growth, which
is what a nitrogen-rich manure alone tends to produce.

**Failure modes.** Over-liming makes some soils worse, not better,
particularly ground that was not actually acidic to begin with; test on a
small strip before committing a whole field. Fresh, unrotted manure applied
directly can scorch young seedlings and also spreads weed seed the compost
process would otherwise have killed through heat. Quicklime handled
carelessly burns skin and eyes badly; always slake before spreading, never
apply the caustic burnt form directly to soil or to a person.

**Cost & labour.** Marling and liming are heavy manual labour, digging,
carting, spreading, ESTIMATED at many cart-loads per iugerum depending on
how depleted the soil is; no reliable Roman-era denarii figure exists for
this, cost it locally against known cartage rates. Bone meal costs little
beyond the labour of collecting and crushing waste that a town butchery
would otherwise discard, which makes it one of the cheapest genuine wins
in this whole module.

**Danger.** Burns from quicklime, as above. No serious social risk; manuring
and marling are entirely unremarkable Roman farm practices, and even bone
meal is unlikely to raise eyebrows since ground bone is a familiar
substance.

**Confidence: HIGH** on Roman practice of manuring, marling and liming
(directly attested, Cato, Columella, Pliny). HIGH on the chemistry of
nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium as plant nutrients (settled modern
agronomy). MEDIUM on bone meal as a genuine gap in specifically Roman
practice, since the sources describe bone and horn shavings used in other
contexts but I am not confident they describe deliberate bone-meal
fertilising. LOW on any near-term path to superphosphate, which depends on
acid-making chemistry not covered in this module.

### selective_breeding — Pedigree records and progeny testing

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already breeds livestock and plants
deliberately and well. Columella (books 6 through 9, attributed) gives
detailed criteria for choosing a stud bull, stallion, or ram by
conformation, temperament and the quality of its own dam and sire; Varro
does the same for horses and cattle; grain growers already save seed from
the best-looking ears each harvest. What you add is not the idea of
selection, which Rome already has, but written pedigree records kept across
generations and progeny testing, judging a sire not by his own appearance
but by measuring how his actual offspring perform, compared across several
mothers and several years. This turns breeding from a skilled art into a
compounding, cumulative, records-based program.

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks like nothing has changed, an
estate still just chooses the best-looking ram to cover the ewes. The
difference is invisible in any single generation and only shows up as a
gap that opens slowly over many years between an estate that keeps records
and tests progeny and one that selects purely on individual appearance,
which can be badly misleading (a fine-looking sire can still throw poor
offspring; you cannot tell without checking).

**Prerequisites.** Literacy and a simple, consistent record-keeping habit
(Module 60 again, since this is fundamentally the same discipline as
agricultural measurement, applied to animals and plants instead of fields).
No new material inputs.

**Roman-available inputs.** The animals and plants Rome already breeds:
cattle, sheep, horses, pigs, poultry, vines (already propagated by
selected cuttings and grafting, a genuinely sophisticated Roman practice),
and grain (selected by ear).

**Procedure.**
1. For every breeding animal, keep a written record: parentage (both sire
   and dam, not just the sire, which Roman practice already tends to favour
   recording), birth date, and simple measured traits relevant to its use
   (wool weight at shearing for sheep, milk yield for cattle, load pulled
   or distance covered for draught and riding horses, litter size for pigs).
2. Do not judge a sire on his own build alone. Track the measured
   performance of his offspring across multiple different dams and multiple
   years, since a sire whose offspring reliably outperform the estate
   average, regardless of which dam, is a genuinely better breeding animal
   than one who merely looks impressive.
3. Cull consistently on the recorded numbers, not on appearance or
   sentiment, and keep the best-performing lines even when a particular
   animal does not look like the ideal type.
4. For grain, extend the same logic beyond simply picking the best-looking
   ears at harvest (already standard Roman practice): keep a small measured
   comparison plot each year of seed saved from your top-performing lines
   against ordinary seed, so you can tell over several years whether your
   selection is actually improving yield or drifting on noise.
5. For vines, record which named cultivars and which specific mother
   vines, grafted or layered, actually perform best on your specific soil
   and aspect, since Roman viticultural literature already recognises that
   varieties perform very differently by region; formalise this into a
   written estate record rather than relying on the memory of one
   experienced vinedresser who may not live forever.

**How you know it worked.** After several generations (a handful of years
for sheep and pigs, longer for cattle and horses, several harvests for
grain), compare the measured trait (wool weight, milk yield, grain yield
per plant or per plot) between your record-selected line and an
unselected control line kept on the same land under the same conditions.
The selected line should show a real, if gradual, upward trend; the
unselected line should not.

**Failure modes.** Overzealous culling on a single trait (say, wool weight
alone) can degrade other traits (hardiness, fertility) you were not
tracking; record several traits, not one. Inbreeding from too small a
breeding population causes real problems (weakness, deformity, reduced
fertility) within a few generations; keep the breeding pool as large as the
estate can manage, or exchange breeding stock with neighbouring estates
periodically, which Roman practice already does informally.

**Cost & labour.** Purely a record-keeping habit; the main cost is literate
staff time, ESTIMATED at a modest fraction of a manager's or scribe's time
per season, since the observations (weighing wool, measuring grain,
recording parentage) are things a well-run estate already does in some
form, the addition is writing it down consistently and reviewing it.

**Danger.** None physical or social; this is entirely unremarkable
estate management, if anything it will look admirably diligent to a Roman
landowner already inclined toward Columella's meticulous style.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Selective breeding by observable trait and the value
of tracking parentage across generations is well-established, settled
agricultural science; the gap in Roman practice, systematic multi-
generation written progeny records as opposed to single-generation
selection by appearance, is a reasonable and modest claim, not an
overclaim, since the Roman agronomic sources, thorough as they are, mostly
describe choosing a good individual animal or seed-ear rather than tracking
offspring performance across years.

### irrigation_and_drainage — Archimedes screw, force pumps, and tile field drains

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already has excellent water-lifting
machinery: the Archimedes screw (*cochlea*), used both for irrigation and
for dewatering mines such as the silver-lead workings at Rio Tinto in
Spain, and the force pump (associated with Ctesibius of Alexandria and
described by Vitruvius, De Architectura book 10, attributed), used for
firefighting and water supply. What Roman practice underuses is systematic
land drainage of heavy, waterlogged soil using buried fired-clay pipe
drains, as opposed to open ditches and the occasional drainage tunnel
(*cuniculus*, well attested in Etruria for lake and marsh drainage).

**Why you would never guess this.** It seems like a small, boring
difference between an open ditch and a buried pipe. The difference is
large in practice: open ditches take up farmable land, silt up and need
constant re-digging, and only drain the immediate strip beside them; a
grid of buried tile drains a few Roman feet down, draining into a header
ditch, lowers the water table across a whole field, turning genuinely
unfarmable waterlogged clay, of which northern Gaul, Britain and the Po
valley all have plenty, into productive arable land, all while leaving the
surface completely clear for ploughing and unaffected by the drains beneath.

**Prerequisites.** Tile and brick kilns, which Rome already runs at scale
for roofing tile (*tegula*, *imbrex*) and pipe (Roman water-supply pipe is
often lead but fired clay pipe for drains and small water runs is also
attested); the same kilns and clay can be turned to short lengths of
drainage pipe. Basic surveying tools (the *groma* for right angles, the
*chorobates* or a simple water level for grade) to lay drains on a
consistent, gentle fall toward an outlet.

**Roman-available inputs.** Clay (everywhere), the existing brick and tile
industry, and, for the screws and pumps, bronze and iron for fittings and
timber for the screw barrel and pump housing; both machines are already
manufactured Roman items, not new introductions.

**Procedure.**
1. For drainage: fire short clay pipe sections, roughly hand-length, either
   as plain cylinders or as horseshoe-section tile laid over a channel cut
   in the earth (a form already close to roofing tile manufacture).
2. Dig trenches on a gentle continuous downward grade (check with a water
   level or the chorobates) across the wettest part of the field, feeding
   into a larger header drain or an existing ditch.
3. Lay the pipe or tile in the trench bottom, cover with loose stone or
   coarse gravel if available to keep the joints from silting immediately,
   then backfill with soil, leaving the surface level and ploughable.
4. Space the drain lines according to how wet the ground is; wetter ground
   needs closer spacing. Set this by observation on your own land rather
   than a fixed number, since soil permeability varies enormously.
5. For irrigation on drier land or terraced ground, use the Archimedes
   screw or a chain of force pumps to lift water from a river or cistern to
   channel height, then distribute by gravity-fed channels, exactly as
   Roman market gardens already do around cities.

**How you know it worked.** A properly drained field should be walkable and
workable within a day or two after heavy rain where it previously stayed
waterlogged for a week or more; standing water and rush/sedge growth
(both signs of persistently wet ground a Roman farmer would already read
correctly) should visibly recede over the seasons following installation.

**Failure modes.** Drains laid without adequate fall silt up and stop
working; always survey the grade properly first. Pipe laid too shallow is
crushed by ploughing or cart traffic; lay below normal plough depth.
Outlets that are not kept clear back up the whole system.

**Cost & labour.** Substantial: this is trench-digging labour across a
whole field plus the tile-firing cost. ESTIMATED as a major capital
improvement comparable in effort to any other significant Roman land
improvement (marling, terracing), justified by permanently converting
unusable wet ground into farmland, which is a one-time cost for a
recurring gain; no reliable per-iugerum denarii figure is given here since
it depends entirely on local clay, fuel, and labour cost.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary excavation and kiln-firing hazards.

**Confidence: HIGH** that Rome already has the Archimedes screw and force
pump (well attested, Vitruvius). MEDIUM-HIGH on tile field drains as a
genuine addition: Roman drainage tunnels (cuniculi) for large-scale marsh
and lake drainage are well attested, but I am not confident that fine-
grained buried tile field drains at the scale later used in early modern
Europe were routine Roman practice, so treat the systematic small-scale
tile-drain grid as a real, buildable, but not clearly Roman-attested
improvement.

### food_preservation — Beyond salting, smoking and garum: canning, granary design, ice

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already preserves food extremely
well by salting, smoking, drying, and above all by garum and liquamen,
fermented fish sauces produced at industrial scale (Baetica in Spain,
Pontus on the Black Sea) and traded across the whole Mediterranean. Do not
present any of that as your contribution. What you can genuinely add is
airtight canning by Appert's method (heat-sealing food in a sealed
container to kill spoilage organisms, invented in France in 1809, but
achievable with Roman glassblowing and no germ theory at all, since it
works purely empirically), better-designed granaries against damp and
rodents, and systematic ice houses.

**Why you would never guess this.** Canning looks like it needs to know
about germs. It does not; Nicolas Appert developed and proved it decades
before germ theory existed, purely by observing that food sealed and
heated in a jar kept far longer than food merely sealed, and that longer
heating kept it longer still. It is a genuinely empirical, trial-and-error
discoverable technique that Rome has every physical prerequisite for
(glassblowing, cork, wax, fire) and has simply never stumbled onto in this
specific combination.

**Prerequisites.** Glassblowing (already Roman, and Roman glass can already
be made reasonably clear and colourless) for jars, or failing that, well-
made pottery with a reliable seal. Cork (from cork oak, Iberia, already
known and used by Romans for stoppers and floats) or a tight-fitting
ground glass stopper, sealed further with wax or pitch. A large pot and
fire for the boiling-water bath.

**Roman-available inputs.** Glass jars (blown, Roman glass industry is
already large and skilled). Cork stoppers (Iberia). Beeswax or pine pitch
for sealing over the stopper. Vinegar (*acetum*) for acidifying vegetables
before canning, which matters enormously, see the warning below. Salt and
brine as before, unchanged from existing Roman practice.

**Procedure, canning.**
1. Pack the prepared food into a clean glass jar, leaving a thumb's width
   of headspace at the top.
2. For high-acid foods only (fruit preserved in its own juice or with
   added vinegar or wine must, pickled vegetables in a genuinely sour
   vinegar brine): seat the stopper loosely, not fully sealed, and stand
   the jar in a large pot of water reaching most of the way up its sides.
   Bring the water to a full rolling boil (100 degrees Celsius at sea
   level, recognisable as the point where the water is in constant
   vigorous motion, not just steaming) and hold it there for a period
   ESTIMATED at somewhere in the range of ten to thirty minutes depending
   on jar size and food density, since I do not have a Roman-tested figure
   and you should verify by keeping a small tested batch and opening one
   after a month to check for spoilage before trusting a whole season's
   production to any specific time you settle on.
3. Remove the jar while still hot and immediately press the stopper in
   fully and seal over it with melted wax or pitch, forming an airtight
   cap while the contents are still hot; as the jar cools, the seal should
   draw slightly inward, which is your sign of a good seal.
4. Store cool and dark. A properly sealed jar should show no bulging, no
   escaping gas or hiss when opened, and the food inside should smell
   normal, not sour, foul, or of anything but itself, when you finally
   open it.

**The blunt warning.** Do this only for high-acid foods: fruit, and
vegetables genuinely acidified with vinegar or fermented to real sourness.
Do not can plain low-acid food, meat, plain vegetables, beans, in a sealed
jar processed only in boiling water. The reason is specific and serious:
a spore-forming organism that causes a deadly paralytic poisoning
(botulism) survives ordinary boiling (100 degrees Celsius) entirely
unharmed, and if it is sealed into an airtight, low-acid, oxygen-free
environment it will germinate and produce a lethal toxin with no smell,
no taste, and no visible sign of spoilage in the jar. Reliably killing
those spores requires temperatures well above boiling, achievable only
under pressure, which a simple pot of boiling water cannot reach at all
(water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level regardless of how long or
how hard you heat it, the temperature does not rise further without
raising the pressure). A sealed pressure vessel could reach the needed
temperature but is a genuinely dangerous device to improvise, an
explosion hazard if built without a working pressure-release valve, and is
not covered as a safe recipe here. The safe rule for 100 AD Rome: can
high-acid foods in boiling water as above; preserve low-acid foods (meat,
plain vegetables, beans) by the existing Roman methods, salting, smoking,
drying, or genuine deep cold storage, never by sealing them airtight after
only a boiling-water treatment.

**Procedure, granary design.** Roman grain warehouses (*horrea*) are
already well designed, raised on low walls or piers for underfloor air
circulation, thick-walled for temperature stability, a genuinely good
existing practice (attested archaeologically at Ostia and in the Horrea
Galbae at Rome). The improvement worth insisting on everywhere, not just
at the great state warehouses: raised, ventilated floors on every granary
including small farm stores, not just prestige buildings; tight-fitting
doors and shutters to keep rodents and damp out; and a lime wash on
interior walls, since lime is caustic enough to discourage mould and
insect life on the surface it coats, a genuine minor improvement over bare
stone or timber.

**Procedure, ice houses.** Rome already trades in snow and ice for cooling
drinks in the city; formalise this into food preservation rather than
luxury drink-cooling. Dig a deep pit, line with straw insulation on all
sides, pack with ice or compacted snow cut in winter, cover thickly with
more straw and a roofed structure to shed rain, and include a drain at the
bottom for meltwater so the ice does not sit in its own water. Used well,
such a pit can hold ice through much of a Mediterranean summer, giving you
genuine deep cold storage for foods that spoil too fast for drying or
salting, most usefully fresh meat and fish for short-term use, not
long-term storage.

**How you know it worked.** For canning: open a test jar after a month and
check for bulging, off smell, or foaming, discard immediately and do not
taste anything that shows any of these signs. For granaries: check grain
moisture by feel and for weevil or mould presence after storage compared
to an old-style unraised granary. For ice houses: check how far into
summer usable ice remains.

**Failure modes.** Canning low-acid food as described above: botulism,
potentially fatal, with no warning signs in the jar; this is the single
most dangerous failure mode in this entire module and deserves to be taken
completely seriously. Poor seals on canning jars simply spoil normally,
visibly, which is a nuisance, not a danger, provided you follow the rule
above and stick to high-acid contents. Granaries: damp floors breed mould
that can produce its own dangerous toxins in stored grain even without any
canning involved, a real and separate risk Roman farmers already guard
against instinctively by choosing dry storage sites.

**Cost & labour.** Canning needs glass jars (an existing craft good, cost
varies locally, no reliable denarii figure given) and fuel for the boiling
bath; ESTIMATED as a modest, repeatable household or small-estate cost, not
a large capital project. Ice houses need one substantial dig and structure,
then only seasonal ice-cutting labour to refill.

**Danger.** Botulism as described above, the most serious danger in this
module, treat the high-acid-only rule as non-negotiable. Burns from
boiling water and from handling hot sealed jars. Lime wash handling as in
the manuring entry.

**Confidence: HIGH** on Roman salting, smoking, drying and garum already
being excellent (well attested, Columella book 12, attributed, and Pliny
book 31 on salt and fish products). HIGH on the chemistry and danger
profile of boiling-water canning versus true low-acid canning (settled
modern food science). MEDIUM on exact processing times, explicitly flagged
as estimated above. MEDIUM-HIGH on Roman granary design already being
fairly good, so keep expectations of your own contribution there modest.

### windmill_watermill_grain — Milling capacity as a bottleneck

**What it is / why you want it.** Grinding grain into flour is a real,
often underappreciated constraint on how much bread a city can actually
eat, regardless of how much grain the fields and the grain fleet deliver.
Rome already runs large water-powered mills (the Barbegal complex near
Arles in southern Gaul, a battery of over a dozen water wheels driving
grain mills on a single hillside, is a genuine, MEASURED archaeological
find, generally dated to the Roman imperial period, exact date debated,
treat as ESTIMATED within the 2nd century AD) alongside the huge number of
smaller animal- and slave-powered rotary mills, the hourglass-shaped lava
mills found in bakeries at Pompeii being the best-known example. The
windmill, by contrast, genuinely does not exist yet anywhere in the Roman
world; it is a later invention, and you should not claim otherwise.

**Why you would never guess this.** It is easy to assume that if the grain
supply is secure (the annona, discussed below, obsesses over exactly this)
the food problem is solved. It is not: grain sitting in a horreum still has
to be ground before it becomes bread, and milling capacity, water wheels,
animal mills, hand querns, and the labour or animals to drive them, is a
separate and sometimes binding constraint, particularly in a city the size
of Rome itself.

**Prerequisites.** Water mill construction is already a mature Roman skill
(Vitruvius, De Architectura book 10, attributed, describes the
water-powered mill). Windmill introduction needs only carpentry, sailcloth
or wooden-slat sails, and a pivoting mount, all within existing Roman
craft skill; the missing element is purely the idea and the gearing
arrangement to turn a horizontal sail rotation into a vertical millstone
rotation (or vice versa depending on design), not any exotic material.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber, iron fittings, millstones (already a
Roman industry, notably lava millstones from the Rhineland and volcanic
stone from Italy), sailcloth or canvas (already made for ships).

**Procedure.**
1. Where reliable flowing water exists, prioritise water mills over any
   other option; they are already the highest-capacity, lowest-running-cost
   Roman milling technology, and multiplying their number (more wheels, on
   more sites, better gearing to run more stones per wheel, as Barbegal
   already demonstrates is achievable) is the single most effective lever
   for genuinely relieving urban milling bottlenecks.
2. Where water power is unavailable or unreliable (flat country, low
   rainfall regions, or windy coastal and island sites), introduce a
   windmill: a rotating cap or whole tower carrying angled sails, geared to
   turn a vertical shaft driving a millstone, sited on open high ground to
   catch consistent wind.
3. Continue running animal- and slave-powered rotary mills as a flexible,
   low-capital supplement where neither water nor consistent wind is
   available, exactly as Rome already does.

**How you know it worked.** Track flour output per day against the number
and capacity of mills feeding a given bakery or city district; a genuine
capacity increase should show up as either more bread from the same grain
input in the same time, or the same bread produced with fewer animal- or
slave-hours diverted to milling instead of other work.

**Failure modes.** Water mills fail in drought or flood; windmills fail in
still weather or are damaged in storms if not built to reef or furl their
sails; neither is fully reliable alone, which is why keeping animal-powered
mills as backup capacity matters.

**Cost & labour.** Large capital projects, comparable to any substantial
Roman infrastructure; ESTIMATED as beyond smallholder means, historically
the kind of thing built by the state, a wealthy patron, or a guild, not an
individual farmer, no specific denarii figure is given since none is
reliably known to me.

**Danger.** Normal construction and moving-machinery hazards (crush and
entanglement risk around gearing and millstones).

**Confidence: HIGH** that Roman water mills, including large multi-wheel
installations, are real and already built. HIGH that windmills are a
genuine post-Roman introduction (the horizontal-axis European windmill is
medieval) and should not be presented as anything Rome already has.

### new_crops_reachable — What Roman trade can bring in that Rome underuses

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's trade network, via the Red Sea and
Indian Ocean monsoon route to India, and via the Silk Road overland, is
genuinely global in reach even if the volumes are small and the goods
expensive. A short, carefully hedged list of crops worth deliberately
pursuing through that network, since you cannot invent a new crop, only
route an existing one into Roman cultivation:

- **Rice** (*oryza*): known to Rome mainly as an imported medicinal starch
  or luxury foodstuff, not cultivated within the empire as far as the
  sources show. Genuinely reachable via existing India trade; whether it
  can be grown well within Roman territory depends on finding suitable wet
  ground (the Nile Delta and parts of Mesopotamia are candidates).
  Confidence: MEDIUM that seed stock is reachable via trade, LOW on how
  well it would establish as a Roman-era field crop given the effort
  needed to build paddy infrastructure.
- **Cotton** (*gossypium*): Pliny (Natural History 12, attributed,
  unverified exact chapter) describes a "tree wool" from India and Egypt;
  cotton cloth is already imported and there is some cultivation in Egypt.
  The opportunity is expansion, not introduction. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH
  that Rome already has some cotton, LOW-MEDIUM on how much expansion is
  realistically achievable given climate constraints outside Egypt.
- **Sugar cane** (*saccharum*): known to Rome only as an imported medicine,
  a sweet reed-derived substance from India and Arabia (Pliny, attributed),
  not cultivated anywhere near the Mediterranean; large-scale Mediterranean
  sugar cultivation historically only arrives centuries later. This is a
  real but distant opportunity, worth attempting in Egypt or the Levant if
  planting material can be obtained, but do not expect quick results.
  Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM on both the trade reachability of viable planting
  stock and the climate suitability at this date.
- **Citrus**: the citron (*citrus medica*, the "Median apple") is already
  grown in Italy and well attested since the Republic. Lemon and sweet
  orange are NOT yet available and will not become so through any trade
  route reachable from Rome in 100 AD; do not claim them. Confidence: HIGH
  that citron is already Roman, HIGH that lemon and orange are not
  reachable.
- **Durum ("hard") wheat**: already a Mediterranean-native wheat type and
  already within Roman cultivation in some form; this is not a new crop.
  The genuine opportunity is selecting and propagating the hardest,
  highest-gluten local strains specifically, which pairs with
  `selective_breeding` above rather than being a trade-introduction item at
  all. Confidence: HIGH that hard wheat is already present in some form.
- **Alfalfa / lucerne** (*medica*): already introduced to Italy, by Pliny's
  own account (Natural History 18, attributed, unverified exact chapter)
  brought from Media during the Persian Wars centuries earlier, already
  valued as a fodder crop cut several times a year. This is not a new
  crop at all; see `fodder_and_livestock` below for what the genuine
  addition is. Confidence: HIGH that Rome already has it.
- **Buckwheat**: native to inner Asia, and I do not have good evidence that
  it had reached Mediterranean trade networks by 100 AD; the well-attested
  spread of buckwheat into Europe is medieval, considerably later, and I
  would be inventing a claim to say it is reachable now. Confidence: LOW,
  treat as probably not available, do not budget on it.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive move for a modern person
is to reach for the crops that mattered most in later world history
(potato, maize, sugar, tobacco) without checking whether Rome already has
a near-equivalent (citron, native wheat) or whether the crop that mattered
later (potato, maize, tomato, chilli, cassava) is a New World plant with no
possible Roman-era source at all. Get this list right before spending any
effort on it.

**Prerequisites.** Existing Roman long-distance trade routes (Red Sea and
Indian Ocean monsoon trade to India, overland Silk Road contact). No new
technology needed, only deliberate purchase of seed stock or planting
material through existing merchants, and willingness to experiment with
unfamiliar plants on marginal land rather than betting the main harvest.

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing trade contacts and coin; this is a
commerce and patience problem, not a technical one.

**Procedure.** Commission merchants already trading to India (via Egypt and
the Red Sea ports, a well-established route) to bring back seed or
planting stock of rice and sugar cane specifically, alongside their normal
cargo. Trial each on a small plot before any large commitment. Expect
multiple failed attempts; this is normal crop introduction, not a sign the
approach is wrong.

**How you know it worked.** A trialled crop either germinates, grows, and
produces a harvestable yield on your test plot, or it does not; there is no
subtler test needed here.

**Failure modes.** Seed or planting material that does not survive the
voyage (heat, damp, time) is the most likely failure, well before any
question of whether the plant would grow in Roman soil at all. Climate
mismatch (rice needs consistent standing water management Rome has not
built infrastructure for; sugar cane needs a longer warm season than most
Roman territory reliably offers) is the second most likely failure.

**Cost & labour.** Merchant commissions and trade goods to pay for exotic
seed stock; ESTIMATED as expensive relative to any domestic seed, in line
with the generally high cost of anything routed through the India trade,
no specific figure given since it depends entirely on the merchant and the
year.

**Danger.** None physical. Socially, experimenting with unfamiliar foreign
plants is unremarkable to Romans, who already prize exotic garden and
orchard novelties.

**Confidence: overall MEDIUM**, broken down crop by crop above; the
important discipline in this entry is the honesty about which items are
already Roman (citron, durum wheat, alfalfa), which are plausibly
reachable but unproven (rice, sugar cane, cotton expansion), and which
should simply be written off (New World crops entirely, buckwheat
probably).

### fodder_and_livestock — Winter fodder and the multiplier effect

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's fodder repertoire is already
broad: alfalfa (medica), vetch (vicia), various clover-like legumes, and
turnips (*rapum*, *napus*, already a recognised Roman crop, Pliny Natural
History 18, attributed, unverified exact chapter, discusses both). The
genuine addition is not a new plant but a systems change: deliberately
growing and storing enough winter fodder to overwinter more animals at
full or near-full numbers, rather than the common practice (across most
of pre-modern agriculture, not unique to Rome) of slaughtering down the
herd in autumn because there is not enough feed to carry them through
winter. More animals overwintered means more manure for the fields above,
more draught animals available for spring ploughing without a lean season
first, more meat and milk, and more leather and wool, all compounding.

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks like a minor scheduling
choice, when to slaughter, not a technology at all. It is one of the
highest-leverage changes in this whole module precisely because it is
free of new material inputs, alfalfa, turnips and vetch already exist on
Roman farms, and the only change needed is deliberately growing more of
them as a dedicated winter-fodder course and actually storing (drying,
clamping root vegetables in sand or earth-covered pits) enough to carry
livestock numbers through to spring, rather than accepting the autumn cull
as inevitable.

**Prerequisites.** `crop_rotation` above (alfalfa and turnips slot
naturally into the legume/fodder course), storage capacity (dry loft space
for hay, pit or clamp storage for turnips and other roots).

**Roman-available inputs.** Alfalfa, vetch, turnips, all already Roman
crops as noted. Straw from the cereal harvest as bulk winter feed
supplement.

**Procedure.**
1. Dedicate part of the rotation's legume/fodder course specifically to a
   winter feed crop: alfalfa cut and dried as hay (already done, Pliny
   records multiple cuts per season), and turnips left in the ground or
   lifted and clamped (stored in a pit or heap covered with straw and
   earth to protect from hard frost) for winter use.
2. Calculate, by simple observation and record (Module 60 again), roughly
   how many animals your stored fodder can actually carry through to
   spring grass, and aim to grow that much fodder deliberately, rather than
   discovering the shortfall in midwinter and culling in a panic.
3. Increase the fodder area gradually year over year as storage and
   growing experience improve, tracking how many more animals you
   successfully overwinter each year.

**How you know it worked.** Count livestock in autumn and again the
following spring; a successful winter-fodder program should show a
steadily rising fraction of the herd surviving winter at full condition,
rather than the traditional sharp autumn cull followed by a small
overwintered core breeding stock.

**Failure modes.** Underestimating how much fodder is needed and running
out in late winter, the worst possible time, when animals are already
weakened and spring grass is not yet available; always keep a safety
margin rather than planning to the exact calculated minimum. Poorly
clamped turnips rot in a wet winter; site the clamp on well-drained
ground and check it periodically.

**Cost & labour.** Mostly a reallocation of existing rotation land and
existing storage labour rather than new cost; ESTIMATED as low
incremental cost for a potentially large gain in overwintered livestock
numbers, though the exact multiplier depends entirely on how far short of
sufficient fodder the estate's previous practice actually was, which
varies enormously and should not be guessed at with a single number.

**Danger.** None physical or social; this is unremarkable estate
management that a careful Roman vilicus would recognise and approve of on
its own terms.

**Confidence: HIGH** that alfalfa, vetch and turnips are already Roman
crops (Pliny, attributed). MEDIUM-HIGH that the systematic winter-fodder-
to-overwinter-more-stock strategy, as opposed to simply having these crops
available, is a genuine improvable practice rather than something Roman
farms already optimise, since the historical pattern of a hard autumn cull
followed by a lean spring recovery is well attested across most of
pre-modern European agriculture generally, Roman included.

### fisheries_and_salt — Salt supply, fish preservation, and state control

**What it is / why you want it.** This is mostly a "know your own system"
entry rather than a new-technique entry: Rome already runs a large,
sophisticated salt and fish-preservation industry, and understanding its
strategic and political shape matters more here than any new recipe.

**Why you would never guess this.** It is tempting to assume salt is a
simple commodity. It is not, in Rome: salt production (*salinae*, salt
pans, both coastal solar-evaporation pans around the Mediterranean and
inland sources) has a long history of state interest, taxation, and at
times direct control, and the word for a soldier's pay, *salarium*, derives
from an association with salt allowance or salt money, a sign of how
central it is treated. Salted fish (*salsamenta*) and fermented fish sauce
(garum and liquamen) are already major industries, Baetica in southern
Spain and the Pontus region on the Black Sea being famous production
centres, exported across the empire in distinctive sealed amphorae.

**Prerequisites.** None; this is largely already-functioning Roman
infrastructure. The one forward-looking addition, genuine curing of meat
with true saltpetre (potassium nitrate, which fixes a stable pink colour
in cured meat and adds a real extra barrier against the same botulism risk
flagged in `food_preservation`), depends entirely on deliberately farmed
nitre beds, a separate and much harder chemistry and biology undertaking
covered elsewhere, not something achievable from ordinary Roman *nitrum*
(sodium carbonate, not saltpetre, the single most common anachronism trap
in this whole guide, do not confuse them).

**Roman-available inputs.** Sea salt from Mediterranean and Atlantic
coastal pans, rock and spring salt from inland sources (including central
European sources reaching Roman markets via trade), fish stocks across
the Mediterranean, Atlantic and Black Sea coasts already exploited at
industrial scale for garum production.

**Procedure.** No new procedure is offered here beyond what Rome already
does extremely well: solar evaporation in coastal pans for salt, salting
and pressing for salsamenta, and fermentation in the sun over weeks for
garum and liquamen (already a mature, well-documented Roman industrial
process). The actionable point for you is strategic rather than technical:
know that salt supply lines and salt taxation are politically sensitive,
that interfering with or appearing to threaten an established salt or
garum trade will draw unwelcome attention from people with real capital
and sometimes state backing invested in it, and that if you want to make
a genuinely safe, well-received contribution in this domain, siting and
developing a new solar salt pan on unexploited coastline, rather than
challenging an existing producer, is the low-friction path.

**How you know it worked.** Existing Roman quality controls (taste, smell,
visual inspection for spoilage) already apply and are already well
understood by Roman salters and fishmongers; nothing new to verify here.

**Failure modes.** None new; the existing industry already manages its own
known failure modes (spoilage in transport, adulteration, seasonal
supply gaps) reasonably well.

**Cost & labour.** Established industry with known, if to me unquantified,
Roman-era costs; no new figures needed since no new technique is proposed
beyond the general note above.

**Danger.** Social and political rather than physical: salt has a history
of state monopoly and taxation in various periods and places in the
Roman world, and large-scale salt or fish-sauce production draws the
attention of people with capital and sometimes official backing already
committed to it. Treat this as a domain to enter carefully and
collaboratively, not to disrupt.

**Confidence: HIGH** that Roman salt and fish-preservation industries
(salinae, salsamenta, garum, liquamen) are already large, sophisticated,
and well attested (Pliny book 31, attributed, on salt; the archaeological
and epigraphic record of garum amphorae is extensive). MEDIUM on the exact
historical extent of state monopoly versus taxation versus private
production at any given moment, which varied by period and region and
should not be treated as a single fixed Roman policy.

### agricultural_statistics — Measured plots and controlled comparison

**What it is / why you want it.** This is the single most important entry
in the module and it is not a technique at all, it is a habit: measuring a
plot's exact area, recording seed sown, recording the harvest by weight or
volume with a consistent measure, and comparing treatments against an
untreated control on otherwise-matched ground, over multiple years before
drawing a conclusion. See Module 60 for the underlying measurement and
recording methods; this entry is the specifically agricultural application
of it.

**Why you would never guess this.** Every single entry above depends on
being able to tell, honestly, whether it actually worked on your own land,
in your own climate, with your own soil, rather than relying on a claim
(mine or anyone else's) about what worked somewhere else. Roman agronomic
writers already report yields (Columella and Varro both cite seed-to-yield
ratios for grain in various regions, attributed, exact figures not
repeated here since I do not have reliable access to verify specific
numbers and will not invent one), so the idea of a yield figure is not
alien to Rome. What is genuinely different is treating measurement as a
routine, repeated, comparative discipline applied to your own farm every
year, rather than an occasional reported figure used mainly for
tax and rent assessment.

**Prerequisites.** Module 60 (measurement: consistent units, honest
recording, basic arithmetic for ratios and averages). Literacy, or a
literate steward. No material inputs beyond the wax tablets or papyrus
already used for ordinary estate accounts.

**Roman-available inputs.** The iugerum (a standard Roman land-area
measure, already used for exactly this kind of record-keeping) as your
area unit, and standard Roman dry-volume measures (the modius and its
subdivisions) for grain quantities, so your records are directly
comparable to every other Roman record without conversion.

**Procedure.**
1. Measure and mark plots precisely, using the same iugerum-based
   measurement for every comparison you ever run, so results are
   comparable across years.
2. Record, every season, for every plot: what was sown, how much seed, the
   date, the treatment if any (rotation position, manure type and amount,
   drainage status), and the harvest amount by standard measure.
3. Always run a genuine untreated or old-practice control alongside any
   new treatment on matched ground, not just before-and-after on the same
   plot, since year-to-year weather variation alone can produce swings
   large enough to make an untested change look like a success or failure
   when it was really just a good or bad year.
4. Keep records across multiple years before concluding anything, since a
   single year's result, especially for anything affecting soil
   fertility over time (rotation, marling, manuring), can be badly
   misleading.
5. Write the records down consistently, in a form future estate managers
   (including you, years later) can actually read and compare, not just
   scattered notes.

**How you know it worked.** This entry is the test itself; its own success
criterion is that every other entry in this module can actually be
verified or disproven on your own land, using it.

**Failure modes.** Comparing mismatched plots (different soil, different
aspect, different drainage) and attributing the difference to your
treatment instead of to the mismatch is the single most common statistical
error available to you here; always match your control plot as closely as
you can to the treatment plot in everything except the one variable you
are testing. Drawing conclusions from a single year is the second most
common error.

**Cost & labour.** Negligible beyond the record-keeping time already
implied by Module 60; this is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost entry in
the entire module.

**Danger.** None.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The statistical logic (matched controls, multi-year
replication, consistent measurement) is basic and settled methodology; the
claim that Rome reports yields but does not routinely run this kind of
controlled, replicated, on-farm comparison is a reasonable reading of the
surviving agronomic literature, which reports results and rules of thumb
rather than describing anything resembling a controlled trial.

## How much surplus do you actually need?

Treat everything in this section as illustrative arithmetic, not a
historical measurement. The premise given for this module is that roughly
80 to 90 percent of the Roman population farms. Take the midpoint, 85
percent farming, 15 percent free for everything else, as the starting
point. If total food output is shared roughly evenly across the whole
population (a simplification; real distribution is far less even, but it
is the right simplification for this order-of-magnitude exercise), then
each person working in farming is, on average, currently producing enough
food for about 1 divided by 0.85, or roughly 1.18 people.

Now ask what non-farming fraction you actually need to run a technical
program at all: not just a few court engineers, but a genuine critical
mass of miners, charcoal burners, smiths, glassblowers, instrument makers,
scribes, and eventually the much larger population that manufacturing
beyond hand-craft requires. There is no single correct target, so consider
three benchmarks:

- **Modest gain, 30 percent non-farming (70 percent still farming):** each
  farmer must now feed 1 divided by 0.70, or about 1.43 people. That is
  roughly 1.43 divided by 1.18, or about a 21 percent increase in output
  per farm worker over the current baseline. This is plausibly within
  reach of crop rotation, better fodder and overwintering, drainage, and
  manuring alone, the techniques in this module, without any further
  mechanisation.
- **Significant gain, 50 percent non-farming:** each farmer must feed 1
  divided by 0.50, or 2.00 people, roughly 2.00 divided by 1.18, or about
  a 69 percent increase over baseline. This is a large ask, plausibly
  achievable only after multiple generations of the combined techniques
  in this module (rotation, drainage, breeding, fodder, statistics all
  compounding together), and probably needs real gains in draught power
  and tools from elsewhere in this guide as well, not agriculture alone.
- **Early-industrial gain, 65 to 70 percent non-farming (roughly matching
  a country like Britain around 1800):** each farmer must feed roughly 1
  divided by 0.35, about 2.86 people, or a 142 percent increase over
  baseline, close to two and a half times current productivity per farm
  worker. Historically this level required not just better rotation and
  drainage but selective breeding compounded over many decades, large-
  scale land reclamation, and eventually mechanical traction beginning to
  replace draught animals; it is a reasonable multi-generation target for
  this module's techniques combined with mechanical progress elsewhere in
  the guide, not something agriculture alone delivers.
- **Modern, transistor-adjacent economies typically farm under 10 percent
  of their population**, often under 5. Reaching that requires synthetic
  nitrogen fixation (industrial chemistry, not covered here), engine-
  powered mechanisation replacing draught animals (which themselves
  consume a meaningful share of agricultural land and fodder, so replacing
  them with machines is itself a real productivity gain), and modern
  plant genetics. None of that is available from this module, or from
  Roman-era technology at all; it is the job of the rest of this guide,
  over a much longer time horizon. This module's honest job is to buy the
  population and the calendar time for that longer program, not to reach
  it directly.

The clear conclusion: the techniques catalogued in this module, honestly
assessed, can plausibly deliver something in the range of a fifth to
roughly two-thirds more food output per farm worker over one to several
generations, moving the non-farming population share from around 15
percent toward perhaps 30 to 50 percent at the outer edge of sustained,
disciplined effort. That is a genuinely transformative gain for a Roman-
era economy and is worth pursuing hard. It is not, on its own, enough to
reach a transistor-level non-farming population share; that requires
industrial chemistry and mechanisation this module deliberately does not
claim to deliver.

## The political economy of feeding Rome

The city of Rome, at perhaps a million people by the early empire, cannot
feed itself from its own hinterland; it depends on grain shipped in bulk
from Egypt and from Africa (roughly modern Tunisia), carried by a
dedicated grain fleet, stored in large state and private warehouses
(*horrea*, including the well-known Horrea Galbae), and distributed to
the urban population partly through ordinary market purchase and partly
through the *annona*, a subsidised or free grain dole to a portion of the
citizen population, administered by a dedicated official, the *praefectus
annonae*, a post generally dated to the reign of Augustus (commonly cited
around 8 AD, treat the precise year as attributed, unverified) as a
permanent equestrian magistracy created after repeated grain crises made
clear that leaving supply to the market and to occasional emergency
measures was not politically survivable.

The number of citizens actually receiving the dole at any given time
varied across the imperial period and is disputed among historians;
ancient sources (Augustus's own Res Gestae, attributed) mention figures on
the order of a couple of hundred thousand recipients at various points,
and modern historians debate the precise number and how it changed over
time. Treat any specific figure, including that one, as an order-of-
magnitude estimate rather than a precise fact, but the scale itself, a
substantial fraction of the city's population directly dependent on a
state-managed grain supply, is not in doubt.

This matters enormously for you because grain supply is, without serious
competition, the regime's single greatest recurring anxiety. Egypt is
governed as the emperor's personal province, its governor (the *praefectus
Aegypti*) reporting directly to the emperor rather than through the
ordinary senatorial provincial system, precisely because whoever controls
Egypt's grain output controls a lever that can starve or feed the capital.
A shortfall in the grain fleet, a bad harvest in Africa, or a delay in
shipping produces immediate, visible, dangerous unrest in the city, not
abstract discontent: Suetonius records that Claudius was once surrounded
and jeered by an angry crowd, pelted with abuse and scraps of bread,
during a period of grain shortage (Suetonius, Life of Claudius, chapter
18, attributed, exact wording unverified), a vivid illustration that an
emperor's own physical safety in his own capital could hinge on whether
ships arrived on schedule. No emperor forgets this, and every prefect and
provincial governor with any responsibility touching the grain supply
knows that failure there is a career-ending, sometimes life-ending,
failure, in a way that failure in most other administrative domains is
not.

This is exactly why agricultural improvement is the single safest lane
available to you for building patronage, protection, and freedom of
action inside the Roman system. It touches nothing politically sensitive:
you are not proposing a change to succession, to the army, to the
provincial power structure, to religion, or to anyone's specific
privileges. You are proposing to grow more food, more reliably, which is
the one outcome every single actor in the Roman system, from the humblest
tenant farmer to the emperor himself, unambiguously wants more of and
fears the lack of. A demonstrated, measured (Module 60 again) improvement
in yield or in storage loss reduction on an imperial estate, in Egypt or
Africa specifically, is the kind of result that gets reported upward
through exactly the administrative channel (the *cura annonae*) that
already exists to notice it, and gets rewarded, because it directly
reduces the regime's worst recurring fear. Route your early, most visible
demonstrations of this module's techniques through grain-producing
estates and toward measurable annona-relevant outcomes (more grain
delivered, less spoiled in transit and storage, more reliable year-to-
year supply) rather than toward showier but politically riskier domains,
and you buy yourself exactly the kind of protected, resourced position
from which the rest of this guide's much harder technical program becomes
possible at all.

## Sources and confidence

Confident citations (HIGH confidence these authors cover the topic,
specific chapter numbers flagged as attributed/unverified where I am not
certain of the exact reference): **Columella**, *De Re Rustica*, twelve
books, the core Roman agronomic manual, covering fallow and cropping
practice, manure, viticulture and orcharding, and detailed animal
husbandry criteria in books six through nine. **Varro**, *De Re Rustica*,
three books in dialogue form, an older and somewhat more discursive
agronomic tradition covering fields, livestock, and villa gardens
including fishponds and aviaries. **Cato**, *De Agri Cultura*, the oldest
surviving work of Latin prose, a terse practical manual for running a
mid-Republican villa estate, strong on manure types, viticulture and
olive-growing, and household preservation recipes. **Pliny**, *Naturalis
Historia*, especially books 17 and 18 on trees, soils, and cereal and
fodder crops, the source for the marl (*marga*) reference, the alfalfa
(*medica*) introduction story, and the Raetian wheeled-plough passage,
and book 31 for salt. **Vitruvius**, *De Architectura*, book 10, the
source for Roman water-lifting machinery including the Archimedes screw
and force pump, and for water-mill construction. **Suetonius**, in his
imperial biographies, for the anecdote about Claudius and the grain-
shortage crowd.

Areas of genuine uncertainty, honestly flagged rather than smoothed over:
exact yield figures and percentage gains throughout this module are
ESTIMATED, based on general historical reasoning about the shift from
two-field to three-field-style rotation, not on any surviving Roman-era
measurement, since no reliable controlled Roman yield comparison of this
kind survives to my knowledge. The magnitude of the true horse-collar
efficiency gain is an active, unresolved historiographical dispute and
should be treated as such, not as settled fact in either direction. The
precise scale of the annona dole population and the exact founding date
of the praefectus annonae office are both debated among historians and
are given here as order-of-magnitude, attributed figures only. Whether
bone meal specifically, as opposed to other bone uses, was Roman practice
is a genuine gap in my confidence and is flagged as such in that entry.
Finally, and most importantly for anyone using this module: the New World
crops that most transformed post-1500 Old World agriculture, potato,
maize, tomato, chilli pepper, and cassava, have no possible source in the
Roman trade world of 100 AD and are not addressed as reachable anywhere
in this module, because they are not reachable, at any cost, through any
route available to you.
