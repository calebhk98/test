# Module 75: Agriculture, Food and Surplus

Rome is not agriculturally ignorant. Columella, Varro, Cato and Pliny
describe crop rotation of a sort, manuring ranked by dung type, marling,
liming, grafting, controlled viticulture and animal selection by
conformation. A competent Roman *vilicus* already does most of what a good
18th-century English farmer does, by instinct and inherited lore rather than
theory. Do not try to teach farmers to farm; you will lose that argument to
a man who owns Cato's text.

What you actually have: the germ theory of soil fertility (you know *why*
legumes work, not just *that* they do), the habit of measurement and
controlled comparison (Module 60), and a short list of specific techniques
and crops that exist somewhere in the world in 100 AD but have not reached
Roman practice. The honest bad news: the crops that transformed Old World
farming after 1500, potato, maize, tomato, chilli, cassava, are American and
permanently unavailable. Your gains here are real but modest, and mostly
about systematising and cross-pollinating regional practice, not miracles.

### crop_rotation - Three-course rotation with a legume break

**What it is / why you want it.** Standard Roman practice alternates two
fields: half cropped, half bare fallow, swapping yearly. Only about half the
arable land produces food in any given year. Replace much of the fallow with
a legume course (beans, vetch, lentils, chickpeas) and more land produces
every year, and the following cereal crop grows measurably better than it
would after plain fallow.

**Why you would never guess this.** A Roman farmer already sees that land
which grew legumes yields good wheat afterward and calls legumes "gentle"
on soil. He does not know why, so he cannot optimise how much legume land
he needs, which legume, or whether to plough it in or graze it off. You can,
because you know the mechanism.

**Prerequisites.** None beyond existing ploughing and seed stock. Module 60
to verify the gain on your own land.

**Roman-available inputs.** Faba bean (*faba*), vetch (*vicia*), lentil
(*lens*), chickpea (*cicer*), lupin, all already cultivated throughout the
empire.

**Procedure.**
1. Split arable land into three blocks instead of two: cereal, legume, true
   fallow, rotating position each year.
2. Harvest the legume for food or fodder, then either plough the residue and
   roots under, or graze livestock on the aftermath so dung returns with it.
3. Keep true bare fallow only on the poorest third; drop it further once
   measured yields justify it.
4. The mechanism, stated so you can act on it without chemistry: legume
   roots carry small nodules (visible if you pull and wash a healthy plant)
   that pull a fertilising substance from the air and fix it in the soil.
   Cereal roots cannot do this, which is why land that grew beans grows
   better wheat than land that grew wheat twice, or than land merely rested.

**How you know it worked.** Mark two adjoining strips of matched soil, same
seed and sowing date, one on old two-field fallow, one on three-course
rotation, run for at least three full cycles (six years), and weigh the
harvest by a consistent measure (Module 60).

**Failure modes.** Legume disease (bruchid weevils, vetch rust) can wipe a
course in a bad year; keep seed reserve. On very poor or dry soil legumes
may simply fail to nodulate well. Overgrazing the aftermath on wet soil
compacts it and undoes the gain.

**Cost & labour.** No new capital, same plough, oxen and seed types already
present. ESTIMATED (basis: the historically attested medieval European shift
from two-field to three-field systems): moving roughly a sixth of arable
land from fallow to legume, plus the fertility carried to the next cereal
crop, plausibly raises cereal yield per sown area by something on the order
of 10 to 30 percent, and raises total output per unit of farmland by more
than that, since more land is productive each year. Treat this as a range,
not a promise; your own trial is the number that matters.

**Danger.** None physical. Do not claim to have invented rotation to a man
who owns Columella; present it as a variant worth testing his way.

**Confidence: HIGH** on the nitrogen-fixation mechanism and on Rome's
existing two-field fallow (directly attested). MEDIUM on the magnitude of
gain, which is extrapolated, not a Roman-era measurement.

### heavy_plough - Mouldboard plough with coulter and wheeled forecarriage

**What it is / why you want it.** A wheeled plough with an iron coulter (a
vertical knife ahead of the share), a share that cuts horizontally, and a
mouldboard that turns the slice completely over, burying weeds and creating
a drained furrow. This differs fundamentally from the Mediterranean ard
(*aratrum*), which scratches a groove and is cross-ploughed rather than
turned.

**Why you would never guess this.** The mistake is assuming "heavier
plough" is a universal upgrade. It is not. On the thin, dry, stony soils of
central Italy the ard is correct: light, minimally disturbing, and it does
not invert and expose scarce moisture to the sun. The heavy plough earns
its keep specifically on the deep, heavy, wet clay of northern Gaul,
Britain and Germania, where the ard cannot break the ground and drainage
via ridged furrows genuinely matters. Sell it as a universal replacement in
Italy and you will be correctly laughed off an estate.

**Prerequisites.** Good iron (Noricum, Spain, or local British ore) for
share and coulter, blacksmithing for shaping and resharpening, a
wheelwright for the forecarriage, oak for the beam and mouldboard, oxen able
to pull the heavier load (or see `horse_collar_harness`).

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron, oak. The concept is not wholly foreign:
Pliny (Natural History 18, attributed, exact chapter unverified) records a
plough with a pair of small wheels recently used among the Raeti in the
Alpine provinces. You are taking a marginal regional device, adding a true
mouldboard, and deliberately routing it to the provinces that need it.

**Procedure.**
1. Forge a coulter, a straight iron blade mounted vertically ahead of the
   share to slice turf and roots.
2. Fit a share cutting a consistent horizontal slice, set by trial on your
   own soil rather than a fixed dimension.
3. Fit a curved mouldboard (wood, or iron-faced wood for longevity) behind
   and above the share so the cut slice rides up and flips over.
4. Mount the assembly on a two-wheeled forecarriage so ploughing depth is
   set by the axle, not fought by hand.
5. Plough heavy ground in autumn so turned clods weather over winter, and
   lay furrows with a slight camber so they drain like informal ditches.

**How you know it worked.** Compare a ridge-and-furrow field against an
ard-ploughed control on the same heavy clay: the heavy-ploughed field
should shed standing water within a day or two where the ard field puddles,
and should out-yield it (Module 60).

**Failure modes.** On thin dry Mediterranean soil it exposes and dries
topsoil; do not use it there. On very wet heavy clay the mouldboard clogs
and stalls the team; wait until soil crumbles rather than smears in the
hand. Coulter and share need frequent resharpening on stony ground.

**Cost & labour.** More iron and a wheelwright's forecarriage versus a
simple ard a farm carpenter can build alone; ongoing smith time for
resharpening is the recurring cost. No denarii figure given; it depends on
local iron and labour prices.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary plough-team work. Socially: present this
as a specialised provincial tool, not an Italian upgrade.

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.** The soil-type distinction is well-established
agricultural history. The Pliny passage is a genuine attestation but brief,
and whether the Raetian device was a true mouldboard or a wheeled ard is
debated; treat that detail as attributed, unverified.

### horse_collar_harness - see Module 40 for construction; agronomic case here

**What it is / why you want it.** A rigid padded collar resting on the
horse's shoulders, letting it push with its full body weight, versus the
Roman throat-and-girth harness (throat strap plus belly girth) common for
horses. Full construction is in Module 40; this covers only whether it is
worth the trouble.

**Why you would never guess this.** Popular history claims Roman
throat-and-girth harness choked horses and that the medieval collar gave a
fourfold gain in pulling power. That figure comes from Lynn White's 1962
book and has been substantially challenged since (notably by Georges
Raepsaet and John Langdon), since ancient harness was not uniformly
throat-and-girth (breast-strap types existed and perform much better), and
Rome's main heavy-draught animal is the ox, yoked at the withers, to which
the throat-choke criticism barely applies. The honest range for sustained
heavy horse draught is more like a few tens of percent up to perhaps a
doubling, not fourfold, and the gain is largely irrelevant to ox-ploughed
fields.

**Prerequisites.** Module 40. Leatherworking, felt or straw padding, wood or
iron for the rigid frame.

**Roman-available inputs.** Tanned leather, wool felt, straw. No exotic
material; the missing piece is the rigid load-bearing design itself.

**Procedure.** See Module 40.

**How you know it worked.** Harness two comparable horses to identical
loads, one on each harness type; the collar horse should show less visible
distress and sustain heavier loads or longer work before tiring. This is a
comparative field test, not a precise measurement.

**Failure modes.** A badly fitted rigid collar causes worse shoulder sores
than the harness it replaces; fit to the individual animal.

**Cost & labour.** As Module 40.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary animal handling.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The dispute itself is real and well documented in
the historiography; any specific multiplier, including the range above,
should be treated as an informed estimate, not settled fact.

### manuring_and_lime - Marl, lime, compost, bone meal, and the road to N-P-K

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already manures, marls and limes.
The genuine additions: systematising scattered practice with measurement,
bone meal as a phosphorus source (a real gap in the sources), and
superphosphate (bone treated with strong sulfuric acid) as a large but
distant win once you have that acid.

**Why you would never guess this.** Cato and Columella already rank dung by
potency (pigeon and poultry rated highest, human waste next, then sheep,
goat and larger stock, attributed, exact order unverified) and already
marl, a practice they attribute to Gaul and Britain (Pliny, Natural
History 17, on *marga*, a term he treats as Gallic, attributed, exact
chapter unverified). What is missing is any concept of specific elements
in specific ratios: a Roman farmer manures because land "looks tired," not
because it is short of a named nutrient, and so has no route to bone meal
or mineral fertiliser as distinct from organic waste.

**Prerequisites.** None for marling, liming, composting, already Roman
practice; your contribution is measurement (Module 60). Bone meal needs
only a mill or mortar. Superphosphate needs concentrated sulfuric acid, a
separate and much harder chemistry prerequisite.

**Roman-available inputs.** Farmyard and pigeon dung, marl (chalky Gaul and
Britain, and parts of Italy), limestone burned in existing mortar kilns
(Vitruvius book 2, attributed), butchery bone waste, sulfur (Sicily), iron
and copper vitriol.

**Procedure.**
1. Rot manure covered rather than leaving it exposed to rain, which leaches
   its value before it reaches the field.
2. Marl heavy, acid, sticky soil: dig, spread thickly, plough in; effects
   last years.
3. Lime acid soils: burn limestone to quicklime, slake with water
   (it hisses and heats, handle like mortar lime, it burns skin), let it
   weather weeks to a fine powder, spread where sorrel or rushes signal
   acidity.
4. Bone meal: dry and crush butchery bone as fine as possible, spread on
   demanding crops; expect stronger root growth and seed set rather than
   just leafier growth.
5. Superphosphate: treat crushed bone with strong sulfuric acid for a
   faster-acting, more soluble phosphorus source. Rome has vitriol and
   sulfur but not the concentrated acid; flag this as a future goal tied to
   a separate chemistry program, not a near-term recipe.

**How you know it worked.** Matched split-plot comparison (Module 60);
bone meal specifically should show stronger stems and seed set, not just
extra leaf growth the way manure alone does.

**Failure modes.** Over-liming non-acid soil makes it worse; test small
first. Fresh unrotted manure scorches seedlings and spreads weed seed.
Quicklime burns skin and eyes; always slake before spreading.

**Cost & labour.** Marling and liming are heavy manual labour, many
cart-loads per iugerum depending on depletion; no reliable Roman denarii
figure given, cost it locally. Bone meal costs little beyond collecting and
crushing waste a butchery would discard, making it one of the cheapest
genuine wins here.

**Danger.** Quicklime burns as above. No social risk; all of this is
unremarkable Roman practice.

**Confidence: HIGH** on Roman manuring, marling and liming (Cato, Columella,
Pliny). HIGH on N-P-K chemistry. MEDIUM on bone meal specifically being a
gap in Roman practice. LOW on any near-term path to superphosphate.

### selective_breeding - Pedigree records and progeny testing

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already breeds well. Columella
(books 6-9, attributed) gives detailed criteria for choosing breeding stock
by conformation and ancestry; grain growers already save seed from the best
ears. What you add is written pedigree records kept across generations and
progeny testing, judging a sire by measuring his actual offspring across
several dams and years rather than by his own looks.

**Why you would never guess this.** Nothing looks different in any single
generation; the gap only opens slowly, over years, between an estate that
tracks progeny and one that selects purely on appearance, which can be
badly misleading since a fine-looking sire can still throw poor offspring.

**Prerequisites.** Literacy and consistent record-keeping (Module 60). No
new material inputs.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cattle, sheep, horses, pigs, poultry, vines
(already propagated by selected cuttings and grafting), grain.

**Procedure.**
1. Record parentage (both sire and dam), birth date and measured traits
   (wool weight, milk yield, load pulled, litter size) for every breeding
   animal.
2. Judge a sire by his offspring's measured performance across multiple
   dams and years, not his own build.
3. Cull on the recorded numbers, not appearance.
4. For grain, keep a small measured comparison plot each year of seed from
   top lines against ordinary seed.
5. For vines, record which named cultivars and mother vines actually
   perform best on your soil, in writing, not just in one vinedresser's
   memory.

**How you know it worked.** After several generations, compare the measured
trait between your selected line and an unselected control on the same
land; the selected line should show a real upward trend.

**Failure modes.** Culling on one trait alone degrades others you were not
tracking. Too small a breeding pool causes inbreeding problems within a few
generations; keep it large or exchange stock with neighbours.

**Cost & labour.** Mostly literate staff time; ESTIMATED as a modest
addition to record-keeping an estate already does in some form.

**Danger.** None; unremarkable estate management.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The value of tracked pedigree and progeny testing is
settled agricultural science; the claim that Roman sources describe
selecting good individuals rather than tracking multi-generation offspring
performance is a reasonable, modest reading of the texts.

### irrigation_and_drainage - Archimedes screw, force pumps, and tile field drains

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already has excellent water-lifting
machinery: the Archimedes screw (*cochlea*), used for irrigation and mine
dewatering (Rio Tinto), and the force pump (Ctesibius; Vitruvius book 10,
attributed). What Roman practice underuses is systematic buried fired-clay
pipe drainage, as opposed to open ditches and drainage tunnels (*cuniculi*,
well attested in Etruria).

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks like a boring difference
between a ditch and a pipe. It is not: open ditches take farmland, silt up,
and drain only the strip beside them; a grid of buried tile drains a few
feet down, feeding a header ditch, lowers the water table across a whole
field, turning waterlogged clay (plentiful in northern Gaul, Britain, the
Po valley) into arable land while leaving the surface clear to plough.

**Prerequisites.** Tile and brick kilns, already running at scale for
roofing tile; surveying tools (*groma*, *chorobates*) to set a consistent
fall.

**Roman-available inputs.** Clay everywhere, the existing tile industry;
bronze, iron and timber for screws and pumps, both already manufactured
Roman items.

**Procedure.**
1. Fire short clay pipe or horseshoe-section tile.
2. Dig trenches on a gentle continuous fall (checked with a water level)
   into a header drain or ditch.
3. Lay pipe on the trench bottom, cover with gravel if available, backfill
   to a level, ploughable surface.
4. Space drain lines by observation, closer on wetter ground.
5. For irrigation, lift water by screw or pump to channel height and
   distribute by gravity, as Roman market gardens already do.

**How you know it worked.** Drained ground should be workable within a day
or two of heavy rain where it previously stayed waterlogged for a week;
rush and sedge growth should recede over seasons.

**Failure modes.** Drains laid without proper fall silt up. Pipe laid too
shallow is crushed by ploughing. Blocked outlets back up the whole system.

**Cost & labour.** Substantial: trench-digging across a whole field plus
tile-firing; ESTIMATED as a major one-time capital improvement comparable
to marling or terracing, justified by permanently converting unusable land.
No reliable per-iugerum figure given.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary excavation and kiln hazards.

**Confidence: HIGH** that Rome has the screw and force pump. MEDIUM-HIGH
that systematic buried field-tile drainage, as opposed to large drainage
tunnels, was routine Roman practice; treat it as a real, buildable
improvement rather than a clearly Roman-attested one.

### food_preservation - Beyond salting, smoking and garum: canning, granaries, ice

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already preserves food well by
salting, smoking, drying and above all garum and liquamen, produced
industrially in Baetica and Pontus. The genuine additions: airtight
canning by Appert's method (1809, but achievable with Roman glassblowing
and no germ theory at all, since it was discovered empirically), better
granary design, and systematic ice houses.

**Why you would never guess this.** Canning looks like it needs
germ theory. It does not; Appert proved it decades before germ theory
existed, by observing that sealed, heated food kept longer. Rome has every
physical prerequisite, glassblowing, cork, wax, fire, and has simply never
combined them this way.

**Prerequisites.** Glassblowing (already Roman, reasonably clear glass) or
well-sealed pottery. Cork (Iberia, already used for stoppers) or ground
glass stoppers, sealed with wax or pitch.

**Roman-available inputs.** Blown glass jars, cork, beeswax or pitch,
vinegar (*acetum*), salt.

**Procedure, canning.**
1. Pack food into a clean jar, leave a thumb's width headspace.
2. For high-acid foods only, fruit, or vegetables genuinely acidified with
   vinegar or must, seat the stopper loosely and stand the jar in a pot of
   water reaching most of the way up its sides. Bring to a full rolling
   boil (100C at sea level, water in constant vigorous motion) and hold for
   ESTIMATED ten to thirty minutes depending on jar size, since no
   Roman-tested figure exists; verify by opening a test jar after a month
   before trusting a whole season's batch.
3. Remove hot, press the stopper fully home, and seal over it with melted
   wax while still hot; the seal should draw slightly inward as it cools.
4. Store cool and dark; a good jar shows no bulge, no hiss, and normal
   smell on opening.

**The blunt warning.** Only can high-acid foods this way. Never can plain
low-acid food (meat, plain vegetables, beans) in a jar processed only in
boiling water. The organism causing botulism survives ordinary boiling
entirely, and if sealed airtight in a low-acid, oxygen-free jar it
germinates and produces a lethal, odourless, tasteless toxin with no
visible sign of spoilage. Killing those spores needs temperatures well
above boiling, reachable only under pressure, which a simple pot cannot
provide; an improvised pressure vessel is a genuine explosion risk without
a working relief valve and is not given as a safe recipe here. Preserve
low-acid foods only by the existing Roman methods, salting, smoking,
drying, or true cold storage, never by sealing airtight after a mere
boiling-water treatment.

**Procedure, granaries.** Roman *horrea* are already well designed, raised
on piers for airflow, thick-walled (attested at Ostia and the Horrea
Galbae). Push this down to every farm granary, not just prestige
buildings: raised ventilated floors, tight doors against rodents and damp,
and a lime wash on interior walls, since caustic lime discourages mould
and insects on the surface.

**Procedure, ice houses.** Rome already trades ice for cooling drinks;
formalise it for food. Dig a deep pit, line with straw, pack with winter
ice or snow, cover thickly with more straw and a rain-shedding roof, and
drain meltwater from the bottom. Well built, such a pit holds usable ice
through much of a Mediterranean summer for short-term meat and fish
storage.

**How you know it worked.** Open a test jar after a month; discard on any
bulge, hiss or off smell, do not taste it. Check granary grain for
moisture, weevils or mould against an old-style store. Check how far into
summer ice lasts.

**Failure modes.** Botulism from canning low-acid food is the single most
dangerous failure mode in this module. Damp granary floors breed mould
that can itself produce dangerous toxins, independent of any canning.

**Cost & labour.** Canning needs jars and boiling fuel; ESTIMATED as a
modest, repeatable household cost, not a major capital project. Ice houses
need one substantial dig, then seasonal ice-cutting labour.

**Danger.** Botulism, treated above as non-negotiable. Burns from boiling
water and hot jars, and from lime handling.

**Confidence: HIGH** that Roman salting, smoking, drying and garum are
already excellent (Columella book 12, Pliny book 31, both attributed).
HIGH on canning chemistry and the botulism danger profile (settled modern
food science). MEDIUM on exact processing times, explicitly flagged as
estimated.

### windmill_watermill_grain - Milling capacity as a bottleneck

**What it is / why you want it.** Grinding grain into flour is a real
constraint on how much bread a city can eat, regardless of how much grain
arrives. Rome already runs large water mills (Barbegal near Arles, a
battery of over a dozen wheels driving grain mills on one hillside, a
MEASURED archaeological find, generally dated within the 2nd century AD,
exact date ESTIMATED/debated) alongside countless animal- and slave-powered
rotary mills, such as the hourglass lava mills found in Pompeii's bakeries.
The windmill genuinely does not exist anywhere in the Roman world yet; do
not claim otherwise.

**Why you would never guess this.** It is easy to assume secure grain
supply (the annona's whole obsession) solves the food problem. It does
not: grain in a horreum still has to be ground, and milling capacity is a
separate, sometimes binding constraint, especially in a city the size of
Rome.

**Prerequisites.** Water-mill construction is already mature Roman skill
(Vitruvius book 10, attributed). Windmills need only carpentry, sailcloth
and a pivoting mount, all within existing craft skill; the missing piece
is the idea and gearing, not any exotic material.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber, iron fittings, millstones (already a
Roman industry, Rhineland lava and Italian volcanic stone), sailcloth.

**Procedure.**
1. Where reliable flowing water exists, multiply water mills, more wheels,
   more sites, better gearing, as Barbegal already shows is achievable;
   this is the single most effective lever for relieving urban milling
   bottlenecks.
2. Where water power is unavailable (flat, dry, or windy coastal sites),
   build a windmill: a rotating cap or tower carrying angled sails geared
   to a vertical shaft driving a millstone.
3. Keep animal- and slave-powered rotary mills as flexible backup capacity,
   as Rome already does.

**How you know it worked.** Track flour output per day against mill
capacity feeding a bakery or district; more bread from the same grain, or
the same bread with fewer diverted animal- or labour-hours, is the signal.

**Failure modes.** Water mills fail in drought or flood; windmills fail in
still weather or storm damage without reefing. Keep animal mills as
backup for both.

**Cost & labour.** Large capital projects, ESTIMATED as beyond smallholder
means, historically state-, patron-, or guild-funded; no reliable denarii
figure given.

**Danger.** Ordinary construction and gearing crush/entanglement hazards.

**Confidence: HIGH** that large Roman water mills, including multi-wheel
installations, are real. HIGH that windmills are a genuine post-Roman
introduction (the horizontal-axis European windmill is medieval) and
should not be presented as anything Rome already has.

### new_crops_reachable - What Roman trade can bring in that Rome underuses

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's trade reaches India via the Red
Sea monsoon route and Central Asia via the Silk Road. A short, hedged list
of crops worth routing into Roman cultivation, since you cannot invent a
crop, only bring one in:

- **Rice** (*oryza*): known mainly as an imported medicinal starch, not
  cultivated in the empire. Confidence MEDIUM that seed is reachable via
  the India trade, LOW on how well it would establish given the paddy
  infrastructure it needs.
- **Cotton** (*gossypium*): Pliny (Natural History 12, attributed) describes
  Indian "tree wool"; cotton cloth is already imported and grown in a
  limited way in Egypt. The opportunity is expansion, not introduction.
  Confidence MEDIUM-HIGH that Rome already has some, LOW-MEDIUM on how far
  it can expand given climate.
- **Sugar cane** (*saccharum*): known only as an imported medicine from
  India and Arabia, not cultivated near the Mediterranean; large-scale
  cultivation there historically arrives centuries later. A real but
  distant opportunity in Egypt or the Levant if planting material can be
  obtained. Confidence LOW-MEDIUM.
- **Citrus**: the citron (*citrus medica*) is already grown in Italy, well
  attested since the Republic. Lemon and sweet orange are NOT reachable
  through any route available in 100 AD; do not claim them. Confidence HIGH
  both ways.
- **Durum ("hard") wheat**: already Mediterranean-native and already grown
  in some form; not a new crop. The real opportunity is selecting and
  propagating the hardest local strains, which belongs under
  `selective_breeding`. Confidence HIGH it is already present.
- **Alfalfa / lucerne** (*medica*): already introduced to Italy, by Pliny's
  account (Natural History 18, attributed) brought from Media during the
  Persian Wars, already cut several times a year as fodder. Not new; see
  `fodder_and_livestock`. Confidence HIGH.
- **Buckwheat**: native to inner Asia; I have no good evidence it had
  reached Mediterranean trade by 100 AD, and its well-attested spread into
  Europe is medieval. Confidence LOW; treat as probably unavailable, do not
  budget on it.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive move is to reach for the
crops that mattered most later (potato, maize, sugar, tobacco) without
checking whether Rome already has an equivalent (citron, native wheat, or
alfalfa) or whether the crop is American and unreachable by any route at
all. Get this list right before spending effort on it.

**Prerequisites.** Existing Roman long-distance trade. No new technology,
only deliberate purchase of seed stock and willingness to trial it on
marginal land.

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing trade contacts and coin.

**Procedure.** Commission merchants already trading to India via the Red
Sea to bring back rice and sugar cane planting stock alongside normal
cargo, trial each on a small plot before any large commitment, and expect
several failed attempts as normal.

**How you know it worked.** A trialled crop either germinates and produces
a harvestable yield on the test plot, or it does not.

**Failure modes.** Seed or planting material dying in transit (heat, damp,
time) is the most likely failure, before any question of Roman soil.
Climate mismatch (rice's water management, sugar cane's season length) is
the second most likely.

**Cost & labour.** Merchant commissions and trade goods for exotic seed;
ESTIMATED as expensive relative to domestic seed, in line with India-trade
costs generally, no specific figure given.

**Danger.** None physical; exotic garden novelties are unremarkable to
Romans.

**Confidence: overall MEDIUM**, broken down above; the discipline that
matters is honesty about what is already Roman (citron, durum wheat,
alfalfa), what is plausibly reachable but unproven (rice, sugar cane,
cotton expansion), and what should be written off entirely (New World
crops, probably buckwheat).

### fodder_and_livestock - Winter fodder and the multiplier effect

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's fodder repertoire is already
broad: alfalfa, vetch, and turnips (*rapum*, *napus*, Pliny Natural History
18, attributed). The genuine addition is not a new plant but a systems
change: deliberately growing and storing enough winter fodder to overwinter
more animals near full numbers, instead of the common pre-modern practice
of slaughtering down the herd each autumn for lack of feed. More animals
overwintered means more manure, more draught power ready for spring
ploughing, more meat and milk, and more leather and wool, all compounding.

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks like a scheduling choice, not
a technology. It is one of the highest-leverage changes here precisely
because it needs no new material, alfalfa, vetch and turnips already exist,
only deliberately growing and storing more of them as a dedicated winter
course rather than accepting the autumn cull as inevitable.

**Prerequisites.** `crop_rotation` above, storage capacity (hay loft, root
clamp).

**Roman-available inputs.** Alfalfa, vetch, turnips, straw.

**Procedure.**
1. Dedicate part of the fodder course to winter feed: alfalfa cut and dried
   as hay, turnips clamped in earth-and-straw-covered pits.
2. Estimate, by record (Module 60), how many animals the stored fodder can
   carry to spring grass, and grow that much deliberately.
3. Increase fodder area gradually year over year, tracking how many more
   animals survive winter at full condition.

**How you know it worked.** Count livestock in autumn and again the
following spring; a rising fraction surviving winter at full condition,
against the old sharp autumn cull, is the signal.

**Failure modes.** Underestimating need and running out in late winter, the
worst possible timing; keep a safety margin. Poorly drained clamps rot
turnips in a wet winter.

**Cost & labour.** Mostly reallocation of existing rotation land and
storage labour; ESTIMATED as low incremental cost for a potentially large
gain, the exact multiplier depending on how short of fodder prior practice
was, which varies too widely to give one number.

**Danger.** None; unremarkable estate management.

**Confidence: HIGH** that alfalfa, vetch and turnips are already Roman
(Pliny, attributed). MEDIUM-HIGH that systematic winter overwintering, as
opposed to simply having these crops, is a genuine improvable practice,
since a hard autumn cull followed by a lean spring is well attested across
pre-modern agriculture generally, Rome included.

### fisheries_and_salt - Salt supply, fish preservation, and state control

**What it is / why you want it.** This is mostly a know-your-own-system
entry. Rome already runs a large, sophisticated salt and fish-preservation
industry, and its strategic shape matters more here than any new recipe.

**Why you would never guess this.** Salt looks like a simple commodity. It
is not in Rome: salt production (*salinae*) has a long history of state
interest and taxation, and a soldier's pay, *salarium*, derives from an
association with salt. Salted fish (*salsamenta*) and fermented fish sauce
(garum, liquamen) are already major industries, Baetica and Pontus being
famous centres, exported in distinctive sealed amphorae across the empire.

**Prerequisites.** None; largely already-functioning infrastructure. True
saltpetre curing of meat (potassium nitrate, which fixes colour and adds a
real extra barrier against botulism) depends on deliberately farmed nitre
beds, a much harder undertaking covered elsewhere, not on ordinary Roman
*nitrum* (sodium carbonate, not saltpetre, a common anachronism trap).

**Roman-available inputs.** Sea salt from coastal pans, rock and spring
salt inland, fish stocks across the Mediterranean, Atlantic and Black Sea.

**Procedure.** No new procedure beyond what Rome already does well: solar
evaporation for salt, salting and pressing for salsamenta, sun fermentation
for garum. The actionable point is strategic: salt and garum trade lines
are politically sensitive and often capital-backed; siting a new solar
pan on unexploited coastline, rather than challenging an existing
producer, is the low-friction path.

**How you know it worked.** Existing Roman quality checks (taste, smell,
visual inspection) already apply.

**Failure modes.** None new; the industry already manages spoilage,
adulteration and seasonal gaps reasonably well.

**Cost & labour.** Established industry with unquantified Roman-era costs;
no new figures needed since no new technique is proposed.

**Danger.** Social and political rather than physical: large-scale salt or
fish-sauce production draws attention from people with capital and
sometimes official backing already invested in it.

**Confidence: HIGH** that Roman salt and fish-preservation industries are
already large and sophisticated (Pliny book 31, attributed; the
archaeological record of garum amphorae is extensive). MEDIUM on the exact
extent of state monopoly versus taxation at any given period, which varied.

### agricultural_statistics - Measured plots and controlled comparison

**What it is / why you want it.** The single most important entry here,
and not a technique but a habit: measuring plot area, recording seed sown,
recording harvest by a consistent measure, and comparing treatments
against a matched control over multiple years before concluding anything.
See Module 60 for the underlying method.

**Why you would never guess this.** Every entry above depends on being
able to tell, honestly, whether it worked on your own land, not on anyone's
claim about elsewhere. Columella and Varro already cite seed-to-yield
ratios (attributed, specific figures not repeated here since I cannot
verify them), so yield figures are not alien to Rome. What is different is
treating measurement as a routine, comparative discipline on your own farm
every year, not an occasional figure used mainly for tax assessment.

**Prerequisites.** Module 60, literacy or a literate steward.

**Roman-available inputs.** The iugerum as area unit, the modius as grain
volume unit, both already standard.

**Procedure.**
1. Measure plots precisely with a consistent unit every time.
2. Record, every season: what was sown, seed amount, date, treatment, and
   harvest by standard measure.
3. Always run a genuine matched control alongside any new treatment, not
   just before-and-after on the same plot, since weather alone can swing a
   single year's result badly.
4. Keep records across several years before concluding anything.
5. Write records so a future manager, including yourself years later, can
   actually compare them.

**How you know it worked.** This entry is the test itself; its success is
that every other entry can be verified or disproven using it.

**Failure modes.** Comparing mismatched plots (soil, aspect, drainage) and
crediting the treatment is the most common error; match controls closely.
Drawing conclusions from a single year is the second most common.

**Cost & labour.** Negligible beyond record-keeping time already implied by
Module 60; the highest-leverage, lowest-cost entry in the module.

**Danger.** None.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The statistical logic is basic, settled methodology;
the claim that Rome reports yields but does not routinely run matched,
replicated on-farm trials is a reasonable reading of the surviving
agronomic literature, which gives results and rules of thumb, not
controlled trials.

## How much surplus do you actually need?

Treat this as illustrative arithmetic, not history. Take the given
premise, 85 percent farming (midpoint of 80-90), 15 percent free for
everything else. If food is shared roughly evenly (a simplification), each
farm worker currently feeds about 1 divided by 0.85, roughly 1.18 people.

- **Modest gain, 30 percent non-farming:** each farmer feeds 1/0.70, about
  1.43 people, roughly a 21 percent increase over baseline. Plausibly
  reachable from rotation, fodder, drainage and manuring alone, without
  further mechanisation.
- **Significant gain, 50 percent non-farming:** each farmer feeds 1/0.50,
  2.00 people, roughly a 69 percent increase over baseline. A large ask,
  plausibly achievable only after generations of these techniques
  compounding together, and probably needs draught and tool gains from
  elsewhere in this guide too.
- **Early-industrial gain, roughly 65-70 percent non-farming** (near
  Britain around 1800): each farmer feeds 1/0.35, about 2.86 people,
  roughly a 142 percent increase, nearly two and a half times baseline.
  Historically this took decades of compounded breeding, drainage and land
  reclamation, plus mechanical traction beginning to replace draught
  animals; a reasonable multi-generation target for this module combined
  with mechanical progress elsewhere, not agriculture alone.
- **Modern economies typically farm under 10 percent, often under 5.**
  Reaching that needs synthetic nitrogen fixation, engine-powered
  mechanisation replacing draught animals (which themselves consume real
  land and fodder), and modern plant genetics, none available here or from
  Roman-era technology at all. This module's honest job is to buy
  population and calendar time for that program, not to reach it directly.

Conclusion: these techniques can plausibly deliver, over one to several
generations, something like a fifth to two-thirds more output per farm
worker, moving the non-farming share from roughly 15 percent toward
perhaps 30 to 50 percent at the outer edge of sustained effort. That is
transformative for a Roman-era economy and worth pursuing hard. It does not
alone reach a transistor-level non-farming population; that needs
industrial chemistry and mechanisation this module does not claim to
deliver.

## The political economy of feeding Rome

Rome, at perhaps a million people, cannot feed itself from its own
hinterland; it depends on grain shipped from Egypt and Africa (roughly
modern Tunisia) by a dedicated fleet, stored in state and private
warehouses (*horrea*, including the Horrea Galbae), and distributed partly
by market and partly through the *annona*, a subsidised or free grain dole
administered by the *praefectus annonae*, a post generally dated to
Augustus's reign (commonly cited around 8 AD, attributed, unverified),
created after repeated grain crises made clear that leaving supply to the
market alone was not politically survivable.

The number of dole recipients varied across the imperial period and is
disputed among historians; ancient sources (Augustus's Res Gestae,
attributed) cite figures on the order of a couple of hundred thousand at
various points. Treat any specific figure, including that one, as
order-of-magnitude, but the scale itself, a large fraction of the city
directly dependent on state-managed supply, is not in doubt.

This makes grain supply the regime's single greatest recurring anxiety.
Egypt is governed as the emperor's personal province, its governor
reporting directly to him rather than through the senatorial system,
precisely because whoever controls Egypt's grain controls a lever that can
starve or feed the capital. A shortfall produces immediate, dangerous
unrest, not abstract discontent: Suetonius records Claudius once
surrounded and jeered by an angry crowd during a grain shortage
(Suetonius, Life of Claudius, chapter 18, attributed, exact wording
unverified), a vivid sign that an emperor's own safety in his own capital
could hinge on whether ships arrived on schedule.

This is exactly why agricultural improvement is the safest lane available
for building patronage and protection inside the Roman system. It touches
nothing politically sensitive, not succession, the army, provincial power,
or religion, only the one outcome every actor from tenant farmer to emperor
wants more of and fears the lack of. A demonstrated, measured improvement
in yield or storage-loss reduction on a grain-producing estate, in Egypt or
Africa specifically, is exactly the kind of result the *cura annonae*
already exists to notice and reward, because it directly reduces the
regime's worst recurring fear. Route your earliest, most visible
demonstrations of this module's techniques through grain estates and
annona-relevant outcomes, more grain delivered, less spoiled in transit,
more reliable year to year, rather than toward showier but riskier domains,
and you buy the protected, resourced position from which the rest of this
guide's harder technical program becomes possible at all.

## Sources and confidence

Confident citations, chapter numbers flagged attributed/unverified where I
am not certain: **Columella**, *De Re Rustica*, twelve books, the core
agronomic manual, covering fallow, manure, viticulture, orcharding, and
animal husbandry criteria in books six through nine. **Varro**, *De Re
Rustica*, three books in dialogue, covering fields, livestock, and villa
gardens including fishponds and aviaries. **Cato**, *De Agri Cultura*, the
oldest surviving Latin prose work, terse and practical, strong on manure
types, viticulture, olives, and household preservation. **Pliny**,
*Naturalis Historia*, books 17 and 18 for trees, soils, cereals and fodder,
the source for marl, the alfalfa introduction story, and the Raetian
wheeled-plough passage, and book 31 for salt. **Vitruvius**, *De
Architectura*, book 10, the source for Roman water-lifting machinery and
water-mill construction. **Suetonius**, imperial biographies, for the
Claudius grain-shortage anecdote.

Genuine uncertainties, flagged rather than smoothed over: all yield
percentages in this module are ESTIMATED from general historical reasoning
about the two-field to three-field shift, not from a surviving Roman
measurement, since none is known to me. The true magnitude of the
horse-collar efficiency gain is an active, unresolved historiographical
dispute. The exact scale of annona recipients and the founding date of the
*praefectus annonae* office are both debated and given as order-of-
magnitude only. Whether bone meal specifically, as opposed to other bone
uses, was Roman practice is a genuine gap in my confidence. Finally: the
New World crops that most transformed post-1500 Old World agriculture,
potato, maize, tomato, chilli pepper, cassava, have no source anywhere in
the Roman trade world of 100 AD and are not addressed as reachable in this
module, because they are not reachable, at any cost, through any route
available to you.
