# Module 85: Transport, Mining and Civil Engineering

Say this plainly first: Roman civil engineering in 100 AD is not a problem you are here to fix. The Via Appia has stood since 312 BC. Rome's aqueducts (nine lines by the time Frontinus writes his survey under Nerva/Trajan, right around now) cross valleys on arcades and pressure siphons that Europe will not match again for a millennium and a half. Hydraulic concrete made with Puteoli pozzolana sets underwater and gets stronger, not weaker, with decades of seawater exposure, a property not well understood again until modern materials science. The Pantheon's unreinforced concrete dome, being finished under Hadrian right now, remains the largest of its kind ever built. Tell a Roman engineer you can teach him roads, aqueducts, or domes and you deserve the laughter you get, and you will have burned the credibility you need for what actually is missing.

What is missing is narrow and specific, and it is worth enormous effort because of one fact: sea or river freight costs roughly one fortieth to one sixtieth of land freight per tonne-kilometre (ESTIMATED, basis: the ratio modern historians commonly derive from Diocletian's Price Edict of 301 AD, attributed generally to analyses in the tradition of R. Duncan-Jones; treat as order-of-magnitude, not a measured constant). A related, widely quoted figure from the same evidence base: hauling grain about 500 km overland roughly doubles its price. Every entry below either fills a genuine gap in Roman technique, or exists purely to push that 40-to-60-times number toward the water-freight end for more cargo and more places. Read "The freight arithmetic" before deciding what to build first.

### roman_baseline_civil - What Rome Already Does Well (*survey, no single Latin term*)

**What it is / why you want it.** An honest inventory of Roman strengths, so you don't spend years "inventing" what a Roman architect already has.

**Why you would never guess this.** Modern intuition says pre-industrial equals primitive. Rome's failures are specific and structural (see the rest of this module), not a general skill gap.

**Prerequisites.** None; background only.

**Roman-available inputs.** N/A.

**Procedure.** Facts to hold in your head, not steps: (1) Roads: a paved trunk network, commonly cited around 80,000 to 100,000 km of first-class road by the 2nd century (ESTIMATED from milestones and the Antonine Itinerary), multi-layer construction (statumen, rudus, nucleus, pavimentum), cambered, drained, with a state courier system. (2) Aqueducts: Frontinus (*De Aquaeductu*) describes nine lines serving Rome around this date, gravity-fed over arcades and through inverted siphons; his flow figures in *quinariae* are regarded by modern engineers as unreliable in detail (MEDIUM confidence on numbers, HIGH on general competence). (3) Concrete: opus caementicium, in routine use for centuries, including underwater. (4) Harbours: Portus and Caesarea Maritima (built for Herod, c. 15 BC), both using poured hydraulic concrete in forms or caissons in open sea. (5) Bridges: segmental stone arches, some (Pons Fabricius, 62 BC) still standing two thousand years later. (6) Lifting: the treadwheel crane (*polyspastos*, Vitruvius, *De Architectura* X), a handful of men raising loads of several tonnes. (7) Surveying: groma (right angles), chorobates (water-trough level, for aqueduct gradients), Hero's dioptra (angles). See surveying_precision for their limits.

**How you know it worked.** Go look at the structure before assuming a gap that isn't there.

**Failure modes.** Proposing something Rome already has, and burning trust.

**Cost & labour.** N/A, inventory only.

**Danger.** Social: looking ignorant of Roman achievement to an educated patron costs credibility hard to rebuild.

**Confidence: HIGH** - all attested in surviving structures, Vitruvius, Frontinus.

### concrete_and_cement - Concrete and Cement (*opus caementicium*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Roman concrete is already excellent; real gains are systematic testing now, and, far down the tree, Portland cement, which is not clearly better for the marine/hydraulic work Rome already excels at.

**Why you would never guess this.** "Concrete" means Portland cement to a modern visitor. Roman pozzolanic concrete is chemically different and, for seawater, arguably superior.

**Prerequisites.** Lime burning (already routine), pozzolana access, basic formwork carpentry.

**Roman-available inputs.** *Calx* (quicklime, any limestone district), *pulvis puteolanus* (pozzolana, Bay of Naples and other volcanic districts), *caementa* (stone/brick/tuff rubble), fresh water, timber.

**Procedure.** Slake quicklime; mix with pozzolana (Vitruvius II.6 gives a ratio; treat as attributed, verify by testing) and rubble aggregate; pack in formwork, sealed and pumped/bailed dry if underwater; cure. The genuine addition: cast standard test cylinders from each batch, label with mix and date, load to failure with known stone weights at fixed intervals, keep written records, something not routinely done in any surviving Roman source. This converts craft intuition into real materials testing. Portland cement, for reference only: crush limestone and clay (roughly 4:1, ESTIMATED), calcine to full clinkering at roughly 1450°C (versus lime burning's roughly 900-1000°C), grind fine with a little gypsum. This is a major industrial project (kiln, refractories, fuel supply, grinding), well beyond near-term reach.

**How you know it worked.** A cured test specimen fails at roughly the load your records predict for that mix. An opened underwater caisson section is hard and uniform, not crumbly.

**Failure modes.** Too little or poor pozzolana gives a weak set. Contaminated sand weakens the bond. Underwater pours disturbed before initial set wash out.

**Cost & labour.** Testing: DERIVED, an attentive foreman plus record-keeping, negligible extra material. Portland kiln: ESTIMATED as a major standalone project, out of near-term scope.

**Danger.** Lime burns skin/eyes on contact while slaking, wash with water immediately. No social danger; concrete work is respectable.

**Confidence: HIGH** on chemistry and Roman practice; MEDIUM on Vitruvius's exact ratios; LOW on Portland timelines/ratios, given only to mark the far end of the branch.

### canals_and_locks - Canals and Pound Locks (*fossa navigabilis*, "navigable ditch"; no Roman term exists for a pound lock)

**What it is / why you want it.** The single highest-value entry here. Rome canals but has no pound lock, only flash locks and portages, capping how far cheap water freight reaches inland. A working lock removes that cap across whole river basins.

**Why you would never guess this.** A lock looks trivial once seen, yet nobody in the Mediterranean built one this way for over a thousand years after this point. The non-obvious part is the mitre gate: two leaves meeting at a shallow angle pointing upstream, so pressure from the higher side wedges them tighter shut, yet one man can swing them open on a balance beam once levels equalise.

**Prerequisites.** Timber carpentry, basic ironwork (hinges, pintles), surveying_precision, a river/canal route with a real change in level.

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak timber, iron for pintles and straps, stone or timber chamber walls, a windlass or balance beam.

**Procedure.** (1) Pick a site with a modest step in water level rather than trying to cut it away. (2) Build a chamber, timber or masonry, sized for your boat, between two gate positions. (3) Hang mitre gate pairs at each end, pivoting on iron pintles, meeting on the centreline pointing toward the higher water (roughly 10-20 degrees off straight across, ESTIMATED from later European practice, no Roman precedent exists). (4) Cut a small sluice in each gate or wall to fill/empty the chamber without opening a gate against a pressure difference. (5) To raise a boat: close the lower gate, open the upper sluice to fill the chamber, then swing the now-unloaded upper gate. (6) To descend: reverse, draining through the lower sluice before opening the lower gate. (7) Build a flight of chambers where one step is not enough.

**How you know it worked.** Water level inside the chamber visibly equalises with the side about to open, and that gate swings with one man's push, not against a torrent.

**Failure modes.** Wrong mitre angle binds or leaks. Green timber warps. A misjudged sill (a surveying error) leaves the gate unable to seal or the boat grounding. Opening a gate against an unequalised head tears it off and can drown people in the chamber.

**Cost & labour.** DERIVED, no Roman figure exists since the device never did; by analogy with similar-scale masonry/carpentry (a small bridge pier, a bath wing), expect a master carpenter, a smith, and a labour gang for one to a few building seasons per chamber, plus ongoing light maintenance. Treat any denarii figure as ESTIMATED until built.

**Danger.** Physical: drowning or crushing from an unequalised gate opening or unshored chamber walls. Social: water rights are jealously guarded and canals cross boundaries; secure landowners and provincial/imperial sanction first, or you will be treated as a boundary-violator.

**Where to build first:** a short flight closing a known portage (for instance around the Diolkos at Corinth, where the land-freight problem across the isthmus is already recognised) or across a low watershed between two river systems in Gaul or the Po valley, converts an entire basin from land-freight to water-freight economics.

**Confidence: HIGH** on the mitre-gate mechanism and lock physics, well documented from later European practice; **LOW** on any cost or dating figures, since no Roman pound lock is attested.

### harbours_and_dredging - Harbours and Dredging (*portus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already builds excellent harbours; the remaining gain is mechanised dredging against silting, and faster caisson dewatering.

**Why you would never guess this.** Little to invent in construction itself. Portus and Caesarea Maritima are first-rate. The gap is maintenance: a perfect harbour in year one can silt shut by year fifty (Portus itself historically does).

**Prerequisites.** roman_baseline_civil, the treadwheel crane, the force pump.

**Roman-available inputs.** Barges/pontoons, iron for a grab bucket, rope, the existing treadwheel crane, existing Ctesibius-type force pumps.

**Procedure.** Mount a grab bucket or small bucket chain on a boom worked from a moored barge by an existing treadwheel crane, to lift silt into a spoil barge, mechanising what is otherwise diver or hand-basket labour and enabling continuous maintenance rather than periodic rebuilding. For construction, dewater the existing Roman double-walled timber caisson (Vitruvius) faster with relayed force pumps rather than bailing.

**How you know it worked.** A sounding line shows channel/basin depth holding or increasing over seasons, not shoaling. A caisson dewaters in hours, not days.

**Failure modes.** A grab undersized for silt load loses ground, especially after floods. Spoil dumped too near the mouth just relocates the problem.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED, by analogy with existing harbour maintenance labour: a barge crew and crane gang, an ongoing seasonal cost.

**Danger.** Physical: drowning, ordinary crane-over-water hazards. No social danger; unremarkable public works.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - the component devices are solidly attested; the combination into a mechanised dredge is a reasonable extrapolation, not documented Roman practice.

### ship_improvements - Rudder, Rig, Compass, Log and Chronometer (*navis*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Separable gains: sternpost rudder, better windward rig, magnetic compass, log line, better hull construction, and, far down the tree, the marine chronometer. None is a magic wand; Mediterranean sailing is already good.

**Why you would never guess this.** Roman quarter (steering-oar) rudders work well in Mediterranean conditions, and how close Roman rigging could sail to the wind is genuinely disputed among historians (LOW-MEDIUM confidence either way).

**Prerequisites.** Existing Roman shipbuilding, lodestone (already known, Pliny NH 36), needle-grade iron, a sandglass or clepsydra.

**Roman-available inputs.** Lodestone (*lapis magnes*), iron needles, wax/cork floats, fine linen/hemp sailcloth, timber and iron for rudder fittings.

**Procedure.** (1) Sternpost rudder: replace paired quarter oars with a single rudder on iron pintles/gudgeons and a tiller; concentrates steering force, most useful on large deep-draft hulls, limited extra value on coasters that already steer well. (2) Rig: add or improve a lateen sail combined with square rig for better ability to sail across or somewhat into the wind; do not promise a specific windward angle, gains are uncertain and hull-dependent. (3) Compass: stroke an iron needle repeatedly along a lodestone to magnetise it, float it on a wax/cork disc in water or balance it on a pin; it aligns roughly north-south. Most valuable on long open-water legs out of landmark sight (Atlantic coast, Red Sea/Indian Ocean monsoon routes), less so in the Mediterranean where pilots already navigate a named wind system (Vitruvius I). (4) Log line: a knotted line on a weighted board paid out over a timed interval (sandglass) gives speed through water; with compass heading and elapsed time, gives dead-reckoning position. (5) Hull construction: switch from labour-intensive shell-first mortise-and-tenon planking to frame-first (skeleton, then planked), trading some rigidity for far fewer skilled-labour hours per hull, a cost win, not a strength win. (6) Marine chronometer: far down the tree (historically Harrison, mid-18th century), a temperature-compensated timepiece finds longitude from local noon versus a reference time; needs precision alloys and gear-cutting well beyond near reach, noted only so you know what it eventually solves.

**How you know it worked.** Rudder: helm response at least as good as quarter oars in a following sea. Compass: needle returns to the same alignment regardless of heading changes. Log line: repeated timed casts at known oar rate give consistent knot counts.

**Failure modes.** Weakly magnetised needle wanders. Nearby iron or cargo deflects the compass. Green timber or badly fitted futtocks can make a frame-first hull weaker than shell-first, cheaper is not automatically stronger.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED: compass and log line are cheap (a smith, a few hours); rudder refit is a modest shipwright job; frame-first construction is a multi-season shipyard change.

**Danger.** Physical: ordinary seafaring risk. Social: none for the devices themselves, but demonstrate the compass plainly (a rubbed needle and a stone) rather than as a trick, to superstitious sailors it could otherwise read as magic.

**Confidence: HIGH** on compass, log line, rudder mechanics; **LOW-MEDIUM** on achievable windward performance, a genuinely open scholarly question, do not overclaim a specific angle.

### road_improvements - Horse Collar, Wheels and Macadam (*via*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's roads are already excellent; gains here are marginal and mostly about draft efficiency and cheap secondary construction, not beating the existing viae.

**Why you would never guess this.** "Better roads" sounds obviously valuable. It mostly isn't, Rome's trunk roads are near the practical limit for animal/human power. The gap is draft efficiency and cheap network extension.

**Prerequisites.** Basic leather/carpentry work; iron for tyres and fittings (already routine).

**Roman-available inputs.** Leather, felt/straw padding, wood, iron tyres and fittings, crushed stone.

**Procedure.** (1) Horse collar: Roman harness is throat-and-girth, which chokes a horse under load, hence Rome's reliance on oxen for heavy haulage. A rigid, padded collar resting on shoulders and rib cage carries the load on the skeleton instead, letting a horse haul substantially more per unit effort (commonly cited as several times a throat harness, ESTIMATED and attributed to later medieval comparisons, directional only) and letting horses replace oxen for time-sensitive cargo, not for the bulk freight this module is mainly about. (2) Wheels/axles: Roman wheels are often already iron-tyred; improve with dished wheels for lateral strength, standard axle gauges for easy repair, and greased iron bushings. (3) Macadam: for secondary roads not justifying a full via, lay graded crushed stone, cambered, without the deep multi-layer Roman foundation. Far cheaper and faster to build and repair than a proper via, but a cheaper road, not a better one, do not use it for a trunk route.

**How you know it worked.** A collared horse visibly hauls a load that previously needed a comparable ox-team, without laboured breathing. Macadam stays firm and drains after rain rather than rutting.

**Failure modes.** Ill-fitting collar chafes the shoulders. Macadam without adequate camber waterlogs into mud like any unpaved track.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED: a collar is a day or two of leatherworker's time per animal. Macadam is DERIVED at roughly a third to a half (ESTIMATED) of full Roman via labour/material per km, owing to no dressed paving and a shallower base.

**Danger.** Physical: ordinary harness/roadwork injury. No social danger; entirely conventional activity.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - the collar's mechanism is well understood from later agricultural history; the precise size of the gain, and macadam's exact cost ratio, are analogy-based estimates, not Roman measurements.

### railway - The Railway (*no ancient term; does not exist*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Iron rail, permanent way, and eventually steam locomotives, to move bulk freight at a fraction of cart cost. Honest framing: the marginal gain over Rome's already-good roads is smaller than it was in 18th-century Britain, but bulk inland freight cheaper than cart cost still changes what can be built away from navigable water.

**Why you would never guess this.** A horse pulling a cart on rigid iron rail with a flanged wheel can haul on the order of ten times the load the same horse manages on an ordinary road (ESTIMATED, attributed to early industrial-era horse tramway comparisons, directional not precise), because the rail removes most rolling resistance, and this works with a horse alone, long before any locomotive.

**Prerequisites.** Iron in bulk for rail bar, timber sleepers, surveying_precision for grading, road_improvements' horse collar for efficient traction. Full steam operation additionally needs bulk cheap steel and high-pressure boilers, both well down the metallurgy/machine-tool tree; treat this entry as the horse-drawn tramway stage only.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wrought (bloomery) iron for rail bar, timber sleepers, gravel ballast, existing cart/axle technology adapted to flanged wheels.

**Procedure.** (1) Grade a route as gently as terrain allows, using road/canal surveying methods. (2) Lay timber sleepers on compacted gravel ballast. (3) Fix iron rail bar along the sleepers, either flanged rail with plain wheel or plain rail with flanged wheel, whichever suits your ironworking capacity (plain rail is simpler to forge). (4) Build carts to match the rail gauge, hauled by collared horses. (5) No steam or bulk steel needed for this stage; most useful for a short run from a mine or quarry face to navigable water.

**How you know it worked.** A single horse hauls a load on rail that would need several horses or oxen on an equivalent road.

**Failure modes.** Thin or poorly welded rail bends or cracks under load. Inadequate ballast settles unevenly and derails wheels. Too steep a grade defeats the purpose.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED: iron consumption per km of light tramway is substantial and competes directly with iron needed elsewhere in the tech tree; cost any specific figure against your own local iron price before committing.

**Danger.** Physical: derailment and crush injury on a grade. No unusual social danger for a horse tramway; a full steam railway will eventually raise the same "infernal machine" concerns as any large fire-and-steam device (see hot_air_balloon for managing that).

**Confidence: MEDIUM** on the horse-drawn tramway stage, closely following documented pre-steam mining tramway practice; **LOW** on any near-term steam railway, gated on prerequisites outside this module.

### mining_deep - Deep Mining: Blasting, Pumping, Ventilation, Pithead Engine (*metallum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already mines at industrial scale with real drainage technology. Genuine additions: gunpowder blasting, better pumping, forced ventilation, and, once basic steam exists, a Newcomen-type pithead pumping engine.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rio Tinto (Spain) shows preserved wooden compartmented drainage wheels and Archimedes screws; Las Medulas (Spain) shows hydraulic gold mining by *ruina montium*, "wrecking of mountains" (Pliny, NH 33); Dolaucothi (Britain) shows the same aqueduct-fed hushing. The gap is rock-breaking, air, and pump power at depth, not scale or drainage.

**Prerequisites.** Gunpowder chemistry (saltpetre from farmed nitre beds, NOT Roman *nitrum*/natron which is sodium carbonate; sulfur, from Sicily; charcoal; see the gunpowder module for nitre-bed procedure), existing drainage wheels and screws, the force pump, and, for the pithead engine, basic cylinder-and-piston steam technology.

**Roman-available inputs.** Sulfur (Sicily and other volcanic districts), charcoal, farmed saltpetre, iron tools and pump fittings, timber for shoring and wheels.

**Procedure.** (1) Blasting: drill/chisel a hole, pack with gunpowder, seal, fire a slow fuse from a safe distance, breaking far more rock than fire-setting (heat then quench, the existing Roman method) or hand tools. The single biggest productivity gain here, and the most politically dangerous item in this module (see Danger). (2) Pumping: scale up the existing force pump, or a chain-of-pots pump, driven by a water wheel where available, to reach depths beyond a hand-worked screw. (3) Ventilation: for deep or enclosed galleries, light a fire at the base of an updraft shaft (a "fire drift") to pull foul air out through one shaft while fresh air enters another, or feed air down a pipe with bellows. Test air with a lowered flame before sending men in, a dimming or dying flame signals dangerous air. (4) Pithead engine: once basic steam exists (gated, see other modules), a Newcomen-type atmospheric engine, steam condensed by a water spray creating a vacuum that drives a piston, works a pump rod continuously; its poor thermal efficiency does not matter here because mine timber offcuts and low-grade fuel are effectively free on site, unlike an engine that must move itself.

**How you know it worked.** A test blast breaks rock over a visibly larger volume than the same labour-hours by hand. A lowered lamp burns steadily at the working face.

**Failure modes.** Damp or poorly packed powder, or firing too close, kills or maims. Poor ventilation planning starves a fire drift or feeds a gas build-up into an open flame. Undersized pump capacity loses the race and the working floods.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED throughout; gunpowder cost is dominated by saltpetre, which must be farmed over months, so treat blasting as capital- and time-intensive to establish even though each charge is cheap once supply exists.

**Danger.** Physical: gunpowder is lethal if mishandled; bad air kills silently. Social: the most politically dangerous item in this module. Anyone who can make rock, and by extension walls, ships, or men, explode on command is one bad conversation from being denounced for plotting against the state, or simply disappeared by whoever controls the mine (many are imperial property under a *procurator metallorum*). Introduce this only through official mining administration, demonstrate openly under supervision as a rock-breaking tool, never stockpile privately or discuss it outside mining.

**Confidence: HIGH** on Roman baseline capability (Rio Tinto, Las Medulas, Dolaucothi, Pliny) and on gunpowder chemistry/blasting mechanics; **LOW** on the pithead engine, gated on prerequisites outside this module.

### surveying_precision - Precision Surveying: Theodolite and Triangulation (groma, chorobates, dioptra already exist)

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome's instruments are already good; the theodolite (a refined dioptra with a finely graduated circle) and triangulation give a real jump in angular precision, decisive for canal levelling, accurate mapping, and long alignments.

**Why you would never guess this.** A groma or dioptra looks like it already does the job. The difference is purely in how finely you can read the angle, a fine index or vernier reads to a fraction of a degree versus the dioptra's coarser practical field resolution (ESTIMATED at around a degree or worse, a modern reconstruction, not a Roman-stated figure), and that gap compounds badly over a long triangulation chain.

**Prerequisites.** Existing groma/chorobates/dioptra practice, basic bronze/brass instrument-making, Greek chord-table methods (Hipparchus's chord tables are the accessible equivalent of trig tables; how much of this is practically accessible to a Roman-era surveyor by 100 AD is MEDIUM confidence).

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze/brass for a graduated circle and alidade, a stable tripod, the existing chorobates water-trough level for calibration.

**Procedure.** (1) Build a dioptra-derived instrument with a horizontal (ideally also vertical) graduated circle and a fine index or vernier, mounted for precise levelling. (2) Measure one baseline very carefully on flat, accessible ground with a checked chain or rod. (3) From each end, sight a third point and record angles, fixing its position by triangle geometry without measuring the distance directly. (4) Extend the chain using each new point as a fresh baseline end. (5) Use the network for accurate mapping, and, most valuably given the freight arithmetic below, for canal and lock levelling, since a misgraded canal won't hold water and a misjudged sill won't seal.

**How you know it worked.** A chain closed back on a second independently measured baseline agrees within a small stated tolerance; if not, find and redo the bad angle.

**Failure modes.** An unlevelled instrument corrupts every sighting from that station silently. An uncorrected sloped baseline measurement propagates error through the whole chain.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED: instrument-making is days of skilled bronzework; a significant triangulation survey is weeks to a season of fieldwork for a small trained team.

**Danger.** No unusual physical danger. No social danger; surveying is entirely conventional, often state-sponsored.

**Confidence: HIGH** on existing Roman instruments and their limits; **MEDIUM** on the exact precision gap and on how much usable trigonometric method is really available in 100 AD.

### structures_iron_steel - Iron and Steel Structures, and Reinforced Concrete (*no ancient term*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Iron, later steel, framing allows longer, lighter spans than masonry/timber; reinforced concrete eventually combines Roman-grade compressive concrete with steel's tensile strength, but only once cheap bulk steel exists and thermal-expansion matching is understood.

**Why you would never guess this.** Plain concrete, like stone, is excellent in compression and essentially useless in tension, it cracks abruptly if bent, which is why Roman engineers always shape it into arches, vaults and domes, never flat beams. The non-obvious part of reinforcement is not "add metal," it is that steel and set concrete must expand and contract together with temperature or the bond cracks apart over repeated cycles; by good fortune their thermal expansion coefficients closely match (roughly 10-14 millionths per °C for both, a modern measured property, not something a Roman could verify without precise thermal measurement).

**Prerequisites.** Bulk cheap iron/steel (well down the metallurgy tree; Roman iron cramps and dowels are already routine for stonework, but structural framing needs far more output than bloomery alone gives), concrete_and_cement above.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wrought iron (already used for cramps and dowels), timber for existing roof trusses, existing hydraulic concrete.

**Procedure.** (1) Iron framing: where Roman practice uses heavy timber trusses (attested in general terms, exact truss geometry is debated, MEDIUM confidence) or thick vaulting, an iron-framed truss of the same span can be much lighter, since iron in tension beats timber or masonry in compression for the same weight. Most valuable for wide clear-span buildings, less so where Roman vault/arch technique already handles the span well. (2) Reinforced concrete, once cheap steel rod exists: cast concrete around steel bars placed in the tension zones (the underside of a beam between supports). Cured concrete's alkaline pore water passivates the steel against rust as long as it stays uncracked and fully encased; if concrete cracks or degrades, the steel rusts, and rust's greater volume forces the concrete apart from inside, a slow failure with no Roman precedent since plain concrete has no embedded metal to rust. (3) Use reinforcement only for flat slabs, beams, and cantilevers, shapes plain concrete cannot do; keep using plain concrete in arches, vaults and domes, where it already wins on cost.

**How you know it worked.** A reinforced test beam carries a load that snaps an equivalent unreinforced one; an opened section shows the steel still fully bonded and encased.

**Failure modes.** Steel placed in the wrong (compression) zone does almost nothing. Inadequate cover starts corrosion early. Reinforcing an arch or dome already working in pure compression wastes steel for no benefit.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED and heavily gated: reachable only once bulk cheap steel exists elsewhere in the programme; do not attempt as an early project.

**Danger.** Physical: a wrongly reinforced member can fail suddenly without the warning cracking plain masonry usually gives first. No unusual social danger.

**Confidence: HIGH** on why plain concrete needs compression-only shapes and on reinforcement's thermal/passivation requirements, well-established materials science; **LOW** on any near-term Roman timeline, gated on iron output far beyond bloomery capacity.

### hot_air_balloon - The Hot Air Balloon (*no ancient term*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A tethered, hot-air-filled linen envelope for military observation and signalling, buildable now from materials Rome already has: fine sailcloth, oil sizing, pitch, and asbestos.

**Why you would never guess this.** The physics is simple once stated (hot air is less dense, so a big enough bag of it lifts a load), but nobody had reason to connect that with a basket and a man. The real obstacles, an envelope that doesn't burn from its own heat source and a controllable fire, are solvable with existing Roman materials.

**Prerequisites.** Fine woven linen sailcloth (already produced to a high standard), oil for sizing, asbestos (already known, from sources such as Cyprus), rope and netting work.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen sailcloth, boiled oil or pitch-resin varnish (*pix*, from pine resin, already used for ship caulking, Pliny NH 16) for sizing, asbestos cloth (already used for wicks and fireproofing) for a burner-mouth skirt, rope netting, a wicker basket, charcoal or dry wood.

**Procedure.** (1) Sew a large envelope from closely woven linen panels, sized inside with boiled oil or pitch-resin varnish to cut porosity, following existing Roman tent/sail waterproofing practice. (2) Fit a rope net over the outside, gathered to load lines at the base, so basket weight is carried across the whole envelope. (3) Line the throat and burner-mouth skirt with asbestos cloth so nearby fabric cannot scorch. (4) Suspend a fire basket of charcoal or dry wood below the throat, venting heat and gas directly up, with a way to control fuel feed. (5) Anchor on a strong rope and windlass; fly tethered only until extensively tested, a captive balloon needs no navigation and simply winches down. (6) Lift arithmetic: air density falls in inverse proportion to absolute (Kelvin) temperature at constant pressure. Using standard sea-level air density of about 1.225 kg/m³ at 15°C (288 K, a physical constant, not a Roman figure), lift (kg) is approximately V (m³) times 1.225 times (1 minus 288/T), T being the heated internal temperature in Kelvin. Worked example: a 1,000 m³ envelope heated to 100°C (373 K), safely below where oil-sized linen begins to scorch (keep well under roughly 150°C as a conservative margin, ESTIMATED, the scorch point of a specific dressing is untested): internal density falls to about 0.946 kg/m³; the difference, 0.279 kg/m³, times 1,000 m³, gives roughly 279 kg gross lift. Subtract envelope, netting, basket, fire basket and fuel weight for usable payload; size the envelope generously above the intended payload, then scale this formula to whatever size and safe temperature you can actually build and test.

**How you know it worked.** Before anyone rides, fill and heat the tethered, lightly loaded envelope and confirm it lifts its own weight clear of the ground with rope tension slack, a float test, before adding payload or crew.

**Failure modes.** Fabric too porous never develops enough lift. Overheating scorches or ignites fabric, especially near an inadequate throat skirt. An unbalanced fire basket or gust while tethered can tip fire against the envelope wall.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED: material cost is modest, sailcloth and sizing are routine ship-supply items; the main cost is skilled sailmaker/ropemaker labour comparable to a large ship's sail, plus a carpenter for the basket.

**Danger.** Physical: fire, falls if manned and unrestrained, rope failure; always tether and test unmanned or lightly loaded first. Social: an object rising into the sky trailing fire, with no visible cause, sits squarely in the Roman category of prodigy and omen; a magistrate, priest, or frightened crowd could read it as sorcery or a bad omen for the state. Arrange the first flight as a public, daylight demonstration under military or magisterial authority, with the presiding officer or a priest present and briefed beforehand, so it is witnessed and explained on the spot rather than left as an unexplained apparition. Avoid night flights and unannounced flights, and lead with the practical military case (observation over walls, signalling by flag or smoke from height), since that is what gets it funded and protected rather than suppressed.

**Confidence: HIGH** on the lift physics, standard and directly checkable; **MEDIUM** on the safe operating temperature for a specific oil-sized linen, untested and to be approached cautiously and incrementally; **MEDIUM** on the specific danger, the general prodigy/sorcery risk is well grounded in Roman religious practice, the precise legal mechanism that might be invoked is not asserted with confidence here.

### water_supply_sanitation - Water Supply and Sanitation (*aqua ducta*, *cloaca*)

**What it is / why you want it.** An honest account of what Rome's aqueducts and sewers actually do and do not do for public health, including the lead pipe question, so you neither dismiss the system nor overestimate it.

**Why you would never guess this.** The instinct is either "Roman aqueducts, nothing to add" or the popular myth "lead pipes poisoned Rome and caused its fall." Both miss the narrower, more useful truth.

**Prerequisites.** roman_baseline_civil; no new construction needed, mainly correct use of what already exists.

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing aqueduct channels (masonry, concrete, or lead pipe *fistulae plumbeae* on final runs), existing sewers (Cloaca Maxima and similar), earthenware pipe (*fistulae fictiles*), a lead-free alternative already known and already recommended by Vitruvius over lead.

**Procedure.** (1) What genuinely helps: abundant flowing water for drinking, bathing, and especially flushing public latrines (attested, for instance at Ostia) and sewers, removing waste faster than it would otherwise accumulate, real and valuable at a scale unmatched for over a millennium after Rome. (2) What does not follow: germ theory doesn't exist, so contamination at point of use (open basins also used for washing clothes or animals) and disease routes aqueducts don't touch (mosquito-borne malaria, endemic around Rome's marshland regardless of water quality; contaminated food; person-to-person contact) remain unaddressed. Do not promise a patron that clean water alone controls epidemic disease. (3) Public bathing is a genuine amenity, but treat "communal bathing was straightforwardly hygienic" with caution (MEDIUM confidence); pools not fully drained and refilled often can spread skin/eye infection even from a clean aqueduct supply. (4) The lead pipe question: final-distribution pipe is routinely lead; Vitruvius himself warns against it in favour of earthenware (*De Architectura* VIII.6, a genuine primary source). Modern lead-isotope studies of ice cores, sediment, and skeletal remains confirm real, measurable exposure (HIGH confidence exposure was real). That lead pipes were a dominant cause of population-wide poisoning or Rome's decline is not well supported (LOW-MEDIUM confidence, contested scholarship): most aqueduct length was open channel or masonry conduit, not lead; mineral scale ("sinter," seen in surviving pipe sections) coats and reduces further leaching; continuously flowing water leaches far less than standing water in lead plumbing. A better-documented, more concentrated dietary lead source is *defrutum*/*sapa* (grape must boiled down as a sweetener in lead-lined pots, producing lead acetate, "sugar of lead"), used mainly by those wealthy enough to afford both process and cookware, so elite dietary exposure may have exceeded ordinary fountain-water exposure. (5) Practical advice: prefer earthenware or masonry pipe over lead where the choice is yours, as Vitruvius recommends; avoid boiling, storing, or preparing acidic liquids (wine, vinegar, fruit musts, any defrutum or sapa) in lead vessels, acid plus heat leaches lead fastest, an avoidable, well-attested, high-value change, more actionable than re-piping a whole aqueduct system.

**How you know it worked.** No sense-based test for lead exposure exists to a Roman; the actionable step is avoidance, not verification.

**Failure modes.** Assuming clean aqueduct water alone prevents disease and neglecting drainage, food handling, and standing water. Assuming all lead-pipe use is equally dangerous and wasting effort re-piping low-risk continuous-flow public supply while ignoring the higher-risk practice of cooking acidic food in lead vessels.

**Cost & labour.** N/A, advisory only.

**Danger.** Physical: chronic lead exposure from acidic food in lead vessels is real and cumulative; avoid as above. No social danger; citing Vitruvius approvingly is not a controversial position.

**Confidence: HIGH** on aqueduct/sewer engineering and on the reality of lead exposure and the Vitruvius citation; **MEDIUM-LOW** on the population-level health magnitude of lead exposure and on the bathing-hygiene nuance, both genuinely contested or thinly evidenced, present with that caveat attached.

## The freight arithmetic

Here is the calculation that should decide where everything else in this programme is sited, using the two figures given above: land freight costs roughly 40 to 60 times water freight per tonne-kilometre, and hauling grain about 500 km overland roughly doubles its price. The whole calculation below is ESTIMATED, it uses a stated ratio as its basis, not a measured Roman price schedule (the closest real figures, Diocletian's Price Edict, are from 301 AD under a debased currency and should not be read back into 100 AD directly).

Take a blast furnace needing 180 tonnes of charcoal delivered, compared at two sites. Site A sits directly on a navigable river; charcoal travels 80 km entirely by barge. Site B sits 40 km inland from that same river; charcoal travels the same 80 km by barge to the nearest wharf, then a further 40 km by ox cart.

Let water transport cost 1 unit per tonne-km, and land transport, using the midpoint of the given range, about 50 units per tonne-km (shown at both ends below too).

Site A: 180 t times 80 km times 1 = 14,400 tonne-km-units.
Site B: the same river leg, 14,400 units, plus the land leg, 180 t times 40 km times 50 = 360,000 units, total 374,400 units.

Site B costs about 26 times as much to supply as Site A (374,400 / 14,400), even though the land leg is only 40 of the total 120 km, one third of the distance. At the low end of the ratio (40 times) the multiple is about 21; at the high end (60 times) about 31. Call it, honestly, a twenty-to-thirty-fold increase in delivered charcoal cost for sitting the same furnace 40 km off the water, ESTIMATED and sensitive to the exact multiplier, but not sensitive enough to change the conclusion. Put differently, the last third of the journey accounts for roughly 95 to 97 percent of the total transport cost, while the first two thirds accounts for only 3 to 5 percent.

Sanity check against the other given figure: if land transport costs about 50 times water per tonne-km, and 500 km overland roughly doubles price, then treating that as roughly linear over these distances (a simplification, the real relationship likely compounds, but adequate here), overland transport adds roughly a fifth of one percent of a commodity's value per km, water about a two-hundred-and-fiftieth of that. A full-length Mediterranean voyage of some 4,000 km would then add only on the order of 15 to 20 percent to a cargo's price, comparable to perhaps 100 to 150 km of overland cartage, matching a widely repeated observation in modern discussions of the ancient economy (attributed, working from the Price Edict, exact original source disputed) that shipping grain the length of the Mediterranean could be cheaper than carting it a modest distance inland. The two given figures are consistent with each other, some reassurance both are the right order of magnitude.

Act on this: every furnace, foundry, mill, or workshop in this programme that consumes bulk material, ore, charcoal, timber, stone, grain for a workforce, should be sited on navigable water, or as close as the ore or fuel source allows, before any other siting consideration. Where that is impossible, the canal and lock programme above, or, much further down the tree, the railway, are the only ways to avoid paying the land-freight penalty on the inland leg indefinitely.
