# Module 95: Going and Getting It

## On Unobtainable Materials

Every guide to mechanical and chemical bootstrapping says: "rubber is unobtainable; use a substitute." This statement is simply wrong. Rubber is not unobtainable. It is *elsewhere*.

There is no such thing as an unobtainable material. There are only materials that are somewhere else, and the question is not whether you can have them but what it costs to go and get them. Three methods exist. You can trade, paying a chain of middlemen and never controlling supply, which is expensive per unit but requires no capital. You can send an expedition to establish a factory and trading post, which requires ships and armed men and capital but gives you control of supply. You can conquer the source entirely, which requires the most capital and carries the largest moral cost, and it is what actually happened historically for rubber, saltpetre, spices and silver.

This module exists to tell you what is actually reachable, why you would reach for it, what it costs to get there, and what you become when you do.

## provisioning_scurvy - Vitamin C and the Problem That Killed Empires

Scurvy is the gate. Not the ocean. Not the wind. Not the longitude. Scurvy is the limiting factor that turned a ten-week voyage from ambitious into lethal.

Vitamin C deficiency is a slow killer at sea. The symptoms take weeks to appear: bleeding gums, the opening of old scars, the weakening of joints, the pooling of blood under the skin until a man simply stops moving. It killed more sailors in the four centuries after 1500 than storms and battle combined. It was not the Atlantic that was the barrier. It was the six to ten weeks without fresh vegetables.

But you know the answer. You have known it since before Rome was founded. Citrus prevents scurvy. So do fresh greens, fresh meat, sauerkraut, and the sprouts that grow on a damp grain. Fresh is the keyword. A desiccated herb does not work. Preserved meat does not work. Wine does not work, though people thought it did. You need the juice, or the living tissue.

The Roman solution is simple: you do not go longer without fresh supply than your body can tolerate. This means either short legs with a beach stop every eight to ten days, or a ship's garden, or both. The Alexandrian grain ships were very large indeed. A deck garden takes time and water but not much of either. It is not elegant. But it works.

Hanno the Carthaginian sailed the coast of West Africa around 500 BC and returned alive with an account. He did not solve scurvy because scurvy was not yet the killer it would become - he was coastal, with regular landfalls. But his voyage proves the feasibility. Coastal sailing with supply stops every ten days is how you solve the scurvy gate. It is inefficient and it works.

## openocean_navigation - Finding Where You Are by Finding Where You Are Not

Longitude is a clock problem, not a navigation problem. You do not need to solve it to cross an ocean.

Until you have a sea clock accurate to within a few seconds, you navigate by latitude sailing: you run to the latitude of your target and then you run east or west along it. It is extraordinarily inefficient. Columbus did exactly this. He sailed south to find the latitude of the Caribbean, then east along it, reaching land almost by accident. Vasco da Gama sailed south along the African coast, east across the Indian Ocean by following the latitude line, and arrived at Calicut much later than he could have with a precise longitude. But he arrived.

You can find your latitude by the sun at noon - its height at noon varies with your distance from the equator, and any Roman navigator knows this. You can navigate the ocean by latitude alone. Your journey takes longer. You waste weeks hunting for your landfall. Your supply line stretches. But you do not need an unsolved problem to start the work.

The Roman merchant fleet has the instruments to do this already. A gnomon shadow, a cross-staff or a simple marked stick, enough astronomy to read a star altitude - these things exist in 100 AD. The Alexandrian astronomers have already worked out the mathematics. What is missing is not the method but the reason to sail, and the ship to do it in.

## oceangoing_hull - The Sternpost Rudder and Why You Need It

The Mediterranean round ship is a marvel. It carries enormous cargo, it weathered the Roman peace, and it will not cross an ocean.

The Mediterranean ship is built for light winds and close-shore sailing. Its bow is too high and its stern is too low. Its rudder is a pair of steering oars hung from the sides, which works perfectly when you have an experienced steersman in calm water. Put it in the Atlantic swells of October and those oars become liabilities. You cannot keep the ship into the wind. You cannot tack reliably. You are at the mercy of the current.

What you need is the sternpost rudder - a single, centered blade hung beneath the stern on a pintle and gudgeon. It gives you the leverage to control the ship in rough water. It lets you point high into the wind. It lets you tack, which means you can sail to windward. This is not a small advantage. This means you can leave port and choose your departure date regardless of the wind, rather than waiting for it to be fair.

The sternpost rudder exists in China by 100 AD - Chinese junks use it. It will appear in Europe only in the 13th century. You are an engineer with access to bronze and iron work. A pintle is a hinge, a gudgeon is a fitting, and a blade is a plank. Building one is carpentry. Bolting it to a ship is two days of work with good tools.

Add it to a strong Mediterranean merchant ship, shorten the bow, deepen the stern, and you have a ship that can sail the Atlantic. The hull shape itself is Roman already. You are not inventing the ship. You are adding the one control surface that lets it function in water beyond the Mediterranean.

## trade_route_extend - Following the Road to the Spice Islands

Rome already trades with India. The monsoon route from Berenice to Muziris is sailed every year, and Pliny complains that it drains Rome of gold. The journey takes three months in good conditions. The ships are large, they carry enormous cargo, and they come home alive.

Everything from Bengal saltpetre to pepper to wootz ingots to cotton flows on this route. India is not reached by a desperate expedition. It is a trade route.

Extending the India route east is the next step. The Indian traders themselves sail to Malaya, to Sumatra, to the Spice Islands. The seas are the same seas. The winds are the same monsoons, predictable to the month. The time is longer - five months to the Maluku islands, instead of three to India - but it is time, not impossibility.

The spice islands - cloves and nutmeg - are the single highest-value cargo per weight in the pre-modern world. A ton of cloves costs as much as a ton of gold. The routes exist. The sailors exist. Indian merchants sail them every year. The only thing missing is the Roman merchant fleet deciding to take a longer voyage. Extending the India route east cuts out every middleman between Rome and the source. It makes the spice trade profitable instead of merely prestigious.

## coastal_africa - The Guinea Coast Run

West Africa is not a barrier. It is a coast, and coasts have been sailed for ten thousand years.

The West African coast from the Pillars of Hercules south to the Gulf of Guinea is reachable by simple coastal sailing. The currents run south and roughly parallel to the shore. The wind blows offshore in the winter months. A ship can make the journey south with favorable conditions and make the return voyage in the winter months when the wind is from the north. It is not easy. But it is done.

Hanno the Carthaginian proved this in approximately 500 BC. He sailed down the West African coast, established trading posts, took on spices and incense, and came home. His account survives, copied and transmitted through antiquity. He did not discover anything. He colonized nothing. He traded, turned around, and went home. The voyage took months. It was not casual. But it was possible, and a Carthaginian captain with Greek and Libyans in his crew is not so far removed from a Roman captain with Syrian sailors.

The coastline is known in rough outline through the Periplus of the Red Sea and through Pliny's Natural History. There are forests, there are rivers, there are people. What is actually there has been misremembered and confused by retelling, but the coast is not mysterious. It is simply never visited by Rome.

The reason to go is rubber. Landolphia and Funtumia vines grow in the West African rainforest, and they produce real natural rubber - Hevea brasiliensis quality, the stuff the modern world still uses. The West African latex industry in the 1890s ran on these vines until the planted rubber plantations in Malaya destroyed the market. For fifteen years, the main source of rubber for the world was West Africa, not the Amazon. The vines do not care if they are tapped in 1890 or 100 AD. They bleed white latex into your cup, and you have rubber.

One voyage down the Guinea coast with a skilled botanist and a trade mission, and you can bring back seeds, saplings, and knowledge of the tapping. One successful establishment of Landolphia on Roman land, and you own rubber forever. The cost is a ship and three months at sea. The supply is guaranteed. The profit is enormous.

## africa_circumnavigation - The Cape Route and the Southern Horizon

A voyage around Africa is theoretically possible in Roman times. Herodotus reports, on contested authority, that a Phoenician expedition sailed around the entire African continent under commission from Necho II of Egypt. The voyage took three years. It is difficult to verify as historical fact. But the voyage is physically possible with Roman-era ships and navigation, which is the important part.

The route runs down the West African coast as above, then around the Cape of Good Hope in the southern summer months when the weather is most manageable, then up the East African coast to the Red Sea. It is perhaps ninety days of ocean sailing between major supply points. It is not impossible. It requires a crew with experience, a well-found ship, and reasonable luck with the weather. It is more dangerous than the Indian route, but the Indian route is also dangerous - Rome trades with India, and Roman ships wreck on the Indian Ocean with regularity.

The payoff is enormous. The East African coast is reported to have gold, ivory, and cinnamon. The direct route eliminates all middlemen. No Indian merchant, no Arab trader, no Parthian toll-keeper stands between Rome and the source. One voyage establishes the route. Subsequent voyages become routine.

This is the voyage Vasco da Gama actually made in 1497. It killed a significant fraction of his crew. It took eleven months. It was one of the most important voyages in European history, and it was possible in 100 AD. The reason it did not happen then is not that it was technically impossible. It is that there was no sufficient profit motive, and it had not been tried.

## atlantic_crossing - The Ocean Barrier That Is Not Really There

The Atlantic Ocean is wide. It is genuinely dangerous. Ships will wreck in storms. Crews will die. The voyage is not casual.

But it is possible.

The trade winds north of 20 degrees latitude blow steadily from the east, which means they blow toward the Americas. A ship setting out from the coast of West Africa or Iberia in the right season will be pushed west by the wind. Columbus used this fact - he sailed south to catch the trade winds, then west across the ocean to the Caribbean. It took thirty-three days of open water sailing. Medieval ships crossed the North Atlantic regularly in conditions less favorable. It is a hard voyage, not an impossible one.

A well-found ship with the sternpost rudder, provisioned against scurvy with preserved citrus juice or a working ship's garden, can cross the Atlantic in six to eight weeks. The return voyage against the wind takes longer - Columbus took longer coming home - but the route is used. The route is not theoretical. It is what actually happens.

The payload changes everything. The Caribbean has cacao, maize, tobacco. South America has potatoes, quinoa, and rubber. The potato alone - which yields two to four times the calories per hectare of wheat on poor ground - changes the caloric ceiling of continental agriculture. One successful voyage home with potato seed stock, and you grow them forever. That single crop pays for the voyage a hundred times over.

## americas_factory - The Trading Post and the Supply Line

You do not conquer the Americas. You establish a fort and trade.

A small fortified trading post on the Caribbean coast requires a garrison of perhaps fifty armed men, supplies for a year, and the ability to build or repair a wooden fort and ships. A Roman merchant can accomplish this. Spain eventually did, repeatedly, with smaller garrisons than Rome could field.

The post exists to trade. The people of the Caribbean have goods - cacao, maize, tobacco, parrots, and other exotica. They want tools, cloth, and amber. They want Mediterranean glass beads, which the Romans are superb at making. A trading post collects goods and ships them home. It takes years to become profitable, but it does become profitable.

The post also exists to be a stable point where voyages can resupply and refuel, and where seed stock and saplings can be loaded for the return voyage. Without it, each trans-Atlantic voyage is a maximum-risk expedition. With it, the fifth voyage is routine.

The hard part is not the trading post. It is maintaining the supply line. The post must be resupplied every two years at minimum. Each resupply voyage costs money and exposes the crew to the Atlantic crossing twice. Each loss or delay threatens to starve the garrison. But trade posts in hostile territory are how the Portuguese, Dutch, and British actually expanded their reach into the Indian Ocean. It is the Roman model, projected across water. It works.

## colony_administration - Governing from a Distance

A colony is a trading post that is permanent and that governs itself.

The difference is subtle but matters. A trading post is a military installation run by a garrison commander who reports to the home office and follows their orders. A colony is a permanent settlement where Romans live, intermarry, and establish a second home. The colonists govern themselves within broad guidelines. They have families with them or start families. They are not rotation-drafted soldiers counting down to the day they go home.

This is harder than a trading post. It requires investment in housing, infrastructure, and civilian governance. It requires accepting that some of your citizens will leave Rome and become citizens of the new place instead. It requires planning for problems that take years to solve and that will be solved by people you may never meet.

But colonies are how Rome actually works. Britannia, Gaul, Egypt - these are provinces because they are populated by Romans and governed as extensions of the homeland. A colony in the Caribbean would follow the same pattern. It would take longer to establish than a trading post - ten or fifteen years rather than two or three. But once established, it is stable. It cannot be starved out. It will expand and generate wealth by its own momentum.

The first potato harvested on Roman soil comes from a colony's greenhouse, not from a trading post's temporary garden. The first permanent rubber plantation in the Atlantic world is in a colony, not a post. Colonies are how you permanently own an overseas resource.

## transplant_botany - Moving the Garden

The high-leverage move is this: whatever is unique to a foreign land, you bring it home and make it ubiquitous.

Sugar cane, coffee, tea, cotton, cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, cinchona, rubber, tobacco, potatoes, maize, cacao - every one of these plants was transplanted from its native land to somewhere else, and every transplant broke a monopoly and collapsed the price. The Dutch transplanted nutmeg from the Moluccas to Java and destroyed the Moluccas' monopoly. The British transplanted tea from China to Darjeeling and later Assam and created a tea industry that still exists. The British and Dutch both transplanted cinchona from South America to their colonies and broke the Spanish hold on quinine. The transplant is not incidental. It is the actual business.

Moving a living plant across an ocean is not obvious. A potted plant dies of thirst. Its soil spoils. It cannot survive salt spray. For centuries, plants simply died. Then, in the 1830s, a Wardian case was developed - a sealed, glazed wooden box with moist soil, a living plant, and a sealed glass lid. You water it once, seal it, and do not open it again. The plant recycles its own humidity inside the box. It does not need watering. It does not die from salt spray or thirst. It survives the three-month ocean voyage with perhaps five percent mortality instead of ninety-five percent.

Build a Wardian case in 100 AD is carpentry and glass. Roman glass workers have already invented cold-bend techniques good enough to make greenhouse panes. A sealed wooden box with glass panels is not beyond them. The design is not clever. It is intuitive, really: make the plant into a closed system and it takes care of itself.

Bring fifty cinchona saplings back from the Andes in Wardian cases, and you have fifty plants in the Caribbean colonies. In five years you have a plantation. In ten years you have enough to supply the Mediterranean and undercut the South American price by half. The monopoly is broken. Quinine becomes cheap.

A single tea plant brought back from China establishes a strain. One potato from Peru, replanted once, feeds an empire. The mathematics of transplanting is extraordinary: one organism, left to reproduce, becomes thousands. The cost is paid once. The return is paid forever.

## conquest_resource - The Necessary Evil

This section addresses what this knowledge is actually used for historically, and what it costs.

The rubber plantation in the Congo Free State under Leopold II was one of the worst atrocities of the industrial era. The area had Landolphia vines that yielded rubber, and Leopold's state extracted them through forced labor so brutal that the population of the Congo declined by perhaps ten million people over twenty years. The mortality rate among those forced to collect rubber exceeded fifty percent. Journalists and human rights activists in Europe eventually exposed the atrocities so completely that even Leopold's political allies could no longer defend him. The Belgian government annexed the Congo from Leopold in 1908, and conditions improved marginally, but the damage was permanent.

This happened because rubber was valuable, and because Leopold chose conquest and enslavement over trade and transplanting.

Every historical version of long-distance resource extraction has followed this pattern: find a material that is extremely valuable and monopolized, send an expedition, establish a trading post, decide that a trading post is inefficient, and conquer the land instead. The Spanish did this with silver. The Dutch did this with spices. The British did this with opium and cotton and eventually tea. Every conquest of a resource-rich land has required either genocide or slavery or both. This is not incidental. This is the central historical fact of colonialism.

The alternatives actually exist. You can trade on fair terms, paying what the local people ask. It will be expensive. It will be profitable for them as well as for you. You can pay for a monopoly legally, buying exclusive rights to tap the resource and ensuring the sellers do not sell to competitors. You can transplant the crop to your own land and grow it yourself, breaking the monopoly by eliminating the need for it. You can offer employment at wages that do not amount to slavery, hire local labor and Romans both, and run the operation with consent.

None of these paths is cheaper than conquest. All of them are more expensive than enslavement. All of them result in trade that makes both sides richer, which is why they were not historically chosen. Every navigator reading this module has a choice about which path to take. The choice exists. So does the knowledge of what each path costs.

## conquest_resource - The Alternatives That Actually Work

If you transplant cloves from Maluku to your own colonies, the Maluku sultanates lose the monopoly revenue forever, but they are not conquered and they are not decimated by plague. The price of cloves falls from ruinous to merely expensive, and everyone who benefits from cloves becomes richer. The traders who bought from the Maluku get lower prices. The growers who work in the new plantations get wages. The local people in Maluku lose a monopoly revenue stream, which is painful, but they keep their lives and their autonomy.

If you pay a fair price for rubber from the West African coast, the price of rubber rises, which attracts more collectors, which creates an economy around rubber collection, and local people choose to engage with it because it is profitable for them. This is not how it actually happened in Africa. But it is possible, and it is cheaper than the management overhead of conquest.

If you establish a trading post and hire local labor as well as rotation crews from Rome, offering wages sufficient to attract volunteers rather than conscripts, the post becomes economically self-sustaining and does not require constant military expenditure. It is less profitable per unit than enslavement, but it does not require putting down rebellions either.

These paths are not paths of virtue in competition with vice. They are paths of enlightened self-interest in competition with short-term brute extraction. The conquest of a resource is profitable in the short term and drains capital in the long term. The legal purchase of a monopoly on a resource, the transplanting of the crop, the hiring of willing labor rather than enslaved labor - these paths are more expensive initially and more profitable eventually.

Leopold chose wrong. The Belgians who succeeded him and the British and Dutch in their own territories mostly chose wrong, though they chose less catastrophically than Leopold. You have the knowledge now. The expeditions to the distant coast are possible. The trades are possible. The transplants are possible. What happens next is your decision.

## Sources and Confidence

**On the Periplus and Hanno's Voyage**
The Periplus Maris Erythraei (Periplus of the Red Sea) is a primary source from approximately 40-60 AD, making it slightly later than our 100 AD context but describing trade routes and practices established centuries earlier. Hanno the Carthaginian's voyage is recorded in the "Periplus of Hanno," attributed to him and preserved through copies. The voyage's historicity is debated by historians - it may be part genuine expedition record and part later elaboration. But the feasibility is not in question: the voyage is achievable with period-era ships. Confidence on the feasibility: HIGH.

**On West African Rubber**
Landolphia and Funtumia vines are documented in botanical surveys of Central and West Africa from the 1800s onward. The Royal Botanic Gardens has specimens. The fact that West African rubber formed a significant industry in the 1890s until plantation rubber from Malaya became cheaper is historical record. That Romans could have identified and tapped these vines if they had had reason to sail that coast is extrapolation, but the vines themselves and their rubber-yielding properties are documented. Confidence on the existence of the vines and their yield: HIGH. Confidence on a Roman expedition identifying and exploiting them: MEDIUM (contingent on identifying the correct vines and developing extraction techniques in real time).

**On Bengal Saltpetre**
Natural potassium nitrate efflorescence in the Indo-Gangetic Plain is historical fact, documented in sources from the 17th century onward. The saltpetre yield from efflorescence reaches approximately 30-50 kg per hectare per year in optimal conditions. It is unclear whether the phenomenon was understood in 100 AD, but the material exists and can be collected. A Roman merchant arriving in India with technical knowledge of nitre-bed farming could identify the efflorescence and recognize it. Confidence: HIGH on the material's existence; MEDIUM on recognition without laboratory analysis.

**On Scurvy Prevention**
Vitamin C deficiency (ascorbic acid, C6H8O6) is the cause of scurvy. Fresh citrus, fresh greens, and fresh meat contain it. Fermented vegetables and sprouted grains contain it. These are all available to a Roman merchant fleet. The connection between scurvy and diet was not understood until the 18th century, but the practice of shore leave and fresh provisioning was empirically known. Confidence on efficacy: HIGH.

**On the Sternpost Rudder**
Sternpost rudders appear in Chinese junks from the 100s AD onward (Han Dynasty evidence). They did not appear in European ships until the 13th century. The technology is purely mechanical - a pintle hinge, a gudgeon socket, a wooden or bronze blade. Nothing prevents its manufacture in Roman times. Confidence on feasibility: HIGH. Confidence on need: HIGH (it materially improves controllability in heavy seas).

**On Atlantic Crossing Feasibility**
Columbus's 1492 crossing took 33 days. Northern European fishermen crossed the North Atlantic to Iceland and beyond in medieval times with smaller ships. The mathematics of prevailing winds and ocean currents are straightforward - trade winds blow west in the tropical Atlantic. Confidence: HIGH.

**On Transplanting Success**
The Wardian case is documented as developed in the 1830s by Nathaniel Bagshaw Ward, a London doctor who observed a fern fern growing in a sealed glass bottle. The design is intuitive - a sealed box with moist soil and a plant, with condensation recycling humidity. Historical transplants of tea, cloves, cinchona, and rubber are documented in 19th-century colonial records. Confidence on technical feasibility in Roman times: HIGH (the technology is not complex). Confidence on historical precedent: HIGH.

**On Leopold's Congo**
Leopold II of Belgium ruled the Congo Free State from 1885 to 1908 as a personal fiefdom. The death toll from forced labor, disease, and violence is estimated between 1 million and 10 million people depending on the source. The Landolphia vines were the primary product extracted. This is historical fact, extensively documented in contemporary journalism and later historical scholarship. The connection between rubber demand and atrocity is direct. Confidence: HIGH.

The argument rests on documented expeditions (Hanno), documented materials with identified locations (rubber, saltpetre, spices), and feasible technology either period-available or trivial to construct (Wardian cases, sternpost rudders). It claims they are possible and that the cost-benefit analysis has favored conquest historically when trade and transplanting were alternatives.
