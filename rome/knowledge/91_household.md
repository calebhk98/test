# Module 91: Household, Comfort and Daily Life

A technology tree that only covers the path to a transistor is dishonest about what technology is for. The transistor powers the tools; household technologies ARE the tools. The benefit of human life is concentrated here: comfortable sleep, hot water without labour, food kept fresh, light without smoke, clothes washed without damage, a meal cooked without watching it burn. Rome already commands extraordinary comfort through slave labour, hypocaust heating, running water in elite homes, oil lamps, and cosmetics. The next frontier is the same comfort and freedom without the enslaved people - which is why every entry in this module displaced household labour, which is precisely why the elite will resist it most fiercely.

This module documents the `hom_` branch of the tech tree. It builds on the sanitary and heating technologies from Module 53 (Plumbing) and Module 54 (Heating), and the basic chemistry of combustion from Module 30 (Fuels).

### chimney_fireplace - Chimney and Fireplace (*caminus Romanus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A chimney is a vertical flue that draws smoke out of a room into the open air. Instead of a brazier filling a room with acrid smoke that blackens eyes and lungs, you heat a room with a fireplace - an open fire set in a masonry throat that forces smoke upward. This is the largest single improvement to daily comfort available: a warm, well-lit room you can actually see and breathe in, without a servant waving a fan.

**Why you would never guess this.** Romans heat with braziers and hypocausts and understand smoke quite well - they know it rises. But the non-obvious fact is that a *cold* room above the fireplace creates back-pressure. You need the chimney to rise through the roof and extend 1-2 metres above the peak, and you need the fireplace throat to narrow to force a draught. A flue that is simply a hole in the roof will back-smoke in certain winds and pull cold air. The Roman gap is not smoke-awareness but the specific design of the throat and the commitment to rising height.

**Prerequisites.** Masonry (Module 40). Clay brick and lime mortar production (Module 41). An understanding that draft depends on temperature difference and flue area (implicit in water-raising devices like Hero's siphon, Module 55).

**Roman-available inputs.** Fired brick (*tegulae*, from any kiln); lime mortar (*caementa*, from any amphora kiln); clay for sealing gaps. All available anywhere in the empire with a clay bed and lime. Iron ties (*vincla ferrea*) from any metalworker.

**Procedure.**
1. Identify the room to heat and mark the fireplace location on the outside wall, centered if possible. The fireplace sits at floor level (or raised 30 cm for ash removal).
2. Set the fireplace throat: a trapezoidal opening, 60-90 cm wide at the floor, narrowing to 20-30 cm at the lintel, rising 30 cm. Both side walls tilt inward at roughly 15 degrees.
3. Above the throat, lay a flat arch or lintel stone. This lintel must support the weight of brick and is the critical structural element.
4. Stack brick in a tapering column rising vertically through the roof to 1.5-2 metres above the roofline, roughly 30 cm square in cross-section. Use lime mortar in all joints.
5. Seal all roof penetrations with tile, lead, or clay to prevent water entry.
6. Optional: add an iron hook (*uncus*) inside the throat, 30 cm below the lintel, to hang a kettle or pot.

**How you know it worked.** Light a wood fire in the fireplace. Within 30 seconds, smoke should rise and leave the room. No smoke should linger at eye level. If it does, the flue is too narrow or too short - raise the chimney or widen the throat.

**Failure modes.** 
- Smoke backs into the room on certain wind directions - the flue is too short or the roof opening too large; extend the chimney 2-3 metres above the peak. 
- Water leaks around the roof penetration - the seal was not tight; redo with clay and lead patches.
- The chimney draws cold air down in winter when the fire is not lit - this is normal; seal with a wooden panel or cloth. 
- The throat is too wide and smoke escapes around the sides - narrow by building out the walls or hanging a board.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: 200-300 fired bricks (roughly 100 sesterces), 50 kg lime mortar (25 sesterces), iron lintel or three heavy timbers (150-200 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 3 person-days for construction, 1 person-day for materials prep. 50 denarii for a mason. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 300-400 denarii for a basic fireplace and 2-metre chimney. ESTIMATED (basis: Roman labour rates from Pompeii inscriptions, brick prices from Pliny).
- Calendar time: 5-7 days if materials are on hand, longer if bricks must be fired.

**Danger.** Physical: fire in masonry can crack if exposed to high heat repeatedly; use unfired clay to dampen the interior of new chimneys. Structural failure if the throat lintel is undersized or the chimney too tall without supports. Social: installing a chimney proves you have wealth and heat-worthy space; a slave owner disrupting slave labour patterns. A magistrate would find nothing criminal.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Chimneys are archaeologically attested in medieval Europe and China, and the physics of draught is straightforward. Romans understood hypocausts and water siphons, so the concept is not novel. The gap is practical habit, not principle.

### enclosed_stove - Enclosed Stove (*fornax clausa*)

**What it is / why you want it.** An enclosed ceramic stove with a fire chamber below and a cooking surface above, surrounded by thick walls. The heat is contained and radiated slowly, so you burn half the fuel to cook the same amount of food. The cooking surface is hotter and more even than a brazier, and hands and clothes do not catch fire.

**Why you would never guess this.** A brazier sits in the open; a fire is just a fire. But if you wrap it in thick, slow-cooling ceramic, the heat bounces around inside and escapes steadily through the walls. A brazier wastes 80% of the heat to the air; an enclosed stove wastes 40%. Romans have hypocausts that heat via enclosed flues, so the principle exists - but they do not apply it to cooking because they cannot yet make airtight doors.

**Prerequisites.** Pottery (Module 35). Brick and kiln construction (Module 41). Dry-stack door construction from Module 90 (Fire Management).

**Roman-available inputs.** Fired clay in the form of bricks, tiles, or purpose-built ceramic blocks. Iron hinges and a handle for the door (*cardines* and *ansae*, from any blacksmith). Ceramic grates made by the kiln operator.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a rectangular box of brick, 60 cm wide, 40 cm deep, 80 cm tall, using lime mortar in all joints. The walls should be at least 10 cm thick.
2. Leave a door opening on the front, 30 cm wide and 20 cm tall, 15 cm above ground level.
3. Inside, place a grate of iron bars or ceramic, 10 cm above the floor, to hold burning wood or charcoal.
4. Above the grate, inside the chamber, place a second grate or ceramic shelf, 30 cm higher - this is the cooking surface.
5. Cut a flue opening in the back wall, 10 cm wide and 15 cm tall, near the top, to allow smoke to escape. Connect this to a chimney (previous entry) or let it vent directly outside.
6. Make a door of wood and hinges to close off the front. The door should fit snugly but does not need to seal perfectly.
7. Optional: pack sand or ash between the inner wall and an outer casing to improve insulation.

**How you know it worked.** A fire inside burns hotter and longer than an open brazier with the same fuel. The top surface is hot enough to cook or heat water. Feel the outside of the stove - it should be warm but not so hot you cannot hold your hand on it for 3 seconds. If it is cool, you have poor insulation or the flue is drawing too much air.

**Failure modes.** 
- The wood smolders and does not burn fully - the flue is drawing too much cold air, or the wood is wet; check the wood quality and close the door more.
- Cracks appear in the masonry after a few months - thermal stress from uneven heating; use smaller, shorter fires or line the inside with unfired clay.
- Food burns on the bottom and remains raw on top - the cooking surface is not level; rebuild with a slope or add a metal grate.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: 150 fired bricks (75 sesterces), 30 kg mortar (15 sesterces), iron door hinges and handle (30-40 sesterces), ceramic grates (20 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 2 person-days assembly, 1 person-day materials. 30 denarii for a mason. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 200-250 denarii. ESTIMATED (basis: incremental cost over chimney).
- Calendar time: 5-7 days including mortar drying.

**Danger.** Physical: fire risk if the door is left open or the stove overheats. The top surface reaches 150-200 °C (hot enough to ignite cloth instantly) and the sides are hot enough to blister. Structural: if the chimney is not well sealed, carbon monoxide can accumulate. Social: a luxury item; no magistrate objects.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Enclosed stoves with cooking surfaces are attested in China by 3000 BC and in Europe by the medieval period. The physics is straightforward, but the exact performance improvement depends on insulation quality and flue design, both of which require iteration.

### kitchen_range - Kitchen Range (*focaria larga*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A long, narrow enclosed stove with multiple cooking surfaces - perhaps a water boiler on one end, a grill in the center, and an oven on the other. All share the same fire and flue. This is the appliance that makes a kitchen a kitchen: different tasks at different temperatures, all happening at once.

**Why you would never guess this.** A single enclosed stove is better than a brazier; a larger one with multiple zones is just... more of the same, you might think. But the non-obvious advantage is that you can rearrange the fire to heat different surfaces to different temperatures. The oven end (closed) is hotter; the water boiler end (exposed) is cooler. By moving pots around, you use the same fuel for six tasks.

**Prerequisites.** Enclosed stove (previous entry). Metalworking at scale to build iron grates and dampers (Module 20). Brick arches to create the oven chamber (Module 40).

**Roman-available inputs.** Fired brick. Iron bars, grates, and hinges. Ceramic or iron pots. Clay to seal gaps.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a large rectangular box, at least 120 cm long, 60 cm wide, 90 cm tall, again with 10 cm thick brick walls. Divide it into three sections.
2. The left section is the water boiler - leave it open to the top with an iron grate 10 cm above floor level.
3. The middle section is the griddle - place a ceramic or iron grate as the cooking surface, 30 cm above floor level.
4. The right section is the oven - close the top and build a brick arch over it. Leave a small opening (30 cm x 20 cm) at the front for inserting and removing trays.
5. Run a single fire lengthwise through the bottom, with adjustable grates to direct heat to different sections. Use a damper (an iron plate on a hinge) to redirect hot gas to the section that needs it most.
6. Collect smoke in a central flue at the peak and vent it outside.

**How you know it worked.** Place a pot of water on the boiler section; it should simmer gently. Place a skillet on the griddle; it should be hot enough to sear meat in 10 seconds. Place bread in the oven; it should bake evenly and brown in 15-20 minutes. Different sections reach different temperatures using the same fire.

**Failure modes.** 
- The oven and boiler compete for heat - adjust the damper to redistribute flow.
- The entire range is too hot or too cold - the fire is too large or small, or the flue is drawing too hard; add or remove dampers.
- Cracks develop in the arch - thermal cycling; use a gentler, longer fire.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: 400-500 fired bricks (200-250 sesterces), 100 kg mortar (50 sesterces), iron grates and dampers (100-150 sesterces), arch stones or special bricks (50 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 5-7 person-days for construction and fitting. 100 denarii for skilled mason. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 600-800 denarii for a full kitchen range. ESTIMATED (basis: multiple enclosed stoves plus metalwork).
- Calendar time: 10-14 days including mortar curing.

**Danger.** Physical: all the hazards of the enclosed stove, multiplied by scale. An entire kitchen fire if the flue backs up or a pot overturns. Structural: the brick arch is complex and can fail if undersized. Social: a sign of serious wealth and a major kitchen renovation; slaves are being displaced by technology.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Kitchen ranges with multiple zones are attested from the 16th century in Europe. The modular principle is sound, but the exact layout and damper mechanism require field testing to optimize heat flow.

### pressure_cooker - Pressure Cooker (*lebeta clausum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A heavy pot with a sealed lid, designed to trap steam and raise the internal pressure above atmospheric. At high pressure, water boils at 120 °C instead of 100 °C, dramatically shortening cooking time. A tough cut of meat or dried legumes can be rendered edible in one-third the time.

**Why you would never guess this.** You know heat cooks food and steam rises out of a pot; the leap is that *trapping* the steam so it builds pressure cooks even better. This is unintuitive because it seems to violate the principle that food cooks faster in boiling water. But high-pressure steam penetrates the food more effectively. Also unintuitive: the danger. An overpressurized pot is a bomb.

**Prerequisites.** Bronze or iron casting (Modules 13, 20). Thick-walled pot fabrication. An understanding of fire and heat control from Module 30 (Fuels).

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze ingots (expensive, from Cyprus or the Balkans) or iron (cheaper). A tight-fitting cast lid. A valve mechanism - either a weight on a stem, or a simple brass ball held in place by a spring (which Rome does not yet have, so use a weighted plug instead). Beeswax or clay for sealing.

**Procedure.**
1. Cast or forge a thick-walled pot of bronze or iron, at least 3 mm thick, 20-30 cm in diameter, 20 cm tall. The interior must be absolutely smooth - any rough spot will become a stress point.
2. Cast a matching lid, domed slightly to shed moisture, with a 3 cm diameter hole in the center. The rim of the lid should rest on a lip cast into the pot's rim, creating a seal.
3. Build a valve mechanism: drill a 1 cm hole through the center of the lid. Cut a bronze plug, 2 cm in diameter and 5 cm tall, that fits snugly in the hole. Weight the top of the plug so it sits under gentle pressure (roughly 50-100 grams of lead hammered onto the head). When internal pressure exceeds roughly 30-40 kPa (3-4 atmospheres), the plug lifts and steam hisses out, regulating the pressure.
4. To use: fill the pot half full of liquid (water or stock), place food in the liquid, seal the lid with beeswax or wet clay around the rim (not required if the fit is tight), and place the weighted plug in the hole.
5. Heat over a fire. Once steam begins to emerge around the sides, press the plug firmly into place. Do not seal it yet - allow steam to vent for 30 seconds to displace air.
6. Block the vent with the weighted plug. The pressure will rise. When the plug begins to lift and hiss regularly (every 2-3 seconds), you have reached operating pressure.
7. Reduce the fire to maintain a steady, gentle hiss. For dried legumes, cook for 20-30 minutes. For tough meat, 30-45 minutes.

**How you know it worked.** Beans that would take 3 hours in boiling water are soft in 30 minutes. Meat that would boil for 2 hours until tender is soft in 45 minutes. Remove from heat and allow the pressure to drop naturally for 5 minutes before opening.

**Failure modes.** 
- The pot ruptures or explodes - the metal was faulty or too thin, or the valve was blocked; never seal the vent and then forget about the pot.
- The food burns and sticks to the bottom - the pot was empty or too dry; use at least 2 cm of liquid in the bottom.
- The seal leaks and steam escapes - the lid rim is not clean or the beeswax seal is broken; wipe and reseal, or refit the lid.
- The weighted plug does not lift even though you know pressure has built - the hole has swollen from steam, or the plug is stuck from mineral deposits; remove and refit.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: 5-10 kg of bronze or iron for the pot and lid (200-400 sesterces), lead for the valve weight (20 sesterces), beeswax (10 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 2-3 person-days for a metalworker to cast or forge the pot, 1 person-day to build the valve. 50-75 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 300-500 denarii for a working pressure cooker. ESTIMATED (basis: copper and iron prices, skilled metalwork rates).
- Calendar time: 10-14 days for metal work plus cooling.

**Danger.** Physical: this is a pressure vessel. If the valve jams or the pot is overfilled or sealed without venting, it will rupture violently, spraying hot liquid and steam. Everyone in a kitchen where a pressure cooker explodes is badly burned. A rupture can also shatter masonry around the fire. Do not experiment; test any new pot design on a tether or behind a shield. Social: a wealthy person's luxury that also demonstrates engineering ambition. A magistrate would be curious but not disapproving.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Pressure cookers are theoretically straightforward (Boyle's law and specific heat), but the practical design of a safe valve requires precise metalworking. Hero of Alexandria described a pressurized vessel (his wind-powered organ), so the principle is known. The danger lies in the precision required - a sloppy valve is a bomb.

### matches_friction - Friction Matches (*sulphur phosphorus ignite*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A stick of wood tipped with sulfur and a phosphorus-containing paste. Strike it against a rough surface and it ignites, giving you fire in 3 seconds without a flint striker, without tinder, without any delay. This is the single largest time-saving device in the kitchen and workshop.

**Why you would never guess this.** Fire-making is a skill practiced for hundreds of thousands of years - you strike flint and steel, catch the spark in char cloth, blow the ember into a flame, then light kindling. It takes 5-10 minutes if you are fast and your materials are dry. The leap is that certain phosphorous compounds (white phosphorus, phosphorus sesquisulfide) spontaneously ignite at very low temperatures - around 35-40 °C - when exposed to oxygen. A match does not exploit a complex chain; it exploits a chemical edge case that seemed useless until it was concentrated on the tip of a stick.

**Prerequisites.** Phosphorus isolation (Module 26). Sulfur (Module 23). Wood or rush stems. Paste-making skill (any apothecary or dye worker).

**Roman-available inputs.** Phosphorus is not naturally available in the Roman world. It must be manufactured by calcining animal bone in a controlled kiln and then extracting the phosphorus by heating with charcoal - a process discovered in the 1700s, not available to Rome. Sulfur is available from volcanic regions (Sicily, the Bay of Naples) or from mineral springs. Wood and rush stems are universal.

**Procedure.** Phosphorus cannot be obtained in the Roman context. **This technology is anachronistic and cannot be implemented before the 18th century AD.** Skip this entry or mark it as "Post-Roman, not available at 100 AD."

**Confidence: LOW.** Matches require white phosphorus or a phosphorus-sulfur mixture, both of which require industrial calcination of bone and chemical extraction that lies centuries beyond Roman capability.

### argand_lamp - Argand Lamp (*lucerna Argand*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A lamp with a tubular wick instead of a flat or twisted wick. Air flows through the inside of the tube as well as outside it, so the flame burns much hotter and brighter - roughly ten times the light of a simple oil lamp. Oil consumption is higher, but you get so much light that any task done by lamplight becomes practical instead of a strain on the eyes.

**Why you would never guess this.** A wick is a wick; air surrounds it in any lamp. The insight is that if you force air up *through* the center of the wick, you get a complete combustion envelope around the tube, not just on the outside. You also need a glass chimney to force a draught and stabilize the flame. The combination gives light equivalent to a candle ten times larger.

**Prerequisites.** Oil lamp fabrication (inherited from Rome). Precision metalwork to build the tubular wick holder (Module 20). Glass tube or chimney construction (Module 50).

**Roman-available inputs.** Olive oil (any region). Bronze or brass for the wick holder (*lucerna corpusculum*) - shaped like a flat tube, roughly 3 cm in diameter and 2 mm thick. A circular glass tube, 8-10 cm tall, 4-5 cm in diameter, with walls at least 2 mm thick. A ceramic or metal base.

**Procedure.**
1. Create a cylindrical bronze tube, 3 cm in diameter and 2 mm thick, open at both ends, 8 cm tall. Solder or rivet a horizontal pipe to the bottom, 5 mm in diameter, angled upward at 30 degrees - this will sit in the oil reservoir and draw oil up by capillary action.
2. Wrap a flat wick around the tube - it should sit in a slot around the circumference, so it forms a ring 3 mm thick and 8 cm tall. The wick is twisted plant fibers (papyrus, rush, or linen thread).
3. Insert the tube assembly into the oil reservoir (a ceramic or bronze bowl, 10 cm in diameter and 5 cm deep).
4. Set a glass tube around the wick, held 1-2 cm away from the flame by a metal ring or clip. The glass tube should extend 3-4 cm above the top of the wick.
5. Light the wick. The flame will stabilize quickly as the glass forces air flow.

**How you know it worked.** A single Argand lamp produces light equivalent to 8-12 small oil lamps. You can read by it. The flame is tall (6-8 cm) and blue at the bottom, yellow at the top, and steady. The oil lasts about half as long per unit light as a simple wick, but the light output is so much greater that the cost per usable illumination is lower.

**Failure modes.** 
- The wick smolders instead of burning - the tube is clogged with carbon; clean it with a small wire.
- Smoke pours out sideways - the glass chimney is not sealing properly; adjust the ring or replace the glass.
- The flame is dim - the oil is not reaching the wick; check that the feed tube is clear and the oil level is high enough.
- The glass chimney breaks - inevitable after weeks of heating and cooling; keep spare tubes on hand.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: 500-1000 grams of bronze or brass (50-100 sesterces), a glass tube (30-50 sesterces), ceramic or bronze base (20 sesterces), wick material (5 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 1 person-day for a metalworker to fabricate the tube and feed mechanism. 20-30 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 150-200 denarii per lamp. ESTIMATED (basis: metalwork rates and material prices).
- Calendar time: 3-5 days for metalwork.

**Danger.** Physical: the glass gets very hot and can burn exposed skin. The lamp consumes more oil than simple lamps, increasing fire risk if not attended. The glass can break and spray shards. Social: an expensive lighting upgrade; only the wealthy can afford to replace all lamps in a house.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The Argand lamp was invented in 1783 by Swiss chemist Aimé-Argand and is a simple application of fluid dynamics and combustion chemistry. The physics is textbook. The engineering is precision metalwork and glass-blowing, both of which Rome has. The gap is the specific insight about tubular wicks and air flow.

### kerosene_lamp - Kerosene Lamp (*lucerna petroleum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A lamp burning kerosene (petroleum distillate) instead of oil. Kerosene is cheaper than oil, does not go rancid, and produces a hotter, brighter flame with less smoke. It is the lamp of choice for any place that needs reliable, inexpensive light.

**Why you would never guess this.** You would never guess it because kerosene does not exist in the ancient world - it is a product of petroleum refining. Petroleum seeps from the ground in the Caucasus, Mesopotamia, and other regions, but it is used only as a waterproofing agent (bitumen) or as crude oil for ritual purposes. No one in the Roman world distills it into fractions.

**Prerequisites.** Crude petroleum access (from natural seeps in the Caucasus region, or North Africa). Fractional distillation apparatus (Module 27). Lamp technology from the Argand lamp entry, above.

**Roman-available inputs.** Crude petroleum (bitumen or naphtha from seeps in Armenia, Mesopotamia, Lydia). A distillation setup with a heated pot, a condenser, and collection vessels (all available from Module 27: Distillation).

**Procedure.**
1. Collect crude petroleum from surface seeps or through shallow wells (not feasible in 100 AD without drilling technology; assume you are near a natural seep).
2. Heat the crude petroleum in a sealed pot to roughly 150-200 °C (orange-hot iron, or a small candle under a pot until the oil begins to vaporize). The vapors rise into a condenser - either a copper tube cooled by running water, or a series of ceramic bowls with ice or snow packed around them.
3. The condensed liquid that emerges is kerosene (approximate range 150-300 °C boiling point). Collect this fraction separately from the heavy black oil that remains in the pot and the lighter naphtha that evaporates first.
4. Use kerosene in an Argand lamp or a simple wick lamp. The flame is brighter and steadier than oil.

**How you know it worked.** Kerosene burns with a blue-white flame instead of the yellow-orange of oil. The light is brighter and whiter. The smell is sharper and less musty than burned oil.

**Failure modes.** 
- The product smells bad and burns with sooty flames - the distillation temperature was too low or the crude oil was heavily contaminated; redistill or find a cleaner source.
- The lamp leaks - kerosene is less viscous than oil and seeps through small cracks; ensure all seals are tight.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: travel to and collection of crude petroleum (depends on location; assume nearby seeps). Equipment for distillation (Module 27). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 3-5 person-days to distill a large batch of crude petroleum (100-200 liters). 50-100 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 100-150 denarii for a batch yielding 50 liters of kerosene. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 2-3 days for active distillation.

**Danger.** Physical: kerosene is flammable and volatile. Distillation over high heat is a fire risk. The vapor is toxic at high concentrations. Spilled kerosene evaporates quickly and can ignite from a spark. Social: a luxury for the wealthy; no social objection unless the production is visibly wasteful or dangerous.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Kerosene is a simple distillation fraction, and fractional distillation is within Roman chemical capability (Module 27). The gap is access to crude petroleum seeps and the knowledge that this particular fraction is useful for lamps. The physics is sound; the history is that kerosene lamps did not appear until large-scale petroleum drilling began in the 1850s.

### gas_lighting - Gas Lighting (*lucerna vitriolum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Burn an artificial gas (coal gas or oil gas) instead of oil or kerosene. The gas flows through a pipe network to multiple jets, each producing a flame. You get bright, distributed light without the fire hazard and inconvenience of carrying lit lamps to each room. A single burner can light ten rooms.

**Why you would never guess this.** Gas lighting requires: coal or oil distillation to produce gas, a complex pipe network, gas-tight fittings, pressure regulation, and a burner design that works with gas. It is not an incremental improvement on a lamp; it is a complete reimagining of lighting infrastructure.

**Prerequisites.** Fractional distillation (Module 27). Metallurgy for pipes and fittings (Module 20). Sealed pipe construction (Module 53: Plumbing). Understanding of pressure and flow (Hero of Alexandria, Module 55).

**Roman-available inputs.** Coal (from Britain or local deposits) or crude petroleum. Copper or bronze pipe and fittings. Ceramic or iron burner heads.

**Procedure.**
1. Distill coal or petroleum to produce gas. Heat coal or heavy oil in a sealed retort to roughly 400-600 °C, allowing the vapors to escape through a pipe. The gas that emerges is a mixture of methane, hydrogen, and other hydrocarbons - suitable for burning.
2. Cool and filter the gas to remove tar and condensates.
3. Feed the gas into a main pipe running through the building, with branches to individual rooms.
4. Regulate the pressure using a mercury column or a weighted float valve to maintain steady flow (roughly 2-10 cm H2O pressure).
5. At each outlet, install a burner - a small ceramic or iron head with a small orifice (2-3 mm) that ejects the gas. The flame is small but very bright because the gas burns completely.

**How you know it worked.** A single gas burner produces light equivalent to an Argand lamp with 1/10 the apparatus. Light multiple burners in a room and it is as bright as daylight. The flame is steady and blue. Turning a valve shuts off the light instantly.

**Failure modes.** 
- The gas leaks from the pipe joints - the fittings are loose; tighten or refit with thread sealant.
- The burner flame is dim or sooty - the gas is wet or contains tar; improve the cooling and filtration stage.
- The pressure drops over time - a leak somewhere in the system or a depleted gas source; check for bubbles and recharge the retort.
- Explosion if a pipe is overpressurized or a jet is blocked and gas accumulates - maintain pressure regulation and ensure venting.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: a coal gasification retort (large ceramic or iron vessel, 50-100 liters) - 100-200 sesterces; copper piping and fittings for a 10-room house (200-300 meters of pipe) - 400-600 sesterces; burner heads (20-30 sesterces per room). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 10-15 person-days for pipe fitting and pressure regulation testing. 100-150 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 1000-1500 denarii to install gas lighting in a large house. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 3-4 weeks for planning, fabrication, and testing.

**Danger.** Physical: explosive. If a valve fails or a pipe breaks, gas accumulates and a single spark ignites it violently. Any house with gas lighting must have rigorous inspection. Asphyxiation if a sealed room fills with gas (displaces oxygen). Chronic low exposure to gas and tar vapor is poisonous. Social: a statement of wealth and technological ambition. A magistrate would want assurance of safety.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Gas lighting worked well from the 1800s onward, and the underlying chemistry and engineering are sound. The gap is the need for a large infrastructure - the system is economical only if serving many rooms or many buildings. In a single small house, the cost and complexity are high.

### electric_lighting - Electric Lighting (*lucerna galvanus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Generate electricity via a chemical cell (a battery) or a mechanical generator, and burn a carbon arc or incandescent filament to produce light. Electric light is instant, clean, bright, and uses no combustible fuel in the room. It transforms any space where light is used.

**Why you would never guess this.** Electricity seems abstract; light seems elementary. The leap is understanding that you can convert chemical energy or motion into flowing electric charge, and that charge can heat a material to incandescence. This is invisible and counterintuitive and requires a sophisticated grasp of physical principles that Rome does not yet have.

**Prerequisites.** Chemical batteries (Module 28: Electrochemistry). Generators using Faraday's induction principle (Module 76: Electromagnetism). Thin metallic filaments or carbon arcs (Module 20: Advanced Metalwork). An electrical circuit and control switches (Module 76).

**Roman-available inputs.** Zinc and copper for battery cells, dilute acid (vinegar or sulfuric acid from Module 27). Iron and magnets for a generator. Thin platinum or tungsten wire (platinum is unavailable in 100 AD; tungsten is unavailable until the 1700s). Carbon from charcoal.

**Procedure.**
1. Build an electric generator: a copper or iron coil rotating in the field of a powerful lodestone magnet (Module 77: Magnetism). As the coil rotates, it generates alternating current - roughly 2-10 volts and 1-5 amps, depending on the size and speed of rotation. Alternatively, use a battery made of 50-100 Voltaic cells (zinc-copper-acid) in series to generate roughly 100 volts and a few hundred milliamps.
2. Run insulated wire from the generator to the lamp location.
3. At the lamp, use either an incandescent filament (a thin wire of a refractory metal, heated until it glows) or a carbon arc (two carbon rods separated by 2-3 mm, with current jumping the gap in a bright blue-white flame).
4. For an incandescent lamp, suspend a thin tungsten wire inside a glass globe filled with nitrogen or evacuated. When current flows, the wire heats to 2500-3000 °C and glows white.
5. For an arc lamp, mount two carbon rods vertically with adjustable spacing. Current flows through them, heating the gap to 3000 °C and creating an intense light.

**How you know it worked.** The lamp produces instant, steady, bright light without smoke or heat radiation into the room (compared to burning lamps). A single electric lamp is brighter than five Argand lamps. It can be turned on and off with a switch.

**Failure modes.** 
- The generator does not produce enough current - the magnets are weak or the coil is small; use more magnets or more turns of wire.
- The filament burns out - overheating because the wire is too thin or the current is too high; use thicker wire or reduce the circuit voltage.
- The carbon rods burn away - they oxidize in air; run the arc in a sealed, nitrogen-filled chamber.
- The electrical circuit fails - loose connections, broken insulation, or a short circuit; check all joints and re-insulate.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: a generator or 100 battery cells (zinc, copper, acid) - 200-400 sesterces; wire and insulation - 50 sesterces; lamp apparatus - 100-200 sesterces. ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 5-10 person-days to assemble the generator and wire the circuit. 75-150 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 500-900 denarii for a working electric lamp. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 2-3 weeks for construction and testing.

**Danger.** Physical: electric shock from the bare wires. High-voltage circuits (100+ volts) can cause cardiac arrest. The incandescent filament and carbon arc are hot enough to ignite cloth or hair. Arc light produces ultraviolet radiation that burns eyes and skin. Social: a marvel of engineering that demonstrates wealth and knowledge. No magistrate objects, but people are amazed and possibly unsettled.

**Confidence: LOW.** Electric lighting requires materials and knowledge that Rome does not yet have in 100 AD - specifically, knowledge of electromagnetism and the ability to produce a perfect vacuum or a reliable incandescent filament. The chemistry of batteries is understood, but the applications are far ahead of the practical engineering.

### water_trap_toilet - Water Trap Toilet (*latrina aqua trapezium*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A toilet seat over a ceramic or bronze bowl connected to a pipe that feeds it water. Beneath the bowl is an S-bend or U-bend pipe filled with water - the crucial part - that separates the toilet from the sewer and prevents sewer gas and vermin from coming back up through the seat. This is the invention that makes indoor plumbing sanitary instead of a hazard.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome has had flush latrines and running water for centuries. The Cloaca Maxima is famous. But Roman latrines have no water trap - they are open holes connected directly to the sewer. Sewer gas (methane and hydrogen sulfide) seeps back into the room and can accumulate and ignite from an open flame, which has actually caused explosions in Roman latrines. The non-obvious insight is that a simple plug of water - not a complex mechanism, not a valve, just water sitting in a pipe - stops gas from flowing backward. The shape of the pipe (S or U, rather than straight) ensures the water stays trapped.

**Prerequisites.** Plumbing with cast bronze or ceramic pipes (Module 53). Water supply (running water from Module 53 or a hand pump). Waste pipe to a sewer or cesspool. Ceramic or bronze bowl manufacturing. Carpentry for a wooden seat.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze pipe, cast ceramic pipes, or lead pipe. Water from a public fountain or private conduit. A ceramic bowl, roughly 30 cm in diameter. Wood for the seat frame. Clay for sealing joints. Possibly a cistern or pump to provide water for flushing.

**Procedure.**
1. Install a ceramic or bronze bowl at a convenient height (roughly 40 cm above floor level, mounted on a wooden frame for comfort).
2. Beneath the bowl, connect a vertical pipe downward roughly 50 cm. At the bottom, bend the pipe horizontally and run it 30 cm, then bend upward again, creating an S or U shape. All internal dimensions should be at least 5 cm in diameter to avoid clogging.
3. The upward section of the U or S extends to the waste pipe that leads to the sewer or cesspool. Seal all joints with clay and fibrous material, or solder them.
4. Pour water into the bowl until it overflows into the downward pipe. The water fills the U-bend, creating a seal.
5. To use: sit on the seat, use the toilet, and flush by pouring a pitcher of water (roughly 5-10 liters) into the bowl. The water carries waste down the vertical pipe, displaces the water in the U-bend (which is pushed into the sewer), and refills the U-bend, leaving a fresh water seal.
6. If running water is available, install a hand pump or a cistern with a valve that can flood the bowl with a large volume on demand - far better than carrying pitchers.

**How you know it worked.** Sewer smells do not emanate from the toilet. No flies or vermin come up through the seat. After flushing, water remains in the bowl at rest - this indicates the trap is full and sealed.

**Failure modes.** 
- Foul smells rise through the toilet - the water seal was broken (someone poured drain cleaner or boiling water) or the trap was clogged and drained; refill the bowl and check the pipe.
- Waste backs up into the bowl - the waste pipe to the sewer is blocked; clear the pipe or re-route it.
- The pipe is clogged with mineral deposits or solid waste - inadequate water volume during flushing; flush with more water and consider adding a hand pump.
- The seal leaks - the joints were not sealed properly; dig into the wall and reseal with clay and tar, or lead solder if you have a metalworker.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: ceramic or bronze bowl (20-40 sesterces), bronze or ceramic pipe (30-50 sesterces per 5-metre length), wood for the frame (10 sesterces), clay and sealant (5 sesterces). For a 5-metre run to the sewer, roughly 100-150 sesterces. ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 1-2 person-days for a plumber to install the trap and seal the joints. 30-50 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 150-200 denarii for a single trap toilet. ESTIMATED (basis: plumbing labour rates).
- Calendar time: 2-3 days including sealing time.

**Danger.** Physical: sewer gas (methane, hydrogen sulfide) is toxic and explosive. If the trap fails and gas accumulates, a candle or spark ignites it explosively. This has happened in Roman latrines and killed people. However, proper ventilation (a pipe leading sewer gas safely outside) mitigates this. Social: no objection from a magistrate; plumbing is normal and expected in a wealthy home.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The water trap is the foundational principle of modern sanitary plumbing, and it is attested from the 1600s onward in Europe. The principle is simple: a plug of water held in place by gravity. The physics is trivial; the Roman gap is not lack of knowledge but lack of practice and standardization.

### sewer_separation - Sewer Separation (*cloaca separatus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A plumbing system where waste water (from baths, kitchen, washing) is piped separately from human waste (from toilets). Each stream goes to its own treatment or disposal. Waste water can be filtered and reused for gardens or cleaning; human waste requires deeper treatment or a distant cesspool. This prevents the entire system from being contaminated and backs up efficiently.

**Why you would never guess this.** Water is water, and waste is waste; why separate them? But the non-obvious fact is that feces carry disease (cholera, typhoid, dysentery) and contaminate everything they touch, while wash water is nearly benign. If you mix them, the disease spreads to the wash water. If you separate them, you can safely reuse the wash water. Also, treatment and disposal are easier when you handle small volumes of concentrated waste and large volumes of dilute waste separately.

**Prerequisites.** Plumbing infrastructure (Module 53). Water trap toilet (previous entry). Gravel and sand for filtration (Module 54: Water Supply). Slope engineering for gravity-fed drainage.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze or ceramic pipes. Gravel and sand. Clay for sealing. Possibly a lagoon or marsh for final disposal of waste water.

**Procedure.**
1. Design a two-pipe system: one set of pipes for toilet waste, one for bath and kitchen waste.
2. The toilet waste pipe runs directly to a deep cesspool, lined with clay and located at least 50 metres downslope from any water source or well. No mixing with other waste.
3. The wash water pipe runs to a settling tank (a large ceramic or stone basin, roughly 2 metres x 1 metre x 1 metre, filled with gravel). Solids settle; water overflows into a drainage field (gravel trenches running downslope) or into a distant marsh.
4. Ensure the toilet waste pipe has a slope of at least 1% (roughly 10 cm drop per 10 metres) for gravity flow. Same for the wash water pipe.
5. Place inspection ports (removable lids) every 20-30 metres in each pipe for clearing blockages.

**How you know it worked.** Wash water is relatively clear in the settling tank, and the drainage field does not produce foul smells. The toilet waste is confined to the cesspool and does not mix with wash water. After 1-2 years, the cesspool becomes a solid, inert sludge that can be cleaned out or buried.

**Failure modes.** 
- The pipes clog and back up - solids accumulate; clear the pipes or increase the water flow (flush more frequently).
- The cesspool overflows - it fills faster than expected (possibly due to heavy rain or additional usage); dig it deeper or add additional cesspools.
- Foul smells emanate from the wash water drainage - the settling tank is too small and solids are reaching the drainage field; clean the tank and add more gravel.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: pipe (bronze or ceramic) for roughly 50-100 metres total (200-400 sesterces), gravel for the settling tank and drainage field (50 sesterces), clay for sealing (10 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 5-10 person-days for a plumber to design and install the system. 100-150 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 400-600 denarii for a medium-sized house. ESTIMATED (basis: multiple plumbing runs).
- Calendar time: 2-3 weeks for planning and installation.

**Danger.** Physical: sewer gases, especially from the cesspool. Ventilation is critical - run a pipe from the cesspool to the roof to vent methane safely. Social: a luxury item that indicates wealth and engineering knowledge. No objection from a magistrate.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Sewer separation is a well-established sanitary principle, and Rome already has the infrastructure (Cloaca Maxima) to suggest the concept. The implementation is straightforward plumbing.

### hot_water_bath - Hot Water Bath (*balneum aqua calidus**)

**What it is / why you want it.** Running hot water supplied on demand to a bath or washing station in a private home, without servants constantly heating and carrying water. A cistern is heated by the kitchen range or a dedicated fire, and pipes deliver it to the bath. This transforms bathing from a laborious ritual to a quick pleasure.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome has had hot baths for centuries - the public baths are famous. But they are communal and require a heating plant. The insight is that you can heat a single cistern in a private home and pipe the hot water just a short distance, losing very little heat. A servant fills the cistern once; hot water is then available all day without further effort.

**Prerequisites.** Plumbing (Module 53). A heat source: the kitchen range (previous entry) or a dedicated stove (Module 31: Heating). Copper or bronze pipes. A cistern or tank for water storage. Thermostatic or manual mixing valve to prevent scalding.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze or copper pipes. A ceramic or lead-lined wood tank, 50-100 litres capacity. Water from a well or public fountain. Heat from a nearby stove.

**Procedure.**
1. Position a large cistern (roughly 1 metre x 1 metre x 0.6 metres, holding 500-600 litres) in the attic or on the roof of a building. This height is crucial - it creates pressure for gravity-fed water.
2. If using a kitchen range, pass the hot water outlet of the range through the base of the cistern, or wrap pipes around the exterior of the cistern to absorb heat from the stove.
3. Alternatively, place a dedicated fire beneath the cistern to heat it directly.
4. Run copper pipes from the bottom of the cistern downward through the building, with branches to the bath, washing stations, and kitchen.
5. At the bath, install a mixing valve - either a simple T-pipe with two hand valves (one for hot, one for cold, blend manually) or a thermostat valve that balances pressure and temperature automatically (requires precision metalwork).
6. Include a drain valve at the lowest point of the system to empty the pipes in winter.

**How you know it worked.** Hot water flows from the tap within 5-10 seconds. The temperature is controllable from cold to roughly 50-60 °C (too hot to hold your hand for more than a few seconds). The flow rate is steady.

**Failure modes.** 
- No hot water flows - the cistern is empty or the heating element is not hot enough; fill the cistern and increase the fire.
- The water is scalding and burns skin - the mixing valve is not working; adjust the valve or use the cold water tap more.
- The pipes freeze in winter - uninsulated pipes burst; drain them or wrap them in straw and cloth.
- Mineral deposits clog the pipes - hard water contains calcium and magnesium; descale by circulating vinegar through the system or replace the pipes.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: copper pipes (100-150 sesterces for a full installation), a large cistern (50-100 sesterces), mixing valve (30-50 sesterces), installation fittings (20 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 2-3 person-days for a plumber to connect the heating source and pipes. 50-75 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 300-450 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 3-5 days for installation and testing.

**Danger.** Physical: scalding from hot water (70 °C causes third-degree burns in seconds). Burns also from contact with hot pipes or the cistern exterior. Social: a luxury; wealthy households have hot baths; no objection.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Hot water systems are simple thermodynamics - heat the water, gravity feeds it down, mix with cold water to control temperature. This was common in Roman baths and can be adapted to private homes.

### central_heating - Central Heating (*hypocaustum centrale*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A single heat source (a furnace or stove) supplies warm air or hot water through ducts or pipes to every room in a building. Instead of a brazier or fireplace in each room, occupants enjoy steady, even warmth throughout. Fuel consumption is lower because a single large fire is more efficient than many small ones.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome has hypocausts - a heat source below the floor sends hot air through cavities beneath the pavement. The non-obvious leap is that the same principle can be extended throughout an entire building, with hot air or water reaching every room, with control valves in each room to adjust the flow. The return air or water flows back to the furnace to be reheated. It is a closed-loop system, not just heating one space.

**Prerequisites.** Hypocaust construction (Module 54). Large furnace or stove (Module 31). Ductwork in ceramic, bronze, or wood (Module 40). Dampers or valves to control flow (Module 20). Sealing and insulation (Module 40).

**Roman-available inputs.** Brick and tile for the furnace and ducts. Bronze or bronze-lined wood for supply and return pipes. A large furnace or stove. Dampers of bronze or iron.

**Procedure.**
1. Locate a large furnace in the basement or a central location (roughly 2 metres x 2 metres x 2 metres, with a fire chamber, a heat exchanger, and ash collection). Build it from brick with an iron door and grate.
2. From the furnace, run a main hot-air or hot-water duct upward through the center of the building, sized roughly 30 cm square in cross-section.
3. Branch this main duct into smaller ducts running to each room, with a damper or valve in each branch.
4. In each room, place a heat outlet (a simple opening or a perforated bronze grate) at floor level. Hot air or steam rises and warms the room.
5. Install return ducts (running cold air or cooled water back to the furnace) in each room, typically near the ceiling. These ducts are insulated to minimize heat loss.
6. At the furnace, the return air or water is reheated and the cycle repeats.

**How you know it worked.** Every room warms evenly and steadily. Cold spots (like an exterior wall) are mild instead of frigid. The entire building reaches a comfortable temperature within 2-3 hours of starting the furnace fire. Opening a damper increases flow to that room; closing it (partially) reduces it.

**Failure modes.** 
- Some rooms stay cold - the dampers are not opening properly, or the furnace is too small; check the dampers and increase the fire.
- The furnace room becomes excessively hot - the return ducts are blocked; clear them and improve insulation.
- Hot air leaks from the ducts (smell of smoke in unexpected places) - the ducts are cracked or poorly sealed; locate and seal the cracks.
- The return air does not flow - back-pressure is too high; enlarge the return ducts or reduce the furnace air pressure.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: a large brick furnace (200-300 sesterces), ductwork for a 10-room house (roughly 200 metres of duct, 200-300 sesterces), dampers (50-100 sesterces), sealing and insulation (50 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 10-15 person-days for a mason to build the furnace and install ducts. 150-200 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 800-1000 denarii for a complete central heating system. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 4-6 weeks for planning and construction.

**Danger.** Physical: carbon monoxide if the furnace is not properly vented. Fire if ducts pass near flammable materials. Burns from touching hot ducts. Structural: the weight of a large furnace requires strong foundation. Social: a major capital investment and a statement of wealth. Magistrates would be curious about the engineering.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Central heating using hot air ducts is attested from the medieval period. The principle is sound, but the design details (duct sizing, damper placement, furnace efficiency) require iteration and testing. Roman hypocausts prove the feasibility; the gap is the integrated, multi-room design.

### washing_machine - Washing Machine (*machina lavature*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A wooden drum or chamber with paddles inside, rotated by a hand crank or a water wheel, that agitates clothes in soapy water. This mimics the motion of hand-scrubbing but applies it to many clothes at once with a fraction of the labour.

**Why you would never guess this.** Washing clothes by hand in a tub or stream is the standard method. The leap is that agitation is the key variable - if you move the clothes through the soapy water vigorously enough, the motion does the scrubbing work. A machine provides this motion more efficiently than a person with forearms in the tub.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry (Module 10). Barrel or wooden drum construction (Module 40). A rotating mechanism (hand crank or water wheel). Gears if using a water wheel to reduce speed (Module 52: Mechanical Power).

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the drum (oak or elm, from timber yards). Iron or bronze axles, bearings, and a hand crank. Soap (Module 52: Soap Production, or simply animal fat and wood ash lye).

**Procedure.**
1. Build a wooden drum 60 cm in diameter, 60 cm long, with wooden slats roughly 2 cm x 10 cm forming the sides. Seal the joints with pitch or clay so the drum is watertight.
2. Mount the drum horizontally on iron axles set into wooden supports.
3. Inside the drum, attach 4-6 wooden paddles (roughly 30 cm x 10 cm each) to the interior wall, evenly spaced around the circumference and along the length. The paddles are fixed to a central shaft that extends through the drum to the exterior.
4. At one end of the shaft, attach a hand crank (a 50 cm arm with a handle). One rotation of the crank turns the drum one full rotation.
5. Alternatively, connect the shaft to a water wheel (Module 52) so the drum rotates continuously.

**How to use:**
1. Fill the drum half-full of water mixed with soap (a few handfuls of soap shavings per 100 litres of water).
2. Load wet clothes loosely into the drum - no more than 30-40 kg.
3. Rotate the crank at a steady pace (roughly 20-30 rotations per minute) for 10-15 minutes. The paddles agitate the clothes continuously.
4. Drain the soapy water.
5. Refill with clean water and rotate again for 5 minutes to rinse.
6. Drain thoroughly and wring out the clothes.

**How you know it worked.** Clothes emerge clean, with less manual scrubbing than hand-washing. A drum load can be washed faster than by hand (a person scrubbing takes 30-45 minutes; the machine does it in 15). The key is that clothes emerge with fewer wrinkles and less fiber damage than from rough hand-wringing.

**Failure modes.** 
- Clothes get tangled around the axle - they are overloaded or the paddles are catching; reduce the load and check the paddle clearance.
- The drum leaks - the pitch seal has failed; drain and reseal with fresh pitch.
- The crank is stiff to turn - the axles are stiff or rusted; oil them and ensure smooth rotation.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: wood for the drum and frame (50 sesterces), iron axles and crank (50-75 sesterces), pitch or clay for sealing (5 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 2-3 person-days for a carpenter to build and assemble the drum. 40-60 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 150-200 denarii for a hand-crank machine. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 4-6 days for construction and seasoning of wood.

**Danger.** Physical: fingers caught in the crank or paddles. Scalding if the water is hot. Social: a labour-saving device that displaces washerwomen - a point of social friction if implemented at scale.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Barrel washers with paddles are attested from the 1700s onward. The principle is simple mechanics, but the design details (paddle shape, drum size, rotation speed) affect efficiency and fabric damage.

### mangle_wringer - Mangle and Wringer (*pressa pannorum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Two heavy wooden or leather rollers mounted parallel, with a gap of 3-5 mm between them. Clothes are fed through the gap, and the rollers squeeze out water. This is the fastest, least labour-intensive way to extract water from wet fabric.

**Why you would never guess this.** Wringing by hand twisting is standard. The leap is that pressure applied uniformly across the width of the fabric extracts more water than twisting can. A hand-operated mangle squeezes a bed sheet dry in 10 seconds; hand-wringing takes 2-3 minutes and tires the arms.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry (Module 10). Woodworking to create smooth, heavy rollers (Module 40). Metal axles and bearings (Module 20). A screw mechanism or lever arm for pressure (Module 50: Screws and Levers).

**Roman-available inputs.** Hard wood (oak or elm) for the rollers. Iron axles and bearings. A screw (Archimedean screw, Module 50) or a lever system to apply downward pressure.

**Procedure.**
1. Create two heavy wooden rollers, each 60 cm long and 10 cm in diameter, made from seasoned oak or elm. The surface must be very smooth (finish with fine sand).
2. Mount the rollers horizontally, parallel to each other, 10 cm apart, on iron axles set into wooden supports.
3. Create a pressure mechanism: a large screw thread (roughly 2 cm pitch) mounted above the rollers, with a handle. Turning the screw lowers the upper roller against the lower one, adjusting the gap from 5 mm (tight) to 1 cm (loose).
4. Alternatively, use a lever arm and weights to apply constant pressure.
5. Build a trough or deck beneath the rollers to catch the water.

**How to use:**
1. Loosen the pressure screw so the gap is roughly 1 cm.
2. Feed one end of a wet garment (sheet, shirt, etc.) between the rollers.
3. Tighten the screw moderately to bring the rollers closer, reducing the gap to 3-5 mm. The fabric is pinched between them.
4. Gradually feed the garment through by hand, keeping it centered. The rollers squeeze out water as it passes.
5. As the fabric emerges, collect it on the far side. Loosen the screw to avoid pinching fingers.

**How you know it worked.** A bed sheet that drips water when hand-wrung emerges nearly dry from the mangle - dry enough to hang and air-dry rather than requiring another wringing.

**Failure modes.** 
- The rollers squeak or stick - they are not aligned or are dirty; realign and clean with a damp cloth.
- Fabric gets caught or torn - the gap is too narrow or the fabric is caught on a splinter; widen the gap and sand the rollers.
- The screw is difficult to turn - the rollers are applying too much pressure; adjust the design or use a longer handle for mechanical advantage.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: wood for the rollers (30-50 sesterces), iron axles and a large screw (50-75 sesterces), wooden frame and screw handle (30 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 2-3 person-days for a carpenter and metalworker. 50-75 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 150-250 denarii for a functional mangle. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 4-6 days.

**Danger.** Physical: fingers caught between rollers can be crushed. Clothing can be torn if the gap is too tight. Social: displaces labour (wringing by hand).

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Mangles with pressure screws are attested from medieval times. The engineering is straightforward: smooth rollers, adequate pressure, and mechanical advantage.

### dishwasher - Dishwasher (*machina lavare vasa*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine that sprays hot water and soap onto dishes loaded in a chamber, then drains the soapy water and sprays clean water as a rinse. All the scrubbing work is done by the spray pressure and heat, not by hand. A load of dishes is cleaned in 10 minutes instead of 30.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hot water and soap are the active ingredients in dishwashing; the scrubbing motion is labour, not cleanliness. The leap is that pressurized spray provides enough agitation to substitute for hand-scrubbing. This is especially powerful with hot water because heat loosens grease.

**Prerequisites.** Pressurised hot water system (Module 31: Central Heating or hot water bath, above). A pump to deliver pressure (Module 55: Pumps). A rotating spray head or nozzles. Control valves for water and drain (Module 53: Plumbing). A heating source.

**Roman-available inputs.** A ceramic or lead-lined wooden chamber, 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm. Bronze or copper pipes and fittings. A hand pump or water wheel pump. A drain to a sewer or waste water system. Hot water from a nearby stove or dedicated heater.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a watertight chamber from ceramic tiles or wood lined with lead, roughly 60 cm on each side and 60 cm tall. Mount it on legs 50 cm above the floor for access to the drain.
2. In the base, install a drain valve and a strainer to catch food debris.
3. Inside the chamber, mount a rotating spray arm on a central pivot, with nozzles pointing outward and upward at roughly 45 degrees. The nozzles are small holes (2-3 mm diameter) drilled in a bronze tube.
4. Connect the spray arm to a hot water inlet under pressure (from a pump, roughly 100-200 kPa). As water flows through the arm and emerges from the nozzles, the reaction force causes the arm to rotate.
5. Connect the drain to a waste water pipe that runs to a sewer or external disposal point.
6. Install a valve to control water flow and a second valve for the rinse cycle.

**How to use:**
1. Load dirty dishes vertically into the chamber, leaning them against the walls or on small racks.
2. Close the door or lid.
3. Fill the chamber with hot water (roughly 50 °C) and add soap (a handful of soap shavings per 100 litres).
4. Open the water supply valve, which pressurizes the spray arm through a hand pump or a water wheel pump, causing it to rotate rapidly, spraying all surfaces.
5. After 5-10 minutes, close the valve and open the drain to remove the soapy water.
6. Refill with clean water and spray again for 3-5 minutes to rinse.
7. Drain fully and remove the dishes.

**How you know it worked.** Dishes emerge clean and dry-able, without hand scrubbing. Grease is removed from plates. Dried food on bowls is loosened.

**Failure modes.** 
- The spray arm does not rotate - the pressure is insufficient; increase the pump flow or reduce the nozzle size.
- Water leaks from the chamber - the seals or welds failed; reseal or repair.
- Dishes are not fully clean - the water is not hot enough, the soap concentration is too low, or the spray time is too short; increase temperature, soap, or time.
- The drain backs up - food debris has accumulated; clean the strainer more frequently.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: a ceramic or wood chamber (100 sesterces), bronze pipes and fittings (50 sesterces), pump (30-50 sesterces), spray arm and nozzles (20 sesterces), drain pipe (20 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 3-4 person-days for a plumber and carpenter. 75-100 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 350-450 denarii for a functional dishwasher. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 5-7 days for construction and testing.

**Danger.** Physical: scalding from hot water spray. High pressure (100+ kPa) can hurt if a nozzle is aimed at skin. Social: a luxury that displaces kitchen slaves or servants.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Mechanical dishwashers with spray arms are attested from the late 1800s. The principle is straightforward: pressure drives spray, which agitates. The engineering details (nozzle design, water temperature, soap chemistry) require refinement.

### electric_fan - Electric Fan (*flabellum electricus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A small electric motor with a propeller, mounted so it drives a flow of air across a room. In a Mediterranean summer, this creates an immediate sense of cooling and comfort without any fuel consumption in the room.

**Why you would never guess this.** A fan seems pointless - it just blows air, and air is free. But the non-obvious fact is that moving air across skin speeds evaporative cooling dramatically. Even air at 35 °C feels tolerable when moving at 2-3 metres per second. Moreover, a small electric motor is extremely cheap to run if electricity is available. Rome would not have invented the electric fan because electricity does not exist in Rome, but a Roman who experienced one would value it above almost any other comfort device.

**Prerequisites.** Electric motor (Module 76: Electromagnetism). A lightweight propeller (Module 50: Materials). A battery or electrical generator (Module 28: Electrochemistry or Module 76: Electromagnetism).

**Roman-available inputs.** An electric motor, small enough to hold in one hand (roughly 100-200 watts). A propeller, 30-40 cm in diameter, made of thin wood or woven cloth. A small battery or access to a generator. Electrical wiring.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a small electric motor: a copper coil rotating in a magnetic field (lodestone or a permanent magnet, Module 77). As the coil rotates at 500-1000 RPM, it produces a small amount of torque (roughly 0.1 N-m).
2. Attach a propeller (a wooden or cloth fan blade, 30-40 cm in diameter, tilted at 15-20 degrees) to the motor shaft. As the motor rotates, the propeller pushes air.
3. Mount the motor on a pedestal or wall bracket.

### refrigerator_domestic - Domestic Refrigerator (*glaciarium domesticum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** An insulated box containing a cooling mechanism (an evaporative chamber, or later, a compressed-gas cycle) that keeps food cold without ice or salt. Food stays fresh for days instead of hours. In Mediterranean climates where ice is rare, this is revolutionary.

**Why you would never guess this.** Cooling is the opposite of heating, and heating requires fuel and flame. But a refrigerator can cool either by allowing a liquid to evaporate (which absorbs heat from surroundings), or by compressing a gas (which heats it), then expanding it (which cools it). Neither requires burning fuel in the box.

**Prerequisites.** Thermodynamics and phase change (Module 29: Heat and Thermodynamics). For evaporative cooling: a fluid (water or diethyl ether) that evaporates readily. For compression cycle: a compressor (a pump mechanism), a condenser, and an expansion valve (Module 50). Insulation (Module 54).

**Roman-available inputs.** For simple evaporative cooling: a cloth or porous pot, water, and an insulated outer box. For compression cycle: a pump (motor-driven), copper tubing, and a volatile liquid (ammonia, which Rome could produce from urine). Insulation materials: cork, sawdust, or straw.

**Procedure - Evaporative Cooling (simplest):**
1. Build an insulated box, 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm, with 10 cm of cork or sawdust between inner and outer wooden walls.
2. Inside, place a porous pot (clay, partially glazed so it absorbs water slowly).
3. Fill the pot with water daily. As water seeps through the clay and evaporates on the outside, it removes heat from the interior.
4. The interior air temperature drops 5-15 °C below ambient, depending on humidity.

**Procedure - Compression Cycle (more complex):**
1. Build a compressor: a pump driven by a hand crank or motor, with two check valves (one-way valves). The pump compresses ammonia gas (or another volatile liquid) from 1 atmosphere to 5-10 atmospheres.
2. The high-pressure gas flows through copper tubing to a condenser - a section of tubing cooled by ice, running water, or air circulation. The gas liquefies.
3. The liquid ammonia flows through an expansion valve (a small orifice that drops the pressure). As pressure drops, the liquid evaporates rapidly, absorbing heat.
4. The evaporator is the interior of the refrigerator - the evaporating ammonia cools the space.
5. The ammonia vapor is drawn back to the compressor, completing the cycle.

**How you know it worked.** Food placed in the box stays cold for days. Milk does not sour, butter does not melt, meat does not spoil. The interior temperature is roughly 5-10 °C when the outside is 25-35 °C.

**Failure modes.** 
- Evaporative cooling: humidity is too high (tropical coastal areas); the cooling effect is reduced; switch to compression cycle or use a larger evaporator surface.
- Compression cycle: ammonia leaks (toxic smell); reseal the tubing and check for cracks.
- The interior does not get cold enough - the heat load is too high or the cooling power is insufficient; insulate better or run the compressor faster.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials for evaporative: insulated box (50 sesterces), porous pot (10 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Materials for compression: pump (50 sesterces), copper tubing (40 sesterces), ammonia (20 sesterces), expansion valve (15 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 1-2 person-days for carpentry and assembly. 30-50 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: 60-130 denarii for evaporative cooling, 150-200 denarii for compression cycle. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 3-5 days.

**Danger.** Physical: ammonia is toxic. A leak in a compression cycle refrigerator is dangerous - ammonia vapor causes respiratory damage. Evaporative cooling has no chemical hazard but requires water management. Social: a luxury that preserves food without labour (servants no longer need to run to the ice house daily).

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Evaporative cooling is ancient (used in Egypt and Persia). Compression cycle cooling is more complex and requires ammonia synthesis and precise valve control, but the principle is understood (Carnot cycle). The Roman gap is not fundamental physics but practical engineering - building a reliable, leak-proof system.

### pencil - Pencil (*graphium lignum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A stick of graphite encased in wood, sharpened to a point, used for writing or drawing. It requires no ink, does not smudge easily, and can be erased with a rubber (if available).

**Why you would never guess this.** Graphite is a form of carbon, and pure graphite deposits are rare. The leap is recognizing that graphite is usable as a writing medium without ink or binding agents. The Roman world has access to some graphite (from small deposits in the Alps and Cornwall), but it is used only for metallurgical purposes until someone thinks to write with it.

**Procedure.**
1. Mine or obtain graphite ore. Crush it and wash it to remove impurities. Allow it to dry.
2. Sort the graphite by size: finer particles are better for pencils.
3. Compress the dried graphite powder into a solid graphite rod, roughly 5 mm in diameter, using heat and pressure (or a hand press). This is difficult without industrial equipment; a simpler method is to use natural graphite deposits where graphite already occurs in solid form.

### fountain_pen - Fountain Pen (*calamum aquarium*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A pen with an internal reservoir of ink and a mechanism to deliver it steadily to the tip as you write. This eliminates the need to dip the pen in an inkwell between words, making writing faster and less messy.

**Why you would never guess this.** A quill dipped in ink is standard for centuries. The leap is that you can fill the quill with ink and have it flow out slowly enough to write for minutes or hours without redipping. This requires a mechanism to control the flow - a capillary channel or a valve.

**Prerequisites.** Metalworking for a fine tube or nib (Module 20). A method to create a capillary channel (Module 50: Materials). Ink fabrication (Module 27: Organic Chemistry). An elastic member or valve to regulate flow (Module 50: Mechanisms).

**Roman-available inputs.** A thin bronze or iron tube (1-2 mm inner diameter), perhaps 5 cm long, sealed at one end. A quill feather (goose, raven, or duck). A small slit at the writing end to create a split nib. An elastic band or spring (Module 20). A reservoir (a bamboo tube or a bronze bulb, 2-3 cm long) to hold ink.

**Procedure.**
1. Create a nib by flattening the end of a bronze tube to a width of 2 mm, then splitting the flat end lengthwise with a fine saw, creating a gap of 0.5 mm.
2. Wrap the tube tightly with fine linen thread to create a capillary channel - the thread holds ink by surface tension and releases it slowly to the split nib.
3. Attach a small reservoir (a bronze bulb with a small opening) behind the nib, connected by a thin bronze or wooden tube. The reservoir holds 1-2 ml of ink.
4. Create a valve or wick to regulate flow: a small twist of linen that sits in the tube and allows ink to escape only when the nib is moving (the capillary action pulls ink out as the paper pulls ink from the nib).
5. Alternatively, use an elastic band or a small spring to adjust the pressure in the reservoir, moderating ink flow.

**How to use:**
1. Fill the reservoir with ink (a small bottle with a pipette or dropper).
2. Press the nib against paper and draw. The capillary channel releases ink as the nib writes.
3. A single filling of the reservoir allows writing for 10-30 minutes, depending on the size of the reservoir and the writing speed.
4. Refill by removing a small plug and adding ink.

**How you know it worked.** Ink flows steadily from the nib as you write. The flow is even - not too fast (bleeding through the paper) and not too slow (running dry). The nib does not clog as long as the reservoir remains filled.

**Failure modes.** 
- Ink does not flow - the wick is too tight or the capillary channel is dry; flex the wick or refill the reservoir.
- Ink flows too fast and the writing is sloppy - the gap in the nib is too wide or the capillary channel is too loose; adjust the nib split or wrap the thread more tightly.
- The nib dries out when not in use - the capillary channel is not sealed properly; store the pen with the cap on and the reservoir sealed.
- Ink leaks from the reservoir - a crack in the bronze or the valve is faulty; check for cracks and adjust the valve.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: bronze tubes and bulb (30-40 sesterces), fine linen thread (5 sesterces), spring or elastic band (5 sesterces), quill or wood for handle (5 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 3-4 hours for metalworking and assembly. 30 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 75-85 denarii per pen. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 2-3 days for metalworking.

**Danger.** Physical: minimal. Ink can stain fabric. Social: a luxury item suggesting literary activity.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Fountain pens were invented in the 1600s-1700s. The principle is sound - capillary channels and gravity can regulate ink flow. The Roman gap is not technical but cultural - Rome has the skill for metalworking but not the market demand for fountain pens.

### perfume_attar - Attar of Roses (*perfumus rosae oil*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Rose oil, extracted from rose petals by steam distillation or solvent extraction. It is vastly more concentrated than the rose water or rosewater enfleurage that Rome already produces. One litre of attar is worth more than its weight in silver.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rose-scented oils are made by steeping petals in oil (enfleurage) or distilling rose water. Attar is different - it is the essential oil extracted by distillation alone, without dilution in water or fats. Rome does not yet produce this because the process requires precise temperature control and a specially designed still.

**Prerequisites.** Fractional distillation (Module 27). A small copper still with a tall reflux column. Temperature control (ice or running water for cooling). Carrier alcohol (ethanol, from Module 27, though wine can substitute).

**Roman-available inputs.** Rose petals (from garden cultivation). A small still (copper, 5-10 litre capacity). Running water for cooling. Wine or grain alcohol (Module 27: Distillation).

**Procedure.**
1. Harvest fresh rose petals in the early morning when the scent is strongest.
2. Fill a copper still halfway with rose petals, pressing them down gently.
3. Add water to just above the level of the petals (roughly 5 litres).
4. Heat the still to gentle boiling (roughly 80-100 °C) over a charcoal fire.
5. Steam rises from the petals, carrying the aromatic oils. The steam enters a condenser (a coil of copper tubing cooled by running water), where it cools and condenses into a liquid.
6. The condensed liquid is a mixture of rose water and a small amount of oil - the oil floats on top of the water.
7. Collect the liquid in a clean ceramic pot and allow it to sit for 24 hours. The oil separates and floats on the surface.
8. Carefully pour or skim off the oil layer into a separate, dark glass bottle.
9. Optional: redistill the oil alone (without water) to concentrate it further, or dissolve it in grain alcohol (ethanol) to make perfume.

**Yield:** Roughly 3000-4000 kg of rose petals yields 1 kg of attar oil - a striking inefficiency that is also the source of attar's value.

**How you know it worked.** The collected oil has an intense rose scent. A single drop, when diluted in 100 times its volume of alcohol, produces a noticeable fragrance. The oil is golden or pale pink in colour.

**Failure modes.** 
- No oil appears, only rose water - the petals were stale or the temperature was too low; use fresh petals and increase the heat slightly.
- The oil smells musty or burnt - the temperature was too high; reduce the heat and use more running water to cool the condenser.
- The still leaks - the seals or solder have cracked; repair or resolder.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: rose petals (3000-4000 kg for 1 kg of attar, which would cost a fortune if purchased; ESTIMATED as 500-1000 sesterces per kg of attar based on labour and fuel).
- Labour: 10+ person-days to harvest petals and distil. 100-200 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 700-1500 denarii per kilogram of attar. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 2-4 weeks depending on petal availability and batch size.

**Danger.** Physical: burn hazard from boiling water and steam. Alcohol vapours are flammable. Social: attar is a luxury product. Selling attar is profitable; it is one of the best revenue sources for a new settlement in the early years.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Attar production by steam distillation is well-established historically (attested in Persia by the 10th century). The chemistry is trivial; the engineering is straightforward. Rome has all the necessary equipment (stills from Module 27). The gap is market demand and cultural valuation of rose oil.

### mirror_silvered - Silvered Mirror (*speculum argentum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A piece of polished metal or glass coated with a thin layer of metallic silver, which reflects light and images. This creates a clear, undistorted reflection far superior to polished bronze or obsidian mirrors.

**Why you would never guess this.** Polished bronze mirrors exist in Rome - they are reflective but tarnish easily and are heavy. The insight is that a thin coating of silver, bonded to glass or a smooth backing, provides superior reflectivity and does not tarnish as quickly (though silver still oxidizes eventually). Roman technology can produce thin silver coatings (silverware exists), so the only gap is applying this technique to mirrors.

**Prerequisites.** Glass-making (Module 50). Silver working (Module 20). A method to bond or deposit silver onto glass - either by mechanical adhesion, or by chemical reduction (Module 26: Electrochemistry or Module 27: Organic Chemistry).

**Roman-available inputs.** High-quality glass, cleared of impurities (from Module 50). Silver sheet or wire (from any silversmith). Quicksilver (mercury, from Spain or mines - scarce but available). An adhesive or backing.

**Procedure - Mechanical Adhesion (simpler):**
1. Polish a piece of high-quality glass until it is perfectly clear and smooth.
2. Create a thin sheet of silver by hammering silver wire flat and burnishing it smooth. The sheet should be 0.5-1 mm thick.
3. If using mercury: spread a thin layer of mercury on the glass, then press the silver sheet onto it. The mercury acts as an adhesive and allows the silver to conform to the glass surface.
4. Clamp the assembly and allow the mercury to evaporate (roughly 24 hours).
5. Seal the back of the mirror with a wooden or bronze frame to protect the silver.

**Procedure - Chemical Reduction (more complex):**
1. Coat the glass with a thin layer of a copper or zinc alloy (by electrodeposition if you have a battery, or by hot-dipping).
2. Dissolve silver nitrate in water.
3. Reduce the silver nitrate to metallic silver using a reducing agent (glucose or formaldehyde, if available; otherwise a reducing metal like zinc).
4. The metallic silver deposits as a thin layer on the glass.
5. Dry and seal.

**How you know it worked.** You can see a clear, undistorted reflection of your face in the mirror. The reflection is bright (80%+ reflectivity) compared to polished bronze (60-70%).

**Failure modes.** 
- The silver coating flakes off - the adhesion was poor; repolish the glass and try again with a stronger adhesive (pitch or varnish mixed with mercury).
- The mirror darkens over time - silver is oxidizing; clean with a gentle cloth and mild acid (vinegar), then re-seal.
- The glass breaks - it is too thin or the edge was chipped; use thicker glass or reinforce the edge with a metal frame.

**Cost & labour.** 
- Materials: high-quality glass plate (50-100 sesterces), silver sheet (100-200 sesterces), mercury (30 sesterces), adhesive and backing (20 sesterces). ESTIMATED.
- Labour: 4-6 hours for glass polishing and silver application. 30-50 denarii. ESTIMATED.
- Total: roughly 250-400 denarii per mirror. ESTIMATED.
- Calendar time: 2-3 days including drying time.

**Danger.** Physical: mercury is toxic. Inhaling mercury vapours causes nervous system damage. Avoid prolonged exposure and ensure good ventilation. Broken glass is sharp. Social: a luxury suggesting wealth and personal grooming.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Silvered mirrors were invented around 1500 AD in Venice, using a tin-mercury amalgam. The chemistry of thin-film deposition is complex, but Rome already uses mercury in small quantities and has glassmakers. The gap is not capability but the specific innovation.

## Sources and Confidence

This module documents technologies for comfort, cleanliness, and daily life - the domains where human benefit is most immediate and personal. Rome already commands extraordinary comfort through infrastructure (hypocausts, running water, public baths) and slave labour (every household task can be delegated). The technologies documented here replace that labour with machines and fuels, or improve on existing Roman systems.

The module draws on:

- Pliny the Elder, *Naturalis Historia* (books on glass, metals, water, and domestic customs) - for attested Roman capabilities and raw materials.
- Vitruvius, *De architectura* - for heating, plumbing, and architectural principles.
- Hero of Alexandria, *Pneumatica* and *Automatica* - for mechanical principles underlying pumps, valves, and automation.
- Frontinus, *De aquaeductu* - for water system design and pressurisation.
- Galen, *Hygiene* - for bathing customs and health considerations.

Specific technical entries draw on:

- Argand lamps: attested 1783 onward in Europe; the principle of tubular wicks and glass chimneys is sound.
- Water trap toilets: the S-bend principle is attested in Victorian plumbing (1850s onward) but the mechanism is simple enough for Roman practice.
- Kitchen ranges: attested from the 1600s in Europe; the modular principle is a natural extension of enclosed stoves.
- Pressure cookers: mechanically feasible with Roman metallurgy, though the safety risks require iteration.
- Central heating: hypocausts are Roman; extending them to multiple rooms with dampers is straightforward.
- Washing machines and mangles: attested from the 1600s-1800s; the mechanics are simple.
- Dishwashers: attested from the late 1800s; the principle requires pressurised hot water and mechanical agitation.
- Electric devices (fans, lighting, refrigeration): depend on electrochemistry and electromagnetism (Modules 28, 76, 77), placing them beyond 100 AD Roman practice but not beyond Roman potential with knowledge transfer.
- Friction matches: require white phosphorus, which cannot be synthesized in Rome and are anachronistic.
- Perfume attar: well-established by the medieval period; the chemistry is straightforward.

The confidence for each entry varies. HIGH confidence indicates that the underlying physics/chemistry is textbook and the engineering is within Roman reach or well-attested historically. MEDIUM confidence indicates feasibility but some uncertainty about optimal design or performance in practice. LOW confidence indicates materials or knowledge gaps that Rome cannot overcome.

The module concludes with an emphasis: domestic appliances displace household labour, which represents enslaved human beings in Rome. The benefit of these technologies - comfort and freedom from drudgery - is real and powerful. The objection to implementing them is equally real and sharp, because they threaten the property (slaves) on which elite wealth and leisure depend.
