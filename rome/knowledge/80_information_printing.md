# Module 80: Paper, Printing and the Survival of Knowledge

## Why this module outranks every furnace in the tree

Ablation testing on this programme's own simulation of the tech tree shows
four effects larger than any single piece of metallurgy or motive power in
the whole plan. Deleting `corpus_written` (the act of writing the technical
corpus down at all) delays the appearance of the transistor by roughly 90
years. Deleting `corpus_dispersed` (copying and scattering that corpus)
costs roughly 50 years. Deleting `printing_press` costs roughly 38 years.
Deleting `rag_paper` costs roughly 32 years. Nothing else in the tree, not a
blast furnace, not a steam engine, not electricity, comes close. These are
the four biggest single levers you have.

The reason is not that writing things down is clever. It is that the
Roman world you have landed in is going to be hit by three separate,
well-attested demographic and institutional catastrophes before anything
like a transistor is possible: the Antonine Plague (roughly 165-180 AD,
believed by ancient sources such as Galen to have killed a substantial
fraction of the population in affected areas), the Plague of Cyprian
(roughly 249-262 AD, similarly severe), and the Crisis of the Third Century
(235-284 AD, a sustained collapse of central authority, civil war,
currency debasement and provincial fragmentation). Any programme that
depends on you personally, or on one workshop, or on one patron's
goodwill, dies in one of these windows. A programme that depends on
information existing in enough independent, geographically separated
copies survives them, because plagues and civil wars kill people and burn
buildings, but they cannot simultaneously destroy every copy of a text
held in Antioch, Alexandria, Massilia, Ctesiphon and a Buddhist monastery
in the Kushan realm at once.

This is the single strategic insight of this module: **your most durable
invention is not a device, it is a document, copied enough times and
distributed widely enough that it survives you, survives your workshop,
and survives three empire-wide catastrophes.** Paper, ink and the press
are the machinery that makes that document cheap enough to exist in
dozens of copies instead of three. Everything else in this module (codices,
libraries, cryptography, the semaphore) exists to write, protect, move,
or fund that corpus. Read this module before you spend serious effort on
any single workshop technology, because a working furnace that only you
know how to run is a rounding error next to a corpus that a stranger can
pick up in 280 AD and rebuild the furnace from your description.

### rag_paper — Rag paper (*charta linea*, linen-rag paper)

**What it is / why you want it.** A cheap, foldable, mass-producible
writing surface made by pulping old linen and hemp cloth, felting it on a
screen, and drying it into sheets. It gives you a writing material whose
raw input (worn-out cloth) is available in every city in the Empire,
independent of the Egyptian papyrus trade and far cheaper per page than
parchment once a mill exists.

**Why you would never guess this.** Papyrus and parchment both look like
"paper" to a modern eye, so the instinct is to improve one of those. The
actual insight, that you can beat old rags into a fibre slurry and felt it
into sheets on a mesh, is the technique China already has (Cai Lun's
process is conventionally dated to 105 AD) but which historically does not
reach the Mediterranean world for roughly a thousand years (papermaking
enters Muslim Spain around the 12th century and does not become common in
Christian Europe until the 13th-14th century). Nobody in the Roman world
has any reason to think of cloth as a writing-material feedstock. The
mould-and-deckle workflow, not the chemistry, is the genuinely novel part.

**Prerequisites.** A water-powered trip hammer or stamping mill (an
existing hydraulic-milling capability, the same kind of mechanism used for
grain hammer mills), a screw press (already exists for oil and wine, see
`printing_press` below), lime burning (already exists for mortar), and
either fine bronze/brass wire-drawing or basic reed/rush weaving for the
mould screen.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen rags (*linteum*, worn tunics and togas,
recycled through the existing rag trade), hemp rope offcuts (*cannabis*,
grown in Italy and Gaul), quicklime (*calx viva*, universally produced),
wool felts (*coactilia*), thin bronze or brass wire if you draw it
yourself, or woven reed/rush screens as an easier first-generation
alternative (closer to the earliest Chinese practice than a European wire
mould), animal glue (*gluten*, from boiled hide and horn, already a Roman
product) or wheat starch (*amylum*, already used as a cosmetic and sizing
agent per Pliny) for sizing the finished sheet so ink does not feather.

**Procedure.**
1. Sort rags by fibre: pure linen and hemp only, no wool (wool does not
   felt into paper pulp the same way and rots differently). Cut into
   fist-sized pieces.
2. Ret the rags: soak in slow-moving or regularly changed water for
   several days to weeks, until the weave loosens and fibres separate
   easily when rubbed. This is the same retting principle already used to
   process flax and hemp for spinning.
3. Lime the rags: steep in a lime-water bath (a dilute slurry of quicklime
   in water, milky but not thick) for several days. This loosens residual
   fats, dyes and lignin-like binders and begins breaking the fibre
   bundles apart. Rinse thoroughly afterward; residual lime will make the
   paper brittle and eventually eat through itself.
4. Beat to pulp: feed the wet, limed rag into a water-driven stamping
   mill, a trough with iron-shod wooden hammers lifted by cams on the
   waterwheel shaft and dropped repeatedly onto the rag mass. Beat for
   several hours (the historical process, run on comparable stamping
   mills, is measured in hours, not minutes) until the fibres are
   separated into an even, creamy, waterlogged pulp (Latin: this stage has
   no Roman name; call it *pulpa*) with no visible thread fragments.
5. Dilute the pulp in a large vat of clean water to a thin, uniform
   suspension, just cloudy enough that you cannot read text through a
   double thickness of it.
6. Dip the mould (a wooden frame with a taut wire or woven-reed screen)
   with its removable deckle (a raised wooden lip that sits on top of the
   screen and defines the sheet's edge) flat into the vat, lift it level,
   and shake gently side to side and front to back. This step, called
   "shaking the mould" in later European practice, tangles the fibres in
   two directions and is what gives paper its strength; a sheet shaken
   only one way tears like cloth along the fibre grain.
7. Drain the water through the screen. A thin, even mat of felted fibre
   remains on the mould.
8. Couch the wet sheet: invert the mould onto a damp wool felt and press
   or roll it off in one motion, so the sheet transfers to the felt. Lay
   another felt on top, then the next wet sheet, building an alternating
   stack ("post") of felt/paper/felt/paper.
9. Press the whole post in the screw press to squeeze out most of the
   remaining water. Separate the sheets from the felts while still damp
   (they will not stick once pressed) and hang them to air-dry on ropes
   or racks.
10. Size the dried sheet: dip or brush it with a warm, dilute solution of
    animal glue or cooked wheat starch, then dry again. Unsized paper
    ("waterleaf") is too absorbent and any ink will feather and spread
    illegibly; sizing closes the surface so a pen or later a metal-type
    impression leaves a crisp line.

**How you know it worked.** A finished sheet held up to light should show
an even, uniform mottled texture (the "laid" or felted pattern) with no
thin translucent patches or thick opaque lumps. It should not tear along
a single straight line when pulled in any direction, only in an irregular
path, proof the fibres are tangled two ways. A drop of ink on the sized
sheet should sit and soak in slightly without spreading into a fuzzy halo.

**Failure modes.** Under-retted or under-beaten rag leaves visible thread
fragments and lumps that show as dark flecks and weak spots. Residual lime
left unrinsed makes brittle, chalky paper that cracks on folding within
months. Too little size and the sheet blots any ink; too much size and the
surface is glassy and repels ink entirely. Uneven shaking of the mould
gives a sheet with a strong grain direction that curls badly on drying and
splits when folded against the grain.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED (basis: extrapolated from documented
medieval and early modern European hand-mill practice, since no
Roman-era papermaking data exists to measure against). A trained two-person
vat crew (one dipping and shaking the mould, one couching) in a mature
European hand mill of the medieval/early modern period is commonly
reported to have produced on the order of 1,000-2,000 sheets per working
day; treat that as an upper bound for a crew that has been doing this for
years. A freshly trained Roman crew in year one should be planned for
perhaps a quarter to a half of that, so ESTIMATED 250-1,000 sheets per
crew per day while the workforce learns, rising toward the mature figure
over a few years. Against papyrus: papyrus is grown as a near-monopoly
crop in the Nile Delta (Pliny, *Naturalis Historia* 13.68-83, describes
the plant, the manufacturing process of laying and pressing strips, and
several graded qualities), so its price outside Egypt carries transport
and monopoly rent on top of manufacturing cost, and it is a fixed-geography
crop, not something you can grow near the Rhine or in Britain. Rag paper's
main input, worn cloth, exists wherever people wear clothes, so its supply
is completely decoupled from any one province. Against parchment: a
prepared skin (*membrana*) yields only a handful of leaves depending on
the animal's size and the page format (ESTIMATED at roughly 2-4 leaves,
4-8 pages, per average sheep or goat skin at a typical codex page size),
so a substantial book consumes dozens to hundreds of animals, a real
capital cost in livestock plus many hours of skilled scraping and stretching
labour per skin. Late medieval European sources commonly report paper
costing on the order of a fifth to a sixth of parchment per equivalent
page once paper mills were established (MEASURED, historical figure, but
from a much later European economy; treat the direction, paper
undercutting parchment sharply at scale, as reliable, and the specific
ratio as a rough guide only, not a Roman price). The strategic case is as
important as the cost case: papyrus makes you dependent on Egypt and
parchment makes you dependent on the livestock economy, while rag paper
makes you dependent only on cloth and water power, both of which you can
control directly.

**Danger.** Lime baths are caustic to skin and eyes; workers should rinse
hands frequently and never rub eyes with wet hands. The stamping mill's
hammers can crush fingers if the trough is loaded or cleared while the
waterwheel is engaged; always disengage the drive (or stop the wheel) before
reaching into the trough. Socially, this is a low-risk craft; a Roman
magistrate will see a fuller's-adjacent trade (rag sorting, retting,
pounding) and a mill, nothing suspicious.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the chemistry and mechanics (this is well-documented,
textbook hand-papermaking, feasible with entirely Roman-available materials
and mill technology). **MEDIUM** for the specific throughput and cost
figures, which are extrapolated from a much later European industry and
flagged as such throughout.

### ink_oil_based — Printer's ink (*atramentum pingendi*, an invented term; no Roman name exists)

**What it is / why you want it.** A thick, oil-based black ink that wets
and clings to metal type and transfers a clean, even film to paper. Without
it, movable type is decorative, not usable.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious move is to use the ink
Rome already has, iron gall ink (a solution of iron sulfate and
gallotannic acid, extracted from oak galls, in water with gum arabic as a
binder) or plain soot-and-gum ink (*atramentum librarium*). Both are
water-based, made to be drawn by a reed or quill into the porous fibres of
papyrus, parchment or paper, where capillary action pulls the ink in and
a mild chemical reaction fixes it. Metal type is smooth, dense and
essentially non-porous. Water-based ink does not wet a clean metal surface;
it beads into droplets (the same reason water beads on a greasy pan),
so it cannot form a thin even film across a page of type, and what little
does transfer smears and offsets unevenly, dries irregularly, and
corrodes the type by leaving it wet with a mildly acidic solution between
uses. This is a real, well-attested historical blocker: even Gutenberg's
early experiments with conventional scribal ink failed, and a workable
oil-based ink had to be separately developed before movable type became
practical.

**Prerequisites.** Pressed linseed oil (from flax, already cultivated
across the Empire for cloth; oil pressing uses the same screw-press
technology as olive oil), pine resin/rosin (*resina*, an established Roman
forest product), lampblack (fine soot, already known and named,
*atramentum*, collected from oil lamps or burning resinous wood).

**Roman-available inputs.** Linseed (*semen lini*), pine or fir resin
(*resina*), soot from lamps or pitch-pine burning, an iron cauldron.

**Procedure.**
1. Press flax seed in a screw press to extract linseed oil, as you would
   press olives.
2. Heat the oil slowly in an iron cauldron, outdoors, well clear of any
   wooden structure. Bring it to a slow simmer and hold it there for
   several hours. The oil will darken and thicken as it partially
   polymerises. A traditional test (used long after this period by
   varnish-makers) is to let a drop fall from a stirring rod: when the oil
   "ropes," forming a thin continuous thread rather than a drop, it has
   cooked enough. Do not let it exceed a gentle simmer; a hard boil risks
   the oil flashing into flame.
3. Add crushed pine rosin to the hot oil, roughly one part rosin to
   several parts oil by weight (ESTIMATED, basis: reconstructed early
   European printers' ink formulas reported by book historians; exact
   historical ratios varied by workshop and were adjusted by eye, so treat
   this as a starting point to be tuned by testing, not a fixed recipe).
   The rosin increases tack (stickiness, needed so the ink transfers from
   type to paper rather than staying on the type) and gloss.
4. Let the mixture cool to a heavy, dark varnish.
5. Grind lampblack into the varnish on a stone slab with a muller (a
   hand-held grinding stone), working it in gradually until the mixture is
   a smooth, stiff, glossy black paste with no visible grit. Under-ground
   pigment shows as grey specks and poor black density on the printed
   sheet.

**How you know it worked.** Dab a small amount on a clean piece of bronze
or iron. It should spread into a thin, even, tacky film rather than
beading into droplets. Pressed onto a scrap of sized paper, it should
transfer a sharp black mark without spreading or soaking through.

**Failure modes.** Under-cooked oil stays thin and runny, beads on metal
just like scribal ink, and never develops tack. Over-cooked or
uncontrolled oil can scorch, smoke heavily, or ignite. Too little rosin
gives poor transfer and dull, patchy printing; too much makes the ink
gummy and it fails to release cleanly from the type. Coarse or
insufficiently ground lampblack prints grey and grainy instead of a dense
black.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. A day's cooking of oil by one worker,
tended throughout, plus a day of grinding by a second worker, should
produce enough ink for a substantial run of sheets; exact batch yield
depends on ink film thickness and print run size, neither of which has a
measured Roman-era analogue, so no specific batch size is given here.
Materials (flax oil, rosin, lamp soot) are all cheap and already produced
in quantity across the Empire.

**Danger.** Serious fire hazard. Boiling linseed oil that overheats can
ignite (this is the same chemistry, oxidative polymerisation, behind the
well-known modern hazard of linseed-oil-soaked rags spontaneously
combusting). Cook outdoors, never inside a wooden building, keep a lid or
wet cloth on hand to smother a flare-up (never water on a hot-oil fire),
and never leave the cauldron unattended while heating. Socially harmless;
a magistrate will see a varnish-maker or a painter's supplier.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The chemistry (oxidative thickening of drying oil,
tack from resin addition, pigment loading) is textbook and all inputs are
confirmed Roman goods. The exact proportions are the only soft part, and
are flagged as such.

### punchcutting_and_type — Movable type (*typi mobiles*, an invented term)

**What it is / why you want it.** A system for cutting one master letter
per character, then casting that letter thousands of times identically in
metal, so a page of text is assembled from reusable pieces instead of
re-carved or re-written every time. This is the actual bottleneck of
printing; the press itself is a minor adaptation of equipment Rome already
has.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already has every piece of the
underlying craft, in a different application: die engravers cut hardened
steel dies to strike coins, which is a punch-and-matrix process in all but
name. Nobody has redirected that skill at letterforms meant to be cast and
reused as individual, interchangeable pieces. This is squarely "obvious
once you want it": the metallurgy and the hand skill both already exist in
the Roman world (in the mint), the idea to apply them to a reusable
character set for texts simply has not occurred to anyone.

**Prerequisites.** Hardened and tempered tool steel (bloomery iron
case-hardened by charcoal cementation, an established ancient process,
sometimes called blister steel; Noricum steel, *chalybs Noricus*, is a
real attested product of this kind), copper or copper-alloy stock for
matrices, lead, tin and antimony for type metal, an existing die-engraving
or fine-metalwork craftsman (the mint's die-sinkers are the natural
recruiting pool).

**Roman-available inputs.** Cemented (case-hardened) iron/steel rod,
copper sheet or bar, lead (widely mined, Britain and Spain), tin
(Cornwall), stibnite ore (*stibium* or *stimmi*, antimony sulfide, already
used and traded as a cosmetic per Pliny and Dioscorides), scrap iron (used
to reduce stibnite to metallic antimony), beeswax and tallow for release
agents.

**Procedure.**
1. Cut the punch: engrave a mirror-image, raised (relief) letterform onto
   the flat end of a steel rod using small gravers and files. For letters
   with enclosed spaces (o, e, a, b), first cut a small hardened
   counterpunch shaped to the interior space and drive it into the punch
   blank to sink the counter cleanly, then finish the outer letter shape
   by hand. This is the same fine hand-engraving skill already used for
   coin dies and gem-cutting (glyptic art), redirected.
2. Harden and temper the finished punch: heat to a bright cherry-red,
   roughly 750-800 degrees C (an experienced smith judges this by eye
   against the colour scale already used for tool steel), quench in water
   or oil, then temper by gently reheating until the surface takes a
   straw-to-bronze temper colour, roughly 220-280 degrees C, and quench
   again. This gives a punch hard enough to strike copper repeatedly
   without deforming, but tough enough not to shatter.
3. Strike the matrix: hammer the hardened punch squarely into a bar of
   softer copper (or a lead-tin-copper alloy), sinking a mirror-image
   intaglio impression of the letter. This is the reverse of the punch: a
   raised letter on the punch becomes a recessed mould cavity in the
   matrix.
4. Justify the matrix: file and true up the copper bar so the sunk letter
   sits at a consistent depth and consistent position relative to the
   bar's edges, so that every letter cast from every matrix in the set
   lines up on the same baseline and stands at the same height when
   assembled into a line. This step, tedious hand-fitting, is the real
   labour bottleneck of the whole craft; a full alphabet's punches can be
   cut in weeks, but justifying a full, consistent matrix set takes
   longer and any single badly justified letter will visibly sit high,
   low, or crooked on every page it appears in.
5. Cast type: clamp the justified matrix into the base of an adjustable
   two-part hand mould (wood or bronze jaws, sized to the body height, or
   "point size," of the letter, with a spring-loaded or wedge clamp
   holding the matrix in place). Pour molten type metal in through the
   top, allow a few seconds to solidify, open the mould, and knock the
   cast letter ("sort") out. Trim the surplus metal ("jet" or "tang") from
   the base.
6. Repeat step 5 for as many copies of each letter as needed. A single
   matrix, reused, can cast an effectively unlimited number of identical
   sorts; a skilled caster working a hand mould can be expected to cast on
   the order of a few thousand sorts in a working day (ESTIMATED, basis:
   commonly cited figures for hand-mould casting in the early European
   printing era, on the order of 3,000-4,000 pieces per skilled caster per
   day; no Roman-era analogue exists, so treat this as an achievable target
   once the crew is skilled, not a first-week result).

**Type metal alloy.** Lead, tin and antimony, roughly (ESTIMATED, basis:
widely reported ranges from later typefounding practice, which itself
varied by foundry and was tuned empirically rather than fixed by theory)
lead as the majority by weight, antimony as a substantial secondary
component, and tin as a smaller addition. Antimony is the component that
matters most and is the non-obvious ingredient: unlike almost every other
metal, antimony expands very slightly on solidifying rather than
shrinking. Mixed into the lead-tin base, this counteracts the alloy's
natural shrinkage on cooling, so the cast letter fills every fine detail
of the matrix crisply instead of pulling away from the mould walls as it
sets, and it substantially hardens the alloy (pure lead type is too soft
and batters flat after a few thousand impressions). Tin improves the flow
of the molten metal into fine detail and moderates brittleness. Rome does
not produce metallic antimony directly but has ready access to stibnite
ore; heating stibnite with scrap iron causes the iron to take the sulfur
(iron has a stronger affinity for sulfur than antimony does), releasing
metallic antimony, a straightforward smelting reaction using materials
and furnace heat Rome already has.

**How you know it worked.** A well-cast sort should have crisp, fully
formed serifs and counters with no rounding or gaps, should stand exactly
as tall as its neighbours when a row of different letters is stood
upright on a flat surface (checked by eye and by running a straightedge
across the tops), and should not deform when squeezed in the fingers.

**Failure modes.** An under-justified matrix casts a letter that sits high
or low, ruining line alignment. Too little antimony gives soft type that
wears down quickly and casts with rounded, mushy detail from shrinkage.
Molten metal poured too cool fails to fill fine counters; poured too hot
warps the wooden mould or over-erodes the matrix over repeated use. A
punch tempered too hard (skipped the temper step) will chip when struck
into the matrix.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED throughout, no Roman-era or closely analogous
data exists. Punch-cutting a full working alphabet (uppercase, lowercase,
numerals, common punctuation) is a job of weeks for one skilled engraver.
Striking and justifying matrices from those punches is comparable or
longer. Casting, once matrices exist, is comparatively fast, on the order
of the few-thousand-per-day figure above. Materials cost is modest (lead
and tin are cheap bulk metals in the Roman economy; antimony production
from stibnite is a minor additional smelting step).

**Danger.** Molten type metal causes serious burns; lead fumes from
overheated metal are a genuine poisoning hazard with prolonged exposure,
work in a ventilated space and do not overheat the melt beyond what casting
requires. Socially, this looks like fine metalworking or minting-adjacent
work; a magistrate familiar with the mint will recognise the die-and-strike
process immediately and it should read as respectable skilled craft, not
suspicious.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the metallurgy and mechanical process, this is
well-understood, textbook casting and hardening chemistry using confirmed
Roman materials. **MEDIUM** for the exact alloy ratios and daily output
figures, both flagged as estimates drawn from later, non-Roman practice.

### printing_press — The printing press (adapted from *prelum*, the existing screw press)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine that brings a full sheet of
paper into even, controlled contact with a locked page of inked type in
one motion, replacing hand-rubbing or stamping. It is what turns
individually cast letters into pages per hour instead of pages per day.

**Why you would never guess this.** Obvious once you have type: Rome
already operates large wooden screw presses for pressing olive oil and
wine (*prelum*, described in agricultural writers such as Cato and
Columella and attested archaeologically across the Empire), so the heavy
lifting, cutting an accurate wooden screw and a frame strong enough to
resist it, is already solved. The adaptation is entirely about what sits
under the screw: a flat platen instead of a pressing board, a way to hold
the paper steady and the type inked without wasting the whole crew's time
resetting it, and a way to move the heavy type bed in and out without
moving the screw mechanism itself.

**Prerequisites.** An existing screw press (already in wide use for oil
and wine), `punchcutting_and_type`, `ink_oil_based`, `rag_paper` or an
equivalent writing surface.

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or elm timber for the press frame and
screw (already used in oil and wine presses), iron fittings, wool felt or
leather-covered ink dabbers ("ink balls"), parchment or oiled paper for
the tympan.

**Procedure (the machine, not a single print run).**
1. Build a standard screw press frame, scaled down from an oil press but
   using the same joinery and screw-cutting skill.
2. Fit a flat wooden platen to the base of the screw, faced if possible
   with a thin metal plate for an even bearing surface, in place of the
   pressing board used for oil.
3. Build the "coffin," a shallow wooden tray on wheels or rollers that
   slides in and out under the platen on a rail, or "carriage." The forme
   (a locked wooden frame, or "chase," holding the assembled type,
   wedged tight with wooden or metal quoins so no letter can shift) sits
   in the coffin. This travelling bed is what lets the crew ink the type
   and load fresh paper away from directly under the screw, then slide the
   whole assembly into position for the pull.
4. Build the tympan, a light wooden frame covered with taut parchment or
   oiled paper, hinged to the near edge of the coffin, on which the blank
   sheet is laid and held by folding pins or a light frame.
5. Build the frisket, a second, lighter hinged frame with a window cut to
   match exactly the printed area, that folds down over the tympan (and
   the paper) before the whole assembly is folded down onto the forme.
   The frisket's solid margins mask any part of the sheet that should not
   receive ink, keeping the page margins clean.
6. Working procedure per sheet: ink the type using leather ink balls
   (dabbed, not brushed, to avoid dragging ink into the counters of the
   letters), lay a fresh sheet on the tympan, fold the frisket down over
   it, fold both down onto the forme, slide the coffin under the platen,
   and pull the press bar to bring the platen down with one firm, even
   motion. Release, slide the coffin back out, lift frisket and tympan,
   remove the printed sheet, and repeat.

**How you know it worked.** A correctly pulled sheet shows a clean,
even, dark impression across every letter with no ghosting (double image
from movement during the pull) and no blank or grey patches (uneven
pressure, usually from an unlevel platen or an unevenly packed tympan).

**Failure modes.** Too little pressure leaves faint or patchy type;
too much crushes the finer letters and can crack the platen or strain the
screw. An improperly justified forme (letters not standing at a uniform
height, see `punchcutting_and_type`) prints some letters darker than
others, or not at all. A shifting forme (loose quoins) blurs or
misaligns lines between pulls. Uneven inking (too little ink loaded on
the balls, or dabbed unevenly) gives patchy blacks.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED (basis: figures commonly reported for the
"common press" era of hand printing in early modern Europe, roughly the
15th-17th centuries, the closest comparable technology; no Roman data
exists). A trained two-person crew (one inking, one operating the bar and
sheets) working a full day is commonly reported, in that later European
context, to have produced on the order of 2,000-3,000 sheet impressions.
A newly trained Roman crew, working with unfamiliar equipment and less
practised type-setting, should be planned for substantially less at
first, perhaps a third to a half of that mature figure, rising as the
crew gains experience. Capital cost is modest relative to the type itself:
the press is built from timber and iron fittings the Empire already
produces in quantity; the expensive, slow part of the whole system is
cutting punches and casting type, not building the press.

**Danger.** Crushed fingers are the main physical risk, from the platen
or from the coffin's rollers; keep hands clear during the pull. No
poison or fire risk beyond the ink's own hazards. Socially unremarkable,
reads as a mechanic's or craftsman's workshop built around a press type
every Roman town already recognises from oil and wine production.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The mechanics are a direct, well-understood
adaptation of an existing, widely attested Roman machine. **MEDIUM** for
the specific throughput figures, extrapolated from a later European
industry rather than measured in a Roman context.

### binding_and_codex — Binding and the codex (*codex*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Folding printed or hand-copied sheets
into gathered sections ("quires"), sewing them through the fold, and
attaching boards and a cover, to make a compact, durable, randomly
accessible book rather than an unwieldy roll.

**Why you would never guess this.** You would not need to guess it; it
is Obvious once you want it, and more usefully, it already exists in
Rome. The codex is attested by the late 1st century AD (the poet Martial,
writing under Domitian, describes parchment codices explicitly as a
compact, portable book format, offered as gifts), and wax-tablet
notebooks bound with cord through drilled holes (*pugillares*,
*tabulae*) are already completely ordinary. What Rome has not yet done is
make the codex the default format for serious literature, that remains
the roll (*volumen*) through this period and for a couple of centuries
after; you are accelerating a transition that is already technically
possible and already starting, not inventing binding from nothing.

**Prerequisites.** `rag_paper` or parchment or papyrus sheets, cord or
sinew thread, wooden boards (oak or beech), leather (vegetable-tanned,
already a well-established Roman craft).

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or beech boards, waxed linen thread or
sinew, vegetable-tanned leather (calf or goat), animal glue.

**Procedure.**
1. Fold sheets into gatherings ("quires"): a common convenient size is
   four sheets folded together, giving eight leaves and sixteen pages per
   quire, though this can be varied.
2. Sew each quire through the centre fold, along a set of raised cords or
   leather thongs stretched on a sewing frame, linking successive quires
   together along the same cords so the whole book's spine is structurally
   continuous.
3. Attach the ends of the sewing cords into or through drilled channels
   in wooden boards front and back, so the boards are mechanically laced
   to the sewn block, not merely glued on.
4. Cover the boards and spine in leather, glued and often laced through
   small slits at the board edges for extra strength.
5. Fit a clasp or tie (leather thong and a bone or bronze pin/peg is
   sufficient) to hold the book closed under tension. This matters more
   than it looks: a closed, clamped book resists the pages cockling and
   warping from damp far better than an open stack of sheets, which is a
   direct defence against the poor preservation conditions described in
   `corpus_dispersed` below.

**How you know it worked.** The book should lie reasonably flat when
opened at any point without the sewing pulling loose, and the boards
should not rack or twist when the closed book is held by one board and
gently shaken.

**Failure modes.** Sewing thread too thin or poorly waxed cuts through
the fold over repeated use. Boards laced too loosely allow the whole
book to rack sideways and eventually tear the sewn cords out. Leather
glued rather than laced onto the boards peels off with use and humidity.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. Binding a modest-sized book (a few hundred
pages) is a job of a day or two for one skilled binder once boards and
leather are prepared; materials cost is small relative to the cost of the
writing surface and the writing or printing labour that fills the pages.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary craft-tool hazards (awls, knives).
Socially unremarkable; codices in this period already circulate, notably
in early Christian and Jewish communities and as informal notebooks, so
this reads as an existing, if not yet dominant, book format.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Both the materials and the binding technique are
well within confirmed Roman-era craft and the codex format's existence by
this date is textually attested.

### corpus_written — Writing the corpus so a stranger 200 years later can use it

**What it is / why you want it.** A deliberate standard for how the
technical corpus itself is written: plain declarative language, explicit
numbers with stated units, worked numeric examples, labelled diagrams, no
allegory, no deliberately withheld steps, and built-in redundancy within
each document, so that someone who has never met you, reading a single
surviving copy generations later, can actually reconstruct the process.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious model for "important
technical knowledge" available in the Roman world is the craft-guild and
proto-alchemical tradition: recipes handed down orally from master to a
single chosen apprentice, or, where written at all, written in
deliberately allegorical, riddling language meant to be unintelligible to
outsiders (this style is visible in the surviving Greco-Egyptian
alchemical and craft-recipe papyri, and becomes pronounced in the
alchemical corpus of the following centuries). This is the single worst
intellectual habit of the ancient and medieval world, and you should name
it explicitly to whoever reads this: deliberate obscurity does not
protect knowledge, it kills it, because the moment the one master-to-apprentice
chain is broken (death, plague, war, the apprentice simply not being told
in time), the knowledge is gone, and no reader centuries later can recover
a working recipe from a riddle. Entire bodies of ancient craft knowledge
are lost for exactly this reason, not because the materials or techniques
were forgotten by everyone at once, but because they were only ever known
by one line of people who did not write them down plainly. Everything in
this module is built to be the opposite of that tradition.

**Prerequisites.** `rag_paper`, a stable writing implement and ink,
literacy in the intended reader's language (see `corpus_dispersed` for
translation strategy).

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond ordinary writing materials; this
entry is about method, not materials.

**Procedure (the standard to write to).**
1. Define your units before using them. Rome has weights and measures
   (*libra*, *uncia*, *pes*) but no metric system and no guarantee that
   local measures stay consistent over centuries or across provinces.
   Anchor at least one unit to something physically reproducible rather
   than to an authority's foot or a mint's standard weight, for example
   defining a unit of mass as the weight of a stated volume of clean
   rainwater. A physically reproducible definition can be re-derived from
   the text alone even if every physical reference standard in every
   archive has been lost or debased, which matters directly given the
   currency debasement of the Crisis of the Third Century (see
   `record_keeping_and_accounting` below).
2. Write procedures as numbered steps, exactly as this module does, with
   real numbers, real units, and a real observable test for success at
   the end of each major procedure. State temperatures both as a number
   and as a sense-observable proxy (colour of hot iron, texture of a
   liquid) because your reader has no thermometer.
3. Include at least one fully worked numeric example per procedure that
   has quantities, not just the abstract steps. An example with real
   numbers survives an ambiguous instruction; a purely qualitative
   instruction does not.
4. Include diagrams wherever a shape, an assembly order, or a spatial
   relationship matters, labelled with the same plain names used in the
   text. A picture of a press or a mould survives a reader whose Latin or
   Greek has drifted from yours over two centuries better than prose does.
5. Never encode anything for secrecy. If a technique is genuinely
   dangerous to hand to just anyone (nothing in this module qualifies,
   but future modules on chemistry might), control access to the physical
   copy and the institution holding it, never obscure the content of the
   text itself. An encoded secret can only be recovered by someone who
   already has the key; a controlled-access plain text can be recovered
   by anyone the institution chooses to trust, including a future
   custodian you will never meet.
6. Build redundancy inside a single document, not only across copies:
   restate key numbers in a summary line at the end of a procedure,
   repeat critical warnings in more than one place, and include a full
   index and a glossary of any technical term you had to coin, so that a
   reader who only has a damaged or partial copy (missing pages,
   water-stained margins) can still recover the most important facts from
   what remains.
7. Write in the plainest register of the language you are using. Avoid
   metaphor, poetic circumlocution, and rhetorical flourish, all of which
   are the normal register of educated Latin and Greek prose in this
   period and all of which actively work against a reader trying to
   extract an exact procedure two hundred years later.

**How you know it worked.** Hand a draft procedure to someone who was not
present for the original work and who has no more background than the
corpus itself provides, and have them attempt the procedure using only
the text. If they succeed, or fail in a way the text's failure-modes
section already predicted, the writing has worked.

**Failure modes.** Writing that assumes shared context with the author
(a specific master's specific workshop, a specific batch of materials)
fails silently: it reads as complete to the writer and is unusable to
the reader. Writing that hides genuinely important steps out of habit or
guild instinct (the historical default) fails the entire purpose of the
corpus.

**Cost & labour.** Not a materials cost; a discipline cost. Writing to
this standard takes longer per page than writing loosely, ESTIMATED at
perhaps double the drafting time of an unstructured account, because of
the worked examples, diagrams and index. That cost is trivial compared to
the value of the document surviving intact use two centuries on.

**Danger.** None physical. The social risk runs the other way: a corpus
written this plainly and this completely is also legible to a rival or a
hostile power. Weigh what you disperse widely (general craft and
science) against what you keep to controlled, trusted institutional
custody (anything with clear military or destructive application), but
control that through who holds the copy, not through obscuring the text.

**Confidence: HIGH.** This is a methodological argument, not a physical
claim; the failure mode it warns against (loss of knowledge through
deliberate obscurity) is well attested across the ancient and medieval
craft and alchemical traditions.

### corpus_dispersed — Dispersing the corpus

**What it is / why you want it.** A deliberate plan for how many copies of
the corpus exist, where they are physically located, in what institutions,
in what languages, and how far outside the Roman Empire's own political
and climatic risk some of them sit. A single, brilliant, complete corpus
sitting in one library in Rome is one fire away from the 90-year delay the
ablation study warns about.

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks like the job is done once
the corpus is written. It is not. The single most important fact bearing
on this decision is archaeological, not historical: papyrus and other
organic writing materials survive for two thousand years almost
exclusively in Egypt's exceptionally dry climate (hence the enormous
volume of surviving papyri from sites such as Oxyrhynchus). In the
western provinces, Italy, Gaul, Britain, Germany, organic materials
essentially do not survive two thousand years of normal damp-temperate
conditions at all; the rare exceptions (the carbonised scrolls at
Herculaneum, preserved only because they were baked and sealed by
volcanic material; the waterlogged, anaerobic mud at Vindolanda that
preserved wooden writing tablets) are freak, unrepeatable microclimates,
not something you can plan around. This means a copy left in a normal
building in a normal western province, however well bound, is playing a
losing game against damp, insects and fungal decay even before you count
fire, war and simple neglect. You must plan for climate and geography, not
just for institutional stability.

**Prerequisites.** Multiple physical copies (`printing_press` makes this
cheap), `binding_and_codex`, trusted institutional custodians
(`libraries_and_institutions`), and, for the farthest-flung copies,
existing Roman trade contacts with Parthia/Persia, India and, indirectly,
China.

**Roman-available inputs.** The Empire already has extensive trade
infrastructure that reaches well past its own borders: the Red Sea and
Indian Ocean trade with India is well documented (the *Periplus of the
Erythraean Sea* describes these routes, and papyri such as the Muziris
papyrus record actual India-trade cargoes), and there is at least one
recorded direct diplomatic contact between Rome and China in this general
period (Chinese court records describe an embassy from "Andun," identified
with Marcus Aurelius, arriving via the southern sea route in 166 AD),
proving a physical channel exists even though it is thin and indirect.

**Procedure (the dispersal plan).**
1. Place copies in institutions with different failure modes, not just
   different locations, so that no single kind of disaster can take out
   more than a fraction of your copies at once. Recommended categories:
   temple archives (priesthoods have unusually long institutional
   continuity and existing custom of preserving records), municipal
   archives (*tabularia*, but note these are vulnerable to the exact civic
   and military disruption the Crisis of the Third Century brings),
   private family libraries of patrons you cultivate (individually
   fragile, tied to one family's fortune, but numerous and cheap to
   seed), and the licensed collegium library described in
   `libraries_and_institutions`, which is the one you can actually govern
   directly.
2. Place copies in more than one climate. Prioritise at least one copy in
   Egypt specifically, for the dry-climate preservation advantage
   described above, in addition to copies in Italy, Gaul, and the eastern
   provinces.
3. Place copies outside the Empire's own political risk entirely. A copy
   in Parthian or later Sassanid territory, or reachable via the Indian
   Ocean trade into India, is immune to a crisis that only afflicts Roman
   central authority; the Crisis of the Third Century is a crisis of the
   Roman state, not of Persia or India. This is expensive and slow to
   arrange (you are relying on merchants and, ideally, on a
   scholarly or religious institution abroad willing to hold a foreign
   text), but even one or two such copies materially change your worst-case
   outcome.
4. Translate at least a summary or index into a second language early,
   even a rough translation, so that a foreign custodian institution has
   some way to know what it is holding and to value keeping it, rather
   than discarding an unreadable foreign text.
5. Treat this as an ongoing programme, not a one-time seeding. See "The
   redundancy arithmetic" below for why active, periodic recopying matters
   more than any fixed initial count.

**How you know it worked.** You will not, personally; this is a
generational bet. The nearest check available to you is process, not
outcome: confirm each custodian institution has actually logged the text
into its own catalogue (see `libraries_and_institutions`) and has a
copying rota that includes it, not merely that a copy was dropped off.

**Failure modes.** Concentrating copies in one city or one family
correlates their risk (one fire, one confiscation, one bankruptcy takes
out several "independent" copies at once). Leaving a copy with an
institution that never actually reads or values it means it is the first
thing discarded or recycled (parchment and paper are both valuable enough
to be scraped and reused, a real and attested fate for texts that fall
out of active use, as in later palimpsests). Sending a copy abroad in a
language nobody there reads risks the same fate faster.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. Once `printing_press` exists, the marginal
cost of an additional copy is mainly paper and press time (cheap, see
`rag_paper` and `printing_press` above), so the constraint on dispersal
is not manufacturing cost, it is the labour and trust needed to place and
maintain each copy with a genuine institutional custodian, and, for
distant copies, the cost and risk of the trade journey itself.

**Danger.** Politically, exporting Roman technical knowledge to Parthia
(a rival power Rome fights repeated wars against) could plausibly be read
as treasonous by a hostile magistrate; route such copies through
merchant and religious/scholarly channels rather than official ones, and
weigh carefully what categories of knowledge (see the secrecy note in
`corpus_written`) are safe to disperse that widely.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The archaeological preservation-bias argument
(Egypt versus the western provinces) is well attested. The specific
institutional and geographic dispersal plan is sound reasoning from that
fact but is inherently a forward-looking strategy, not a measured result.

### semaphore_telegraph — Optical telegraph chain (modelled on Chappe's semaphore system)

**What it is / why you want it.** A chain of towers, each within sight of
the next, each carrying a mechanical arm signal that can be set into a
small number of distinct positions read through a telescope at the next
tower and relayed onward, turning a message that takes weeks to carry by
horse into one that crosses the same distance in hours.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already has excellent
long-distance road and courier infrastructure (the *cursus publicus*), so
the instinct is to make couriers faster, not to replace them with a
line-of-sight relay. The design here is modelled directly on Claude
Chappe's semaphore system (built in France beginning in the 1790s): a
tall mast carrying a long pivoting crossbar (the "regulator") with a
shorter pivoting indicator arm at each end, together capable of a large
number of distinct, unambiguous shape-positions, read by the next tower's
operator through a telescope and copied onto their own tower to relay it
further down the chain. The genuinely non-obvious part for a Roman
engineer is not the tower or the arm (carpentry Rome can do easily), it is
the discipline of a fixed, shared alphabet/codebook so that an arbitrary
shape reliably means one specific thing to every operator on the line, and
the dependence on optical aid: without ground glass telescopes (a later
invention, conventionally dated to the early 17th century, and not
something in the "Romans already have" list even though Rome does have
good clear glass and glassblowing), tower spacing is limited to whatever
the unaided eye can resolve a large arm's shape at, which is much shorter
than Chappe's historical spacing.

**Prerequisites.** Timber and iron fittings for the tower and mechanism
(routine Roman carpentry and blacksmithing), a fixed shared codebook
(pairs well with `cryptography_and_codes` below), and, for full-scale
long-distance spacing, a working telescope, itself dependent on a
lens-grinding capability that is a separate prerequisite technology not
covered in this module.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber, iron fittings and cordage (all
routine), clear or near-colourless blown glass (a confirmed Roman
capability, per the anachronism notes above, though turning it into a
ground telescope lens is a distinct, harder skill not assumed here).

**Procedure.**
1. Site towers on high ground or existing structures (city walls, hilltop
   watch-forts) at whatever spacing your available optics support (see
   cost note below for the two cases, with and without telescopes).
2. Build each tower with a mast carrying a long pivoting regulator bar
   and two shorter indicator arms, one at each end of the regulator, each
   independently rotatable to a small number of fixed positions
   (historically, Chappe's regulator had a small number of overall
   angles and each indicator arm had several positions relative to the
   regulator, together giving on the order of a couple of hundred
   distinct combinations, MEASURED as a historical figure for the actual
   Chappe system, though the precise count reported varies by source).
3. Establish a fixed codebook shared by every station: the most common
   words, phrases, numbers and place names are each assigned to a single
   arm-position combination, so an operator sets one shape for a whole
   word rather than spelling letter by letter; rarer words or names are
   spelled out using a second set of positions mapped to individual
   letters. This dual system (fast codebook mode plus a slow
   letter-by-letter fallback) is what makes the historical system usable
   at any real speed, and it has the secondary benefit of being an
   effective code even for someone who can see the tower but does not
   have the codebook (see `cryptography_and_codes`).
4. Station one or two operators per tower, one watching the previous
   tower through a telescope (or the naked eye, at closer, no-telescope
   spacing) and calling out the position, one setting the same position
   on their own tower's arms for the next station to see.
5. Relay a message by repeating this operator-to-operator handoff down
   the whole chain.

**How you know it worked.** A test message sent from one end of the
chain and independently transcribed at the far end should match the
original when compared, with any discrepancy traceable to a specific
tower for retraining.

**Failure modes.** Fog, rain, snow, and simple darkness stop the whole
chain (this is a real, well-attested limitation of the historical
system, which operated only in daylight and clear weather). A single
inattentive or poorly trained operator garbles the message for every
tower beyond them. Spacing set too far for the available optics means
operators simply cannot resolve the arm positions reliably.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED throughout, no Roman-era figures exist for
any of this. Historically (MEASURED, but again a later European figure,
not Roman), Chappe's network spaced towers roughly 10-15 km apart where
telescopes were used, and a signal could reportedly cross several hundred
kilometres of chain in well under an hour in good conditions, the
practical throughput advantage being end-to-end message latency, not
bulk data volume; each individual symbol still took several seconds to
set and confirm at each relay. Without telescopes, plan conservatively
for unaided-eye spacing of perhaps a few kilometres at best in good
visibility for a large arm against the sky (ESTIMATED, basis: rough
visual-acuity reasoning, not measured), meaning several times as many
towers for the same distance until lens-grinding is available.
Construction cost per tower: ESTIMATED at a few hundred to roughly one to
two thousand denarii for the timber mechanism, iron fittings and modest
operator housing (basis: rough comparison to the cost of small timber
structures and several weeks of skilled carpenter and smith labour,
carpenters being paid on the order of a few denarii per day per the
wage schedules in Diocletian's Price Edict of 301 AD, a source two
centuries later than 100 AD used here only as a rough order-of-magnitude
wage proxy), and substantially less if the tower reuses an existing
Roman watchtower or fort structure the army has already built along a
frontier, since in that case only the arm mechanism and operator housing
are new cost. Per-kilometre capital cost is simply tower cost divided by
spacing (DERIVED): at, say, 1,000 denarii per tower and 10 km spacing
with telescopes, roughly 100 denarii per km; at a few-km spacing without
telescopes, several times that per km for the same line length.

**Danger.** Minimal physical risk (working at height on a mast is the
main one). This is the best single demonstration to buy imperial or
provincial patronage with: an empire's basic administrative problem is
that a message from Rome to a legion on the Rhine takes on the order of
weeks by the fastest normal courier relay (a sustained courier rate on
the order of 70-80 km per day is a reasonable planning figure for the
*cursus publicus*, ESTIMATED/DERIVED from commonly cited ranges for
Roman courier speed, giving roughly three weeks for a Rome-to-Rhine
distance of some 1,500 km; exceptional emergency riders could do
somewhat better but not by an order of magnitude). A demonstrable
same-day or next-day signal over even a modest distance is a dramatic,
immediately legible proof of value to anyone who has ever had to wait
weeks for frontier news, and is a far stronger patronage pitch than
almost anything else in this tech tree.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The tower-and-arm mechanism itself is simple,
well-attested (by its later, better-documented French implementation)
carpentry and organisational discipline, well within Roman capability.
The specific throughput, spacing and cost figures are extrapolated from
that later system or are order-of-magnitude estimates, and are flagged
as such throughout; treat none of the numbers in this entry as more than
a planning baseline.

### cryptography_and_codes — Ciphers beyond the Caesar shift

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already uses a simple substitution
cipher for military correspondence (Suetonius, *Divus Iulius* 56,
attributes to Julius Caesar a cipher that shifts each letter a fixed
number of places through the alphabet). You can offer meaningfully
stronger methods at essentially no material cost, which makes this one of
the cheapest, fastest ways to become useful and trusted by the state,
particularly by anyone running the *cursus publicus* or military
communications, which also happens to put you exactly where you want to
be to get support for `semaphore_telegraph` and `libraries_and_institutions`.

**Why you would never guess this.** Obvious once you know it is weak: a
single fixed shift has only as many possible keys as the alphabet has
letters, and is additionally vulnerable to frequency analysis (some
letters are simply far more common than others in any given language,
and a fixed shift preserves that pattern, just relabelled), so anyone who
tries all shifts or studies letter frequency can break it quickly. Rome
in this period has no concept of either weakness or of a systematic
countermeasure.

**Prerequisites.** Literacy, `corpus_written` style plain documentation
(so the method can actually be taught and audited by the state, not just
performed by you personally).

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond paper/parchment and ink; this is
purely a method.

**Procedure.**
1. Teach frequency analysis as an attack, first, so your patron
   understands why the existing Caesar shift is weak: count how often
   each letter appears in a body of intercepted or sample text and match
   the pattern against known letter frequencies in the target language.
   This alone is a valuable, sellable skill, it lets you read enemy or
   provincial correspondence that others cannot.
2. Introduce polyalphabetic substitution as the defence: instead of one
   fixed shift, use a short repeating keyword, and shift each letter of
   the message by an amount determined by the corresponding letter of the
   keyword, cycling the keyword across the whole message. This defeats
   simple frequency analysis because the same plaintext letter now maps
   to different ciphertext letters depending on its position, smoothing
   out the frequency pattern an attacker would otherwise exploit.
   (Historically this class of cipher is not formalised in Europe until
   the 15th-16th centuries, so this is a genuine multi-century advance
   for Rome to receive.)
3. Introduce transposition as a second, independent layer: rearrange the
   letters of the message according to a fixed pattern (for example,
   writing the message into a grid of an agreed width and reading the
   columns off in an agreed order) rather than, or in addition to,
   substituting them. Combining substitution and transposition is
   considerably harder to break than either alone.
4. Mention the theoretical limit case, a key used only once and never
   reused, as unbreakable in principle, but note plainly that it is
   impractical for routine correspondence because it requires securely
   distributing a fresh key at least as long as every message, which is a
   logistics problem rather than a code-making one; keep expectations
   realistic for your patron.
5. Pair a codebook (word-to-symbol mapping, as described for
   `semaphore_telegraph`) with a cipher layer for anything sent by
   semaphore, since the codebook alone is not secret (anyone who watches
   the towers long enough and gets a copy of the codebook can read it),
   but an additionally enciphered codebook value is much harder to
   recover.

**How you know it worked.** Give a sample enciphered message to a
literate person who does not have the key and see how long straightforward
frequency analysis takes them to break it; a well-applied polyalphabetic
cipher should resist casual attack far longer than a Caesar shift does.

**Failure modes.** A short, reused keyword is itself vulnerable to more
advanced (later-developed) attacks, though these are well beyond what a
100 AD adversary is likely to attempt; do not oversell the security as
absolute. Poor key management (writing the keyword somewhere an
adversary can find it, reusing the same keyword indefinitely across
sensitive traffic) undermines even a strong cipher.

**Cost & labour.** Essentially free; this is pure method, requiring only
paper, ink, and a trained clerk. This is precisely why it is a good early
move: near-zero capital outlay, immediate, legible value to a patron
(readable enemy mail, secure friendly mail), and it buys you a legitimate,
trusted reason to be near official correspondence and courier
infrastructure.

**Danger.** Politically double-edged: being visibly good at codebreaking
makes you valuable, but also makes you someone a suspicious regime might
worry about turning against them, or simply want to control closely.
Being seen as loyal and useful, not independent, is the safer posture.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Both the weakness of the Caesar cipher and the
mechanics of polyalphabetic substitution and transposition are basic,
well-understood classical cryptography, and the Caesar cipher's Roman
attestation is solid (Suetonius).

### libraries_and_institutions — A library that is also a school and an archive

**What it is / why you want it.** A durable legal and organisational
structure, a licensed corporate body with its own permanent endowment,
that holds, catalogues, copies and teaches the corpus, so the whole
programme does not depend on your personal survival, memory, or presence.

**Why you would never guess this.** It is not actually a stretch for a
Roman audience: the Hellenistic world already has the model of a great
library with a systematic subject-and-author catalogue (Callimachus's
*Pinakes*, a bibliographic catalogue of the Library of Alexandria's
holdings, is attested by later sources), so "a library with a real
catalogue" is culturally legible, not a foreign idea. The genuinely
non-obvious part is the legal and financial engineering: how you make an
institution outlive its founder in a world with no concept of a
perpetual corporation as we would define one, and no protection at all
against the specific financial shock coming in the third century.

**Prerequisites.** `corpus_written`, `binding_and_codex`, physical space,
and Roman legal recognition as a *collegium* (a licensed association;
Roman law from Augustus onward, following the *lex Iulia* on collegia,
requires such associations to be licensed rather than forming freely,
so this step needs actual imperial or provincial administrative
approval, not just intent).

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing Roman legal forms (the licensed
*collegium*, the endowed foundation), existing scriptorium-style copying
labour (literate slaves or freedmen scribes, already a normal part of a
wealthy household or public institution).

**Procedure.**
1. Petition for recognition as a licensed *collegium* with an explicit
   charter: purpose (holding, copying and teaching the corpus), officers,
   and succession rule for who appoints the next head when the current one
   dies.
2. Catalogue every work held by subject and author, on the model of
   Callimachus's *Pinakes*, cross-referenced with the index each
   individual work should already carry per `corpus_written`. A library
   nobody can navigate is functionally a warehouse, not a working
   institution.
3. Run a standing copying rota: assign scribes on a rotating schedule to
   copy works showing wear or that exist in too few copies, with a second
   scribe cross-checking each finished copy against the exemplar before
   it is accepted and catalogued. A trained scribe's realistic daily
   output for careful copying is ESTIMATED (basis: commonly cited ranges
   for medieval scriptorium productivity, no Roman-specific figure
   exists) at a small number of pages per day, so plan copying capacity
   and staffing around that being a slow, steady, ongoing process, not a
   one-time task.
4. Fund it with an endowment in land, not a sum of coin. This is the
   single most important financial decision in the whole module, and it
   has a real Roman precedent: Pliny the Younger's letters describe
   converting a cash benefaction into a land-secured endowment
   specifically so its income would not be exposed to the value of coin
   itself (Pliny, *Letters* 7.18, attributed; consult the source directly
   for exact wording), and Pliny separately endowed a library and an
   alimentary foundation at his hometown of Comum (*Letters* 1.8). A
   coin-denominated endowment is directly exposed to currency debasement,
   which happens catastrophically during the Crisis of the Third Century
   as the silver content of the denarius collapses; an endowment in
   productive land (an estate whose rents or harvest, paid in kind or sold
   fresh each year, fund the institution) keeps producing real value
   regardless of what happens to the coinage.
5. Attach a teaching function, not just storage: train new scribes and
   readers in the plain, index-heavy style of `corpus_written`, so the
   institution keeps producing people capable of maintaining and
   extending the corpus, not merely people capable of copying it letter
   for letter without understanding it.

**How you know it worked.** The clearest test you personally can apply
is succession: does the institution have a written, followed rule for
appointing its next head that does not require your involvement? If yes,
you have built something that can survive you; if every important
decision still routes through you, you have built a workshop, not an
institution.

**Failure modes.** A coin endowment that looks generous at founding can
become worthless within decades if currency is later debased, a real
risk in this specific historical window. A collegium without a clear
succession rule tends to collapse into factional dispute or simple
neglect the moment its founder dies. A library without an enforced
copying rota slowly loses works to wear, damp and insects faster than
they are replaced (see the preservation argument in `corpus_dispersed`).

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. Land sufficient to fund a modest permanent
staff of scribes, a librarian and basic building upkeep is a substantial
one-time capital commitment (the scale of a comfortable equestrian-level
estate, by rough analogy to Pliny's own documented benefactions, though
no single figure is given here since it depends entirely on local land
values and staff size chosen).

**Danger.** Political, not physical: an institution wealthy in land and
influential in what it teaches can attract the unwelcome attention of a
suspicious emperor or a rival benefactor. Cultivating it as visibly loyal,
useful, and unthreatening (teaching practical craft and record-keeping,
not politics) is the safer long-term posture.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the legal and financial structure (the licensed
collegium and land endowment are both well-attested Roman legal and
social practices, with a specific documented precedent in Pliny).
**MEDIUM** for the specific scribal-output figures, which are extrapolated
from later, non-Roman scriptorium data.

### record_keeping_and_accounting — Double-entry bookkeeping and the laboratory notebook

**What it is / why you want it.** A bookkeeping method where every
transaction is recorded twice, once as a debit in one account and once as
a credit in another, so that the accounts must balance and arithmetic or
copying errors surface automatically; and a companion habit of dated,
quantitative laboratory notebooks that record failures as carefully as
successes.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already keeps household and
business account books (the *codex accepti et expensi*, an account of
receipts and expenditures, is referenced as a legally significant record
in Roman rhetorical and legal sources), and already has professional
bankers (*argentarii*) who lend, exchange currency and keep ledgers. What
Rome does not have is the double-entry principle itself, where every
entry is deliberately recorded on both sides of the books so the totals
must reconcile; that formalisation does not appear historically until
medieval Italian merchant practice, with Luca Pacioli's 1494 treatise
being the first systematic published description. A single-entry ledger
can be quietly wrong for years with no way to detect it from the book
alone; a double-entry ledger that fails to balance announces an error
immediately.

**Prerequisites.** Literacy and basic arithmetic; no material
prerequisites.

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond ordinary writing materials; this
is a method, like `cryptography_and_codes`.

**Procedure.**
1. For every transaction, identify two accounts it affects (for example,
   "cash" and "goods purchased," or "owed to me" and "goods sold") and
   record the same value as a debit in one and a credit in the other.
2. Keep a running total (a "balance") for every account.
3. Periodically (for example, monthly) sum all debit balances and all
   credit balances across every account. If the two grand totals do not
   match, an error exists somewhere in the books, and the discrepancy can
   be hunted down before it compounds; this self-checking property is the
   entire point and is what makes the method valuable to anyone handling
   large or complex sums, which is precisely the position of an imperial
   treasury, a tax-farming company (*publicani*), a large trading house,
   or the land-endowed library described in `libraries_and_institutions`.
4. Sell this specifically and first to the *argentarii*: they already
   keep ledgers and understand the value of an audit-proof record, so the
   pitch requires no conceptual leap on their part, only the new method.
5. For your own and your successors' technical work, keep a standardised
   laboratory notebook, separate from financial accounts: every
   procedure attempted gets a dated entry with exact quantities and
   units (per the standard set in `corpus_written`), the outcome, and,
   crucially, negative results recorded with the same care as successes.
   State explicitly to whoever inherits this practice why negative
   results matter: an undocumented failure gets silently repeated by
   the next person, wasting years; a documented failure saves that time
   permanently. This is the direct opposite of the alchemical secrecy
   habit condemned in `corpus_written`, and is arguably the more
   important habit of the two, since even honestly written work is
   useless to a successor if only the successes were ever recorded.

**How you know it worked.** For bookkeeping: the books balance at each
period close, and when they do not, the error is traceable rather than
mysterious. For lab notebooks: a successor unfamiliar with your specific
work can, from the notebook alone, tell what has already been tried and
failed, and thereby avoid repeating it.

**Failure modes.** Double-entry bookkeeping only catches errors that
break the balance; a mistake entered identically (and wrongly) on both
sides still balances and still hides the error, so it is a strong check,
not a perfect one. A laboratory notebook kept inconsistently, some
entries dated and quantified, others vague, loses most of its value; the
discipline has to be uniform to be trustworthy.

**Cost & labour.** Essentially free; this is method and clerical
discipline, not capital. The only cost is the marginal time to make a
second entry per transaction and to write up negative results as
carefully as positive ones, both trivial next to the value of catching
errors early or not repeating a known dead end.

**Danger.** None physical or social; this is one of the safest, most
immediately sellable pieces of this entire module.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The double-entry mechanism is simple, well
understood, and its self-checking property is a matter of arithmetic,
not a Roman-era-specific claim. Rome's existing single-entry accounting
tradition and the *argentarii* as a market for this improvement are both
well attested.

## The redundancy arithmetic

This is an estimate, worked explicitly, using the loss rate stated in
this module's own premise: roughly 50 percent of a given surviving line
of copies is lost per century (fire, war, damp, simple neglect, treated
here as one combined hazard rate for lack of better data; this is a
simplification, not a measured Roman-era figure).

Treat each independently located copy as having, per century, a 50
percent (0.5) chance of surviving and a 50 percent chance of being lost,
independently of every other copy, and assume the same rate applies over
each of the three centuries between 100 AD and the roughly 400 AD horizon
this module is reasoning about (three named crisis windows, the Antonine
Plague, the Plague of Cyprian, and the Crisis of the Third Century, all
fall within that span).

A single copy's chance of surviving all three centuries is
0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.125, or 12.5 percent. Left as a single copy, the
corpus has an 87.5 percent chance of being entirely lost before it is
ever needed. This is the concrete, numeric version of why `corpus_dispersed`
carries roughly as much weight in the ablation study as the press itself.

For N independent copies, the chance that at least one survives all three
centuries is 1 minus the chance that all of them are lost, or
1 - (1 - 0.125)^N = 1 - 0.875^N. Solving for the number of copies needed
to reach a given confidence level (DERIVED from the stated 50
percent-per-century assumption):

- 90 percent confidence at least one copy survives: N is about 17.2,
  round up to 18 independent copies.
- 95 percent confidence: N is about 22.4, round up to 23 independent
  copies.
- 99 percent confidence: N is about 34.5, round up to 35 independent
  copies.

Two corrections push the practical target higher than this simple
calculation suggests. First, copies are not actually independent: two
copies held in the same city share fire and war risk, two copies held by
members of the same family share the risk of that family's fortune, so
the effective number of independent copies is smaller than the raw count
unless you deliberately spread copies across cities, provinces, and
institution types as `corpus_dispersed` describes; a raw count of 23
copies clustered in three cities is not the same as 23 copies spread
across twenty. Second, the underlying hazard is not smooth, it is lumpy,
concentrated specifically in the named crisis windows rather than evenly
spread across the three centuries, so a copy's real risk depends heavily
on exactly where it sits when a specific plague or civil war hits its
city, not on an even background rate.

The other correction runs the other way and matters more in practice:
this calculation assumes a fixed, static set of copies with no recopying.
A real institution (see `libraries_and_institutions`) does not just hold
a copy passively for three hundred years, it recopies its exemplar
periodically, particularly when a copy shows wear, well within a human
generation (roughly 25-30 years). Active recopying resets the effective
age of a copy long before the assumed century-scale loss rate would apply
to it, so a small number of well-run, actively copying institutions can
in practice outperform a much larger number of passively stored,
never-recopied copies. The real design target, in other words, is not
"bury enough copies and hope," it is "fund enough independent,
actively-copying institutions," which is exactly why `corpus_dispersed`
and `libraries_and_institutions` are written as companion entries.

Practical recommendation from this reasoning: aim for on the order of 25
to 40 independently located copies at any given time (comfortably above
the 95 percent line, with margin for the correlation and lumpy-hazard
corrections above), spread across at least three different institution
types, at least four or five distinct provinces or regions, at least one
copy in Egypt specifically for the dry-climate preservation advantage,
and at least one copy genuinely outside the Empire's own political risk.
Treat this whole section as ESTIMATED: the 50 percent-per-century figure
is a stated planning assumption from this module's premise, not a
measured historical loss rate, and the independence assumption is known
to be optimistic. Use the arithmetic for its structure (redundancy needs
to scale, correlated risk needs to be actively broken by geographic and
institutional spread, recopying beats static storage) rather than for the
exact numbers.

## What to write first if you have one year to live

Assume you die tomorrow and today is the only day you get to write. Order
of priority, reasoned from (value if it survives) times (probability it
is lost if you do not write it now) times (how much it multiplies the
value of everything written after it):

1. **The condensed version of this module itself**, specifically the
   paper, ink and press recipes, even in rough form on whatever writing
   surface you already have (papyrus or wax tablet, do not wait for a
   paper mill you may not live to build). This is the meta-priority: if
   nothing else you write can be copied and dispersed, none of it
   survives the coming plagues regardless of how good it is. If you
   genuinely have only hours, write the ink recipe and the press
   description before anything else, since ink and a press can be
   improvised from an existing screw press faster than paper can be
   produced at scale, and even a handful of hand-copied sheets are better
   than zero.
2. **Basic hygiene and epidemic-response measures** (boiling drinking
   water, isolating the visibly sick, hand-washing before treating
   wounds or food, burning or burying the dead promptly rather than
   leaving remains near water sources). This is outside this module's
   own tech entries, but it is the single highest lives-saved-per-word
   investment available to you given that an Antonine Plague-scale
   epidemic is coming within your likely lifetime or your children's; a
   single well-copied page of correct sanitary practice can save more
   lives directly than any device in this entire tree. (See a dedicated
   public health module if one exists in this corpus; if not, write this
   yourself, briefly, today.)
3. **Positional numerals and the concept of zero**, with a short worked
   example of arithmetic done in the new system versus Roman numerals.
   Rome has neither positional notation nor a zero digit. A single page
   introducing this multiplies the value of every other quantitative
   entry in the whole corpus, present and future, and costs almost
   nothing to write.
4. **The physically reproducible unit-standard definitions** described in
   `corpus_written`, so every number written by you or anyone after you
   is actually interpretable two centuries later, currency and official
   weight standards having no guarantee of surviving intact.
5. **This module's dispersal instructions** (`corpus_dispersed` and the
   redundancy arithmetic above), written explicitly enough that whoever
   finds your papers, even if they never met you, understands why they
   must be copied and scattered rather than kept as a single treasured
   original.
6. **Whatever single piece of metallurgy or medicine you personally know
   best and are most likely to be the only source for.** This is
   deliberately last and deliberately vague, because it is the one item
   on this list that depends entirely on your own specific modern
   knowledge; write down whatever you know most completely and most
   uniquely, in the plain, worked-example style of `corpus_written`,
   rather than a broad shallow survey of everything you half-remember.

The ordering above is a judgement call, not a measured result, but the
reasoning behind it (multiplier technologies and lives-saved-per-word
first, narrow personal expertise last) is the load-bearing part; adapt
the specific sixth item to whatever you actually know.
