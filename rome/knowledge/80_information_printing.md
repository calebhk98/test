# Module 80: Paper, Printing and the Survival of Knowledge

## Why this module outranks every furnace in the tree

Ablation testing on this programme's own simulation of the tech tree
shows four effects larger than any single piece of metallurgy or motive
power in the whole plan. Deleting `corpus_written` (writing the technical
corpus down at all) delays the transistor by roughly 90 years. Deleting
`corpus_dispersed` (copying and scattering it) costs roughly 50 years.
Deleting `printing_press` costs roughly 38 years. Deleting `rag_paper`
costs roughly 32 years. Nothing else in the tree, no furnace, no engine,
comes close.

The reason: the Roman world you have landed in is going to be hit by
three empire-wide catastrophes before a transistor is remotely possible,
the Antonine Plague (roughly 165-180 AD, killing a large share of the
population in affected areas per Galen and other ancient witnesses), the
Plague of Cyprian (roughly 249-262 AD, similarly severe), and the Crisis
of the Third Century (235-284 AD, sustained civil war, currency collapse
and provincial fragmentation). Any programme that depends on you
personally, one workshop, or one patron dies in one of these windows. A
programme that depends on information existing in enough independent,
scattered copies survives them, because a plague or a civil war cannot
simultaneously burn every copy of a text held in Antioch, Alexandria,
Massilia, Ctesiphon and a monastery beyond the frontier at once.

**Your most durable invention is not a device, it is a document, copied
and dispersed enough to outlive you and three empire-wide catastrophes.**
Paper, ink and the press make that document cheap enough to exist in
dozens of copies instead of three. Everything else here (codices,
libraries, cryptography, the semaphore) exists to write, protect, move or
fund that corpus. A working furnace only you know how to run is a
rounding error next to a corpus a stranger can rebuild it from in 280 AD.

### rag_paper - Rag paper (*charta linea*, linen-rag paper)

**What it is / why you want it.** A cheap, foldable writing surface made
by pulping old linen and hemp cloth and felting it on a screen. Its raw
input, worn cloth, exists in every city in the Empire, decoupling you
from the Egyptian papyrus trade and, at scale, from the cost of parchment.

**Why you would never guess this.** Cloth as a writing-material feedstock
is not an obvious idea in a world with papyrus and parchment already. Cai
Lun's process is conventionally dated to 105 AD in China and does not
reach the Mediterranean historically for roughly a thousand years
(Muslim Spain, 12th century; common in Christian Europe only by the
13th-14th). The mould-and-deckle workflow, not the chemistry, is the
genuinely novel part.

**Prerequisites.** A water-driven trip hammer or stamping mill (existing
hydraulic-milling capability, as used for grain hammer mills), a screw
press (already exists for oil and wine), lime burning (already exists for
mortar), fine wire-drawing or reed/rush weaving for the mould screen.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen rags (*linteum*, recycled through the
existing rag trade), hemp rope offcuts (*cannabis*, Italy and Gaul),
quicklime (*calx viva*), wool felts (*coactilia*), bronze/brass wire or a
woven reed screen (closer to earliest Chinese practice, an easier first
step), animal glue (*gluten*) or wheat starch (*amylum*, already used by
Romans per Pliny) for sizing.

**Procedure.**
1. Sort rags: pure linen and hemp only (wool does not felt the same way).
2. Ret in slow water for days to weeks until the weave loosens.
3. Lime: steep in a dilute lime-water slurry for several days to loosen
   fats and dyes and begin separating fibre bundles; rinse thoroughly,
   residual lime embrittles the finished sheet.
4. Beat the wet, limed rag for several hours in a water-driven stamping
   mill (iron-shod hammers lifted by waterwheel cams) to an even, creamy
   pulp with no thread fragments.
5. Dilute in a vat to a thin, uniform suspension.
6. Dip a mould (wire or reed screen with a removable deckle) flat into
   the vat, lift level, and shake side to side and front to back so
   fibres tangle in two directions, the source of the sheet's strength.
7. Drain; a thin felted mat remains on the screen.
8. Couch: invert onto a damp wool felt, transfer the sheet, stack
   felt/paper/felt in a "post."
9. Press the post in the screw press to squeeze out water; separate
   sheets from felts while damp and air-dry.
10. Size with warm dilute glue or cooked starch, then dry again.
    Unsized paper blots ink; oversized paper repels it.

**How you know it worked.** Held to light, an even mottled texture with
no thin or lumpy patches. Tears irregularly, not along one line. A drop
of ink sits and soaks slightly, no fuzzy halo.

**Failure modes.** Under-beaten rag leaves lumps and weak spots. Unrinsed
lime makes brittle paper that cracks within months. Too little size
blots ink; too much repels it. One-direction shaking gives a grained
sheet that curls and splits.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED (basis: medieval/early modern European hand
mills; no Roman data exists). A trained two-person vat crew (dipper,
coucher) at a mature mill is commonly reported at roughly 1,000-2,000
sheets per day; a first-year Roman crew should be planned at a quarter to
half that. Papyrus is a near-monopoly Nile Delta crop (Pliny, *NH*
13.68-83), carrying transport and monopoly cost outside Egypt and fixed
to one geography. A prepared skin (*membrana*) yields only ESTIMATED 2-4
leaves depending on animal and page size, so a substantial book consumes
dozens to hundreds of animals plus many hours of skilled scraping. Late
medieval Europe commonly reports paper at roughly a fifth to a sixth of
parchment's cost per page (MEASURED, but from a later economy; treat the
direction as reliable, the ratio as a rough guide only). Strategically,
papyrus makes you dependent on Egypt, parchment on the livestock economy;
rag paper depends only on cloth and water power, both under your control.

**Danger.** Lime baths are caustic; rinse hands, avoid eyes. Stamping
mill hammers can crush fingers; disengage the drive before clearing the
trough. Socially unremarkable, reads as fuller's work plus a mill.

**Confidence: HIGH** for chemistry and mechanics. **MEDIUM** for
throughput and cost, extrapolated from a later European industry.

### ink_oil_based - Printer's ink (no attested Roman name)

**What it is / why you want it.** A thick, oil-based black ink that wets
and clings to metal type and transfers a clean film to paper. Without it
movable type is decorative, not usable.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious move is Rome's existing
ink, iron gall or soot-and-gum ink, both water-based and made to be drawn
by capillary action into porous papyrus, parchment or paper. Metal type
is smooth and non-porous: water-based ink beads off it (the same reason
water beads on a greasy pan) instead of wetting into a thin even film, so
transfer is patchy, drying is uneven, and it corrodes the type. This is a
real historical blocker: even Gutenberg's first experiments with
conventional ink failed, and a separate oil-based ink had to be developed.

**Prerequisites.** Pressed linseed oil (from flax, using the existing
screw press), pine resin/rosin (*resina*), lampblack (*atramentum*).

**Roman-available inputs.** Linseed (*semen lini*), pine or fir resin,
lamp soot, an iron cauldron.

**Procedure.**
1. Press flax seed for linseed oil, as with olives.
2. Heat slowly outdoors, well clear of any wooden structure; hold at a
   slow simmer for several hours until it darkens and thickens. A drop
   from a stirring rod should "rope," forming a thin thread rather than
   falling as a drop, when cooked enough. Never let it hard-boil.
3. Stir in crushed pine rosin, roughly one part rosin to several parts
   oil by weight (ESTIMATED, basis: reconstructed early European
   printers' ink formulas, which themselves varied by workshop; tune by
   testing). Rosin adds tack and gloss.
4. Cool to a heavy dark varnish.
5. Grind lampblack into the varnish on a stone slab with a muller until
   smooth, glossy and gritless.

**How you know it worked.** Dabbed on clean bronze or iron, it spreads
into a thin tacky film, not beads. Pressed onto sized paper, it leaves a
sharp mark with no spreading.

**Failure modes.** Under-cooked oil stays thin and beads like scribal
ink. Over-cooked or unwatched oil can scorch or ignite. Too little rosin
gives poor transfer; too much gives gummy, non-releasing ink. Coarse
lampblack prints grey and grainy.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. A day's cooking by one worker plus a day's
grinding by a second should supply a substantial print run; no measured
Roman batch-yield figure exists. Materials are cheap and already produced
across the Empire.

**Danger.** Serious fire hazard: overheated linseed oil can ignite (the
same oxidative chemistry behind oil-soaked rags spontaneously combusting).
Cook outdoors, keep a lid or wet cloth to smother flare-ups (never water
on hot oil), never leave it unattended. Socially harmless, reads as a
varnish-maker.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the chemistry and confirmed Roman inputs.
Proportions are the only soft part, flagged as such.

### punchcutting_and_type - Movable type (*typi mobiles*, invented term)

**What it is / why you want it.** A system for cutting one master letter
per character, then casting it thousands of times identically, so a page
is assembled from reusable pieces instead of hand-copied each time. This
is the real bottleneck of printing; the press is a minor adaptation.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already has every underlying
skill in a different application: mint die-engravers cut hardened steel
dies to strike coins, a punch-and-matrix process in all but name. Nobody
has redirected that at reusable, interchangeable letterforms. Obvious
once you want it: the metallurgy and hand skill already exist in the
mint, the application to type has simply never occurred to anyone.

**Prerequisites.** Case-hardened tool steel (bloomery iron cemented with
charcoal, an established ancient process; Noricum steel, *chalybs
Noricus*, is an attested example), copper stock for matrices, lead, tin
and antimony, an existing die-engraving or fine-metalwork craftsman.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cemented iron/steel rod, copper, lead
(Britain, Spain), tin (Cornwall), stibnite ore (*stibium*/*stimmi*,
antimony sulfide, already traded as a cosmetic per Pliny and
Dioscorides), scrap iron (to reduce stibnite to metallic antimony).

**Procedure.**
1. Cut the punch: engrave a mirror-image relief letter on a steel rod
   with gravers and files. For letters with enclosed spaces (o, e, a),
   drive a small counterpunch first, then finish the outer shape by hand,
   the same skill already used for coin dies and gem engraving.
2. Harden and temper: heat to bright cherry-red (roughly 750-800 C),
   quench, then reheat gently until the surface takes a straw-to-bronze
   temper colour (roughly 220-280 C) and quench again.
3. Strike the matrix: hammer the punch squarely into a softer copper bar,
   sinking a mirror-image intaglio letter.
4. Justify: file the matrix so the letter sits at a consistent depth and
   position, so every cast letter shares a common baseline and height.
   This hand-fitting is the real labour bottleneck; one badly justified
   sort visibly sits crooked on every page it appears in.
5. Cast: clamp the matrix into an adjustable two-part hand mould sized to
   the letter's body height, pour molten type metal, wait seconds, open
   and knock out the sort, trim the surplus ("jet").
6. Repeat step 5 as needed; one matrix casts effectively unlimited
   identical sorts. A skilled caster can be expected to cast on the order
   of a few thousand sorts per working day (ESTIMATED, basis: commonly
   cited figures of roughly 3,000-4,000 for hand-mould casting in early
   European printing; no Roman analogue exists, treat as a mature target,
   not a first-week result).

**Type metal alloy.** ESTIMATED (basis: widely reported later
typefounding ranges, themselves empirically tuned rather than fixed):
mostly lead, a substantial share of antimony, a smaller share of tin.
Antimony is the crucial, non-obvious ingredient: unlike most metals it
expands slightly on solidifying, counteracting the alloy's shrinkage so
the cast letter fills every fine detail of the matrix instead of pulling
away, and it hardens the alloy (pure lead type batters flat quickly).
Tin improves flow into fine detail. Stibnite heated with scrap iron
yields metallic antimony (iron takes the sulfur, having a stronger
affinity for it than antimony does), a straightforward reaction using
furnace heat Rome already has.

**How you know it worked.** Crisp, fully formed serifs and counters, no
rounding. A row of different sorts stood upright shows level tops against
a straightedge. Sorts do not deform when squeezed.

**Failure modes.** Under-justified matrix casts a letter high or low.
Too little antimony gives soft, mushy, quickly-worn type. Metal poured
too cool fails to fill fine counters; too hot warps the mould or erodes
the matrix. A punch skipped through tempering chips when struck.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED throughout, no Roman-era data exists.
Cutting a full alphabet is a job of weeks for one skilled engraver.
Striking and justifying matrices takes comparable or longer. Casting,
once matrices exist, runs at the few-thousand-per-day figure above.
Materials (lead, tin, and modest stibnite smelting) are cheap.

**Danger.** Molten metal burns; lead fumes from overheated metal are a
genuine poisoning risk with prolonged exposure, work ventilated and avoid
overheating the melt. Reads as fine metalwork or minting-adjacent craft,
socially respectable.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the metallurgy and process. **MEDIUM** for exact
alloy ratios and daily output, both flagged as estimates from later,
non-Roman practice.

### printing_press - The printing press (adapted from *prelum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine that brings a sheet into
even, controlled contact with a locked page of inked type in one motion,
replacing hand-rubbing.

**Why you would never guess this.** Obvious once you have type: Rome
already runs large wooden screw presses for oil and wine (*prelum*,
described in Cato and Columella, attested archaeologically), so the
heavy engineering, an accurate wooden screw and a frame that resists it,
is already solved. The adaptation is what sits under the screw: a flat
platen, a way to hold paper and ink type without wasting the crew's time,
and a way to move the heavy type bed without moving the screw.

**Prerequisites.** An existing screw press, `punchcutting_and_type`,
`ink_oil_based`, `rag_paper` or another writing surface.

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or elm timber (already used for oil and
wine presses), iron fittings, leather-covered ink dabbers ("ink balls"),
parchment or oiled paper for the tympan.

**Procedure (building and operating the press).**
1. Build a standard screw-press frame, scaled down from an oil press.
2. Fit a flat platen, ideally metal-faced, to the screw's base in place
   of a pressing board.
3. Build the "coffin," a wheeled tray sliding on rails, holding the
   forme (a locked frame or "chase" of type, wedged tight with wooden or
   metal quoins). This travelling bed lets the crew ink and load paper
   away from the screw, then slide the assembly under it for the pull.
4. Build the tympan, a light frame covered with taut parchment or oiled
   paper, hinged to the coffin, that holds the blank sheet.
5. Build the frisket, a lighter hinged frame with a window cut to the
   printed area, folding down over the tympan before both fold onto the
   forme, masking margins from ink.
6. Per sheet: ink the type by dabbing (not brushing) with ink balls, lay
   a fresh sheet on the tympan, fold frisket then tympan onto the forme,
   slide the coffin under the platen, pull the bar for one firm even
   motion, release, slide out, remove the sheet, repeat.

**How you know it worked.** Clean, even, dark impression on every letter,
no ghosting (double image from movement) and no grey patches (uneven
pressure or packing).

**Failure modes.** Too little pressure gives faint or patchy type; too
much crushes fine letters or strains the screw. A misjustified forme
prints unevenly. Loose quoins let the forme shift, blurring lines. Uneven
inking gives patchy blacks.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED (basis: the "common press" era of early
modern European printing, the closest comparable technology; no Roman
data exists). A trained two-person crew (inker, bar operator) working a
full day is commonly reported at roughly 2,000-3,000 impressions in that
later context. A newly trained Roman crew should be planned at a third to
half of that, rising with practice. Capital cost is modest, timber and
iron the Empire already produces; the slow, expensive part is cutting
punches and casting type, not building the press.

**Danger.** Crushed fingers from the platen or coffin rollers, keep hands
clear during the pull. No fire or poison risk beyond the ink itself.
Reads as an ordinary mechanic's workshop built around a familiar press
type.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the mechanics, a direct adaptation of a widely
attested Roman machine. **MEDIUM** for throughput, extrapolated from a
later European industry.

### binding_and_codex - Binding and the codex (*codex*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Folding sheets into gathered sections
("quires"), sewing through the fold, and attaching boards and a cover, to
make a compact, durable, randomly accessible book instead of a roll.

**Why you would never guess this.** Obvious once you want it, and it
already exists in Rome: the codex is attested by the late 1st century AD
(Martial describes parchment codices as compact, portable gift books
under Domitian), and cord-bound wax-tablet notebooks (*pugillares*,
*tabulae*) are already ordinary. What is missing is the codex becoming
the default format for serious literature, still the roll (*volumen*)
through this period; you are accelerating a transition already underway,
not inventing binding from nothing.

**Prerequisites.** `rag_paper`, parchment or papyrus sheets, cord or
sinew thread, wooden boards (oak or beech), vegetable-tanned leather.

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or beech boards, waxed linen thread or
sinew, vegetable-tanned calf or goat leather, animal glue.

**Procedure.**
1. Fold sheets into quires, for example four sheets folded together
   giving eight leaves, sixteen pages.
2. Sew each quire through its fold onto raised cords or thongs on a
   sewing frame, linking successive quires along the same cords for a
   structurally continuous spine.
3. Lace the cord ends into drilled channels in wooden boards, mechanically
   attaching the boards to the sewn block, not merely gluing them.
4. Cover boards and spine in leather, glued and laced through slits at
   the board edges.
5. Fit a clasp or tie under tension to hold the closed book flat, which
   resists page cockling and warping in damp far better than loose
   sheets, a direct defence given the preservation problem in
   `corpus_dispersed`.

**How you know it worked.** Lies reasonably flat when opened without the
sewing pulling loose; boards do not rack when the closed book is shaken.

**Failure modes.** Thread too thin or unwaxed cuts through the fold.
Loosely laced boards rack and tear the cords out. Glued-only leather
peels with humidity.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. A day or two for one skilled binder on a
modest book once boards and leather are ready; small relative to the cost
of the writing surface or the writing itself.

**Danger.** None beyond ordinary craft-tool hazards. Codices already
circulate informally and among early Christian and Jewish communities, so
this reads as an existing, if not yet dominant, format.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Materials and technique are well within confirmed
Roman-era craft; the codex's existence by this date is textually attested.

### corpus_written - Writing the corpus so a stranger 200 years later can use it

**What it is / why you want it.** A deliberate writing standard: plain
declarative language, explicit numbers with units, worked examples,
labelled diagrams, no allegory, no withheld steps, and redundancy built
into each document, so a reader who never met you can reconstruct the
process from a single surviving copy generations later.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious model for important
technical knowledge in the Roman world is craft-guild and proto-alchemical
practice: oral master-to-apprentice transmission, or, where written at
all, deliberately allegorical and riddling (visible in surviving
Greco-Egyptian craft-recipe papyri, and pronounced in the alchemical
corpus of later centuries). This is the single worst intellectual habit
of the ancient world, and should be named as such: obscurity does not
protect knowledge, it kills it, because the moment the one transmission
chain breaks (death, plague, war), nothing plain enough to reconstruct is
left behind. Whole bodies of ancient craft knowledge are lost this way,
not forgotten by everyone at once, but known only to one line of people
who never wrote it plainly. This module is built as the opposite.

**Prerequisites.** `rag_paper`, a stable writing implement and ink,
literacy in the intended reader's language.

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond ordinary writing materials; this
entry is method, not materials.

**Procedure (the standard to write to).**
1. Define units before using them. Rome has weights and measures
   (*libra*, *uncia*, *pes*) but no metric system and no guarantee they
   stay consistent across centuries or provinces. Anchor at least one
   unit to something physically reproducible, for example a unit of mass
   defined as the weight of a stated volume of clean rainwater, so it can
   be re-derived even if every physical reference standard is lost or
   debased (see the currency collapse noted in `record_keeping_and_accounting`).
2. Write numbered procedures with real numbers, real units, and an
   observable success test, as this module does. State temperatures both
   as a number and a sense-observable proxy, your reader has no
   thermometer.
3. Include at least one fully worked numeric example per procedure.
4. Include labelled diagrams wherever shape or assembly order matters; a
   picture survives drifted language better than prose does.
5. Never encode for secrecy. Control access to the physical copy and its
   institution if something is genuinely dangerous, never obscure the
   text itself. An encoded secret is only recoverable by someone with the
   key; a controlled plain text is recoverable by anyone the institution
   later chooses to trust.
6. Build redundancy inside a single document: restate key numbers in a
   closing summary, repeat critical warnings, include a full index and a
   glossary of coined terms, so a damaged or partial copy still yields
   its most important facts.
7. Write in the plainest register available, avoiding the metaphor and
   rhetorical flourish that is the normal register of educated Latin and
   Greek prose, which actively works against exact reconstruction later.

**How you know it worked.** Hand a draft to someone with no more
background than the corpus provides and have them attempt the procedure
from the text alone. Success, or a predictable failure the text already
warned about, means the writing worked.

**Failure modes.** Writing that assumes shared context with the author
reads complete to the writer and is unusable to the reader. Withholding
steps out of guild habit defeats the entire purpose.

**Cost & labour.** A discipline cost, not a materials one. ESTIMATED at
roughly double the drafting time of loose prose, trivial against the
value of surviving intact use two centuries later.

**Danger.** None physical. Socially double-edged: a corpus this legible
is legible to rivals too. Control what you disperse widely (general
craft and science) against what stays in trusted institutional custody
(anything with clear destructive application) through who holds the
copy, not by obscuring the text.

**Confidence: HIGH.** A methodological argument, not a physical claim;
the failure mode it warns against is well attested across the ancient and
medieval craft and alchemical traditions.

### corpus_dispersed - Dispersing the corpus

**What it is / why you want it.** A deliberate plan for how many copies
exist, where, in what institutions, in what languages, and how far
outside Rome's own political and climatic risk some sit. One brilliant
corpus in one library in Rome is one fire away from the 90-year delay the
ablation study warns about.

**Why you would never guess this.** It looks finished once written. It
is not. The key fact is archaeological: papyrus and other organic
materials survive two thousand years almost exclusively in Egypt's
exceptionally dry climate (hence the enormous surviving volume from sites
like Oxyrhynchus). In the western provinces, organic material essentially
does not survive normal damp-temperate conditions that long; the rare
exceptions (Herculaneum's carbonised scrolls, sealed by volcanic
material; Vindolanda's waterlogged, anaerobic tablets) are freak
microclimates, not something to plan around. A copy in a normal western
building is losing to damp and insects before fire or war are even
counted.

**Prerequisites.** Multiple physical copies (`printing_press` makes this
cheap), `binding_and_codex`, trusted custodians
(`libraries_and_institutions`), and, for the farthest copies, existing
Roman trade contact with Parthia/Persia, India, and indirectly China.

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing trade infrastructure reaching past
Rome's borders: Red Sea and Indian Ocean trade with India is documented
(the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, the Muziris papyrus), and Chinese
court records describe an embassy from "Andun" (identified with Marcus
Aurelius) arriving by sea in 166 AD, proof a channel exists even if thin.

**Procedure.**
1. Place copies in institutions with different failure modes: temple
   archives (unusually long institutional continuity), municipal
   archives (*tabularia*, vulnerable to exactly the civic and military
   disruption the Crisis of the Third Century brings), private family
   libraries of cultivated patrons (fragile individually, numerous and
   cheap to seed), and the licensed collegium library of
   `libraries_and_institutions`, the one you can actually govern.
2. Place copies in more than one climate, prioritising at least one copy
   in Egypt specifically, plus copies in Italy, Gaul and the east.
3. Place at least one or two copies genuinely outside the Empire's
   political risk, reachable via Parthian/Persian territory or the Indian
   Ocean trade into India; a crisis of Roman central authority does not
   touch those.
4. Translate at least a summary or index into a second language early, so
   a foreign custodian has reason to value, not discard, what it holds.
5. Treat dispersal as ongoing, not a one-time seeding; see the redundancy
   arithmetic below.

**How you know it worked.** Not personally checkable; a generational bet.
The nearest available check is process: confirm each custodian has
actually catalogued the text (`libraries_and_institutions`) and included
it in a live copying rota, not merely accepted a delivery.

**Failure modes.** Copies clustered in one city or family correlate their
risk. A copy an institution never reads or values is the first thing
recycled (parchment and paper scraping and reuse, as in later palimpsests,
is a real, attested fate for texts that fall out of use). A copy sent
abroad in a language nobody there reads fails faster.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. Once `printing_press` exists, marginal
copy cost is mainly paper and press time, cheap; the real constraint is
the labour and trust to place and maintain each copy with a genuine
custodian, and, for distant copies, trade-journey risk and cost.

**Danger.** Politically, exporting Roman technical knowledge to Parthia,
a rival Rome fights repeated wars against, could read as treasonous to a
hostile magistrate; route such copies through merchant or
religious/scholarly channels, not official ones, and weigh what
categories are safe to disperse that widely (see `corpus_written`).

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The preservation-bias argument is well attested.
The dispersal plan is sound reasoning from that fact but is inherently
forward-looking, not a measured result.

### semaphore_telegraph - Optical telegraph chain (modelled on Chappe's semaphore)

**What it is / why you want it.** A chain of towers, each in sight of the
next, each carrying a mechanical arm signal read through a telescope and
relayed onward, turning a message that takes weeks by horse into hours.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already has excellent long-
distance courier infrastructure (*cursus publicus*), so the instinct is
to make couriers faster, not replace them with line-of-sight relay. The
design is modelled directly on Claude Chappe's semaphore (France, from
the 1790s): a mast carrying a pivoting crossbar ("regulator") with a
shorter indicator arm at each end, together able to form many distinct
shape-positions, read at the next tower and copied onward. The genuinely
non-obvious parts for a Roman engineer are the discipline of a fixed,
shared codebook, so an arbitrary shape means one specific thing to every
operator, and the dependence on optical aid: without ground-glass
telescopes (conventionally dated to the early 17th century, and not on
the "Romans already have" list even though Rome has good clear glass and
glassblowing), spacing is limited to what the naked eye can resolve, far
shorter than Chappe's historical spacing.

**Prerequisites.** Timber and iron fittings (routine carpentry and
smithing), a fixed shared codebook (pairs with `cryptography_and_codes`),
and, for long-distance spacing, a working telescope, itself dependent on
a separate lens-grinding prerequisite not covered here.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber, iron fittings, cordage, clear or
near-colourless blown glass (confirmed Roman capability; grinding it into
a telescope lens is a distinct, harder skill not assumed here).

**Procedure.**
1. Site towers on high ground or existing structures (city walls, hilltop
   watch-forts) at whatever spacing your optics support.
2. Build each tower with a mast carrying a pivoting regulator bar and two
   independently rotatable indicator arms, one per end, forming a small
   set of fixed positions (Chappe's system is MEASURED, as a historical
   figure, to have offered on the order of a couple of hundred distinct
   combinations, though reported counts vary by source).
3. Establish a fixed codebook shared by every station: common words,
   phrases, numbers and place names each get a single position, spelled
   letter by letter only when necessary. This dual system (fast codebook,
   slow letter fallback) is what makes it usable, and doubles as a code
   even for someone who can see the tower but lacks the book (see
   `cryptography_and_codes`).
4. Station one or two operators per tower, one watching the previous
   tower (telescope, or naked eye at closer spacing) and calling the
   position, one setting the same on their own tower for the next station.
5. Relay by repeating this handoff down the chain.

**How you know it worked.** A test message sent from one end, independently
transcribed at the other, matches the original, with any mismatch
traceable to a specific tower for retraining.

**Failure modes.** Fog, rain, snow and darkness stop the chain entirely
(a real, attested limitation of the historical system). One poorly
trained operator garbles everything beyond them. Spacing set beyond what
the available optics resolve fails outright.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED throughout, no Roman-era figures exist.
Chappe's network spaced towers roughly 10-15 km apart with telescopes
(MEASURED, later European figure), and a signal could reportedly cross
several hundred km in well under an hour in good conditions; the win is
end-to-end latency, not bulk throughput, each symbol still took several
seconds to set and confirm per relay. Without telescopes, plan
conservatively for perhaps a few km spacing at best (ESTIMATED, basis:
rough visual-acuity reasoning), meaning several times as many towers for
the same distance. Per-tower construction: ESTIMATED at a few hundred to
roughly one to two thousand denarii for the mechanism, fittings and
modest housing (basis: rough comparison to small timber structures and
several weeks of skilled labour, carpenters earning on the order of a few
denarii a day per Diocletian's Price Edict of 301 AD, used only as a
rough later-Empire wage proxy), substantially less if an existing
watchtower or fort is reused. Per-kilometre cost is tower cost divided by
spacing (DERIVED): at roughly 1,000 denarii per tower and 10 km spacing
with telescopes, about 100 denarii per km; several times that per km
without telescopes.

**Danger.** Minimal physical risk beyond working at height. This is the
single best demonstration to buy imperial or provincial patronage with:
an empire's basic administrative problem is that a message from Rome to
the Rhine takes weeks by the fastest normal relay (ESTIMATED/DERIVED at
roughly three weeks for about 1,500 km, from a planning courier speed on
the order of 70-80 km per day; emergency riders do somewhat better but
not by an order of magnitude). A demonstrable same-day or next-day signal
over even a modest distance is a dramatic, immediately legible proof of
value.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The tower-and-arm mechanism is simple, well
attested by its later French implementation, and well within Roman
carpentry. Throughput, spacing and cost figures are extrapolated or
order-of-magnitude estimates, flagged throughout; treat none as more than
a planning baseline.

### cryptography_and_codes - Ciphers beyond the Caesar shift

**What it is / why you want it.** Rome already uses a simple substitution
cipher for military correspondence (Suetonius, *Divus Iulius* 56,
attributes to Caesar a fixed alphabet shift). You can offer meaningfully
stronger methods at essentially no material cost, one of the cheapest,
fastest routes to becoming trusted by the state, particularly whoever
runs the *cursus publicus* or military communications, which also puts
you exactly where you need to be for `semaphore_telegraph` and
`libraries_and_institutions` support.

**Why you would never guess this.** Obvious once you know it is weak: a
fixed shift has only as many keys as the alphabet has letters, and
preserves letter-frequency patterns, so trying all shifts or studying
frequency breaks it quickly. Rome has neither concept.

**Prerequisites.** Literacy, `corpus_written` style documentation so the
method can be taught and audited, not just performed by you personally.

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond paper and ink; a method, like
`record_keeping_and_accounting`.

**Procedure.**
1. Teach frequency analysis as an attack first, so a patron understands
   why the Caesar shift is weak: count letter frequency in a sample and
   match it against known language patterns. Sellable on its own, it lets
   you read intercepted correspondence others cannot.
2. Introduce polyalphabetic substitution as the defence: a short
   repeating keyword shifts each letter by an amount set by the
   corresponding keyword letter, cycling across the message, so the same
   plaintext letter maps to different ciphertext letters by position,
   smoothing the frequency pattern an attacker would exploit. (This class
   of cipher is not formalised in Europe until the 15th-16th centuries, a
   genuine multi-century advance for Rome.)
3. Introduce transposition as a second layer: rearrange letters by a
   fixed pattern (for example, writing into a grid of agreed width and
   reading columns off in an agreed order). Combined with substitution
   this is considerably harder to break.
4. Note the theoretical limit, a key used once and never reused, as
   unbreakable in principle but impractical for routine traffic, since it
   needs a fresh key at least as long as the message securely
   distributed; keep expectations realistic.
5. Pair a codebook (as in `semaphore_telegraph`) with a cipher layer for
   anything sent by tower, since the codebook alone is not secret to
   anyone who watches long enough and obtains a copy.

**How you know it worked.** Give a literate person without the key a
sample message; a well-applied polyalphabetic cipher should resist casual
frequency analysis far longer than a Caesar shift.

**Failure modes.** A short, reused keyword is vulnerable to more advanced
attacks beyond a likely 100 AD adversary, but do not oversell it as
absolute security. Poor key handling undermines even a strong cipher.

**Cost & labour.** Essentially free, paper, ink and a trained clerk.
Near-zero outlay, immediate legible value, and legitimate access to
official correspondence infrastructure.

**Danger.** Politically double-edged: valuable, but visible skill at
codebreaking can make a suspicious regime want closer control of you.
Being seen as loyal and useful, not independent, is the safer posture.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Both the Caesar cipher's weakness and the mechanics
of polyalphabetic substitution and transposition are basic, well-
understood classical cryptography; the Caesar attestation is solid.

### libraries_and_institutions - A library that is also a school and an archive

**What it is / why you want it.** A durable legal and organisational
structure, a licensed corporate body with a permanent endowment, that
holds, catalogues, copies and teaches the corpus so the programme does
not depend on your survival, memory or presence.

**Why you would never guess this.** Not a stretch culturally: the
Hellenistic world already has a great library with a systematic catalogue
(Callimachus's *Pinakes* for the Library of Alexandria, attested by later
sources), so "a library with a real catalogue" is legible, not foreign.
The non-obvious part is the legal and financial engineering: making the
institution outlive its founder with no concept of a perpetual
corporation as we would define one, and no protection against the
specific financial shock coming in the third century.

**Prerequisites.** `corpus_written`, `binding_and_codex`, physical space,
Roman legal recognition as a *collegium* (a licensed association; per the
*lex Iulia* on collegia, Augustus onward requires licensing, not free
formation, so this needs actual administrative approval).

**Roman-available inputs.** Existing legal forms (licensed *collegium*,
endowed foundation), existing scriptorium-style copying labour (literate
slaves or freedmen scribes, already normal in a wealthy household or
public institution).

**Procedure.**
1. Petition for recognition as a licensed collegium with an explicit
   charter: purpose, officers, and a succession rule for who appoints the
   next head.
2. Catalogue every work by subject and author on the *Pinakes* model,
   cross-referenced with each work's own index per `corpus_written`. An
   unnavigable library is a warehouse, not an institution.
3. Run a standing copying rota, rotating scribes to copy worn or
   under-represented works, with a second scribe cross-checking each
   finished copy before it is catalogued. A trained scribe's daily
   careful-copying output is ESTIMATED (basis: commonly cited medieval
   scriptorium ranges, no Roman-specific figure exists) at a small number
   of pages per day, so plan copying as slow and ongoing, not a one-time
   task.
4. Fund it with an endowment in land, not coin. This is the single most
   important financial decision here, with real Roman precedent: Pliny
   the Younger describes converting a cash benefaction into a land-secured
   endowment specifically to protect it from coin's own volatility
   (*Letters* 7.18, attributed, verify wording against the source), and
   separately endowed a library and alimentary foundation at Comum
   (*Letters* 1.8). A coin endowment is directly exposed to the
   denarius's silver-content collapse during the Crisis of the Third
   Century; land keeps producing real value regardless of the coinage.
5. Attach a teaching function, training new scribes and readers in the
   plain, index-heavy style of `corpus_written`, so the institution keeps
   producing people who can extend the corpus, not just copy it blindly.

**How you know it worked.** Does the institution have a written,
followed succession rule that does not require you? If yes, it is an
institution; if every decision still routes through you, it is a
workshop.

**Failure modes.** A generous-looking coin endowment can become worthless
within decades of debasement. A collegium without succession collapses
into faction or neglect at the founder's death. A library without an
enforced copying rota loses works to wear and damp faster than they are
replaced (see `corpus_dispersed`).

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED. Land sufficient to fund a modest permanent
staff and building upkeep is a substantial one-time capital commitment,
roughly the scale of a comfortable equestrian estate by rough analogy to
Pliny's own documented benefactions; no single figure is given, it
depends entirely on local land values and staff size chosen.

**Danger.** Political, not physical: an institution wealthy in land and
influence can draw unwelcome attention from a suspicious emperor or rival
benefactor. Cultivate it as visibly loyal and useful, teaching practical
craft and record-keeping, not politics.

**Confidence: HIGH** for the legal and financial structure, both well-
attested Roman practices with a documented precedent in Pliny. **MEDIUM**
for scribal-output figures, extrapolated from later, non-Roman data.

### record_keeping_and_accounting - Double-entry bookkeeping and the laboratory notebook

**What it is / why you want it.** A bookkeeping method where every
transaction is recorded twice, as a debit in one account and a credit in
another, so the accounts must balance and errors surface automatically;
paired with a habit of dated, quantitative laboratory notebooks that
record failures as carefully as successes.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rome already keeps account books (the
*codex accepti et expensi*, referenced as a legally significant record in
Roman rhetorical and legal sources) and has professional bankers
(*argentarii*) who lend, exchange and keep ledgers. What is missing is
the double-entry principle itself, not formalised historically until
medieval Italian merchant practice, with Luca Pacioli's 1494 treatise the
first systematic account. A single-entry ledger can be silently wrong for
years; a double-entry ledger that fails to balance announces an error
immediately.

**Prerequisites.** Literacy and basic arithmetic; no material
prerequisites.

**Roman-available inputs.** None beyond ordinary writing materials.

**Procedure.**
1. For every transaction, identify two accounts it affects (for example
   "cash" and "goods purchased") and record the same value as a debit in
   one and a credit in the other.
2. Keep a running balance for every account.
3. Periodically sum all debit and all credit balances across every
   account. A mismatch means an error exists somewhere, findable before
   it compounds, the entire point, and precisely why it matters to an
   imperial treasury, a tax-farming company (*publicani*), a large
   trading house, or the land-endowed library of
   `libraries_and_institutions`.
4. Sell it first to the *argentarii*: they already keep ledgers and
   understand the value of an audit-proof record, so the pitch needs no
   conceptual leap, only the new method.
5. For technical work, keep a separate standardised laboratory notebook:
   every attempted procedure gets a dated entry with exact quantities and
   units (per `corpus_written`), the outcome, and negative results
   recorded with the same care as successes. An undocumented failure gets
   silently repeated by the next person; a documented one saves that time
   permanently, the direct opposite of the alchemical secrecy habit
   condemned in `corpus_written`, and arguably the more important habit
   of the two.

**How you know it worked.** Books balance at period close, and mismatches
are traceable rather than mysterious. A successor unfamiliar with your
work can tell from the notebook alone what has already failed.

**Failure modes.** Double-entry only catches errors that break the
balance; an identical mistake on both sides still balances. A notebook
kept inconsistently, some entries dated and quantified, others vague,
loses most of its value.

**Cost & labour.** Essentially free, method and clerical discipline, not
capital. Trivial marginal time next to the value of catching errors or
not repeating a known dead end.

**Danger.** None physical or social; one of the safest, most immediately
sellable items in this module.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The double-entry mechanism is simple arithmetic,
not a Roman-era-specific claim, and Rome's existing single-entry tradition
and the *argentarii* market for this improvement are both well attested.

## The redundancy arithmetic

This is an explicit estimate, using the loss rate stated in this
module's own premise: roughly 50 percent of a given line of copies is
lost per century (a stated planning simplification, not a measured
Roman-era figure).

Treat each independently located copy as having, per century, a 0.5
chance of surviving and a 0.5 chance of being lost, independently of
other copies, over three centuries (spanning the Antonine Plague, the
Plague of Cyprian and the Crisis of the Third Century). A single copy's
chance of surviving all three centuries is 0.5 x 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.125, or
12.5 percent, leaving an 87.5 percent chance of total loss if only one
copy exists.

For N independent copies, the chance at least one survives is
1 - 0.875^N. Solving (DERIVED from the stated assumption):
- 90 percent confidence: N is about 17.2, round up to 18 copies.
- 95 percent confidence: N is about 22.4, round up to 23 copies.
- 99 percent confidence: N is about 34.5, round up to 35 copies.

Two corrections push the practical target higher. Copies are not truly
independent: two held in the same city share fire and war risk, two held
by one family share that family's fortune, so raw count overstates true
independence unless copies are spread across cities, provinces and
institution types as `corpus_dispersed` describes. The hazard is also
lumpy, concentrated in the named crisis windows, not smooth across three
centuries, so real risk depends on where a copy sits when a specific
plague or war hits its city.

One correction runs the other way and matters more in practice: this
calculation assumes a static set of copies with no recopying. A real
institution (`libraries_and_institutions`) recopies its exemplar
periodically, well within a human generation (roughly 25-30 years),
resetting a copy's effective age long before the assumed century-scale
loss rate applies. A small number of actively-copying institutions can
outperform a much larger number of passively stored, never-recopied
copies. The real target is not "bury enough copies and hope," it is
"fund enough independent, actively-copying institutions," why
`corpus_dispersed` and `libraries_and_institutions` are written as
companions.

Practical recommendation: aim for roughly 25 to 40 independently located
copies at any time (comfortably above the 95 percent line, with margin
for correlation and lumpy hazard), across at least three institution
types and four or five regions, at least one copy in Egypt, and at least
one genuinely outside the Empire's political risk. Treat this whole
section as ESTIMATED: the loss-rate figure is a stated premise, and the
independence assumption is known to be optimistic. Use the arithmetic for
its structure, redundancy must scale, correlated risk must be actively
broken by spread, recopying beats static storage, rather than for the
exact numbers.

## What to write first if you have one year to live

Assume you die tomorrow. Order of priority, reasoned by value-if-it-
survives times probability-of-loss-if-unwritten times how much it
multiplies everything written after it:

1. **The condensed paper, ink and press recipes**, even in rough form on
   whatever surface you already have (papyrus or wax tablet, do not wait
   for a mill you may not live to build). This is the meta-priority: if
   nothing else you write can be copied and dispersed, none of it
   survives the coming plagues. With only hours, write the ink recipe and
   the press description first, since both can be improvised faster than
   paper can be produced at scale, and a handful of hand-copied sheets
   beats zero.
2. **Basic hygiene and epidemic-response measures** (boil drinking water,
   isolate the visibly sick, wash hands before treating wounds or food,
   dispose of the dead promptly away from water sources). Outside this
   module's own entries, but the single highest lives-saved-per-word
   investment available, given the Antonine Plague is coming within your
   or your children's lifetime; a well-copied page of sanitary practice
   can save more lives directly than any device in this tree. (Write it
   yourself today if no dedicated public-health module exists in this
   corpus.)
3. **Positional numerals and zero**, with a worked arithmetic example
   against Roman numerals. Rome has neither. A single page multiplies the
   value of every quantitative entry in the whole corpus and costs almost
   nothing to write.
4. **The physically reproducible unit-standard definitions** from
   `corpus_written`, so every number written by you or anyone after you
   is interpretable two centuries later, given official weight and
   currency standards have no guarantee of surviving intact.
5. **The dispersal instructions** (`corpus_dispersed` and the redundancy
   arithmetic above), written explicitly enough that whoever finds your
   papers, even a stranger, understands why they must be copied and
   scattered rather than kept as one treasured original.
6. **Whatever single piece of metallurgy or medicine you personally know
   best and are most likely to be the only source for.** Deliberately
   last and vague, since it depends entirely on your own specific modern
   knowledge; write what you know most completely, in the plain,
   worked-example style of `corpus_written`, rather than a shallow survey
   of everything you half-remember.

The ordering is judgement, not a measured result, but the reasoning
behind it, multiplier technologies and lives-saved-per-word first,
narrow personal expertise last, is the load-bearing part; adapt item six
to whatever you actually know.
