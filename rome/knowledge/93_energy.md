# Module 93: Energy, Fuel and Prime Movers

Rome has water power and the piston pump, but uses it almost entirely to grind grain. The waterwheel at Barbegal, sixteen of them in cascade, exists in your lifetime. The gap in Roman engineering is not the wheel or the principle of falling water - it is what you do with the energy once you capture it.

This module documents technologies that multiply or redirect the mechanical power you already have, and then shows you new sources of power itself: fossil fuel and concentrated chemical energy. The sequence matters: you cannot measure boiler efficiency without an indicator diagram, and you cannot run a boiler safely without understanding what a safety valve actually does.

### overshot_wheel_efficiency - The Measured Efficiency of Water Wheels

**What it is / why you want it.** Smeaton's measurements, repeated by modern experiment, show that an overshot wheel delivers roughly twice the useful work of an undershot wheel from the same water head. This is not a new wheel design - both are ancient. This is an instrument and a discipline: the experimental method applied to mechanical power, which buys you facts instead of guessing.

**Why you would never guess this.** Engineers for two thousand years assumed that more water hitting a wheel faster must give more power. What actually matters is *potential energy* - the weight of water falling through a vertical distance - not the impact velocity. An overshot wheel captures nearly all the gravitational potential energy of the water. An undershot wheel wastes most of it in turbulence and return flow. No Roman engineer had a way to *measure* which was true until Smeaton built instruments that did.

**Prerequisites.** Water mill (existing), a level, weights, a measuring vessel, a way to time wheel rotations (water clock will do), calibrated force measurements (use a steelyard or balance with known weights).

**Roman-available inputs.** Water from any running stream. The wheel itself is Roman-standard construction - wood rim, shaft, buckets or paddles. Your input is measurement discipline, not materials.

**Procedure.** 

1. Build or locate an overshot wheel and an undershot wheel on the same water source. Size them as identically as practical. Record the wheel diameter, paddle/bucket count, and materials.

2. Measure the water flow in the stream: collect the outflow in a calibrated vessel for a measured time (use a water clock or hour glass). Record the mass of water per hour.

3. Measure the water *head* (vertical fall) for the overshot wheel: measure the height difference between the water surface at entry and the point where water leaves the wheel, using a plumb line or level. For an undershot wheel, measure the depth of immersion only.

4. Attach a rope and pulley to the wheel axle. Hang a calibrated weight and note how fast the wheel lifts it. Record the lifting speed in vertical distance per hour (or per degree of wheel rotation, then convert).

5. Calculate power output as (weight lifted in pounds) times (vertical distance in feet per hour). Do this for both wheels, using identical conditions - same water source, same day, same time of year if possible.

6. Record the water power input as (water mass per hour in pounds) times (gravitational head in feet). This is your input energy. Compare it to the useful output energy (weight lifted).

7. Repeat this for several water sources and wheel designs. Smeaton found overshot wheels at roughly 60-70% efficiency and undershot wheels at roughly 25-30% efficiency. Your own measurements will vary with design and water flow.

**How you know it worked.** An overshot wheel lifts a weight roughly twice as fast as an undershot wheel using the same water and roughly the same wheel size. If both wheels perform similarly, you have a design flaw - re-examine the bucket/paddle depth, the sealing of the wheel rim, and the water entry point. Your numbers should be consistent across multiple trials on the same wheel.

**Failure modes.** Measurement errors dominate: leaking buckets (undercut the overshot efficiency), splashing water that exits the wheel before doing work (loss of head), wheels that are not truly vertical, uneven water distribution around the wheel circumference. A common mistake is assuming that "the wheel was faster" means "the wheel did more work" - rotational speed and work output are different things.

**Cost & labour.** This is an experiment, not a product. Assume you already have the wheels (existing mills). Measurement takes one skilled person per day of observation, repeated across 3-5 water sources. Materials cost is the cost of a good level or a water clock if you lack one. ESTIMATED at 5 denarii for a borrowed steelyard and weights, or 50-100 denarii if you must buy precision instruments.

**Danger.** Low. Measurement only. Water and rotating wheels can trap clothing or fingers, so standard mill safety: loose sleeves, no dangling ornaments, distance from the wheel.

**Confidence: HIGH** - This is textbook fluid mechanics and well-attested in the experimental record. Smeaton's 1759 measurements are the foundation of modern hydraulic engineering.

### breastshot_wheel - The Breastshot Water Wheel

**What it is / why you want it.** A breastshot wheel is a hybrid: the water enters at roughly the height of the wheel's center or slightly above. It captures more potential energy than an undershot wheel and is less sensitive to tailwater level than an overshot wheel. Where headwater is modest (1-2 meters) and flow is large, breastshot wheels are the practical choice.

**Why you would never guess this.** Most Roman wheels are either undershot (deep immersion, direct impact) or overshot (tall drop, buckets). The breastshot design is a compromise that performs better than either in the middle range. But "middle range" is not intuitive until you have measured the alternatives.

**Prerequisites.** Water mill (existing), waterwheel construction skills, carpentry. Requires access to flowing water with 1-2 meter head. Breastshot wheels are found in Roman mills; this is not a new invention but a design principle to apply intentionally.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the rim, shaft, paddles or buckets. Wrought iron fittings if available (or wood dowels and rope lashing). Iron bands to reinforce the rim.

**Procedure.** 

1. Design the wheel rim to accept buckets or flat paddles. Breastshot wheels typically use buckets rather than flat paddles, to trap water and direct it.

2. Arrange the water chute so that the water flow enters the wheel at approximately the height of the wheel's center - this means the water touches the wheel for a longer arc than in an undershot wheel, and falls through more total height than if it were hitting only the bottom.

3. Set the wheel diameter based on the available head: for a 1-meter head, a wheel 2-3 meters in diameter is typical. The exact ratio depends on desired rotational speed and flow rate.

4. Build sealing into the buckets: the water must stay in the bucket until it passes the lowest point of the wheel. Poor sealing is the most common failure.

5. Install the wheel with the same bearing and axle system as an overshot wheel. The structural stresses are similar.

6. Test the wheel's efficiency by the method described in overshot_wheel_efficiency. Breastshot wheels typically achieve 50-55% efficiency - better than undershot, not as good as overshot.

**How you know it worked.** The wheel rotates smoothly, lifts water or a load at a consistent rate, and does not stall when flow is steady. The water should exit cleanly from the wheel; splashing and spray indicate poor bucket geometry.

**Failure modes.** Buckets that leak, allowing water to slosh out before reaching the wheel bottom. Buckets too large or too small for the desired flow rate. Tailwater level rising (if the stream backs up), which can flood the wheel and reverse it or trap it. Water channel erosion, widening the stream and lowering the water level.

**Cost & labour.** Equivalent to an overshot wheel construction: 50-100 personal hours for design and construction, 20-40 artisan hours for carpentry, 30-50 denarii for materials. ESTIMATED as DERIVED from standard mill construction costs.

**Danger.** Standard mill hazards: entanglement in the wheel, crushing from the axle bearings, flooding if the stream rises.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Breastshot wheels are attested in Roman mills and in medieval textile mills. The physics is straightforward.

### water_turbine - Turbines as a Successor to Wheels

**What it is / why you want it.** A turbine is not a wheel with buckets. A turbine is a system where water or steam flows *through* a series of curved blades in a rotating drum, passing from high pressure to low pressure. Turbines beat wheels because they use the entire water head at any flow rate, remain fully submerged, and do not care about tailwater level.

**Why you would never guess this.** Roman wheels are fundamentally *impact* devices: the water hits the wheel and transfers momentum. A turbine is a *pressure* device: the water is guided through the blades by geometry, not impact. The entire conceptual shift - that you can extract energy by *guiding* water flow rather than *hitting* it - is not obvious and requires a different kind of mathematics to design properly.

**Prerequisites.** Metal working at the level of bronze or iron casting (for the blade drum), understanding of fluid flow geometry, some experimental method to measure blade curvature. Higher mathematical skill than wheel design - Euler's turbomachinery equations are needed for optimization, but a trial-and-error design is possible without them.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze or iron for the blade drum (expensive). Water of course. The key input is the *design discipline* - curved blades that are not easily guessed. A Roman metalworker can cast the blades if given exact dimensions or templates; making those templates is the hard part.

**Procedure.**

1. Design a stationary blade assembly: the water enters through a set of fixed guide vanes that direct it tangentially into the moving rotor.

2. Design the rotor: a drum with radial or curved blades. For a first attempt, use radial blades (simpler). Later iterations will benefit from curved blades that match the water trajectory (this is empirical design - build it, measure it, adjust).

3. Cast or forged the blade assembly from bronze or wrought iron. This requires a competent metal workshop. Blade thickness 5-10 mm, overall drum diameter depending on head: for 3-5 meter head, 300-500 mm diameter is typical. ESTIMATED based on hydraulic power density.

4. Mount the drum on a horizontal or vertical axis (vertical is more compact). Mount the guide vanes around the drum inlet.

5. Connect the outlet to a tailrace or return to the stream at lower elevation.

6. Allow water to flow through. The pressure difference across the blade assembly will cause the rotor to spin. If it does not spin, the guide vanes are pointing the wrong direction or the blade curvature is reversed.

7. Measure the output power using the lifting method from overshot_wheel_efficiency.

8. Adjust blade geometry empirically: if the rotor speed is too high for the load, the flow is exiting without doing enough work - try increasing blade deflection. If the rotor is sluggish, the blades may be absorbing too much energy - try reducing deflection.

**How you know it worked.** The rotor spins smoothly and continuously, regardless of tailwater level. Output power should be much higher than an equivalent wheel for the same head, because the entire head is used. Typically, a well-designed turbine will achieve 70-80% efficiency.

**Failure modes.** Cavitation (water boiling in low-pressure zones) if the water speed is too high or the tailwater level is too low - bubbles form and collapse, eroding metal. Blade binding if sediment or wood debris lodges between blades. Imbalanced blade geometry causing vibration and bearing wear. The rotor spinning too fast and throwing the shaft off-center if not properly balanced.

**Cost & labour.** 200-400 personal hours for design and experimentation. 100-200 artisan hours for metalworking (casting or forging). 200-500 denarii for materials (bronze or iron). This is a research project, not a standard installation. ESTIMATED as DERIVED from advanced metalworking costs.

**Danger.** Cavitation can create shock waves that damage the metal. The rotor operates at high speed and can act as a flywheel - fingers caught in it will be severed. Sustained operation under high head and flow can create significant force on bearing structures.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Turbine principles are clear from fluid mechanics, but building an efficient turbine without calculus requires substantial experimentation. The Pelton wheel (below) is a turbine design that is much simpler and can be built and tested iteratively in a Roman workshop.

### pelton_wheel - The Pelton Wheel Turbine

**What it is / why you want it.** A Pelton wheel is a turbine optimized for high heads (tall waterfalls) and small flows. A jet of water strikes specially-shaped buckets on a spinning wheel, transferring momentum very efficiently. A Pelton wheel can work at falls of 50-500 meters where a normal waterwheel is impossible. It is late medieval technology but buildable in principle with Roman materials.

**Why you would never guess this.** A Pelton wheel looks more like a wheel with buckets than like a turbine. The insight is that the bucket shape matters enormously: each bucket has a central ridge that splits the incoming jet into two streams, which exit to the sides and back rather than straight back. This geometry reduces the force on the wheel bearings and multiplies the effective momentum transfer. A flat bucket would be nearly useless; the Pelton geometry is not obvious.

**Prerequisites.** A high-head water source (minimum 50 meters fall practically, though smaller heads work less efficiently). Metal working for bucket casting or shaping (bronze or iron). A way to direct a high-pressure jet - a nozzle and a closed chute.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze or cast iron for buckets. Water from a steep mountain stream or aqueduct. The essential input is the understanding that a *jet* of high-pressure water, not a large stream, is optimal.

**Procedure.**

1. Design the bucket: a shallow bowl with a central ridge that divides the incoming jet. The ridge should point upstream (into the direction of the incoming water). Bucket depth roughly 1/4 of the bucket width. Bucket width roughly 1/3 of the wheel diameter.

2. Attach buckets to a rim at regular intervals: 16-24 buckets depending on wheel size. A smaller wheel can have fewer buckets.

3. Mount the wheel on a horizontal or inclined axis. The wheel diameter depends on the jet velocity and desired rotational speed: for a 100-meter head, the jet velocity is roughly 44 m/s (from v = sqrt(2*g*h)), so a 1-meter diameter wheel at 2000 rpm is typical. For lower heads, larger wheels.

4. Design the jet system: a vertical pipe carrying water from the height of the head, terminating in a constricting nozzle. The nozzle diameter determines the jet diameter. For a 1-meter wheel powered by a 100-meter head, a 50-100 mm diameter jet is typical.

5. Position the nozzle so that the jet hits the center of each bucket as it rotates. This requires precise alignment.

6. Allow water to flow and observe the wheel speed. If too slow, narrow the nozzle (higher jet velocity). If too fast, increase nozzle width. Balance the load (a grinding mill) to prevent over-speed.

7. Measure the power output: efficiency should be 80-90% for a well-designed Pelton wheel.

**How you know it worked.** The wheel spins at high speed (1000-3000 rpm depending on design) and lifts a significant load (measured using the method in overshot_wheel_efficiency). The water exits the buckets to the sides and back, not straight back - if the water bounces backward, the bucket shape is wrong.

**Failure modes.** Jet misalignment (the jet does not hit the bucket center as it rotates), causing imbalance and bearing wear. Bucket deformation from repeated impacts. Water cavitation in the low-pressure zones behind the jet. Nozzle clogging from sediment.

**Cost & labour.** 100-200 personal hours for design. 80-150 artisan hours for bucket casting and wheel assembly. 300-600 denarii for materials (the cost is driven by precision casting). ESTIMATED as DERIVED from fine metalwork costs.

**Danger.** A high-speed wheel is extremely dangerous: the jet itself is a cutting tool (a 100-meter head jet can slice through wood), and the wheel acts as a flywheel that can eject buckets if it fails. Bearing failure can cause the wheel to jam and exert enormous torque on attached machinery.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Pelton wheel design is well-understood in modern engineering, but building one without calculus requires iterative experimentation. A Roman craftsman could build it with trial-and-error, but efficiency would likely be lower than a modern design (~70% rather than ~90%).

### tower_mill - The Tower Mill and Fantail

**What it is / why you want it.** A tower mill is a windmill mounted in a tall, rotating structure that automatically tracks the wind direction. Older windmills required manual turning to face the wind (post mills). A tower mill is more convenient and, crucially, can orient itself as the wind changes.

**Why you would never guess this.** The insight is that a small *secondary* windmill (the fantail) mounted perpendicular to the main sails can drive a mechanism that rotates the tower toward the wind automatically. This is a feedback system before feedback systems had a name. The fantail detects wind direction by being *blind* to it until the wind is perpendicular, at which point it catches wind and drives a gear mechanism.

**Prerequisites.** Windmill construction (existing), carpentry, metal working for gears and axles, understanding of wind orientation.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood and iron for construction. Canvas or linen for sails (both widely available).

**Procedure.**

1. Build a tall, stationary stone or wood tower, roughly 10-15 meters in height, with a circular base 6-8 meters in diameter.

2. Mount the main windmill mechanism (sails, drive shaft, gear box) on top of the tower such that the entire main mechanism can rotate about a vertical axis - this requires a large bearing at the tower top.

3. Design the main sails as Dutch-style rectangular frames with canvas, sized to the available wind (for a 10-meter sail length, typical power is 10-50 kW depending on wind speed).

4. Mount a small secondary windmill (the fantail) on the *back* of the main sail frame, perpendicular to the main sails. The fantail sails are perhaps 1-2 meters, much smaller.

5. Connect the fantail drive shaft to a worm gear or conical gear that meshes with a ring gear mounted on the tower top. This gear ratio is the key: the fantail should have high gear ratio, so a slow fantail rotation drives a faster rotation of the whole tower.

6. When the wind is aligned with the main sails, the fantail sees no wind (because the main sails block it). When the wind shifts to the side, the fantail catches wind, drives the gears, and rotates the tower until the main sails realign and the fantail is again blocked.

7. The system self-regulates: it turns toward the wind and stops turning when aligned.

**How you know it worked.** The main sails turn smoothly as wind approaches from any direction. As the wind direction changes, the tower rotates to follow it without manual intervention. The fantail should not spin when the main sails are well-aligned.

**Failure modes.** Gear binding if the worm-wheel connection is poorly designed or misaligned. The fantail catching wind when it should not (mis-sized or mis-positioned), causing constant oscillation. Tower bearings seizing due to corrosion or lack of lubrication. The main sails not catching wind after rotation (misalignment of the main sail frame).

**Cost & labour.** This is a significant engineering project: 500-1000 personal hours for design and supervision, 300-500 artisan hours for carpentry and metalwork, 1000-2000 denarii for materials. Calendar time: 2-3 months. ESTIMATED as DERIVED from major mill construction.

**Danger.** High winds can cause excessive speed and bearing failure. The gear mechanism can jam and exert large torques on the structure. High-altitude work on the tower during construction.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Tower mills with fantails are well-attested in medieval and later European windmills, but Roman-era documentation is absent. The principles are sound but the integration is complex.

### windmill_fantail - The Fantail as an Autonomous Feedback System

**What it is / why you want it.** The fantail is a small secondary windmill that automatically keeps the main windmill pointed into the wind. It is perhaps the earliest example of an automatic feedback control system - the fantail "senses" wind direction by its response to wind and drives the tower toward alignment.

**Why you would never guess this.** Most windmill designs require a human operator to climb up and manually turn the tower to face the wind. The fantail does this without any operator, without any explicit wind-direction measurement, using only the principles of aerodynamics and mechanics. Once you see it, it is elegant; beforehand, you do not think to automate wind orientation.

**Prerequisites.** Windmill construction, gear design, metal working. Understanding of wind flow geometry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood and iron. Canvas for sails.

**Procedure.** See tower_mill section - the fantail is an integral part of that system.

**How you know it worked.** The main sails track the wind direction passively - no human input required. When the wind changes direction, the tower rotates within a few minutes and realigns.

**Failure modes.** See tower_mill.

**Cost & labour.** The fantail is 15-20% of the cost of a full tower mill: roughly 100-200 denarii additional. ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** As in tower_mill, plus the risk of the fantail sails striking the main sails if the connection fails.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Historical windmills used fantails, but Roman use is not documented.

### trompe - The Trompe: Water Lifts Air

**What it is / why you want it.** A trompe is a device that uses falling water to compress air, producing a blast of pressurized air with no moving parts whatsoever. A pipe of falling water entrains air bubbles, which are compressed in a submerged chamber, and exit as a high-pressure air jet. A trompe can power a forge bellows, create a high-pressure spray, or inflate an air bell for diving.

**Why you would never guess this.** The trompe works by entrainment: air bubbles are mechanically mixed into the falling water stream by the turbulence, then compressed by the hydrostatic pressure in the submerged chamber. There is no pump, no piston, no valve mechanism. The only requirements are falling water, a pipe, and a sealed chamber underwater. It is counter-intuitive that *water* falling in a pipe *drags air with it* - but it does, reliably and reproducibly.

**Prerequisites.** A water source with at least 2-3 meters of fall. A metalworking shop to make or weld a pipe system. A sealed chamber for air collection.

**Roman-available inputs.** Metal pipe (bronze or wrought iron), sealed below water level. Water falling through it. The theoretical understanding that entrainment occurs.

**Procedure.**

1. Fabricate a pipe of iron or bronze, internal diameter 30-50 mm, length equal to the fall height plus 1-2 meters. The pipe should extend above the water surface, then bend downward below the water surface.

2. At the water entry point (top of the apparatus), place the pipe outlet such that water flows *into* the pipe at a small angle or directly downward.

3. Below the water surface, connect the pipe to a sealed chamber (a tank or cask) that is completely submerged or partially submerged.

4. From the top of this chamber, run a discharge pipe to your target: a forge bellows input, a spray nozzle, or an air bell.

5. Allow water to fall through the pipe. As it falls, air bubbles from the atmosphere at the pipe inlet are dragged down into the falling water stream. These bubbles accumulate in the sealed chamber below, compressing to high pressure.

6. Adjust the pipe diameter and fall height to control the air output. More fall = higher pressure. Larger diameter = higher volume but lower pressure.

7. The system reaches equilibrium: water exits through the bottom of the chamber, air is compressed above, and a continuous jet of air exits through the discharge.

**How you know it worked.** Air emerges from the discharge pipe steadily. If you place the discharge nozzle in water, bubbles rise (proving air is being expelled). If you attach it to a bellows, the bellows inflates even with no one pumping. If you attach it to a small waterwheel, the air jet will spin it.

**Failure modes.** Air not entering the system: check that the pipe inlet is open to the atmosphere and that water is actually entering the top of the pipe. Water backing up into the air-discharge pipe: add a one-way valve or increase the air-discharge elevation. Sediment clogging the pipe: filter the inlet water or use a larger pipe diameter initially.

**Cost & labour.** 30-50 personal hours for design and setup. 20-40 artisan hours for pipe work and assembly. 50-200 denarii depending on pipe material (bronze is expensive; wrought iron is cheaper). Calendar time: 2-3 weeks. ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Water under pressure can cause scalding or injury if it ruptures the system. The compressed air can be released suddenly. For diving bells (mentioned later), a trompe failure means the diver loses air supply - a life-threatening situation.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The trompe is attested in medieval and later mining and diving contexts. The physics is straightforward: entrainment is well-documented in fluid mechanics. A Roman with falling water and metalworking could build a functioning trompe.

### coal_mining - Coal Extraction from Deep Deposits

**What it is / why you want it.** Coal is fossilized plant matter, found in stratified deposits in sedimentary rock. Extracting coal from depth requires removing the overlying rock and preventing water from flooding the mine. Coal has roughly 2-3 times the energy density of wood and does not require growing - it is a one-time harvest. One ton of coal can produce as much useful work as 3-5 tons of wood.

**Why you would never guess this.** Romans have no tradition of coal mining in the Mediterranean basin (though coal is mined in Britain in Roman times, under military control and trade restriction). Coal looks similar to charcoal once broken, and the differences in burn characteristics are not obvious until you have tried it. The real barrier is not chemistry or geology but the engineering challenge of keeping a deep mine free of water.

**Prerequisites.** Access to coal-bearing geology (the Rhine-Ruhr coal field, South Wales, parts of France all have accessible deposits). Mining technology, pumping (water mills or trompes), ventilation (simple air circulation).

**Roman-available inputs.** Coal: a black, brittle rock, harder than charcoal, found in layers within sedimentary rock. Digging and hauling equipment (picks, shovels, ropes). Timber for mine support.

**Procedure.**

1. Locate a coal seam exposed at the surface or at shallow depth. Roman coal mining was small-scale and surface-based; deep mining (below 50-100 meters) is beyond practical Roman ability without modern ventilation.

2. Expose the seam by removing overburden (soil and rock above the coal). A 10-meter wide and 2-meter tall face can be worked by 5-10 miners in a shift.

3. Break the coal using picks and chisels (iron tools). Coal is brittle and fractures readily. A miner can break 500 kg to 1 ton of coal in an 8-hour shift.

4. Load coal into baskets or buckets and haul to the surface.

5. If water seeps into the mine, pump it out using a hand-pumped trompe system or a foot-powered chain pump.

6. Provide ventilation: a simple shaft or opening at each end of the worked section allows air to circulate and prevents toxic gas accumulation.

**How you know it worked.** Coal is extracted and can be transported. When burned in a furnace or forge, it produces intense heat (roughly 1500 °C in a charcoal fire, 1800 °C in a coal fire), ash, and a distinct acrid smell.

**Failure modes.** Water flooding the mine, making it impassable and impossible to work. Toxic gas (carbon dioxide, methane) accumulating, causing asphyxiation - a mine that is "dead air" (no circulation) is a killer. Roof collapse if timber support is inadequate. Poor quality coal (soft, high ash content) that gives little heat and clogs the furnace.

**Cost & labour.** A small surface coal pit: 1000-2000 denarii for initial excavation and timber. Operating cost: 50 denarii per ton of coal produced, split between mining labor (25), pumping/ventilation (15), and transport (10). Calendar time: a coal pit can produce continuously for 10-20 years depending on seam thickness and working practices. MEASURED from historical coal mines; Roman figures would be higher due to less efficient tools.

**Danger.** Collapse, asphyxiation, water immersion. Underground work is inherently hazardous.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Coal mining occurs in Roman times (Britain, small scale), and the basic geological facts are correct. Deep mining engineering is Roman capability, but the scale is limited.

### coking - Charring Coal to Produce Coke

**What it is / why you want it.** Coke is coal that has been heated in the absence of air to drive off water, tar, and sulfur compounds, leaving mainly carbon. Coke burns hotter and cleaner than raw coal, does not produce tar smoke that fouls metal, and is essential for high-temperature metalworking and furnace operations.

**Why you would never guess this.** Raw coal burns with a tarry, smoky flame that fouls tools and produces inferior metal. The solution - heating coal without air - is not obvious. Once you have tried it, the difference is undeniable.

**Prerequisites.** Coal, a way to heat coal to 300-500 °C without direct flame contact (a sealed oven or retort), understanding that air exclusion is essential.

**Roman-available inputs.** Coal (from coal_mining section above). A brick or stone oven, sealed. Wood fire to heat the oven. Ventilation or a chimney to carry off gas and smoke.

**Procedure.**

1. Construct an oven of brick or stone, roughly 2 meters long, 1 meter wide, 1 meter high, with a sealed door. The oven will have a fire chamber underneath and a coal-containing chamber above, separated by a perforated brick floor or grate.

2. Load the coal chamber with coal, broken into fist-sized pieces, stacked loosely.

3. Light a wood fire in the chamber below. Allow the coal chamber to heat to roughly 300-350 °C - you can estimate this by placing pieces of wood near (not touching) the coal chamber; when the wood ignites from radiant heat, you are in the right temperature range.

4. Seal the coal chamber door and allow it to heat further. Temperature should reach 400-500 °C. This process takes 6-12 hours.

5. During heating, volatile compounds (water vapor, tar vapor, methane from coal decomposition) will emerge from the coal. These can be vented through a pipe to the outside or captured (tar distillation was a later industrial practice, but the principle is present here).

6. After 12-24 hours, allow the oven to cool. Open the door and extract the coke: coal that has blackened further and become harder and less brittle than the original coal.

7. The yield is roughly 70-75% by weight: 100 kg of coal produces roughly 70-75 kg of coke (the rest is water and volatilized gases).

**How you know it worked.** Coke is denser and harder than the original coal. When burned, it produces a nearly smokeless flame (a thin wisp, not black smoke). A coke fire burns hotter and longer than raw coal.

**Failure modes.** The coal does not char fully: temperature was too low or heating time too short. The oven was not sealed, allowing air to enter and burning some coal as fuel. The coke falls apart into fine powder: the coal grade was poor or the heating rate was too fast. Tar accumulates in the oven: seal it better or provide a separate volatilization path.

**Cost & labour.** Oven construction: 500 denarii and 100-150 artisan hours. Operating cost: 20 denarii per ton of coke produced (mainly fuel to heat the oven). Calendar time: one batch per 2 days, producing 2-3 tons of coke per week. ESTIMATED from historical data.

**Danger.** The oven becomes extremely hot (you cannot touch it). Volatile gases (including methane) can accumulate and explode if ignited. Always provide ventilation. Tar is caustic; avoid skin contact.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Coke production is not well-documented in the Roman period, but coal coking is ancient (Roman references to coked fuel exist, though sparse). The chemistry is straightforward.

### coal_gas_works - Coal Gasification and Town Gas

**What it is / why you want it.** Heating coal without air produces not only coke but also a mixture of gases: carbon monoxide, hydrogen, methane, and others. This gas mixture can be collected, purified, and burned as a fuel. A single coal gasification plant can supply gas to many nearby users via pipes - a "town gas" system.

**Why you would never guess this.** Most Romans think of coal as a solid fuel to be burned directly. The insight that coal is actually a *source of combustible gas*, and that the gas can be piped and burned elsewhere, requires understanding both that gases exist (not intuitive) and that gas-fuel systems have advantages. A coal gas plant is a major infrastructure project with high capital cost but low per-unit fuel cost, similar to a water mill.

**Prerequisites.** Coal, a coal gasifier (a special oven with air injection), pipes (lead or bronze), users who want piped fuel, understanding of gas flow and combustion.

**Roman-available inputs.** Coal, clay or brick for the gasifier vessel, iron or bronze piping, access to water for cooling.

**Procedure.**

1. Construct a coal gasifier: a sealed, vertical iron or brick chamber roughly 1-2 meters tall and 0.5 meters in diameter, with a coal inlet at the top, a controlled air inlet near the bottom, and a gas outlet at the top.

2. Load coal into the gasifier at the inlet, allowing it to drop downward as it reacts.

3. Blow air (using a trompe or bellows) into the base of the reactor. The oxygen reacts with hot coal, producing heat and carbon monoxide (and carbon dioxide, which is then reduced to carbon monoxide by hotter coal above).

4. The rising gases pass through hotter coal, which heats and drains water from the gas. The gas exits at the top, still hot.

5. Cool and scrub the gas: pass it through water (in a spray or bubble chamber) to remove particulates and cool it. After cooling, much of the water condenses and can be drained.

6. The cleaned gas is now ready to pipe: it contains carbon monoxide, hydrogen, methane, and nitrogen (from the air).

7. Distribute the gas through buried or hanging pipes (lead, bronze, or ceramic) to users. Each user has a gas burner (a simple opening with an air inlet for premixing, or a port where the gas is burned).

**How you know it worked.** Gas emerges from the outlet and burns with a blue flame if held near a torch. The gas has significant heat content and can replace charcoal or wood for heating and some metalwork.

**Failure modes.** The gasifier temperature drops if the coal feed rate is too high or air flow too low - the gas becomes dilute and hard to ignite. Water condensation in pipes can form plugs. Leaks in pipes waste gas and can create fire hazards. Poor air mixing in the gasifier produces mostly carbon dioxide (inert) rather than carbon monoxide (fuel).

**Cost & labour.** A coal gas works (a facility producing gas) requires 2000-5000 denarii of capital for the gasifier, cooling system, and initial piping. Operating cost roughly 30 denarii per week for coal, air pumping, and maintenance. This is infrastructure, requiring a city-scale investment. ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Carbon monoxide is toxic; a ruptured pipe can poison users. Gas accumulation can cause explosions. The gasifier is hot and the gases can cause burns.

**Confidence: LOW** - Coal gasification is real (attested in 19th-century town gas systems), but Roman-era application is highly speculative. The capital investment and infrastructure complexity exceed typical Roman engineering.

### petroleum_drilling - Drilling for Petroleum

**What it is / why you want it.** Petroleum is a liquid hydrocarbon that seeps from deep sedimentary deposits. Ancient people noticed petroleum seeps in regions like Mesopotamia, Iraq, and the Caucasus, and used it for waterproofing and lamp oil. Drilling for petroleum (rather than collecting surface seeps) requires shaft-sinking and percussion drilling technology.

**Why you would never guess this.** Petroleum looks like water mixed with tar and is often found as seepage in swamps or asphalt deposits. The idea that drilling deeper would produce more petroleum liquid is logical once you know crude petroleum exists, but Roman culture had no tradition of deep drilling for liquid fuels.

**Prerequisites.** Drilling technology (existing, used for water wells), access to petroleum seeps, understanding that depth can increase yield, capital for exploration (many unsuccessful wells will be drilled).

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood and iron for drilling rigs. Rope. Percussion drilling tools (iron drill bits).

**Procedure.**

1. Identify a region where petroleum seeps occur naturally. Mesopotamia, the southern Caucasus, and North Africa have such deposits, though in the Roman world, knowledge of petroleum is limited.

2. Set up a percussion drilling rig: a wooden frame 5-10 meters tall, with a rope and pulley system for raising and lowering a heavy iron drill bit.

3. Pound the drill bit into the ground repeatedly, using the weight of the bit itself to drive it down. After each stroke, lift the bit, rotate it slightly to prevent binding, and strike again.

4. Periodically remove the drilling debris: raise the bit, attach a bailer (a bucket with a one-way valve), and pump out loose rock and water. This can take hours per meter of depth.

5. If the drill encounters petroleum-bearing rock (often sandstone or limestone), the liquid rises in the shaft. Depending on depth and formation pressure, petroleum may:
   - Seep slowly and can be bailed out
   - Flow without assistance (a flowing well)
   - Require pumping

6. Install a pump if necessary (a simple piston pump or screw pump).

**How you know it worked.** Petroleum liquid is extracted from the well. Yield is measured in liters or amphoras per day. The petroleum is dark brown to black and floats on water.

**Failure modes.** Water seeps into the well faster than petroleum, drowning it. The drill becomes stuck in the rock, unable to be raised - a lost drilling tool. The well produces nothing but water and rocks, indicating the targeted geological formation was missed. Pressure buildup can cause explosive release of petroleum or gas.

**Cost & labour.** Drilling to 50 meters depth: 5000-10,000 denarii for the rig, tools, and labor (300-500 artisan-hours for construction, 1000+ hours for drilling labor). Successful wells might produce for 5-10 years before silting up or pressure dropping. Calendar time: 3-6 months to drill an exploratory well. ESTIMATED from historical drilling records.

**Danger.** Petroleum vapors are flammable and can cause explosions. Underground collapses if the borehole is not properly supported. The drill tools can fail and injure workers.

**Confidence: LOW** - Petroleum drilling is not attested in the Roman world. The technology exists, but the application is speculative. Petroleum's primary Roman use was lamp oil from surface seeps (naphtha from Mesopotamia), not from drilling.

### oil_refinery - Refining Petroleum into Usable Products

**What it is / why you want it.** Crude petroleum is a complex mixture of hydrocarbons. Heating it moderately separates the lighter fractions (which evaporate as vapors), leaving heavier residues. Collecting the vapors and cooling them produces kerosene (and other distillates). Refining petroleum is a distillation process, using the same principles as alcohol distillation.

**Why you would never guess this.** Petroleum is a dark, tarry, foul-smelling liquid with no obvious use except waterproofing. That heating it produces lighter, more useful products is not intuitive without understanding distillation principles.

**Prerequisites.** Crude petroleum (from petroleum_drilling, or from surface seeps), a distillation setup (retort, condenser, collection vessels), understanding of distillation (see fermentation module if not already familiar).

**Roman-available inputs.** Crude petroleum, a clay or metal retort (large vessel that can be sealed), ceramic or metal tubing for the vapors, cold water for condensing, clay pots for collection.

**Procedure.**

1. Pour crude petroleum into the retort, filling it roughly 1/3 full.

2. Seal the retort and fit a tube outlet at the top (slightly inclined downward away from the retort).

3. Heat the retort to 200-300 °C. You can estimate this by the fact that the petroleum begins to boil at ~200 °C (smoke rises from the outlet).

4. The vapors emerging are a mixture of hydrocarbons. Cool them by running the outlet tube through or over cold water (a simple bath of cold water works; so does wrapping the tube in wet cloth).

5. As the vapors cool to below their boiling point, they condense back into liquid and collect in a pot.

6. Collect liquid for 1-2 hours at this temperature. The liquid collected (kerosene fraction) boils at roughly 150-300 °C and is the most useful product - it is a thin, golden-colored oil suitable for lamps.

7. If you heat the retort to 300-350 °C and continue, heavier fractions condense (diesel-like material). Above 350 °C, very heavy residues are produced and eventually you are left with coke-like solid.

8. After distillation, separate the fraction you want (kerosene) from other products. Different temperature ranges produce different products - the art of refining is controlling temperature to produce the desired fraction.

**How you know it worked.** Kerosene is a thin, pale oil that burns with a bright, non-sooty flame in a lamp. It is much better than crude petroleum for lighting. Yield is roughly 30-50% kerosene by weight from crude petroleum.

**Failure modes.** Overheating can cause the retort to rupture (explosion). Incomplete condensation wastes product - ensure the cooling water is cold and the tube is large enough. Kerosene contamination with heavier fractions makes it smoke in lamps.

**Cost & labour.** Refinery equipment: 500-1000 denarii. Refining one amphora (25 liters) of crude petroleum: 2-3 hours of labor, yielding roughly 8-12 liters of kerosene. ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Petroleum vapors are highly flammable and explosive. Open flames must be carefully controlled. Retort rupture can scatter hot petroleum, causing severe burns.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Petroleum distillation is straightforward chemistry, and the principles are the same as alcohol distillation (which Romans practiced). Kerosene was produced in the 18th-19th centuries by coal tar distillation, but petroleum kerosene production dates to later. Roman access to crude petroleum is limited (mostly seeps in Mesopotamia, controlled regions), and the motivation to refine it is low when whale oil and vegetable oils are available.

### kerosene - Kerosene Oil as a Lamp Fuel

**What it is / why you want it.** Kerosene is a refined fraction of petroleum (or of coal tar), a thin, golden oil that burns brightly and relatively cleanly in a lamp. It is superior to tallow (which produces smoke and soot) and comparable to whale oil but cheaper if petroleum is available.

**Why you would never guess this.** Kerosene is not a raw material but a refined product. Roman lamp fuel is olive oil (expensive, food-competitive) or tallow (smoky and foul-smelling). That a better fuel exists buried as oil in the ground, and requires heating to extract, is not obvious.

**Prerequisites.** Kerosene (from oil_refinery section above), a lamp designed for liquid fuel, a wick material (linen, papyrus, rush).

**Roman-available inputs.** Kerosene, a clay or metal lamp body, wick material, a way to trim the wick.

**Procedure.**

1. Pour kerosene into a lamp vessel (clay or bronze, with a lip or nozzle to hold the wick).

2. Insert a wick: a twisted bundle of linen fibers or papyrus, 5-10 mm diameter. The wick should extend 10-15 mm above the fuel level.

3. Light the wick with a flame. The kerosene is drawn up the wick by capillary action and vaporizes at the wick tip, feeding a bright flame.

4. Trim the wick occasionally to prevent excessive smoke.

5. Refill the lamp as fuel is consumed.

**How you know it worked.** The lamp produces a bright, white to pale yellow flame, much brighter than tallow and comparable to whale oil. Smoke is minimal - far less than tallow. The fuel burns odorlessly or with minimal odor.

**Failure modes.** The wick does not draw fuel up - try a thinner wick or re-wet it. The flame is smoky - the wick is too thick or burning at the wrong temperature (reduce the wick size). The lamp smells bad - the kerosene is contaminated with heavier fractions or crude petroleum residue.

**Cost & labour.** Kerosene: roughly 1-2 denarii per liter (ESTIMATED from refining costs). A lamp burns roughly 0.5 liters per week, costing roughly 1 denarius per week. Tallow costs roughly 0.5 denarii per liter and provides less light, making kerosene competitive on a cost-per-lumen basis once availability is factored in.

**Danger.** Low once the lamp is lit. Kerosene is flammable and can ignite if spilled and exposed to flame. The lamp can overheat and crack if the wick is too thick.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Kerosene lamps are straightforward and well-attested historically. The chemistry is simple (it is just a fuel that vaporizes and burns).

### boiler_types - Boiler Design and Heat Transfer

**What it is / why you want it.** A boiler is a closed metal vessel filled with water, heated from below or around the sides, producing steam. The steam exits and can drive a piston or turbine. Boiler design determines how efficiently heat is transferred from the fire to the water and how much steam is produced per unit of fuel burned.

**Why you would never guess this.** A boiler seems like a simple vessel, but the difference between a poorly designed boiler (low steam production, short life, explosions) and a well-designed one (steady steam, years of life) is substantial. The factors that matter - surface area between fire and water, water circulation, scale buildup, metal thickness, sealing quality - require experimentation to understand.

**Prerequisites.** Metalworking (bronze or wrought iron), understanding that steam has pressure, a way to heat the boiler (coal, coke, or wood fire), basic thermometry (or Roman-observable proxies).

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or bronze sheet for the vessel, rivets or welds for assembly, a furnace hot enough to heat metal.

**Procedure.**

1. Design and construct a cylindrical pressure vessel of wrought iron or bronze, roughly 1 meter long and 0.5 meters in diameter. Wall thickness: 3-5 mm for iron, 2-3 mm for bronze (bronze is stronger).

2. Closed ends: each end is a flat or slightly curved cap, sealed with rivets or welds.

3. Provide a water inlet at the bottom (for refilling), a steam outlet at the top, a drain plug at the very bottom (for draining and scale removal), and a large inspection door or removable top (for cleaning).

4. Mount the boiler on supports positioned above a furnace. The bottom of the boiler should be 0.5-1 meter above the fire, allowing heat to radiate upward.

5. Run the boiler's outlet through a furnace opening (or directly above the fire) so the hot exhaust gases pass around the boiler, increasing heat transfer.

6. Fill the boiler with water to roughly 2/3 capacity, leaving headspace for steam.

7. Light the fire below. Water temperature rises. At 100 °C (steam temperature), steam begins to form. Pressure builds as steam is trapped.

8. Steam pressure: measure using a simple U-tube manometer (a glass or ceramic tube bent in a U shape, filled with water or mercury). The pressure difference (steam pressure minus atmospheric) equals the height of liquid in the manometer. At 100 °C, steam pressure is 1 atmosphere. At higher temperatures (superheating, below), pressure rises.

**How you know it worked.** Steam emerges from the outlet, condensing as a white plume if the outlet tube is cool. The rate of steam production is measured by the rate at which water must be added to maintain the water level - roughly liters of steam per hour.

**Failure modes.** Scale buildup (calcium and magnesium salts from the water) accumulates inside the boiler, reducing heat transfer and eventually blocking the water level. Poor circulation causes hot spots and local boiling, creating steam pockets that reduce heat transfer. Low water level causes the bottom of the boiler to overheat and weaken. Thick rust (corrosion from iron reacting with oxygen in the water) weakens the metal.

**Cost & labour.** A hand-made boiler: 500-1000 denarii for materials (bronze or iron sheet, rivets, seals). Construction: 200-300 artisan hours. A small cast-iron boiler (if casting technology is available) might cost 200-400 denarii and take 50-100 hours.

**Danger.** Explosions if pressure exceeds the boiler strength (see safety_valve below). Steam burns causing severe blistering. Overheating causing the metal to weaken or rupture.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Boiler principles are straightforward thermodynamics. Roman knowledge of steam power is limited, but Hero of Alexandria (c. 10-70 AD) built a simple steam turbine (aeolipile), proving Roman familiarity with steam.

### superheating - Superheating Steam for Higher Efficiency

**What it is / why you want it.** Saturated steam (steam at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure) contains dissolved water droplets. When this steam expands through a piston or turbine, the water condenses, wasting energy and producing a wet, corrosive exhaust. Superheated steam (heated above 100 °C to 200-300 °C or higher) is completely dry. It expands efficiently and produces more useful work without condensation.

**Why you would never guess this.** The difference between saturated and superheated steam is not visible to the eye. Both look the same in a pipe. The performance difference - roughly 10-20% more work from the same amount of fuel - is only apparent in an indicator diagram (below) or in careful measurements.

**Prerequisites.** A working boiler (boiler_types section above), a superheater, understanding of steam thermodynamics (or just trial-and-error measurement).

**Roman-available inputs.** Saturated steam from a boiler, a superheater (a coil or bundle of tubes that runs through the hottest part of the furnace), understanding that steam can be heated further.

**Procedure.**

1. Construct a superheater: a bundle of iron or bronze tubes that run through the hottest part of the furnace (above the boiler, in direct contact with the flame and exhaust gases).

2. Connect the boiler's steam outlet to the inlet of the superheater.

3. Connect the superheater's outlet to the piston or turbine inlet (where previously the boiler outlet connected directly).

4. Allow steam to flow through the superheater. The tubes heat it to 150-300 °C, depending on furnace temperature and tube length.

5. The hotter steam exiting the superheater is "dry" - it has no suspended water droplets.

6. Measure the performance improvement using an indicator diagram (see indicator_diagram section below). Efficiency increases by roughly 10-20%.

**How you know it worked.** Steam exiting the superheater feels (carefully!) much hotter than boiler outlet steam. A piston engine or turbine driven by superheated steam produces more work than the same engine driven by saturated steam from the same boiler.

**Failure modes.** The superheater is too cool: the tubes do not spend enough time in the hot zone, or the furnace temperature is too low. The superheater tubes develop leaks and rust, reducing steam flow.

**Cost & labour.** A superheater adds roughly 10-20% to boiler system cost. ESTIMATED at 50-100 denarii additional for materials and labor. No significant operating cost beyond the original fuel.

**Danger.** The superheater tubes are extremely hot and can cause severe burns if touched. Leaking superheated steam is more dangerous than saturated steam because the temperature is higher.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Steam superheating is well-understood thermodynamics. The principle applies whenever steam temperature can be raised above 100 °C.

### safety_valve - The Safety Valve and Pressure Relief

**What it is / why you want it.** A safety valve is a spring-loaded valve that opens automatically when boiler pressure exceeds a set limit, releasing steam and preventing dangerous pressure buildup. Early boiler explosions killed many workers; a safety valve is the essential device that prevents them.

**Why you would never guess this.** Operators know intuitively that pressure can build up dangerously, but they have no way to *measure* pressure accurately and no mechanism to relieve it automatically. A safety valve is a feedback controller: it senses pressure and responds, without any operator intervention.

**Prerequisites.** Understanding that steam has pressure (from boiler_types), understanding springs and mechanical leverage (from basic mechanics), metal working for the valve body.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or bronze for the valve body, a spring (bronze or iron, coiled), a weight for adjustment.

**Procedure.**

1. Construct a simple safety valve body: a metal cylinder with a conical seat (the surface where the valve cone will rest), a valve cone (a tapered plug), and a spring mounting above.

2. The valve cone should fit snugly into the conical seat, blocking the outlet when closed.

3. Mount a spring above the valve cone, connected to a metal rod or lever.

4. The spring is pre-compressed to a tension that holds the valve cone closed. This pre-compression is adjusted by a screw or weight: a weight hung on a lever amplifies the spring force.

5. Connect the valve body to the boiler's top, with the inlet port connected to the steam space.

6. When steam pressure (acting upward on the valve cone) exceeds the spring force, the valve cone lifts, opening the outlet and releasing steam.

7. Adjust the spring pre-compression so that the valve opens at a safe pressure: roughly 3 atmospheres (300 kPa) for a typical boiler. You can estimate this by calculation: if the valve cone has a 10 mm diameter and the spring force is equivalent to 30 kg-force, then pressure = (30 kg force) / (π * 0.01 m)^2 ≈ 3 atmospheres.

**How you know it worked.** Build a test apparatus: fill a pressure vessel with water and apply slowly increasing pressure (using a hand pump or a press). Connect the safety valve to this test vessel. When pressure reaches the set point, steam or water should release and pressure should stop rising. Repeat the test several times - the valve should open reliably at the same pressure each time.

**Failure modes.** The valve sticks closed: corrosion (rust and scale) has frozen the valve cone in place. Clean and lubricate it. The valve opens too early: the spring pre-compression is too light, reduce the weight. The valve opens too late or not at all: the spring pre-compression is too high, increase the weight or reduce the spring initial compression. The valve seat wears and no longer seals: the valve cone erodes or corrodes and must be replaced.

**Cost & labour.** A safety valve: 50-150 denarii for a simple design. Construction by a skilled metalworker: 10-20 hours.

**Danger.** A faulty valve can allow a boiler to explode. The released steam is hot and can cause severe burns. Testing the valve requires building up pressure, which is inherently dangerous - confine tests to a robust vessel, and never put your hands near the release opening.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Safety valves are ancient (attested in Hero of Alexandria's writings) and straightforward mechanical devices. The principle is feedback control.

### indicator_diagram - Watt's Indicator Diagram

**What it is / why you want it.** Watt's indicator diagram is an instrument that draws the pressure-volume relationship of steam inside a cylinder during a complete engine cycle. The area of the diagram (in appropriate units) equals the work produced by the engine. By comparing indicator diagrams of different engines or fuels, you can see how efficient each is - turning thermodynamics from philosophy into measurement.

**Why you would never guess this.** Thermodynamic work is invisible: you cannot see energy or pressure directly. Watt's insight was that *drawing* the pressure versus volume graph makes it visible, and the work is literally the area under the curve. This is perhaps the single most valuable measurement tool in all of thermodynamic engineering.

**Prerequisites.** A steam engine with a piston and cylinder (a reciprocating engine), a way to measure pressure, a way to measure piston position, paper and pencil.

**Roman-available inputs.** A U-tube manometer (glass or ceramic tube bent into a U shape, partially filled with mercury or water) to measure pressure. A mechanism to record piston position: a rotating drum covered with paper, with a stylus in contact with the indicator's pressure link, so that as the piston moves, the stylus traces a pressure-position curve.

**Procedure.**

1. Connect one end of the manometer to the steam cylinder (the space above the piston, inside the engine).

2. Connect the other end of the manometer to atmosphere (or to the exhaust).

3. As the piston moves, the pressure changes, and the manometer liquid level changes. The height difference is proportional to the pressure.

4. Link the manometer liquid level to a pencil or stylus that marks paper. As the piston moves and pressure changes, the stylus traces a mark.

5. Mount a rotating drum (a cylinder rotating horizontally at constant speed, driven by the engine itself) under the stylus, with paper wrapped around it.

6. As the engine runs, the piston moves in and out, the pressure changes, and the stylus traces a closed loop on the rotating paper: the indicator diagram.

7. The vertical axis of the diagram is pressure (height of the liquid in the manometer). The horizontal axis is piston position (distance traveled), automatically recorded by the drum rotation.

8. Measure the area of the closed curve using a planimeter (a geometric instrument) or by careful grid counting on the paper.

9. Convert the area to work using the known piston area: Work = Pressure (in Pa) times Volume change (in m^3).

**How you know it worked.** The indicator diagram is a closed loop. A well-designed engine has a loop that is relatively tall and wide - high peak pressure and large volume sweep. A poorly designed engine has a loop that is short and narrow - low pressures or small volume sweep, indicating wasted work.

**Failure modes.** The diagram is not a closed loop but a squiggle: the pressure is fluctuating irregularly, indicating air in the steam or a leaking piston. The loop is compressed vertically: back-pressure (pressure in the exhaust) is too high. The stylus does not record clearly: adjust the paper tension or use a sharper stylus.

**Cost & labour.** Indicator apparatus: 200-400 denarii for a precision U-tube and recording mechanism. Each test takes 1-2 hours of observation and analysis. ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Low once the engine is running. Accessing the engine's pressure port requires opening a valve - be prepared for a hot steam leak.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Watt's indicator diagram is historical (Watt developed it in the 1780s for his steam engines) and the principle is sound. A Roman engineer with precision glass-working and an understanding of measurement could build a functional indicator.

### flywheel_governor - The Flywheel Governor and Automatic Speed Control

**What it is / why you want it.** A flywheel governor is a mechanical device that automatically regulates the speed of an engine by controlling fuel or steam supply. It is a feedback controller: if the engine speeds up, the governor reduces fuel; if it slows down, the governor increases fuel. The result is nearly constant engine speed despite varying load.

**Why you would never guess this.** The insight is that a rotating flywheel's behavior (two weights flying outward as speed increases, drawn inward by springs as speed decreases) can be coupled to a valve or lever that controls fuel supply. The controller does not need to "think" - the physics of the weights and springs does the control automatically. This is feedback without conscious intent.

**Prerequisites.** A steam engine or other prime mover, a flywheel (rotating disk), weights that can move radially, a valve to control fuel or steam supply, linkage connecting the weights to the valve.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron for the flywheel and weights, springs (bronze or iron coiled), linkage rods.

**Procedure.**

1. Mount a flywheel on the rotating shaft of the engine.

2. Attach two or more weights to the flywheel rim, so they can move radially outward and inward (think of them as hanging on hinges).

3. Connect each weight to a spring, pre-tensioned so that at normal operating speed, the springs are partially compressed and the weights are at a mid-range position.

4. Connect the weights' position to a lever that controls fuel or steam supply: when the weights move outward (high speed), the lever reduces fuel; when they move inward (low speed), the lever increases fuel.

5. The fuel supply valve (on a coal fire or a steam throttle) is adjusted by this lever.

6. Adjust the spring tension so that at the desired operating speed, the governor is in balance - the weights are at their neutral position and fuel supply is adequate.

7. Test the governor: if load is suddenly reduced, the engine speeds up, the weights fly outward, the lever reduces fuel, and the engine speed stabilizes. If load increases, the engine slows, the weights drop inward, the lever increases fuel, and speed stabilizes.

**How you know it worked.** The engine speed remains nearly constant even as load varies. The speed variation is typically 1-3% around the target speed (this is excellent - manual speed control varies much more).

**Failure modes.** The governor "hunts" - oscillates between high and low speed without stabilizing: the spring damping is too light or the linkage has too much friction. Increase friction or add mechanical damping. The governor does not respond: the weights are stuck or the linkage is jammed. The engine speed is too high or too low: adjust the spring pre-tension.

**Cost & labour.** Governor mechanism: 100-200 denarii for a simple design, 50-100 artisan hours for machining and assembly.

**Danger.** Rotating parts can catch clothing or hair. A governor failure can cause uncontrolled engine speed increases, potentially damaging the engine or attached machinery.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Flywheel governors are ancient (centrifugal governors for windmill speed control are attested in the 17th-18th centuries, and Hero of Alexandria described mechanical governors). The principle is pure kinematics and dynamics.

### steam_turbine - Steam Turbines as Rotary Prime Movers

**What it is / why you want it.** A steam turbine is a rotary engine that converts steam pressure into mechanical rotation without a piston or reciprocating mechanism. A turbine has advantages over a reciprocating engine at high power levels: no heavy masses accelerating and decelerating thousands of times per minute, better balance, higher rotational speed, and higher efficiency.

**Why you would never guess this.** A reciprocating engine seems like the obvious way to convert steam pressure to motion - the pressure pushes a piston, which moves a rod, which turns a crank. A turbine works differently: steam flows through multiple stages of blades, each stage extracting some of the pressure drop and converting it to rotational motion. The geometry is more complex and the design requires understanding of fluid dynamics.

**Prerequisites.** Metal working for blade casting or shaping, understanding of fluid flow, some experimental method to measure efficiency, high capital investment.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze or iron for the blade assembly, understanding of steam pressure and expansion.

**Procedure.**

1. Design a stationary nozzle stage: a set of stationary vanes that direct the high-pressure steam flow into a particular direction.

2. Design a rotor stage immediately downstream: a drum with blades that intercept the steam jet and extract kinetic energy, converting it to rotational force.

3. Design multiple stages (4-10 stages in a practical turbine), each with a lower steam pressure: the steam expands through the nozzles, does work on the rotors, then expands further through the next stage of nozzles.

4. Mount the rotor stages on a central shaft.

5. Construct the stationary parts (casing and nozzles) from iron or bronze, machined to precision.

6. Allow steam to flow through: it enters at high pressure and temperature, expands and cools as it passes through the stages, and exits at low pressure (near atmospheric).

7. The shaft rotates at high speed (1000-3000 rpm for typical turbines). Attach a load (a generator, a pump, a mill) to the shaft.

8. Measure the work output using indicator diagrams or by measuring the load power delivery.

9. Efficiency for a well-designed turbine is 70-85%, far higher than reciprocating engines (typically 40-60%) at high power.

**How you know it worked.** The shaft rotates smoothly at high speed, producing substantial power. If you drive a generator, electrical output is high for the steam input.

**Failure modes.** The rotor rubs against the stationary parts: thermal expansion has reduced clearance - disassemble and adjust bearing gaps. Steam leaks around the blade edges: the blade edges have eroded or warped, reducing efficiency. The rotor vibrates: imbalance in the rotating mass or instability in the fluid flow - these are complex to diagnose and fix. The turbine does not reach its designed speed: the blades may be deflecting the steam in the wrong direction - this requires experimentation to fix.

**Cost & labour.** A small steam turbine: 1000-2000 denarii for materials and machining. Design and construction: 500+ artisan hours, plus 200-300 hours of design and testing. Calendar time: 3-6 months to complete and test.

**Danger.** A high-speed rotating shaft is dangerous; the rotor can act as a flywheel and eject objects if it fails. Steam under high pressure can cause severe burns.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Steam turbine principles are sound, but designing an efficient turbine without calculus requires substantial experimentation. Hero of Alexandria (c. 10-70 AD) built a simple reaction turbine (the aeolipile), proving the basic feasibility to Romans. Modern steam turbines are highly optimized; a Roman version would be less efficient but functional.

### gas_engine - Gas Engines: Internal Combustion from Coal Gas

**What it is / why you want it.** A gas engine is an internal combustion engine that burns coal gas (or other gaseous fuel) mixed with air inside a cylinder. The explosion of the gas-air mixture drives a piston, similar to a steam engine but using chemical energy directly rather than heat transfer through a boiler.

**Why you would never guess this.** The idea that an explosion inside the cylinder can be controlled and repeated is not intuitive. Explosions are violent and destructive. However, if the explosion is confined and the piston is strong enough to withstand it, the rapid pressure rise can produce work more efficiently than a slow burn of fuel outside the cylinder (like coal in a furnace).

**Prerequisites.** Coal gas supply (from coal_gas_works section above), reciprocating engine design (similar to a steam engine), ignition system (a hot wire or spark), fuel and air mixing, understanding of combustion.

**Roman-available inputs.** Coal gas piped from a gas works, air from the atmosphere, engine block (iron or bronze), piston and cylinder, spark or ignition method (hot wire or open flame port).

**Procedure.**

1. Design a gas engine cylinder: a strong iron or bronze cylinder, similar to a steam engine piston-cylinder but with tighter sealing (internal combustion produces higher pressures than typical steam engines).

2. Design a piston: iron with rings (leather or iron rings in grooves) to seal against the cylinder wall.

3. Design an intake valve: a one-way valve that allows a mixture of coal gas and air to enter the cylinder during piston intake stroke.

4. Design an exhaust valve: opens to allow burned gases to exit after expansion.

5. Design an ignition system: a wire heated in the furnace, positioned inside the cylinder such that it glows red-hot. When the gas-air mixture enters, it contacts this wire and ignites.

6. Operate the engine on a cycle:
   - Intake stroke: piston retracts, intake valve opens, gas-air mixture enters.
   - Compression stroke: both valves close, piston pushes the mixture to higher pressure and temperature.
   - Power stroke: the hot wire ignites the mixture. Pressure and temperature spike, driving the piston out.
   - Exhaust stroke: exhaust valve opens, piston pushes out remaining gases.

7. Adjust the gas-air ratio: too much gas produces incomplete combustion (smoke, low power), too much air produces incomplete combustion and cool flame (no power). The ideal ratio is roughly 1 part gas to 4-5 parts air by volume.

8. Measure power output and efficiency using indicator diagrams.

**How you know it worked.** The engine runs on a cycle, the piston strokes regularly, and mechanical work is produced (measured by lifting a load via a pulley). Efficiency is typically 20-35% for early gas engines, much higher than steam engines (15-25%) due to higher temperature of the combustion, despite the complexity.

**Failure modes.** The ignition system fails: the hot wire cools, or the engine speed becomes too fast for the wire to reliably ignite the mixture - add a secondary ignition method. Back-firing: the mixture ignites during intake rather than at the correct time - adjust ignition timing or gas supply. Incomplete combustion: adjust the gas-air ratio.

**Cost & labour.** A gas engine: 500-1500 denarii for materials and machining. Design and construction: 200-400 artisan hours. This is complex mechanical engineering.

**Danger.** Internal combustion engines have explosions inside the cylinder. Engine failure (piston rupture, valve malfunction) can cause catastrophic injury to the operator or nearby workers. Unconfined gas escapes create fire hazards.

**Confidence: LOW** - Internal combustion engines are late 19th-century technology. The principles are sound, but designing and building one without modern materials and precision tools would be extremely difficult. A Roman craftsman would struggle with the precision required for tight piston seals and reliable ignition.

### thermopile - The Thermopile: Measuring Heat and Converting It Electrically

**What it is / why you want it.** A thermopile is a series of thermocouples (pairs of dissimilar metals in contact) connected in series. When one set of junctions is heated and the other cooled, a small electrical voltage is generated. A thermopile can measure temperature differences or convert heat directly into electrical current.

**Why you would never guess this.** Most Romans would assume that electricity and heat are entirely separate phenomena. That heating certain metal pairs produces an electric current is experimentally discoverable but not obvious.

**Prerequisites.** Two dissimilar metals (copper and iron, or bismuth and antimony), a way to join them reliably (soldering, riveting), a sensitive galvanometer to measure small currents.

**Roman-available inputs.** Copper wire and iron wire (both easily made by Roman metalworkers), solder (tin-lead alloy, available through tinners), lead (for plumbago - graphite - electrodes if building galvanometers).

**Procedure.**

1. Make a thermocouple: join a copper wire to an iron wire using solder, creating a strong joint. Repeat to make 10-20 such junctions, alternating which metal is connected to which.

2. Connect the junctions in series: the free end of the copper from the first junction connects to the iron of the second, the copper of the second connects to the iron of the third, and so on.

3. The ends of this chain (the free end of the last copper and the free end of the last iron) are the electrical terminals of the thermopile.

4. Heat one end of the thermopile (dip it in hot water or hold it above a candle flame) and cool the other end (dip it in cold water or wrap it in wet cloth).

5. Connect a sensitive ammeter or galvanometer between the terminals. A small current flows: typically microamps to tens of microamps, depending on the temperature difference and the number of junctions.

6. The larger the temperature difference, the larger the current.

**How you know it worked.** A galvanometer deflects when the thermopile is heated, indicating current flow. The deflection increases with temperature difference.

**Failure modes.** No current flows: the junctions are not well-connected (bad solder joints), or the two ends are not at different temperatures. The current is too small to measure: add more junctions in series, or increase the temperature difference.

**Cost & labour.** A 10-junction thermopile: 10-20 denarii for materials. Construction: 3-5 hours of careful soldering and assembly.

**Danger.** Low. Hot surfaces can cause burns.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - The thermoelectric (Seebeck) effect is real and well-understood. However, the output power is extremely small - a thermopile is useful for measuring temperature but not for practical power generation. Romans would discover the effect through experimentation but would likely dismiss it as a curiosity.

### selenium_photocell - Selenium Photocells and the Photoelectric Effect

**What it is / why you want it.** Selenium is a metalloid (elemental substance), chemically similar to sulfur, that exhibits a photoelectric effect: when exposed to light, its electrical conductivity increases. A selenium photocell is a thin piece of selenium sandwiched between electrodes. When light shines on it, the resistance drops and current flows. This is one of the earliest light-sensitive electronic devices.

**Why you would never guess this.** The photoelectric effect (light causes electricity) is a quantum phenomenon and deeply non-obvious. However, it is experimentally observable and was discovered in the 19th century, long before quantum mechanics was understood.

**Prerequisites.** Selenium metal (extracted by roasting selenium-bearing sulfide ores), glass to house the photocell, metals for electrodes (copper or platinum), a sensitive galvanometer.

**Roman-available inputs.** Selenium: a rare but available element. Selenium occurs in some copper ores (particularly in Spain), but extraction and purification are challenging. Mediterranean copper miners were aware of the "red dust" (selenium compounds) that appeared in their smelting residues, though they did not know what it was.

**Procedure.**

1. Extract and purify selenium: roast selenium sulfide ore in a furnace, producing sulfur dioxide gas and leaving elemental selenium. Refine the crude selenium by melting and casting into ingots.

2. Cut or grind a thin disk of selenium, roughly 1-2 mm thick and 10-20 mm in diameter.

3. Sandwich the selenium disk between two metal electrodes (copper or platinum), with insulation around the edges. House this in a glass envelope that allows light to reach the selenium.

4. Connect a battery (a voltaic cell, from the battery module if available, or a chemical battery) in series with the photocell and a galvanometer (sensitive ammeter).

5. In darkness, measure the baseline current: it is very small (microamps or less).

6. Expose the photocell to light (sunlight, a lamp, or a reflector concentrating light). The current increases substantially - by 10 to 100 times depending on light intensity.

7. The output current is proportional to light intensity, so the photocell can be used as a light meter.

**How you know it worked.** Galvanometer deflection increases with light intensity. In complete darkness, deflection is minimal or zero. In sunlight, deflection is substantial.

**Failure modes.** No response to light: the selenium is poorly purified or the electrodes are not in good contact. Very small response: the electrode area is too small or the selenium is too thick.

**Cost & labour.** Extracting selenium: 100-500 denarii depending on ore availability. Building a photocell: 10-50 denarii. The photocell is a laboratory curiosity; practical applications are limited.

**Danger.** Selenium is mildly toxic. Avoid breathing fumes during extraction or ingesting powder. The electrical current produced is tiny and poses no danger.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The selenium photocell effect is real and historically attested. The first practical photocells (1873+) used selenium. However, the application is limited to measurement or signaling; the power output is far too small to power anything practical.

### silicon_solar_cell - Silicon Photovoltaic Cells and Modern Solar Power

**What it is / why you want it.** A silicon solar cell is a semiconductor device that converts light directly into electricity. Modern silicon solar cells achieve 15-22% efficiency and can power large installations.

**Why you would never guess this.** The silicon photovoltaic effect is a quantum phenomenon, fundamentally dependent on the band structure of crystalline silicon. Its discovery (20th century) required advanced materials science, quantum theory, and precision manufacturing. It is not obvious from first principles.

**Prerequisites.** Extremely pure silicon (99.9999% purity), crystal growing (Czochralski or Bridgman process), doping (adding trace amounts of phosphorus and boron), diffusion (heating to distribute dopants), photolithography (if making contact grids).

**Roman-available inputs.** None of the prerequisites exist in Roman materials science. Silicon metal can be extracted from sand, but purifying it to 99.9999% purity requires 20th-century chemical processes. Crystal growing requires precision furnaces and inert atmospheres. Doping requires knowledge of semiconductor physics. Photolithography requires vacuum and extreme-ultraviolet light.

**Procedure.** Not provided, as the procedure is beyond Roman capability.

**How you know it worked.** Modern silicon solar cells, illuminated by sunlight, produce electrical current. A 10 cm x 10 cm cell in direct sunlight produces roughly 1-2 watts at 0.5 volts.

**Failure modes.** Not applicable to Roman engineers.

**Cost & labour.** Modern silicon solar cells: 10-20 denarii per watt-peak of capacity (historical cost). This is cheap compared to other power sources.

**Danger.** Crystalline silicon produces no danger. The electrical outputs are modest.

**Confidence: LOW** - The physics of silicon photovoltaics is sound and well-understood. However, every aspect of the technology - from extracting and purifying silicon to growing crystals to doping to manufacturing contacts - requires industrial infrastructure that did not exist until the 20th century. The dependency order is backwards: you cannot have silicon solar cells without first having semiconductor factories, which require computers, which require semiconductors, which require solar cells for power, etc. This is not a shortcut to energy independence for a pre-industrial society.

### lead_acid_battery - Lead-Acid Batteries and Electrical Storage

**What it is / why you want it.** A lead-acid battery is an electrochemical device that stores electrical energy chemically and releases it on demand. A single cell has lead and lead oxide electrodes in sulfuric acid, producing 2 volts. Connecting multiple cells in series produces 12 volts (6 cells) or higher. Lead-acid batteries are rechargeable and have high power density.

**Why you would never guess this.** The electrochemical reactions that produce electricity are not obvious without conducting experiments on metals and solutions. That lead plates in acid can generate electricity is experimentally discoverable but not intuitive.

**Prerequisites.** Lead (readily available from Roman mines), lead oxide (made by roasting lead in air), concentrated sulfuric acid (made from sulfur or from vitriol), lead plates for electrodes, a glass or pottery vessel for the electrolyte, understanding of electrochemistry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Lead: Spain and Greece produce large quantities. Lead oxide: made by heating lead. Sulfuric acid: produced from sulfur via the vitriol process or collected from iron vitriol deposits. Glass vessels: available through glassmakers (blowing or casting).

**Procedure.**

1. Construct a battery cell: a glass or ceramic jar, filled with sulfuric acid (diluted 1:1 with water for safe handling).

2. Prepare electrodes: a sheet of lead and a sheet of lead dioxide (PbO2). The lead dioxide is made by oxidizing lead in a furnace - heating lead in air produces PbO and PbO2 on the surface; scraping this oxidized layer produces lead dioxide.

3. Insert the two electrodes into the acid, separated by 10-20 mm.

4. Connecting a wire between the electrodes allows current to flow externally. The lead is oxidized (losing electrons) and the lead dioxide is reduced (gaining electrons), producing a voltage of roughly 2 volts across the terminals.

5. Connect multiple cells (6-12) in series to produce 12-24 volts.

6. To recharge the battery, apply external current in the reverse direction: this drives the oxidation of lead back to lead dioxide and reduces lead dioxide back to lead.

**How you know it worked.** A voltmeter connected across the battery shows 2 volts per cell. If a load (a resistor, a lamp, a motor) is connected, current flows and the load operates.

**Failure modes.** Voltage drops with use: the electrodes have been consumed (this is normal; recharge to restore). The battery does not hold charge: the electrodes have been damaged or the acid is contaminated with impurities. The acid level drops: water is consumed in the electrochemical reaction; add more dilute acid.

**Cost & labour.** A single-cell battery: 10-20 denarii for materials. A 6-cell, 12-volt battery: 100-200 denarii. Construction: 5-10 hours per cell.

**Danger.** Sulfuric acid is caustic and will burn skin and eyes. Explosive hydrogen gas is produced during charging - do not use an open flame near a charging battery. Electrical shock from a 12-24 volt battery is unlikely to be fatal but can be uncomfortable.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Lead-acid battery chemistry is straightforward and the electrodes and acid are available to Romans. However, the electrochemistry requires understanding the Daniell cell (Volta's original battery), which requires 18th-19th century knowledge. A Roman might stumble on the effect empirically, but would not understand the principles.

### high_voltage_transmission - Long-Distance Electrical Power Transmission at High Voltage

**What it is / why you want it.** Transmitting electrical power over long distances at high voltage is more efficient than at low voltage because resistive losses are lower. A transformer can step up voltage at the power source and step it down at the destination.

**Why you would never guess this.** Power loss in transmission is proportional to the square of the current (P_loss = I^2 * R). Reducing current by increasing voltage is the solution - but transformers require alternating current and an understanding of electromagnetic induction. Romans have neither.

**Prerequisites.** Alternating current (AC) generation, transformers (based on electromagnetic induction), high-voltage distribution infrastructure, understanding of electrical engineering at an advanced level.

**Roman-available inputs.** None. The prerequisites are beyond Roman capability.

**Procedure.** Not provided, as the technology is beyond practical Roman implementation.

**How you know it worked.** Modern power grids transmit power at voltages of 100 kV to 1 MV over hundreds of kilometers with power losses of 5-10%. Without high-voltage transmission, losses would be 50+%.

**Failure modes.** Not applicable.

**Cost & labour.** Modern transmission infrastructure: billions of denarii for a continental system. The investment is justified by efficiency, but the capital requirements are enormous.

**Danger.** High-voltage lines are extremely dangerous - contact is fatal. Lightning can destroy equipment.

**Confidence: LOW** - The physics is sound, but the technology requires AC generators, transformers, and transmission line construction. These are beyond Roman capability.

### hydroelectric - Hydroelectric Power Generation

**What it is / why you want it.** Hydroelectric power generation converts the potential energy of falling water into electrical energy using a water turbine and electrical generator. A single facility can produce megawatts of power continuously, with zero fuel consumption and no air pollution.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hydroelectric power requires a generator (an electrical device that Romans do not have) and a turbine (advanced mechanical engineering). The integration of the two is a later invention.

**Prerequisites.** A water source with sufficient head (at least 10 meters practically), a water turbine (from water_turbine or pelton_wheel sections above), an electrical generator (from the electricity module), civil engineering to dam or channel the water.

**Roman-available inputs.** Water from rivers or aqueducts, turbine and generator as above.

**Procedure.**

1. Locate or build a water source with sufficient head: either a natural waterfall, or a dam creating a reservoir.

2. Install a turbine at the base of the head (or at the dam), with the inlet at the high-elevation water surface and the outlet at the low-elevation tailwater.

3. Connect the turbine shaft to an electrical generator (a rotating coil in a magnetic field, producing AC current).

4. Route the electrical output to users via transmission lines.

5. Power output is proportional to head and flow rate: Power (watts) = 9800 * head (meters) * flow (cubic meters per second), multiplied by efficiency (typically 70-90%).

**How you know it worked.** The generator produces electrical current proportional to water flow and head. A facility with 100-meter head and 10 cubic meters per second flow produces roughly 10 megawatts (after accounting for efficiency).

**Failure modes.** Low water flow: drought or seasonal variation reduces power output. Dam failure: catastrophic flooding if the dam structure is inadequate. Silting: sediment reduces reservoir capacity over decades.

**Cost & labour.** Building a hydroelectric facility: hundreds of thousands to millions of denarii, depending on scale and civil engineering complexity. A small facility (100 kW): 50,000-100,000 denarii. A large facility (10 MW): millions of denarii. Calendar time: 2-5 years of construction.

**Danger.** Dam failure is catastrophic. High-voltage electricity is dangerous. Rotating machinery can entangle workers.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Hydroelectric power generation is straightforward physics. Romans have the materials for water turbines, but generators require Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction (19th century). A Roman without generators could not build a practical hydroelectric facility, though the water turbine part is achievable.

### load_factor - The Load Factor and Capacity Factor

**What it is / why you want it.** The load factor is the ratio of average power demand to peak power demand. A system with consistent demand has a high load factor (good), while one with highly variable demand has a low load factor (bad). Understanding load factor is essential for sizing generators and storage systems.

**Why you would never guess this.** The load factor is an abstract metric - it is not a physical thing but a dimensionless number. However, it directly affects the cost and efficiency of electrical systems.

**Prerequisites.** Measurement of power demand over time, understanding of electrical load, some method of storage (a battery, or a hydroelectric reservoir) if the load factor is low.

**Roman-available inputs.** None directly - load factor is measured, not built. However, understanding the concept is valuable for planning energy systems.

**Procedure.**

1. Measure the electrical demand over a full day or a full year, recording power consumption at regular intervals (hourly or daily).

2. Find the peak demand: the highest power draw.

3. Calculate the average demand: the total energy consumed divided by the time period.

4. Load factor = average demand / peak demand.

5. For example, if a city uses 100 MW on average but peaks at 150 MW, the load factor is 100/150 = 0.67 or 67%.

6. A high load factor (80%+) is ideal: the generator is running near full capacity most of the time. A low load factor (40%-) is problematic: the generator is idle much of the time, wasting capacity.

**How you know it worked.** By tracking load over time and computing the factor, you understand how over-sized your generation capacity is. If load factor is low, adding storage (batteries, pumped hydro) can smooth demand and reduce the required peak capacity.

**Failure modes.** No failures - this is a measurement and analysis tool.

**Cost & labour.** Measurement and analysis: 10-50 hours for a year of hourly data.

**Danger.** None.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Load factor is a straightforward concept and well-attested in modern power system engineering.

## Sources and Confidence

This module synthesizes primary historical sources, engineering handbooks, and scientific experiments conducted over 200+ years. Key sources include:

- Vitruvius, *De architectura* (first century BC): water mills and pumps.
- Pliny the Elder, *Naturalis Historia* (first century AD): mineral resources and methods.
- Hero of Alexandria, *Pneumatica* and *Automatica* (first-second century AD): steam devices and mechanical systems.
- Smeaton, John. "An Experimental Enquiry Concerning the Natural Powers of Water and Wind to Turn Mills and Other Machinery" (1759): experimental measurement of wheel efficiency.
- Newcomen, Thomas and Watt, James (18th-19th centuries): steam engine development and indicators.
- Faraday, Michael and Maxwell, James Clerk (19th century): electrical theory.

The confidence ratings reflect the span from well-attested ancient practices (water mills, coal, simple metallurgy) through medieval and Renaissance advances (windmills, refining) to early industrial technologies (steam turbines, generators) to modern innovations (solar cells, high-voltage transmission). Modules rated HIGH are either ancient or rely on straightforward physics and materials available to Romans. Modules rated MEDIUM involve more complex engineering or less historical precedent. Modules rated LOW are speculative, either because Roman materials science is inadequate or because the underlying theory was unknown until the modern era.

The overarching message is that Rome has access to water power and the measurement discipline to optimize it. Coal and petroleum offer higher energy density but require new infrastructure. Boilers and governors are mechanical feedback systems that can be built with trial-and-error. Steam turbines and electrical systems require a conceptual leap beyond Roman science, but their principles are sound and not forbidden by the laws of physics.
