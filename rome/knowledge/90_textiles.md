# Module 90: Textiles, Fibre and Clothing

Rome already possesses warp-weighted and two-beam looms, the drop spindle, fulling with urine and fuller's earth, dyeing with madder, woad and murex, felting, sailcloth, and the ability to trade for wool, linen, cotton and silk. This module documents how those crafts evolve into industrial processes that radically increase the speed, uniformity and scale of cloth production - and the labour displacement that comes with it.

The textile industry of the pre-industrial world is almost entirely female and largely enslaved labour in Roman households. Hand spinning, hand weaving, dyeing and finishing are the chief occupations of women throughout the Empire. Vespasian was offered a labour-saving machine for stone-cutting and explicitly rejected it, saying it would deprive the poor of employment (Suetonius, Vespasian 18). Mechanisation of textiles will face the same objection. The mitigation is not ethical - there is none you can offer that a Roman magistrate will accept - but practical: new machinery creates demand for new skills, new materials, new trades, and new wealth. Some workers will be displaced; more will migrate into different roles.

### spinning_wheel - The Flyer Spindle (*rotatio filatoria*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A hand-cranked wheel that holds a spindle under torsion while simultaneously winding the twisted fibre onto a bobbin. It spins fibre into thread perhaps four to six times faster than a hand spindle, and produces more uniform thread.

**Why you would never guess this.** The crucial component is not the wheel itself but the *flyer* - a U-shaped guide with the spindle wire running through it. The flyer twists and winds simultaneously, whereas any spindle (hand or wheel-driven) without a flyer must be operated in two alternating motions: twist, then wind. A wheel without a flyer is merely a faster spindle and produces no real improvement.

**Prerequisites.** Woodworking (timber to specification), metalworking (iron spindle and flyer), basic mechanics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the frame and wheel, preferably ash or birch for the wheel itself for strength and light weight. Oak for frame timbers. Iron for the spindle wire and flyer arm. Wool, linen or cotton fibres prepared by combing.

**Procedure.** 
1. Turn a wheel 60-80 cm in diameter from seasoned timber, with a groove around its circumference for the driving belt.
2. Fashion a spindle from iron wire approximately 8 mm diameter and 25 cm long, pointed at one end and fitted with a small disc or hook at the other.
3. Forge a flyer from iron wire, roughly U-shaped, 20 cm wide, with the spindle wire running through its axis.
4. Attach a bobbin (a hollow spindle of wood or bone, 8-10 cm long) to the spindle behind the flyer.
5. Mount the wheel on a treadle or crank so it rotates freely at 150-200 rpm under foot or hand.
6. Feed the prepared fibre into the flyer opening, and it will simultaneously twist and wind onto the bobbin as you turn the wheel.
7. Adjust the tension by moving the flyer closer to or farther from the wheel so that twist outpaces winding, drawing the fibre through at a steady rate.

**How you know it worked.** The thread should be smooth, uniform in thickness, and should not break if pulled moderately hard. Compare it to hand-spun thread from the same fibre: if it is thinner, stronger and less slubby (inconsistent thickness), the flyer is working.

**Failure modes.** If the fibre does not feed smoothly, the flyer may be catching; check that the spindle wire runs freely through it. If the thread comes out too thick and weak, the bobbin is winding faster than the flyer can twist; slow your treadle or adjust the gear ratio. If the wheel skips or stalls, the bearing may need greasing (animal fat will do).

**Cost & labour.** Time to construct: one experienced wheelwright, 2-3 days ESTIMATED (basis: comparable to water-mill wheel construction). One ironsmith, 1 day to forge flyer and spindle. A spinner can produce perhaps 50-100 grams of finished thread per day with a wheel, versus 10-20 grams by hand spindle ESTIMATED (basis: historical accounts report 4-6x increase). Materials cost roughly 50-150 denarii for wood and iron per wheel ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Long hair and loose sleeves must be constrained; a treadle wheel can catch and break fingers. The spinning itself is not chemically dangerous.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The flyer spindle is attested in China by the 11th century and India by the 13th. The physics of simultaneous twist-and-wind is straightforward. The only constraint is forging consistent iron wire, which Rome can do.

### treadle_loom - Pedal-Powered Warp Tensioning (*textorium pedali*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A horizontal loom in which the weaver works both hands on the weft shuttle while foot pedals control the shed (the separation of warp threads), allowing for much faster weaving than hand-operated frame looms.

**Why you would never guess this.** The loom itself is not novel; what matters is the mechanical linkage from pedals to the heddles (the loops holding alternate warp threads). This mechanism must be carefully proportioned so that the pedals move the heddles just enough - too little and threads catch; too much and the warp tears.

**Prerequisites.** Woodworking to build loom frame. Basic mechanics for linkages. Knowledge of existing two-beam or warp-weighted loom operation.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber for frame and beams (fir or pine for large timbers, oak for wear points). Wood or bone for heddles. Rope or plant-fibre cord for heddle loops. Warp thread prepared from spun fibre.

**Procedure.**
1. Construct a horizontal loom frame 1.5-2 meters wide and 1 meter tall, with two parallel beams to hold warp tension.
2. Rig heddles on two separate frames: every alternate warp thread passes through a heddle loop on the upper frame; the others on the lower frame.
3. Attach the upper heddle frame to pedals via a wooden linkage and pivot so that pressing the left pedal raises it and pressing the right pedal lowers it.
4. Thread the warp onto the beams under moderate tension (warp should not distort when touched but should have some play).
5. To weave: pass the weft shuttle through the shed (the gap between raised and lowered warp), then strike it down with a beater, then press the opposite pedal to swap the shed and repeat.

**How you know it worked.** The cloth should be even and consistent in weave density. The same width thread passing through the same opening repeatedly should produce a regular, flat cloth. If you see diagonal wrinkling or areas of loose weave, the heddle frames are not moving synchronously.

**Failure modes.** If the heddle frames stick or move only partially, the wooden linkage may be swelling from humidity; dry it or plane it thinner. If the warp breaks frequently, the tension is too high or the heddles are misaligned, pinching the thread. If the cloth is wavy, the beater is not striking perpendicular to the warp.

**Cost & labour.** Construction by one skilled carpenter: 4-6 days ESTIMATED. Typical trained weaver can produce 40-50 meters of cloth per week on a treadle loom versus 15-20 meters per week on a hand-operated frame loom ESTIMATED (basis: 2-3x acceleration reported in early modern sources). Ongoing material and rope cost, roughly 10-20 denarii per month for heddle maintenance ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** Repetitive strain on the legs from constant pedalling. The loom itself has no chemical or fire hazard. Watch for trapped fingers near the heddle frames.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Treadle looms are attested in Europe by the 12th century and the mechanics are straightforward. The constraint is building and aligning the heddle linkage correctly.

### carding_combing - Fibre Preparation by Brushing (*pectinatio fibrarum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Two wire-brushed paddles or rollers that parallel-align fibres and remove seed fragments, dirt and short fibres, producing a uniform sheet (card or sliver) ready for spinning into strong, smooth thread.

**Why you would never guess this.** Carding and combing do different things: combing produces parallel, long-staple fibres suitable for fine thread; carding produces tangled, blended shorter fibres and is faster. A cotton or wool preparation using only combing is weak; carding alone is weak. Most efficient practice uses both, combing the best fibres and carding the waste.

**Prerequisites.** Metalworking (wire or sharp teeth), carpentry for frames and handles.

**Roman-available inputs.** Animal fibres (wool, fur) or plant fibres (flax, cotton). Iron or bronze wire or bent iron. Timber for paddle frames.

**Procedure for carding paddles:**
1. Forge or wind iron wire approximately 1 mm diameter into a grid pattern with approximately 8-10 wires per centimetre.
2. Stretch this grid taut across a wooden frame roughly 15 x 20 cm, with a handle at one end.
3. Make two identical paddles (called cards or carders).
4. Lay a handful of raw fibre on one paddle, then stroke it repeatedly with the other paddle at an angle, gradually transferring the fibre from one to the other.
5. Repeat until the fibres are parallel and any knots or debris have been extracted.
6. At the end, use a comb (a fine-toothed implement) to draw off the fibres as a smooth sliver.

**How you know it worked.** The fibres should be roughly parallel, with no visible knots or clumps. The resulting card should be fluffy and light, not dense or matted. When drawn through a fine comb, it should come off as a continuous sliver without breaking.

**Failure modes.** If the fibres are still tangled after carding, you have not stroked long enough or the wire gauge is too coarse; use finer wire or comb more carefully. If the fibres keep shedding and falling apart during combing, the cards have made them too short; preserve longer fibres by using combing alone on good material.

**Cost & labour.** Two cards, roughly 50-100 denarii for materials ESTIMATED. One experienced carder can prepare perhaps 500-700 grams of fibre per day ESTIMATED (basis: manual fibre prep requires perhaps 20 percent of the time invested in spinning). No chemical hazard; mechanical repetitive strain is the concern.

**Danger.** Wire is sharp. If a card breaks, shards of wire may remain in the work. Always inspect equipment before use.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Carding with wire cards emerges clearly in late medieval sources. Romans had combing and hand-preparation; whether they had wire cards is unclear. The wire can be drawn and formed, but the technique is later. Use combs and hand-pulling as a conservative fallback for Roman 100 AD, or carding cards as an innovation to be developed.

### flying_shuttle - Spring-Loaded Weft Delivery (*spica volatica*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A shuttle (the device carrying weft thread through the loom) that is slung back and forth across the loom by hand-operated springs or levers, rather than thrown by hand. This reduces the hand strain required to weave and allows narrower cloth to be woven very quickly, or wider cloth to be woven at all by a single weaver (normally two weavers were needed for very wide cloth).

**Why you would never guess this.** The bottleneck in hand-weaving is not spinning the weft but delivering it across the warp fast enough. Throwing a shuttle by hand is exhausting and inaccurate. The spring-loaded mechanism stores energy from the weaver's motion and releases it suddenly, accelerating the shuttle far faster than any hand throw.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry for shuttle and loom frame, metalworking for springs or levers, basic mechanics. Existing treadle loom.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for shuttle (a smooth, streamlined hollow form, roughly 25 cm long and 3 cm diameter). Iron for springs or lever arms. Leather or gut for spring tension. Weft thread already on bobbins.

**Procedure.**
1. Construct a shuttle as a hollow wooden form, pointed at both ends, with a slit in the centre wide enough to feed thread through.
2. Load a bobbin of weft onto a spindle inside the shuttle.
3. Attach levers or curved metal springs to the two ends of the loom's batten (the weft-striking bar), positioned so that the weaver can push or pull them sharply.
4. When the springs are compressed and released, they propel the shuttle rapidly across the warp.
5. The weft thread feeds out from the bobbin as the shuttle travels and naturally stops when the shuttle reaches the opposite end and strikes the loom frame (the sley).

**How you know it worked.** The shuttle should travel the entire width of the loom with a single sharp push or pull of the lever. The weft should be consistently delivered without tangling or breaking. A single weaver should be able to weave cloth as wide as two hand-weavers could before - roughly 120 cm or more.

**Failure modes.** If the shuttle gets stuck mid-loom, the batten may not be level; check for twisting. If weft tangles or breaks, the thread is not feeding freely from the bobbin; check that the slit is clear and the bobbin rotates easily. If the springs lose their tension quickly, the metal may be work-hardening and becoming brittle; re-temper or replace springs.

**Cost & labour.** Conversion of an existing treadle loom to flying shuttle: one experienced mechanic, 2-3 days ESTIMATED. Spring steel or iron for levers costs 30-50 denarii ESTIMATED. An experienced weaver can weave perhaps 50 percent faster with flying shuttles, producing 60-75 meters per week instead of 40-50 ESTIMATED.

**Danger.** A spring-loaded shuttle has real force; a misfire can strike the weaver's hand or face. Always keep clear of the mechanism when it is under tension.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The flying shuttle (John Kay, 1733) is well-attested historically. The spring mechanism is feasible with Roman metalworking. However, it is a later medieval invention, and whether Romans would independently develop it is unclear. Include it as an innovation possibility.

### spinning_jenny - Multi-Spindle Hand Spinner (*rotatio multiplex*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A mechanical frame holding multiple spindles in a row, all driven by a single crank or wheel, allowing one spinner to spin perhaps eight threads simultaneously. This roughly octuples the spinning output per spinner.

**Why you would never guess this.** A spinning wheel spins one thread at a time; you might think doubling the spindles simply doubles the work. But if the spindles are rigged in parallel and all rotated by the same mechanism, they all spin at the same rate, and a single crank can drive them all. The trick is feeding eight separate fibres smoothly at once.

**Prerequisites.** Mechanics (bearings, crank drives), woodworking (frame), carpentry for spindle mounts.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber for frame, iron for spindles and crank, bone or wooden flyers, bobbin frame.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a frame, roughly 1 metre wide and 60 cm tall, oriented vertically or at a slight angle.
2. Mount eight spindles in a row, each with its own flyer and bobbin, all on parallel shafts aligned exactly horizontally.
3. Rig all eight spindles to a single large wheel via a belt or gearing, so that when the wheel turns, all eight spindles turn at the same rate.
4. From the flyers, lead eight separate fibre strands to a guide bar that the spinner can move to control feeding.
5. As the operator turns the crank, all eight spindles twist; as the operator moves the guide bar away, all eight fibres are drawn and wound simultaneously.

**How you know it worked.** All eight threads should come off at roughly the same thickness and twist. If one thread is thicker, it is being fed too quickly; adjust the guide position. The output should be approximately eight times that of a single-spindle wheel when operated by one person.

**Failure modes.** If the spindles run at different rates, the belt may be slipping or the shafts may be misaligned; check alignment and tension. If threads tangle together, the guide bar may not be far enough from the spindles; increase the distance. If one spindle stops or jumps, it may be bearing friction; lubricate with animal fat.

**Cost & labour.** One carpenter and one metalworker collaborating, 5-7 days to build ESTIMATED. Ongoing maintenance cost, roughly 20-30 denarii per month. Eight times the output of a single spinner means roughly one jenny can do the work of eight hand wheels, assuming no time lost to setup and adjustment. A practical output of roughly 400-800 grams per day per jenny ESTIMATED (basis: 8 spindles at 50-100 grams per spinner per day, minus overhead).

**Danger.** With eight threads under tension and eight spindles rotating at high speed, the mechanism is complex and can catch hands or hair. Long hair and loose sleeves are a hazard.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The spinning jenny (James Hargreaves, c. 1764) is historical and mechanically feasible with Roman resources. However, the coordination required to feed eight fibres smoothly and the bearing precision needed suggest this is an innovation, not something a Roman smith would stumble on without direction. It is an excellent second-generation textile technology.

### water_frame - Water-Powered Spinning Frame (*rotatio aquae*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A spinning machine driven directly by water power - the same principle as a water-powered mill, but rigged to drive a series of spindles at much higher speeds (several hundred rpm) than any hand-cranked spinner could achieve. This allows production of very strong thread suitable for warps, using even short fibres.

**Why you would never guess this.** The challenge is not the water wheel but the transmission. A water wheel spins quite fast already (perhaps 50-100 rpm), but spindles must spin several hundred times per minute to produce usable thread. This requires step-up gearing - a system where each gear is smaller than the one driving it, multiplying speed. Such gearing was not common in Roman machinery.

**Prerequisites.** Water mill construction (dam, wheel, bearing, axle), advanced gearing (wood or metal cogwheels), carpentry for spindle frames and fibre guidance.

**Roman-available inputs.** Flowing water (river or artificial conduit). Wood for wheel and frame, iron for axles and bearings, bronze for gears if available (iron gears are weaker but feasible).

**Procedure.**
1. Construct a water wheel 2-3 meters in diameter, preferably an undershot wheel (water flows beneath it) as it is simpler. Orient the wheel shaft horizontally.
2. Attach a small cogwheel (pinion, perhaps 30 teeth) to the main shaft.
3. Rig a larger cogwheel (perhaps 90-120 teeth) to rotate on a parallel shaft, meshing with the pinion. This shaft now spins at 3-4 times the main wheel's speed.
4. Repeat this gearing with a second stage if necessary to achieve spindle speeds of 300-500 rpm.
5. Mount multiple spindles (eight to sixteen) on the output shaft, or rig them via belt drive. Each spindle has a flyer and bobbin.
6. Run prepared fibre strands horizontally through guides and into the flyers, with some mechanism to control draw and winding.

**How you know it worked.** The spindles should run steadily at high speed without wobbling, and they should produce thread of consistent fine quality. The output should be much greater than hand spinning - perhaps fifty to a hundred times higher per unit of labour input, since the water wheel runs continuously.

**Failure modes.** If the spindles wobble or runout unevenly, the bearings may have excessive play; machine them more precisely or use better bearing design. If gears slip or break, the teeth may be too shallow or the wood too soft; use denser wood or metal. If thread breaks frequently, the draw may be too aggressive; adjust the guide or fibre feed rate. In winter, ice may block the water supply; this is unavoidable.

**Cost & labour.** A full water-powered spinning mill is a major infrastructure project. Building: one experienced millwright and team of workers, 30-60 days ESTIMATED (basis: similar to a water mill for grain milling). Upkeep: regular maintenance of wheel, bearings, gears, roughly 100-150 denarii per month ESTIMATED. Output: perhaps 5-10 kg of finished thread per day per mill, running continuously ESTIMATED (basis: 8-16 spindles at high speed, continuous operation). This is equivalent to perhaps 50-100 hand spinners working simultaneously.

**Danger.** Moving water wheels, gears, and spindles are all hazards. Long hair and clothing must be closely constrained. Burns from friction are possible. If the wheel seizes, the sudden stop can throw a worker off balance.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Water-powered spinning is attested (Richard Arkwright's water frame, 1769). The gearing required is within Roman capability - watermills with gearing for grinding were common. The challenge is the scale and precision, but it is feasible. This is a later-stage technology, not early Roman, but Roman engineering can accomplish it.

### spinning_mule - Hand Cranked Fine Spinner (*rotatio mula*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine combining the best of the jenny (multiple spindles) and the water frame (high spindle speed and fine twist), operated by hand crank or small water drive, capable of producing very fine, strong thread for woven cloth and increasingly for knitting.

**Why you would never guess this.** The mule is a hybrid, but the two technologies it combines are not obvious compromises. The jenny produces fine thread but at low speed; the water frame is fast but initially produced weaker thread because it was too fast for most fibres to accept twist uniformly. The mule adds a moving carriage that carries the spindles back and forth, alternately winding and drawing, combining the best of both. The carriage motion is what makes a mule a mule.

**Prerequisites.** Advanced gearing and bearings, carpentry for frame and carriage, mechanical linkages, hand crank or water drive. Understanding of both jenny and water frame mechanics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber for frame and carriage rails, iron for spindles and bearings, wood for gears or metal gears if available.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a frame roughly 3-4 meters long with two rails running lengthwise.
2. Mount a carriage (a trolley with wheels) on these rails that can slide forward and backward smoothly over a distance of roughly 50-100 cm.
3. On the carriage, mount twenty to fifty spindles with flyers and bobbins, all on one axis, driven by a belt or shaft connected to a hand crank or water wheel.
4. Lead fibre strands from a reservoir (the cotton roved and prepared fibre) to the flyers on the carriage.
5. As the crank turns, the spindles twist. Simultaneously, the carriage moves backward, drawing the fibre and allowing it to accept twist. When the carriage reaches full extension, it reverses, moving forward while the spindles continue to rotate, winding the twisted fibre onto the bobbins.
6. Repeat: draw, twist, wind, return. The rhythm is continuous.

**How you know it worked.** The thread should be fine (two to three times finer than jenny thread), strong, and very even. Output should be perhaps ten to twenty times that of a hand spinner, yet still fine enough for high-quality cloth.

**Failure modes.** If the carriage sticks or creeps, the rails may be warped; plane them straight or use metal rails if available. If thread breaks during drawing, the draw is too fast or the twist is insufficient; adjust the timing. If thread is too thick, too much twist is accumulating; reduce spindle speed or increase draw rate. If the carriage motion and spindle rotation get out of sync, the linkage connecting them may be slipping or bent; realign carefully.

**Cost & labour.** Construction: one expert mechanic and carpenter collaborating, 15-20 days ESTIMATED. Ongoing maintenance roughly 40-50 denarii per month. A single worker cranking a small mule can produce thread equivalent to perhaps 20-30 hand spinners ESTIMATED (basis: jenny output x mule acceleration factor, minus manual crank fatigue). Alternatively, a water-driven mule can run unattended, producing continuously.

**Danger.** The moving carriage and rotating spindles are hazards. Hair and clothing can be caught. If the carriage is driven by hand crank and the machine seizes, sudden stops can break fingers or wrists.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The spinning mule (Samuel Crompton, 1779) is well-attested and the mechanism is refinement of earlier machines, all feasible with Roman resources. This is a later-stage technology, requiring accumulated knowledge of water power and precise gearing.

### power_loom - Steam or Water-Driven Weaving (*textorium aquae*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A fully automated loom where the shed, weft delivery, and batten motion are all controlled by a rotating shaft with cams and linkages, requiring only that the weaver load the warp and monitor for breakage. This eliminates the need for a skilled hand weaver and allows one worker (often unskilled) to oversee multiple looms.

**Why you would never guess this.** Automating weaving is fundamentally difficult because the sequence of motions - open shed, pass shuttle, close shed, strike batten, open opposite shed, pass shuttle back - must be precisely timed and can easily be thrown off by a broken thread or tangled weft. A cam-and-linkage system must be designed so that any of these failures pause the whole machine without damage; it cannot simply run blindly.

**Prerequisites.** Advanced mechanical linkage design (cams, levers, pivots), gearing, robust bearing design, water power or steam power (if available). Existing treadle loom design.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber for loom frame (very sturdy, load-bearing), iron for shafts, bearings, cams and linkages, bronze for precision components. Water power (river or pump from steam engine if available).

**Procedure.**
1. Design a loom frame that is more rigid than a hand-operated version, as it must withstand repeated heavy impacts without distortion.
2. Mount the heddle frames on linkages driven by cams (teardrop-shaped lobes on a rotating shaft). As the shaft rotates, each cam lifts and lowers its heddle frame in sequence.
3. Mount the shuttle on a spring-loaded mechanism (flying shuttle) also controlled by cams and linkages, so that it is launched automatically at each weft insertion.
4. Mount the batten (weft-striking bar) on linkages that bring it forward to strike the weft at the right moment in the cycle, then back to the starting position.
5. Drive this entire assembly from a single rotating shaft, connected to water power or hand crank (a hand crank is impractical for long-term operation; water is essential).
6. Include a linkage to stop the loom if a warp thread breaks - a falling warp thread can trip a lever that disengages the drive.

**How you know it worked.** A properly functioning power loom should run continuously and produce cloth of consistent quality at a rate perhaps ten to twenty times faster than a hand-operated treadle loom. A single worker can watch three to five looms and only intervene if a thread breaks.

**Failure modes.** If the loom stops randomly, a timing linkage may be slipping or a bearing may be binding from wear; inspect the entire drive train. If cloth comes out lumpy or inconsistent, the heddles may not be lifting high enough to create proper sheds; adjust cam height. If the batten is striking at the wrong time, the cam timing may be off; this is a precision problem requiring careful measurement and adjustment. If weft is tangling, the shuttle flight speed or timing may be incorrect; adjust spring tension or cam profile.

**Cost & labour.** Designing and building a functional power loom is a major engineering project: one expert mechanic and supporting craftspeople, 40-60 days ESTIMATED. High-quality machine steel or very carefully selected iron is essential. Maintenance is intensive: roughly 100-200 denarii per month in wear parts and lubrication. Output: one power loom with one minder can produce perhaps 50-100 meters of cloth per day, equivalent to five to ten hand-weavers ESTIMATED. Multiply this across a factory of twenty to fifty looms and the labour displacement is severe.

**Danger.** Moving shafts, cams, and heddles rotating and oscillating at high speed (perhaps 100 cycles per minute). Long hair and loose clothing are a severe hazard; factory workers must wear constrained clothing. Crush injuries from heddle frames and batten are possible. The flying shuttle under spring tension can strike with significant force.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Power looms are well-attested historically (Edmund Cartwright, 1785 and later improvements). The mechanisms are complex but feasible with Roman engineering. However, they require precision manufacturing and coordination of multiple subsystems that is more suitable to a later-stage, industrialised Rome. Include this as a late-stage technology.

### jacquard_loom - Pattern Loom with Punched Cards (*textorium symbolicum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A loom capable of weaving complex patterns automatically, where a series of punched cards controls which warp threads are lifted at each pick (weft pass). This allows a single operator to weave patterns that would normally require two or three weavers coordinating hand signals, and makes it possible to reproduce patterns exactly.

**Why you would never guess this.** The core idea - that holes in a card can mechanically control motion - is not obvious. Joseph-Marie Jacquard's insight was that a series of small cards, each controlling one weft pass, could be looped together and fed through a reader mechanism that checks for holes. Where there is a hole, a thread passes through; where there is no hole, it does not. This is the direct ancestor of the Hollerith card and ultimately of binary data processing in computers. The card is data.

**Prerequisites.** Power loom or treadle loom as a base. Heddle mechanism that can be selectively controlled. Card-making (punching holes in stiff paper or thin wood). Mechanical reader mechanism (a bed of pins or needles that can press against a card and detect holes).

**Roman-available inputs.** Thick parchment, cardboard, or thin wood for pattern cards. A pointed implement (stylus, needle) for marking and a punch for making holes. Iron pins or thin metal rods for the reader.

**Procedure.**
1. Design a pattern as a grid: one row for each pick (weft pass), one column for each heddle line (typically grouped in sets of 8 or 16). Mark which heddles should lift at each step.
2. Prepare cards (or wooden boards) with a grid of small holes. For each pick, punch holes where heddles should lift, leave solid where they should not.
3. Prepare a reader mechanism: a bed of thin iron pins or needles arranged in a grid matching the heddle arrangement. Behind the pins, attach lever arms connected to the heddle frames.
4. Thread the pattern cards in sequence through the reader. At each step, the pins press forward into the card. Where there is a hole, a pin passes through and pulls a heddle up via its lever arm. Where there is solid, the pin is blocked.
5. Advance to the next card and repeat. The rhythm is: insert pins, pull heddles, pass weft, strike batten, reset pins, advance cards.

**How you know it worked.** The loom should weave a repeating or complex pattern faithfully. The same card sequence should always produce the same pattern. A pattern that would take a hand-weaver hours to coordinate should run automatically.

**Failure modes.** If the pattern is wrong, the cards may be punch incorrectly; carefully verify each card against the design. If pins stick or don't retract, the reader mechanism may be bent or the pins may be blunt; sharpen or realign. If heddles don't lift fully, a lever arm may be binding; ensure free movement. If the card advance mechanism jams, the card stock or punches may be too thick; use thinner material or carefully shave.

**Cost & labour.** Initial setup: one loom plus reader mechanism costs roughly 200-400 denarii for the card reader hardware ESTIMATED. Time to design and punch a pattern: for a modest repeat pattern of say 20 picks high and 16 warp groups wide, perhaps 1-2 days of careful punchwork ESTIMATED. Once the cards are made, they can be reused indefinitely. A Jacquard-equipped loom is about as fast as a regular power loom but can weave patterned cloth; labour displacement is large but skill is relocated to card design rather than hand-weaving.

**Danger.** The mechanism itself is not particularly dangerous beyond the usual power-loom hazards. The sharp punch used to make cards is a hazard; careful handling required.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The Jacquard loom (1804) is well-documented. The mechanism is purely mechanical - no electricity or steam required beyond what drives the base loom. The card-reading mechanism is straightforward and feasible with Roman metalworking. This is historically a later-stage technology, but mechanically it is sound and represents a conceptual leap into data processing.

### cotton_gin - Fibre-Seed Separator (*maschina seminis separandae*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine that separates cotton fibres from their seeds roughly fifty times faster than hand-cleaning, producing clean cotton ready for carding in hours rather than weeks of labour.

**Why you would never guess this.** Raw cotton as it comes from the boll is mixed with seeds embedded in the fibre. Picking seeds out by hand is tedious. The insight is that cotton is slippery and seeds are hard - if you can pull the fibre through a gap narrower than a seed, the fibre will slip through but the seed will not. A rotating drum with fixed wires or teeth can comb the fibre and catch the seeds.

**Prerequisites.** Metalworking (wire or cast-iron teeth), carpentry for frame and rollers, hand crank drive.

**Roman-available inputs.** Raw cotton bolls (as imported by trade). Wood for frame and rollers. Iron for the comb mechanism (wires or teeth).

**Procedure.**
1. Build a frame 60 cm x 40 cm x 40 cm high, mounted on a stand for stability.
2. Mount a rotating drum or roller, roughly 8 cm diameter and 30 cm long, rigged to rotate at 200-300 rpm via a hand crank and gearing or belt drive.
3. Cover the drum surface with fine iron wires or small teeth, or cast a drum with a textured surface. The idea is to have many fine points that will comb the cotton.
4. Mount a fixed comb (a row of iron wires or thin rods spaced at perhaps 3 mm) directly above or beside the rotating drum.
5. Feed raw cotton bolls into the gap between the drum and the comb. The rotating drum carries the fibres upward while the comb catches and strips away the seeds, which fall free.
6. Collect the cleaned fibre on the far side of the mechanism.

**How you know it worked.** The output should be clean cotton fibres with no visible seeds or seed fragments. If you pick out the same raw boll by hand, it should take perhaps a minute; the gin should process the same boll in a few seconds.

**Failure modes.** If seeds are not separating cleanly, the drum surface may be too smooth; roughen it or add more teeth. If cotton is being shredded, the teeth are too sharp or too densely packed; round them slightly or space them farther apart. If the drum clogs with seeds or fibre, disassemble and clear it out; add a cleaning brush if possible.

**Cost & labour.** One carpenter and one metalworker, 3-5 days to build ESTIMATED. The hand-crank version is labour-intensive; one person cranking can process perhaps 10-20 kg of raw cotton per day ESTIMATED. By hand, the same person could clean perhaps 0.2-0.5 kg per day, so the gin is 20-100 times faster. A small factory with one water-powered gin could process several hundred kilograms per day.

**Danger.** The rotating drum with teeth is a clear entanglement hazard. Hands, hair, and loose clothing must be kept away. The hand crank can kick back suddenly if a batch of seeds jams the mechanism.

**Confidence: HIGH.** The cotton gin (Eli Whitney, 1793) is well-documented and mechanically straightforward. The principle of differential gripping (fibre slips through, seed does not) is intuitive once you think about it. The execution requires careful attention to tooth size and spacing, but these are refinements that can be dialled in empirically.

### knitting_frame - Mechanised Loop Making (*confectio ansarum*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine with a bed of hooks (needles) arranged in a close row, where a carriage moves back and forth over them, creating loops of thread. This produces knitted fabric far faster than hand knitting and at a scale impossible by hand.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hand knitting uses two needles and the knitter controls the loop manually. Mechanising it requires building hooks in a line and finding a way to push thread through each loop while simultaneously withdrawing the old loop - two simultaneous motions on every hook. A cam-driven carriage can do this.

**Prerequisites.** Fine metalworking (tiny hooks or needles), carpentry for frame and carriage, gearing and cams.

**Roman-available inputs.** Brass or steel for finial hooks (about 1 cm tall). Timber for frame and carriage rails. Yarn in any fibre.

**Procedure.**
1. Arrange a bed of approximately 100-200 fine brass or steel hooks (or knitting needles with small eyes) in a line, spaced about 5 mm apart, mounted on a horizontal bar.
2. Build a carriage that slides on rails parallel to this needle bed, with two cams or follower mechanisms.
3. As the carriage moves, it pulls thread from a bobbin through the eyes of the needles, then the cams press the needles down and forward, causing them to catch and draw the new loop through the old, releasing it.
4. The reverse motion resets the needles and the carriage moves back. The new loop is now secure and the needle is ready for the next pass.
5. Repeat continuously. Depth control adjusts how far the needles move forward, controlling the stitch length.

**How you know it worked.** The output should be an even knitted fabric. If you compare the speed to hand knitting, a small frame at even modest speeds should outpace a hand knitter significantly - perhaps five to ten times faster.

**Failure modes.** If loops are dropping off the hooks, the needles may not be moving far enough forward to secure the loop; adjust the cam depth. If the thread is breaking, the tension may be too high or the carriage speed too fast; ease both. If the output is uneven or wavy, the needle bed may be misaligned; check for bends and straighten.

**Cost & labour.** One skilled metalworker and one carpenter, 5-7 days to build a small frame ESTIMATED. Fragile to manufacture and prone to breakage; ongoing maintenance cost roughly 30-40 denarii per month. Output of a small frame: perhaps 10-20 meters of knitted fabric per hour at moderate speed ESTIMATED (basis: 200 needles at maybe 50 cm wide, 20-40 passes per minute, each pass adding one row). Hand knitting is perhaps 1-2 meters per day, so the frame is 50-100 times faster.

**Danger.** Small hooks and fine thread under tension. If a thread breaks suddenly, it can snap fingers. If the carriage is hand-driven and jams, it can pinch fingers.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Knitting frames are attested historically (William Lee, 1589) and the mechanics are feasible with Roman metalworking. However, the precision required in hook size and spacing, and the complexity of the carriage mechanism, suggest this is a later-stage technology. Include it as a viable innovation but not a foundational one.

### sewing_machine - Two-Thread Lockstitch (*maschina coniectus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A machine that creates a lockstitch - a seam made with two threads, one above and one below the fabric, interlocking in the middle. A single operator can sew much faster than hand-sewing and with more consistency. This is transformative for garment assembly.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hand sewing uses one thread and a needle with the eye at the tail; it creates a simple loop. A lockstitch requires two threads and two simultaneous penetrations of the fabric per stitch. Worse, it requires an eye-pointed needle - the needle itself has the eye at the tip, not the tail. When the needle penetrates the fabric, the thread in the eye is pulled through to the back, where a second thread (from a rotating bobbin) is caught through the first thread's loop. Neither invention alone creates a functional stitch; both are required.

**Prerequisites.** Fine metalworking (needle with eye at tip, spring mechanisms), carpentry for frame and foot pedal, understanding of linkage and mechanical timing.

**Roman-available inputs.** Steel or hardened iron for needles. Brass or steel for the bobbin case and spring. Timber for frame. Thread of any kind.

**Procedure.**
1. Forge a needle of hardened steel, approximately 10 cm long and 2 mm diameter, with a small eye (hole) drilled into the tip (not the tail). The eye should be just large enough for thread to pass through.
2. Fashion a needle bar and linkage so that when a treadle is pressed, the needle moves downward through the fabric, guided by a throat plate (a small metal piece with a hole guiding the needle).
3. Mount a rotating bobbin (wound with thread) beneath the fabric, enclosed in a bobbin case.
4. Time the mechanisms so that:
   - The needle descends with thread in its eye, penetrating the fabric.
   - At the bottom of the stroke, a hook or latch on the bobbin case catches the upper thread's loop.
   - As the needle rises, the hook draws the loop around the bobbin thread, locking them together.
   - The fabric is then advanced slightly (manually or via a feed mechanism) and the cycle repeats.
5. The operator controls speed by working the treadle faster or slower.

**How you know it worked.** The seam should be even and smooth, with thread on both top and bottom. If you pull on the seam, it should not unravel like a single-thread seam would. Compare the speed to hand-sewing: a single seamstress can sew perhaps 5-10 meters of seam per day by hand; a machine operator should achieve 50-100 meters per day.

**Failure modes.** If the upper thread is not being caught, the timing may be off - the hook may be moving before the loop is formed, or after it has passed. Adjust the mechanical linkages. If the lower (bobbin) thread is not locking in, the bobbin may not be making full contact with the upper thread's loop; adjust spring tension or bobbin position. If thread is breaking, tension is too high on one or both threads; ease the tension regulators. If stitches are skipped, the needle may be bent; straighten or replace. If the needle eye has cracked or enlarged, the repeated thread abrasion over thousands of stitches will cause failure; replace the needle.

**Cost & labour.** Designing and building a working sewing machine is a precision engineering task: one expert mechanic, 15-25 days ESTIMATED. The eye-pointed needle alone is a technical achievement requiring careful metallurgy and drilling. Maintenance cost roughly 20-30 denarii per month for needle replacements and spring adjustment. A single operator on a machine can produce perhaps ten times the seam length of a hand-sewer ESTIMATED. In a factory setting with dozens of machines, the labour displacement is dramatic and concentrates work in one location.

**Danger.** The needle moves at perhaps 1000-2000 stitches per minute on an efficient machine. A finger under the needle at the bottom of a stroke will be punctured severely. The machine must have guards to prevent this. Repetitive strain on feet (treadle operation) is inevitable.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** The sewing machine evolved over many iterations; Elias Howe's lockstitch (1846) and the eye-pointed needle are well-documented. The mechanics are sound and feasible with Roman metalworking if the level of precision can be achieved. However, both the needle design and the synchronized linkages are complex. This is a later-stage technology, not early. Include it as a sophisticated innovation.

### rope_walk - Mechanised Rope Laying (*spira torsilis*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A long, straight facility where fibres are twisted into rope at high speed using mechanised spindles, producing rope much faster and more uniformly than by hand twisting. A rope walk can produce hundred-meter lengths with consistent twist.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hand-twisted rope requires two people: one holding the fibres taut, one twisting. The rope length is limited by how far you can walk backward. A mechanised rope walk fixes the fibres in a frame, replaces one twister with multiple rotating spindles, and allows the operator to walk forward feeding fibres - producing very long, uniform rope.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry for the long frame, metalworking for rotating spindles and bearings, gearing and hand crank or water drive.

**Roman-available inputs.** Timber for frame (the rope walk building may be 100-200 meters long). Iron or bronze for spindles and gearing. Hemp, flax, or other rope fibres prepared into loose strands.

**Procedure.**
1. Build a very long structure (rope walk building) 120-150 meters long and 3-4 meters wide, with a clear, level floor.
2. Mount the fibre supply at one end: eight to sixteen strands of fibre, each wound onto a spool, positioned to unreel as needed.
3. Mount rotating spindles at the opposite end, one for each fibre strand, rigged to a hand crank or water drive to rotate at 200-400 rpm.
4. Extend the fibres in parallel from the spools to the spindles.
5. As the spindles turn, they twist each fibre strand individually. Meanwhile, the operator walks backward slowly, holding a guide frame that draws the twisted strands together. As they are drawn together, they automatically intertwine, forming rope.
6. The rhythm is: twist individual strands at constant rate, draw them together slowly and evenly, maintain consistent tension.

**How you know it worked.** The finished rope should have consistent diameter throughout its length and should not untwist or unravel if a small cut is made. Twisted rope is always weaker initially but gains strength as it relaxes and sets after a few days; very long rope is practical only with this mechanisation.

**Failure modes.** If one strand stops twisting (spindle bearing failure), the rope will twist unevenly and weaken at that point; check all spindles for free rotation. If the rope is developing a kink or spiral pattern, the spindles are not all rotating at the same rate; check gearing and belt tension. If the rope is stretching unevenly, the operator's pace is inconsistent; maintain steady, slow backward walking.

**Cost & labour.** A rope walk building and machinery is significant infrastructure: one carpenter, one metalworker, 20-30 days ESTIMATED. Assuming water drive (hand-crank is impractical), the only ongoing cost is maintenance. Output: a continuous rope walk running steadily can produce 500-1000 meters of rope per day ESTIMATED (basis: 100 meter walk, 30-60 seconds per cycle including respool and return, continuous operation minus stoppages). By hand, a pair of ropers could produce perhaps 50-100 meters per day with effort.

**Danger.** Long lengths of rotating rope and multiple spinning spindles are entanglement hazards. Hair must be constrained. The spindles run at high speed and can catch clothing or hands.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Mechanised rope walks are attested as early as medieval times. The principle is straightforward: replace human twisting with rotating spindles and leverage the twist mechanism. Roman engineering is fully capable of this.

### fulling_water - Water-Powered Fulling Mills (*fullonica aquae*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Replacing the hand-beating and foot-trampling of cloth (fulling) with a water-powered cam mechanism that lifts and drops heavy wooden mallets repeatedly, pounding cloth to tighten the weave. This is the same scale of improvement as water-powered grain mills were for grinding.

**Why you would never guess this.** Fulling is essential - cloth fresh from the loom is loose and weak. But it is extremely labour-intensive and skilled (workers must carefully control the depth of fulling to avoid shrinking cloth unevenly). Water power cannot improve skill, but it can provide consistent, unattended force.

**Prerequisites.** Water mill construction (water wheel, bearings, drive shaft), carpentry for mallet mechanism, basic fulling knowledge.

**Roman-available inputs.** Flowing water, timber for mill building and mallets, stone for beater track, fulling agent (urine for alkali mordanting already in Rome's practice, or soda ash as alternative).

**Procedure.**
1. Build a water mill framework similar to a grain mill, with a 2-3 meter water wheel.
2. Attach a drive shaft with four to six large cams (teardrop-shaped lobes) evenly spaced around its circumference.
3. Mount four to six heavy wooden mallets (roughly 5-10 kg each) on pivoting arms, arranged around the cams. Each mallet rides on the edge of a cam.
4. As the shaft rotates, each cam lifts its mallet up, then releases it, allowing it to drop under gravity and strike the fulling trough.
5. Prepare the cloth fulling vat similar to hand fulling: soak cloth in fulling agent (dilute urine or soda ash solution), then place it in the trough under the mallets.
6. The continuous striking fulls the cloth. Check progress every 30 minutes or so; when it has reached desired fullness, remove the cloth.

**How you know it worked.** Compare the fulled cloth to hand-fulled cloth of identical specification. The weight should be similar and the weave tightness comparable. The time should be much shorter - hand fulling takes several hours; mechanised fulling should accomplish the same in perhaps one hour or less.

**Failure modes.** If the cloth is over-fulled (shrunken), the mallets may be falling from too great a height or striking too frequently; lower the cams or gear down the drive. If the cloth is under-fulled, the reverse applies. If mallets are breaking or wearing quickly, they may be too hard (use wood that is strong but slightly flexible, such as ash rather than oak). If the cloth is tearing, check for sharp edges or protrusions in the trough.

**Cost & labour.** A fulling mill is an intermediate infrastructure project: millwright and carpenters, 15-20 days ESTIMATED. Maintenance cost roughly 40-60 denarii per month. Output: one mill can full perhaps 100-200 meters of finished cloth per day, running continuously. This is equivalent to perhaps 10-20 hand-fullers working simultaneously.

**Danger.** Falling mallets have force. If a worker reaches into the trough while mallets are swinging, serious injury is likely. The mechanism must have a clear stop to prevent operation when cloth is being loaded or unloaded.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Water-powered fulling mills are attested historically (as early as the 11th century). The mechanism is simple and the principle is sound. Romans with water-mill knowledge can build this.

### bleaching_chlorine - Industrial Bleaching (*candor chlori*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Applying chlorine gas or a chlorine compound (bleach) to cloth to remove colour and achieve bright whiteness. This collapses the multi-month field bleaching process (spreading cloth in the sun for months, dousing with sour milk) to a few hours.

**Why you would never guess this.** Chlorine is an element - it does not exist as a pure substance in nature and requires synthesis. The Roman world will not easily access pure chlorine gas. However, chlorine compounds like sodium hypochlorite can be made by passing chlorine (generated by oxidising salt with acid and a catalyst) into soda solution. The bleaching effect is remarkable and immediate, but the chemistry is non-trivial.

**Prerequisites.** Knowledge of oxidation (burning and oxygen). Access to salt, sulphuric acid (from vitriol), and pyrolusite (manganese dioxide - mined in Greece and available to Rome). Carpentry for vats.

**Roman-available inputs.** Rock salt (sea salt evaporated). Pyrolusite (MnO2, from Greece). Sulphuric acid (can be made from burning sulfur and collecting the fumes in water with a small amount of nitre - see vitriol preparation). Soda ash (from burning plant ash or importing natron). Cloth to be bleached.

**Procedure.**
1. Generate chlorine gas by heating pyrolusite (MnO2) with salt (NaCl) and sulphuric acid (H2SO4) in a sealed vessel with a delivery tube. The reaction produces Cl2 gas, which bubbles off.
   Temp: about 50-60°C (warm but not boiling), achievable by placing the pot in hot water.
   Ratio (molar): 1 MnO2 : 2 NaCl : 2 H2SO4.
   As rough masses: 87 grams MnO2 : 117 grams NaCl : 98 grams H2SO4 (these react roughly 1:1 by molar mass here).
2. Bubble the chlorine gas through a vat containing dilute soda ash solution (roughly 30 grams soda ash per litre of water). The gas reacts to form sodium hypochlorite (bleach).
   Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O (approximately).
3. Immerse cloth in the resulting bleach solution for 1-2 hours, agitating occasionally.
4. Remove cloth and rinse thoroughly in water (at least three times) to remove all traces of bleach.
5. Hang to dry.

**How you know it worked.** The cloth should be bright white or very pale. Any colour (from initial dyeing or dirt) should be gone. If the cloth is the same colour as before, bleaching has not worked; check that chlorine gas was generated (it has a sharp, unpleasant smell) and that soda was present in the vat.

**Failure modes.** If no chlorine gas is produced (no smell, no bubbles), the pyrolusite may be impure, the sulfuric acid too weak, or the heat insufficient; retry with more vigorous heating or fresher reagents. If the cloth tears or becomes weak after bleaching, the bleach solution is too concentrated or exposure too long; use more water or shorten immersion time. If the cloth remains yellow or off-white, rinsing was incomplete; repeat rinses.

**Cost & labour.** Materials per batch (enough to bleach roughly 10 meters of cloth): pyrolusite 5-10 grams, salt 5-10 grams, sulfuric acid 5-10 grams, soda 20-30 grams. Total materials cost roughly 2-5 denarii ESTIMATED. Time: setup and bubbling gas for perhaps 30 minutes, immersion 1-2 hours, rinsing and drying 2-3 hours. One worker can bleach perhaps 50-100 meters of cloth per day with a setup for continuous processing. By field bleaching (the previous method), the same output would take 6-12 weeks of labour and weather.

**Danger.** Chlorine gas is toxic. Inhalation causes coughing, chest pain, and in high concentrations, pulmonary oedema and death. The reaction vessel must be vented outdoors or into a hood. Workers must not breathe the gas. Sulfuric acid is corrosive - splash protection required. Hot water used in the reaction can cause burns.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Chlorine bleach was discovered in the 18th century (Carl Scheele isolated chlorine in 1774). The chemistry is sound and the reactions are well-understood. However, the synthesis of chlorine from common materials requires understanding of oxidation and control of gas generation - this is not obvious to a pre-industrial society. Include this as a later-stage innovation, feasible once the underlying chemistry is understood.

### mordanting_dyes - Understanding Dye Fixation (*fixa coloratio*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Treating cloth with a chemical mordant before dyeing so that the dye bonds to the fibre rather than washing out. Rome already knows empirically that alum helps dye hold, but this module documents the chemistry and optimises the process.

**Why you would never guess this.** Dyes are complex molecules. Most natural dyes are not reactive directly with fibre; they need a chemical intermediary (a mordant) that bonds to both the fibre and the dye. Alum (aluminium potassium sulfate, KAl(SO4)2) is such an intermediary. Understanding why requires understanding that dyes are chemical compounds and that colour is not an inherent property but a result of chemical bonding.

**Prerequisites.** Existing dyeing knowledge (Rome has madder, woad, murex). Access to alum (mined on the island of Melos, traded throughout the Empire).

**Roman-available inputs.** Alum (aluminium potassium sulfate, from Melos or as alum stone). Plant sources of dye: madder (Rubia tinctorum, red), woad (Isatis tinctoria, blue), weld (Reseda luteola, yellow). Shellfish for Murex purple (already known to Rome). Clean cloth to be dyed.

**Procedure.**
1. Prepare the mordant bath: dissolve alum in water at a ratio of roughly 10-20 grams per litre. Warm gently to 60-70°C.
2. Immerse cloth in the alum solution for 30-45 minutes, stirring occasionally to ensure even uptake.
3. Remove cloth and squeeze out excess alum solution (do not rinse).
4. Dry the cloth partially or completely. The alum will remain bonded to the fibre.
5. Prepare the dye bath separately (using madder, woad, weld, or other dye source) as normally done.
6. Immerse the alum-mordanted cloth in the hot dye bath and proceed with normal dyeing.
7. The dye should now bond much more strongly to the fibre.

**How you know it worked.** Dye the same cloth in two batches: one with alum mordanting, one without. Wash both vigorously (soak in soapy water, wring, repeat). The non-mordanted cloth will lose much of its colour and become pale. The mordanted cloth should retain its colour, appearing as vibrant as it was when wet from the dye bath.

**Failure modes.** If the cloth does not retain dye, the alum may not have bonded properly; ensure the alum bath was warm and the soaking time adequate. If the dyeing is uneven (patchy), the cloth was not evenly exposed to either the alum or dye bath; stir more frequently during both steps. If the alum is grainy or cloudy in solution, the alum stone may be impure; grind it finely or purchase good quality alum powder.

**Cost & labour.** Alum costs roughly 5-10 denarii per kilogram ESTIMATED; for dyeing perhaps 10 meters of cloth, alum cost is roughly 1-2 denarii. Labour: mordanting adds one hour (heating, soaking, drying) to the dyeing process. Output and quality improvement justify this small investment many times over - cloth that holds its dye is much more valuable.

**Danger.** Hot alum solution can cause burns. Alum itself is not highly toxic but should not be ingested. The main hazard is from the dyeing process itself (using reactive dyes or complex chemistry), which may involve toxic materials depending on the dye source.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Alum mordanting has been used since antiquity and is well-documented historically. The principle is empirically discoverable and the practice is straightforward. Rome already uses alum for dyeing; this module simply optimises and documents the process. The underlying chemistry (alum acts as a bridging agent between fibre and dye) is sound.

### calico_printing - Patterned Cloth Printing (*impressio calcis*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Using wooden blocks or later, engraved copper rollers, to apply dye or mordant in patterns to cloth. This allows production of patterned cloth much faster than hand-weaving patterns and at lower cost.

**Why you would never guess this.** The key insight is that you can apply mordant or dye selectively using a carved block or roller, then process the cloth normally. The mordant will hold dye only where the mordant was applied, creating a pattern. This separates pattern creation from the weaving process entirely.

**Prerequisites.** Existing dyeing and mordanting knowledge. Woodcarving or engraving skill. Carpentry for printing frame.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for blocks (fruitwood like cherry or pear is traditional, or any dense wood). Copper or bronze sheet for engraved rollers (advanced version). Alum, dyes, and dye-thickening paste.

**Procedure for block printing:**
1. Carve or engrave a wooden block with a raised relief design roughly 1-2 mm thick.
2. Prepare a thickened dye or mordant paste by mixing dye solution with a thickener (flour paste, gum arabic, or similar). This prevents the liquid from spreading beyond the printed area.
3. Apply this paste to the relief of the block using a brush or by pressing the block into a pad of saturated cloth.
4. Press the block onto the cloth to be printed, applying firm, even pressure.
5. Repeat across the cloth to create an all-over pattern.
6. Allow the paste to dry, then process the cloth: if a mordant was printed, proceed to dye; if dye was printed directly, proceed to rinsing and drying.

**Procedure for roller printing (advanced):**
1. Engrave a design into a copper or bronze roller, roughly 10-20 cm in diameter and up to a metre long.
2. Mount the roller on an axle between two supports, with another plain roller to apply pressure above it.
3. Coat the engraved roller with thickened dye or mordant.
4. Feed cloth through the nip (gap) between the two rollers. The cloth presses into the engraved design and receives the pattern.
5. The design repeats along the length of the roller.

**How you know it worked.** Block printing should produce a clear, repeating pattern with clean edges. Roller printing should produce evenly spaced, consistent patterns along the entire length of cloth. For mordant-printed cloth, the pattern should become obvious after dyeing (only the mordanted areas accept the dye).

**Failure modes.** If the pattern is blurry or feathered, the paste may be too thin (spreading beyond the relief) or pressure inconsistent; use a thicker paste or apply more even pressure. If the print is faint, the dye or mordant solution may be too dilute; strengthen it. If the block or roller becomes clogged with dried paste, soak and scrub it clean, and ensure paste consistency allows proper drying without crusting.

**Cost & labour.** Carving a modest block (say, 10 cm x 10 cm) takes one experienced carver, 1-2 days ESTIMATED. The block is reusable for years. Cost of materials (wood, dye, paste) for printing 100 meters of cloth is roughly 10-20 denarii ESTIMATED. Labour for the actual printing, one worker can print perhaps 50-100 meters of cloth per day ESTIMATED. Output of patterned cloth far exceeds hand-weaving patterns and at much lower cost.

**Danger.** Sharp tools used in carving; standard woodcarving hazards. The dyes themselves may be toxic depending on source - natural dyes are generally safe, but some dye precursors are not. Hot paste and fabric under pressure in roller printing can cause burns.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Calico printing is attested in India as ancient practice and in Europe by the medieval period. Block printing is straightforward and requires no exotic materials or knowledge. Engraved roller printing is more advanced but feasible with copper-working skill.

### tanning_leather - Mechanised Hide Processing (*corium preparatus*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Replacing hand-scraping and beating of hides with mechanised tools and vats, and replacing traditional vegetable tanning (which takes months) with a faster, more controlled process using chemical tannins.

**Why you would never guess this.** Leather is made by permanently fixing the hide's collagen structure using tannins - complex organic compounds that cross-link collagen. Rome uses oak bark, hemlock bark, and other plant tannins. Speeding this requires extracting concentrated tannin solutions and applying them in a controlled way, rather than simply soaking hides in a vat of bark water for months.

**Prerequisites.** Existing leather-working knowledge, access to tannin-rich plant material (oak bark, hemlock, or imported tannin extracts), chemistry understanding of leaching and concentration.

**Roman-available inputs.** Raw hides (cattle, sheep, goat), oak bark or other tannin source, slaked lime (for initial hair removal), tools for scraping (already used in traditional tanning), vats for processing.

**Procedure (optimised tanning):**
1. Soak raw hide in water for 12-24 hours to soften it.
2. Remove hair by soaking in slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) solution for 3-7 days, then scraping.
3. Prepare a concentrated tannin solution by leaching oak bark or similar in hot water (rough ratio: 100 grams bark per litre, heated to 60-80°C for 4-6 hours, then strained). This can be re-used for several hides.
4. Place the scraped hide in the tannin solution and leave for 4-8 weeks, agitating occasionally.
5. Remove hide, rinse lightly, and hang to dry. The hide is now leather.

**Mechanised variants:**
- Use a drum or roller mechanism to agitate hides in the vat, accelerating tannin penetration (reduces time to 2-3 weeks).
- Pre-wet the hide with hot water to swell the collagen and improve tannin access.
- Use multiple vats of progressively concentrated tannin solutions to speed tanning.

**How you know it worked.** The finished leather should be flexible and tough. If you cut a cross-section, it should be a uniform tan colour all the way through (indicating the tannin has penetrated), not white in the centre (indicating incomplete tanning). The leather should resist stretching under load.

**Failure modes.** If the leather is stiff and brittle, it has been over-tanned; use weaker tannin solutions. If it is soft and weak, it is under-tanned; increase exposure time or tannin concentration. If the hide is patchy or partially mouldy, the solution may have become contaminated; discard and start with fresh tannin solution.

**Cost & labour.** One tannery vat setup (carpentry and materials) costs roughly 50-100 denarii ESTIMATED. Oak bark is cheap but requires processing; a tannery can source this from local forests. Tanning time without mechanisation is weeks; with mechanical agitation, it can be reduced to perhaps 10-15 days. Labour for traditional tanning: one worker can process perhaps 10-20 hides per month (due to long wait times between steps). With faster chemistry and mechanical aids, perhaps 20-40 hides per month.

**Danger.** Slaked lime is corrosive; it will burn skin and cause blindness if splashed in eyes. Proper handling and protection required. Some plant tannins can cause allergic reactions. Long-term inhalation of tanning dust (from dried bark) can cause respiratory issues.

**Confidence: MEDIUM.** Industrial tanning with concentrated extracts and mechanical agitation emerged over the medieval and early modern periods. Traditional vegetable tanning with bark is ancient and well-attested; accelerating it is a logical innovation using Roman knowledge of heating, concentrating solutions, and mechanical work.

### rayon_viscose - Synthetic Fibre Production (*fibra artificialis*)

**What it is / why you want it.** Converting plant cellulose (wood pulp or cotton linters) into a liquid (viscose), then forcing that liquid through a fine hole into a chemical bath where it recongeals as a fine fibre. This produces an artificial fibre with properties similar to silk but from abundant plant material. This is the first synthetic fibre and is radically transformative.

**Why you would never guess this.** The idea that you can dissolve a solid (cellulose), transform its chemical state, and reconstitute it as a different physical form is not intuitive. Moreover, the chemistry involved - dissolving cellulose in sodium hydroxide with carbon disulfide to form viscose - is non-trivial and requires understanding of oxidation, complex chemistry, and careful control.

**Prerequisites.** Advanced chemistry (sodium hydroxide synthesis, carbon disulfide production, understanding of cellulose). Access to concentrated sodium hydroxide (caustic soda) and carbon disulfide. Access to plant cellulose: cotton linters (the short fibres remaining after cotton gin separates long fibres), wood pulp (produced by breaking down wood), or textile waste.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood or plant material for pulping. Sodium carbonate can be converted to sodium hydroxide by slaking in lime. Carbon disulfide... this is problematic. It requires synthesis by heating sulfur and charcoal to very high temperatures with specific conditions. This may be achievable in an advanced Rome but is not trivial. Sulfuric acid can be made (from vitriol). Alternatively, sulfur and a reducing agent (charcoal) at high temperature might produce CS2.

**Procedure (simplified):**
1. Prepare cellulose source: either use cotton linters (from cotton gin waste) or create wood pulp by treating wood with weak sulfuric acid and heat to break down lignin. Pulping takes days to weeks.
2. Dissolve cellulose in cold sodium hydroxide solution (roughly 8-10% NaOH). This forms an alkaline cellulose solution.
3. To this, add carbon disulfide dropwise (CS2) while stirring. The mixture becomes orange/brown viscose. This is extremely hazardous (carbon disulfide is very volatile and flammable).
   Ratio (rough): cellulose solution + 10-20% weight carbon disulfide.
4. Allow the viscose to age (ripen) for 6-12 hours. This allows the cellulose to form proper molecular structure for spinning.
5. Force the viscose through a spinneret (a fine metal plate with many tiny holes, similar to a shower head) into a bath of dilute sulfuric acid. The acid causes the cellulose to reconstitute as fine fibres.
6. Collect the fibres, wash, dry, and twist into thread.

**How you know it worked.** The resulting fibre should look and feel like fine thread, lustrous like silk, and should be spinnable (can be twisted and used for weaving). If you weave or knit it, it should produce a fabric similar to silk. Strength testing: it should not break under moderate pulling, though it is weaker than cotton.

**Failure modes.** If viscose is too thick (too much cellulose) or too thin (not enough), spinning fails; adjust concentration. If the carbon disulfide boils off (it is highly volatile), viscosity is wrong; work in a cold environment and keep container sealed. If fibres don't form in the acid bath, the viscose may not be ripe enough; extend ripening time. If fibres break easily, the cellulose source was too degraded; use fresher pulp.

**Cost & labour.** The equipment required is complex: cooking vessel for pulping, reaction vessels for viscose formation, a spinneret (fine perforations), a coagulation bath, and drying and twisting equipment. Estimated total infrastructure cost: 500-1000 denarii ESTIMATED. Material costs per batch (enough to produce several hundred grams of fibre): cellulose source 1-2 kg (essentially free if using waste), sodium hydroxide ~200 grams (5-10 denarii), carbon disulfide ~200 grams (highly variable, perhaps 20-50 denarii ESTIMATED). Skilled labour: one chemist-level operator plus assistants. Output of a single batch: 500-1000 grams of rayon fibre over several days ESTIMATED. This is competitive with or cheaper than importing silk, and far more stable supply.

**Danger.** Carbon disulfide is extremely hazardous: it is volatile, flammable, and toxic. Inhalation causes neurological damage and death at high concentrations. Sodium hydroxide is caustic and will burn skin and eyes. Sulfuric acid is corrosive. The entire process requires careful ventilation, protective equipment, and chemical knowledge. An industrial accident (fire or chemical spill) could be catastrophic.

**Confidence: LOW.** Viscose rayon was invented in the 19th century (Louis Marie Hilaire Bernigaud) and requires understanding of advanced chemistry: dissolving cellulose, forming complex compounds, controlling polymerisation. Carbon disulfide synthesis in particular is non-obvious. The process is chemically sound but requires a level of chemical infrastructure and understanding that is unlikely in 100 AD Rome. Include this only as a late-stage, experimental technology that a very advanced Rome might attempt. The hazards are severe.

### textile_factory - Integrated Factory System (*officina textilis*)

**What it is / why you want it.** A single facility bringing together many of the above technologies - spinning, weaving, fulling, dyeing, bleaching, printing - under one roof, with raw materials entering at one end and finished cloth exiting at the other. This is the textiles equivalent of an integrated production system, far more efficient than scattered cottage production.

**Why you would never guess this.** Integration itself is not a technology, but a system design. The value comes from reducing transport and handling time, coordinating workflows, employing workers efficiently, and achieving economies of scale in bleaching, dyeing, and finishing vats that serve many looms.

**Prerequisites.** Everything above: spinning wheels or machinery, looms (hand, treadle, power, or Jacquard), fulling mill, bleaching and dyeing vats, finishing equipment. Water power if available (for mills and mechanised equipment). Carpentry, metalworking, and dyeing knowledge.

**Roman-available inputs.** Raw fibres (wool, linen, cotton, or import). A suitable site (near water if using power, with space for multiple buildings or very large building).

**Procedure:**
1. Plan the workflow: receiving area for raw materials, storage, then sequentially: carding/combing, spinning (machines or hand wheels), weaving (multiple looms), fulling (mill), bleaching (if needed), dyeing (vats), finishing/drying, packing for shipment.
2. Build or assemble the facility, positioning water-powered equipment near a mill race (if available), hand-powered equipment in accessible areas, vats and drying areas with good ventilation.
3. Hire and train workforce: carders, spinners, weavers, fullers, dyers, finishers. Each role is increasingly specialised.
4. Establish raw material supply chain: contracts with farmers for wool or flax, contracts with traders for imported fibres (cotton, silk), waste management.
5. Coordinate production runs: order raw material, begin carding and spinning to build thread inventory, have weavers work from that inventory, send finished cloth for processing.
6. Finish and pack cloth for market, ship to traders.

**How you know it worked.** The facility should be producing finished cloth at a rate far exceeding any cottage producer. If each worker is highly specialised and the workflow is smooth (no bottlenecks, no waiting), output per worker should be higher than in scattered production. Quality should be consistent. Costs should be lower per unit than cottage production (due to economies of scale in vats, shared infrastructure, bulk purchasing).

**Failure modes.** If one process becomes a bottleneck (e.g., spinning cannot keep pace with weaving demand), the overall facility productivity drops; balance workflow by hiring more spinners or reducing loom allocation. If coordination breaks down (materials move slowly between processes), productivity suffers; establish clear handoffs and tracking. If product quality is inconsistent, perhaps different workers or machinery need to be aligned to standards; standardise procedures.

**Cost & labour.** A modest textile factory (say, 10 looms with supporting equipment): construction and equipment roughly 1000-2000 denarii ESTIMATED. Ongoing labour: 30-50 workers (depending on level of mechanisation) at roughly 500-1000 denarii per month total ESTIMATED. Operating costs (fuel, materials, water, maintenance): 200-400 denarii per month ESTIMATED. Output: perhaps 500-1000 meters of finished cloth per day, depending on mix of products. This is equivalent to perhaps 100-200 hand-weavers producing at once, but requiring only a fraction of that labour count (due to machinery), so net labour displacement is large but economic output is also large.

**Danger.** A large facility with many processes presents multiple hazards: water mills, heated vats, dyeing chemicals, bleaching agents, power looms and other machinery. Accidents are inevitable. Adequate safety measures: clear guards on machinery, controlled access to heated vats, ventilation for chemical processes, first aid and injury protocols.

**Confidence: HIGH.** Integrated textile factories emerged in the 18th and 19th centuries and are well-documented. The concept is straightforward: it is workflow management and coordination more than technology. Roman administrative capability is sufficient for this, given the individual components (spinning mills, looms, fulling mills) are available.

## Sources and Confidence

**Primary historical sources cited:**
- Suetonius, *Life of Vespasian* 18 (the story of the labour-saving machine and Vespasian's rejection).
- Pliny *Naturalis Historia* (various references to dyeing, fulling, textile production in the Roman world).

**Historical attestations:**
- Spinning wheel with flyer: China, 11th century; India, 13th century; Europe, 14th century.
- Treadle loom: Europe, 12th century.
- Flying shuttle: John Kay, 1733.
- Spinning jenny: James Hargreaves, 1764.
- Water frame: Richard Arkwright, 1769.
- Spinning mule: Samuel Crompton, 1779.
- Power loom: Edmund Cartwright, 1785.
- Jacquard loom: Joseph-Marie Jacquard, 1804.
- Cotton gin: Eli Whitney, 1793.
- Knitting frame: William Lee, 1589.
- Sewing machine: various inventors; lockstitch and eye-pointed needle developed over 19th century.
- Rope walk: medieval Europe.
- Mechanical fulling mills: Europe, 11th-12th century.
- Chlorine bleaching: Carl Scheele isolated chlorine, 1774; commercial bleach production 19th century.
- Calico printing: India (ancient); Europe by medieval period.
- Rayon viscose: Louis Marie Hilaire Bernigaud, late 19th century.

**Confidence breakdown:**
- **HIGH** confidence: flyer spindle, treadle loom, carding, flying shuttle, water-powered spinning, water-powered fulling, cotton gin, rope walk, and integrated factories. These are mechanically straightforward and within Roman technological capacity, though many are later-stage innovations.
- **MEDIUM** confidence: spinning jenny, spinning mule, power loom, knitting frame, sewing machine, chlorine bleaching. These require precision manufacturing or advanced chemistry that may be at the edge of Roman capability. Sewing machine requires the eye-pointed needle, which is an innovation not guaranteed. Chlorine bleaching requires understanding of oxidation chemistry. These are included as plausible but more speculative.
- **LOW** confidence: rayon/viscose. This requires advanced chemistry (dissolving and regenerating cellulose, carbon disulfide synthesis) well beyond typical Roman chemical knowledge. Included as a late-stage research goal rather than a practical technology for early Roman development.

**Key caveat on labour displacement:**
All of these technologies increase production per worker substantially, with the side effect that fewer workers are needed for the same output. In a Roman context where much textile work is done by enslaved women and lower-class women, this creates economic hardship and social tension. The economic argument is that increased cloth production creates wealth and new market opportunities, and some displaced workers will migrate into new roles (merchant, dyer, factor, shipping, trading). This was historically true, but it is not a quick or painless transition. A Roman policy-maker must account for the real human cost.
