# Module 92: Vehicles, Ships and Flight

This module covers land transport, maritime vessels, and powered flight. The single most important fact is the freight arithmetic: **sea freight costs roughly one fortieth to one sixtieth of land freight per tonne-kilometre**. Everything else flows from this.

Rome already possesses an excellent paved trunk road network (the cursus publicus), carts, spoked wheels with iron tyres, very large merchant sailing ships with mortise-and-tenon hull construction and lead sheathing, artificial harbours in hydraulic concrete, and the established monsoon route to India.

## Land Transport

### suspension_springs - Suspension Springs

**What it is / why you want it.** Metal springs isolating a vehicle's body from road shocks, allowing heavier loads at higher speeds without damage.

**Why you would never guess this.** Springs seem to make things bouncier. A spring defers impact over time; the longer the force acts, the lower the peak stress.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working, geometry, empirical testing.

**Roman-available inputs.** High-carbon iron from a bloomery. Steel made by forge-hardening and tempering.

**Procedure.** (1) Hammer-forge a strip of mild iron 50 mm wide, 8-12 mm thick. (2) Heat to orange-yellow (600-700 C), fold in half, and hammer-weld. (3) Repeat, stacking strips and binding with iron straps. (4) Harden: heat to bright cherry red (800 C), quench in water, then temper at straw-yellow (200-220 C). (5) Mount on axle brackets so the cart body rests on the spring.

**How you know it worked.** Roll a cart with a cup of water aboard; proper springs should lose only a few drops. Load 200 kg; it should not sag.

**Failure modes.** Over-hardening causes cracking. Under-hardening causes permanent deformation. Improper stacking causes leaves to slide.

**Cost & labour.** Four springs for a cart: ESTIMATED 20-30 denarii, ESTIMATED 40-60 labour-hours. Calendar time: 2-3 weeks.

**Danger.** Quenching can cause steam explosions. Springs store enormous energy and can snap violently.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Leaf springs are in continuous use since the 18th century. Roman metallurgy is adequate.

### macadam_road - Macadam Road Surface

**What it is / why you want it.** A road of crushed stone in compacted layers, draining water naturally and resisting deformation.

**Why you would never guess this.** A monolithic surface seems more durable. Macadam works because slight movement and drainage within the structure make it more resilient.

**Prerequisites.** Understanding of drainage. Quarrying and stone-breaking capability.

**Roman-available inputs.** Crushed limestone or granite, sorted by size. Tallow or pitch for binding.

**Procedure.** (1) Excavate roadbed 40-60 cm deep, slope to shed water. (2) Base course of large stones (fist-sized) to 15 cm. (3) Middle course of medium stones (walnut-sized) to 15 cm, rolled firmly. (4) Top course of pea-sized stones to 10-15 cm, rolled. (5) Optional: spray thin tallow on top and re-roll.

**How you know it worked.** Water sheds within minutes of rain. A stick penetrates only 2-3 cm. A loaded cart leaves no rut deeper than 1 cm.

**Failure modes.** Insufficient base causes mud in wet weather. Over-rolling prevents drainage.

**Cost & labour.** Per kilometre: ESTIMATED 200-400 denarii in materials, 5-10 surveyor days, 20-50 labour-days. Maintenance: 1-2 days per 10 km per year.

**Danger.** Stone-breaking produces flying fragments. Rolled stones can crush feet.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Crushed stone roads were built in Scotland in the 1780s-1790s.

### wheelbarrow - Wheelbarrow

**What it is / why you want it.** A one-wheeled hand cart allowing one person to carry 30-50 kg at speed.

**Why you would never guess this.** The weight distribution (most on the wheel, not balanced) is geometrically non-obvious.

**Prerequisites.** Wheel construction, iron working, carpentry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood (ash or elm). Iron axle rod. A completed wheel, 60-80 cm diameter.

**Procedure.** (1) Build a trough body from curved wood, 100 cm long, 50 cm wide. (2) Install iron axle 10-15 cm below the rim at the front. (3) Attach two handles at rear to chest height.

**How you know it worked.** A loaded wheelbarrow balances on the wheel when handles are lifted. A single person should wheel 40 kg of sand 100 metres comfortably.

**Failure modes.** Axle too far forward causes tipping. Too far back makes lifting hard. Thin wood will split.

**Cost & labour.** Materials: ESTIMATED 15-25 denarii. Labour: ESTIMATED 10-15 hours. Lifespan: 3-5 years.

**Danger.** Minimal. Overloading risks tipping.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The wheelbarrow is attested in Roman archaeology.

### flanged_wheel - Flanged Wheel

**What it is / why you want it.** A wheel with an inward-pointing lip allowing it to run on a rail without slipping sideways. This is the founding technology of railways.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious solution is to put the track lip on the rail. This fails: dirt accumulates, the lip bends, and it fouls with switch points. Putting the flange on the wheel means it climbs only when necessary.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working, wheel construction.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast or wrought iron. High-carbon steel for the flange.

**Procedure.** (1) Begin with a standard spoked wheel. (2) Forge or cast an iron ring 4-6 cm tall with an inward-pointing lip 2-3 cm deep. (3) Heat-shrink or weld this ring onto the wheel rim. (4) The lip points inward at roughly 30 degrees.

**How you know it worked.** Mount the wheel on an iron or hardened wood rail 40 cm high. Load applied to the axle should roll smoothly without slipping sideways.

**Failure modes.** Too tall or steep a flange binds the wheel. Outward-pointing flange causes the wheel to fly off. Thin flange bends or shatters.

**Cost & labour.** A 60-90 cm flanged wheel: ESTIMATED 30-50 denarii in iron. Labour: ESTIMATED 20-30 hours. Four wheels: 150-200 denarii.

**Danger.** Casting iron requires careful temperature control.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Flanged wheels are attested from the 16th century. Roman metallurgy can produce them.

### permanent_way - Permanent Way (Railway Track)

**What it is / why you want it.** Parallel iron rails on rigid supports, providing a fixed path for flanged wheels, eliminating road damage and reducing friction dramatically.

**Why you would never guess this.** It seems to require enormous capital. In fact, savings in friction and animal labour pay back the investment in 2-3 years on a well-used route.

**Prerequisites.** Flanged wheels, iron working, surveying, carpentry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast or wrought iron. Timber (oak or pine) for sleepers. Stone or concrete for the base.

**Procedure.** (1) Survey and level the route. (2) Prepare a bed of compacted stone 30-40 cm thick. (3) Lay sleepers (wooden ties) across at 1-metre intervals. (4) Forge or cast I-shaped rails 2-3 cm tall. (5) Fasten rails to sleepers with iron spikes, keeping them parallel and level.

**How you know it worked.** A single horse should pull a 500 kg loaded cart along rails with noticeably less effort than on a normal road.

**Failure modes.** Non-parallel rails cause the wheel to climb and tip. Rotten sleepers allow rails to sag. Poor drainage causes frost heave.

**Cost & labour.** Per kilometre: ESTIMATED 1000-2000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 200-400 person-days. Calendar time: 2-3 months per kilometre.

**Danger.** Heavy objects can crush limbs. Sharp edges hazard.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Cast iron railways were built in the 18th century. Roman metallurgy can produce rails, but high-quality cast iron requires a blast furnace.

### railway_signalling - Railway Signalling

**What it is / why you want it.** A system preventing two trains in the same track section simultaneously. This is fundamentally an information problem.

**Why you would never guess this.** Crashes seem mechanical. With properly designed signals and rules, crashes become nearly impossible. The key invention is the block system: forbid any train from entering an occupied section.

**Prerequisites.** Permanent way, understanding of position and communication.

**Roman-available inputs.** Semaphore arms (wooden poles with movable arms). Bells or gongs. Iron or stone for signal posts. Paper and ink for timetables.

**Procedure.** (1) Divide railway into blocks of 2-3 km. (2) At each block entrance, mount a signal post with a movable arm. Paint clearly: "open" (up at 45 degrees) means clear; "stop" (horizontal) means occupied. (3) Assign a signalman to each block. (4) When a train passes, set the signal to "stop" until the train leaves the block. (5) Only then set the signal to "open". (6) Trains travel to a strict timetable.

**How you know it worked.** Run two trains on the same track 30 minutes apart, strictly to timetable. Neither should encounter the other. If a train breaks down, the signalman spots this and sets the signal to "stop".

**Failure modes.** Inattentive signalmen fail to set signals. Slow clocks cause schedule confusion. Ambiguous signals get misread.

**Cost & labour.** A signal post: ESTIMATED 10-20 denarii. Staffing: roughly 20-30 denarii per month per person.

**Danger.** Minimal if signals are obeyed. Catastrophic if ignored.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Railway signalling is attested from the 1830s. Principles are simple.

### safety_bicycle - Safety Bicycle

**What it is / why you want it.** A pedal-driven two-wheeler with nearly equal wheels, pedals driving the rear wheel via a chain. Much faster and safer than a penny-farthing.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive design makes the front wheel enormous. A chain drive seems wasteful. But the chain decouples pedalling speed from wheel speed.

**Prerequisites.** Wheel construction, metalworking (chain), bearings.

**Roman-available inputs.** Steel or iron for chain links. Wood or bentwood for frame. Leather/iron for solid tyres. Ball bearings.

**Procedure.** (1) Build two wheels 60-70 cm diameter. (2) Build a frame from bent steel tubing. (3) Mount wheels with ball bearings. (4) Forge a chain of linked iron rings (roughly 15-20 mm diameter, 100+ links). (5) Mount sprockets on rear wheel (8-12 teeth) and pedal crank (20-30 teeth). (6) Thread the chain between them. (7) Mount pedal cranks with 17-20 cm radius.

**How you know it worked.** At 60 rpm pedalling, the rider should move at 15-20 km/h. Standing on the pedals should not cause chain slip or frame flex.

**Failure modes.** Too-tight chain requires excessive force. Too loose, it slips. Rust increases friction dramatically. Thin tubing flexes and cracks.

**Cost & labour.** A complete bicycle: ESTIMATED 200-400 denarii in materials. Chain forging: ESTIMATED 30-50 labour-hours. Total: 2-3 weeks.

**Danger.** Broken chain leaves the rider coasting. High speeds increase injury from falls.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The safety bicycle was perfected in the 1880s. Innovations are mechanical and within Roman capability.

## Precision and Rotation

### ball_bearing - Ball Bearing

**What it is / why you want it.** Steel balls rolling between races, reducing friction in rotating axles to a fraction of sliding contact.

**Why you would never guess this.** Rolling friction is far lower than sliding friction, but this is not obvious.

**Prerequisites.** Steel working, hardening and tempering, precision grinding.

**Roman-available inputs.** High-carbon steel. Soft brass or bronze for races.

**Procedure.** (1) Machine an inner race (steel ring 5 cm diameter, 1-2 cm thick) with a groove. (2) Machine an outer race (6-7 cm diameter) with matching groove. (3) Machine steel balls 8-12 mm diameter, as spherical as possible. (4) Harden and temper to maximum hardness. (5) Construct a cage of brass or soft iron. (6) Assemble: balls in cage, into outer race, inner race into centre.

**How you know it worked.** Spin the bearing by hand; it should coast 10-20 seconds. Apply small weight to the inner race; it should lift and lower freely.

**Failure modes.** Non-spherical balls create vibration. Races too tight bind the balls. Too loose, clattering results. Dirt embedded in races causes grinding. Overload flattens balls.

**Cost & labour.** A single bearing: ESTIMATED 15-40 denarii. Four for a cart: 60-160 denarii. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours. Precision requires a lathe.

**Danger.** Hardening produces brittle balls prone to shattering.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Ball bearings appear in European clockwork by the 1400s. Roman metalworking can produce them roughly, but true spheres require precision grinding.

### pneumatic_tyre - Pneumatic Tyre (Filled with Air)

**What it is / why you want it.** A hollow rubber tyre filled with air, providing cushioning and dramatically improving traction compared to iron tyres.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hollow tyres seem fragile. Air pressure allows far lower contact pressure, reducing rolling resistance and wear.

**Prerequisites.** Rubber, vulcanisation, precision moulding.

**Roman-available inputs.** RUBBER IS NOT AVAILABLE. Hevea brasiliensis is native to Brazil and unknown to the Roman world. Roman substitutes are:
- Solid leather tyres (tanned leather wrapped around the rim). Durable for 500-1000 km. Cost: ESTIMATED 5-10 denarii per wheel.
- Iron-reinforced leather. Cost: ESTIMATED 10-15 denarii per wheel.

These provide 30-50% of pneumatic benefits. **Rome is not getting pneumatic tyres.** Vehicles without them accept higher rolling resistance, lower maximum speeds (20-25 km/h for loaded carts), and greater maintenance.

**Confidence: LOW** - Pneumatic tyres were invented in 1888 and depend entirely on vulcanised rubber, unobtainable in the Roman world.

## Internal Combustion

### otto_cycle_engine - Otto Cycle Engine (Spark-Ignition)

**What it is / why you want it.** An internal combustion engine using a four-stroke cycle, producing 1-5 hp from petrol or coal gas.

**Why you would never guess this.** Compressing a fuel-air mixture before ignition produces far more power than burning at atmospheric pressure.

**Prerequisites.** High-precision metalworking, understanding of thermodynamics and valve timing, spark generation.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or brass castings for cylinder and block. High-quality steel for valves and pistons. PETROL IS NOT AVAILABLE. Coal gas (produced by heating coal in an oxygen-free furnace) is accessible but requires industrial coal production.

**Procedure.** (1) Forge or cast a cylinder (iron, bored smooth, 100 mm diameter, 150 mm tall). (2) Forge a tight-fitting steel piston (99.8 mm diameter) with piston rings. (3) Fit piston rod to crankshaft. (4) Machine two exhaust and two intake valves (mushroom-shaped) with springs. (5) Mount spark plug electrodes (1 mm gap) near the top. (6) Construct a manifold to supply coal gas. (7) Connect valves via levers operated by cams. (8) Time valves: at bottom, intake opens for fuel-air mixture; intake closes and piston rises, compressing; at top, spark ignites gas; at bottom, exhaust valve opens.

**How you know it worked.** Run at 100-300 rpm hand-crank start. Engine should run smoothly. Power: ESTIMATED 2-4 hp at 200 rpm.

**Failure modes.** Imperfect piston seals allow gas escape. Carbon buildup reduces compression. Valve timing errors cause power loss or backfiring. Spark plug fouling prevents ignition.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 200-300 hours for a skilled metalworker. Coal gas requires continuous furnace supply.

**Danger.** Explosions can rupture the casting. Poorly timed valves cause backfires. Overheated fuel can ignite.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Otto cycle was running by 1876. Roman metalworking can produce components, but precision for piston seals requires machine tools.

### diesel_cycle - Diesel Cycle (Compression-Ignition)

**What it is / why you want it.** An engine where fuel is injected into cylinder after air has been compressed to high temperature, causing spontaneous ignition. More efficient than spark-ignition engines.

**Why you would never guess this.** Combustion without a spark seems impossible. Compressing air to high temperature (above 600 C) is straightforward.

**Prerequisites.** Otto engine prerequisites, plus a fuel injection pump, very high compression ratio.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or brass castings. High-quality steel. Copper or brass for fuel injectors.

**Procedure.** (1) Forge or cast a cylinder with thicker walls than an otto engine (to withstand higher pressure). (2) Achieve compression ratio of 15-20:1 (otto engines are 5-8:1). (3) Use a fuel injection pump to spray fuel into the cylinder during compression stroke. (4) Omit spark plugs. (5) The cylinder heats incoming air to 600+ C, so injected fuel ignites spontaneously.

**How you know it worked.** Run at 200-400 rpm. Power: ESTIMATED 3-6 hp for similar displacement. Fuel efficiency: roughly 40% better than otto engine.

**Failure modes.** Injection timing errors cause knock. Fuel too volatile may ignite too early. Carbon buildup fouls the injector. High compression stresses piston rod.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED 800-1500 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 300-400 hours. Injection pump adds complexity.

**Danger.** Very high pressure and temperature. Failure can cause explosion.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Diesel engines were running by 1897. Roman metallurgy can produce components.

### carburettor - Carburettor

**What it is / why you want it.** A device that mixes liquid fuel with air in the correct proportion (roughly 1:14 by weight) for combustion.

**Why you would never guess this.** Liquid fuel must vaporize and mix with air in precise ratio. A carburettor evaporates fuel by passing hot air over it.

**Prerequisites.** Otto or diesel engine, understanding of air/fuel ratios.

**Roman-available inputs.** Brass or bronze for the main body. Precision-drilled iron for jets. Copper tubing. Leather washers.

**Procedure.** (1) Machine a brass body (barrel, 50 mm diameter, 30 mm tall) with a bore through the centre. (2) Drill a fine hole (jet) 1-2 mm diameter on one side, slightly above centre. (3) Connect a fuel reservoir above; fuel flows by gravity. (4) A needle valve controlled by a float maintains constant fuel level. (5) As the engine draws air, moving air reduces pressure (Bernoulli principle), drawing fuel from the jet. (6) The jet size controls fuel-to-air ratio. (7) Engine heat evaporates the fuel.

**How you know it worked.** Start the engine at part throttle; it should run smoothly. Increase throttle and it should accelerate without coughing. Light grey or colourless exhaust means correct mixture; black means too much fuel.

**Failure modes.** Too-large jet allows excess fuel. Too small starves the engine. Dirt or varnish clogs the jet. Fuel of insufficient volatility will not evaporate in cold weather.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED 30-50 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 15-20 hours.

**Danger.** Carburettors leak easily; fuel near a hot engine can ignite.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Carburettors were standard by 1900. Roman metalworking can produce them.

### magneto_ignition - Magneto Ignition

**What it is / why you want it.** A device generating high-voltage electrical pulses using a rotating permanent magnet and copper wire coil, producing sparks for engine ignition.

**Why you would never guess this.** A rotating magnet induces current in a nearby coil; a secondary coil with many more turns steps up this current to very high voltage.

**Prerequisites.** Permanent magnets (lodestone or magnetized iron), copper wire, understanding of electromagnetic induction.

**Roman-available inputs.** Lodestone (naturally magnetized magnetite ore) from Egypt. Soft iron. Copper wire. Copper foil. Leather or ceramic for insulation.

**Procedure.** (1) Magnetize an iron bar by stroking repeatedly with lodestone. (2) Machine the bar into a cylindrical rotor (5 cm diameter). (3) Wind a primary coil (roughly 100 turns of thin copper wire) around the rotor. (4) Mount rotor on a shaft connected to camshaft or crankshaft. (5) Position a stationary core (soft iron horseshoe shape) around the rotor with a secondary coil (1000-2000 turns of very thin wire). (6) As rotor rotates, the magnet's field sweeps past the secondary coil, inducing voltage. (7) Connect secondary coil output to spark plug.

**How you know it worked.** Rotate the shaft at moderate speed (300 rpm). Observe the spark plug; a small spark should jump the gap with each rotation. The engine should ignite consistently without misfiring.

**Failure modes.** Poor magnetic contact reduces induced voltage. Broken secondary coil wire causes no spark. Spark plug fouling prevents spark. Timing errors cause backfiring.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED 100-150 denarii in copper, iron, and lodestone. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-80 hours (secondary coil hand-winding is tedious).

**Danger.** Secondary coil delivers 10000+ volts. High-voltage arcing can cause burns or ignite fuel vapour.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Magnetos were standard on early petrol engines (pre-1920). Roman metalworking can produce them.

### gearbox_differential - Gearbox and Differential

**What it is / why you want it.** A transmission allowing an engine at fixed rpm to drive wheels at different speeds. The gearbox steps down speed; the differential allows rear wheels to turn at different rates when cornering.

**Why you would never guess this.** Direct coupling means the engine stalls if load is too high. Intermediate gearing reduces speed and increases torque.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working (precision gears), understanding of gear ratios.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast iron for gear blanks. Steel for precision cutting. Lubricating oil.

**Procedure for gearbox.** (1) Machine precision gears: engine pinion (5 cm diameter, 20 teeth); wheel gears (20 cm diameter, 80 teeth). (2) Fit in a steel housing with precisely-aligned shafts. (3) Select for 4:1 reduction ratio. (4) Lubricate all bearings and meshes. (5) For multi-speed transmission, add intermediate gears.

**Procedure for differential.** (1) In rear axle, replace rigid connection between rear wheels with four small gears (spider gears) in rotating case. (2) These gears allow wheels to turn at slightly different speeds while sharing engine torque. (3) Straight driving: gears all turn together. Cornering: gears slip slightly.

**How you know it worked.** Engage gearbox; wheels should turn at roughly 1/4 engine rpm. Increasing engine speed should increase wheel speed smoothly. On a tight circle: outside wheels should turn faster.

**Failure modes.** Imprecise tooth spacing causes vibration. Over-torque strips gears. Insufficient lubrication causes overheating. Misaligned gears bind.

**Cost & labour.** Complete gearbox and differential: ESTIMATED 300-500 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 100-150 hours. Precision gear-cutting requires a lathe.

**Danger.** Exposed gears can catch fingers. Overheated oil can ignite.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Gear trains are well-understood mechanics. Roman metalworking can produce them.

### assembly_line - Assembly Line Production

**What it is / why you want it.** Manufacturing where a product is assembled in stages by multiple workers, each performing a simple repetitive task. Reduces time per unit from days to hours.

**Why you would never guess this.** Skilled workers building complete units seems more efficient. Specialization and parallel work reduce fatigue and each station checks the previous station's output.

**Prerequisites.** Multiple identical products, standardized parts, organization and labour discipline.

**Roman-available inputs.** Workshops with multiple stations. Carts or continuously-moving platform (rope-driven or human-powered) to carry work between stations.

**Procedure.** (1) Divide the build into 10-20 stages, each taking 5-10 minutes. (2) Arrange workstations in sequence. (3) Provide each with required tools and parts. (4) Move product from station to station. (5) Train each worker to perform only their assigned task. (6) Measure cycle time; line rate is limited by the slowest station.

**How you know it worked.** Traditional method (one worker per vehicle): roughly 15-20 days per vehicle. With 20-station assembly line, cycle time per station is 2-3 hours; one vehicle exits every 2-3 hours. Throughput increases 5-10 fold for the same total labour.

**Failure modes.** Poorly trained workers make errors. Imbalanced stations become bottleneck. Supply chain disruption stalls the line. Worker fatigue causes errors.

**Cost & labour.** A 20-station line producing motorcars: ESTIMATED 20-30 workers, producing 2-3 complete vehicles per day. Capital cost: ESTIMATED 1000-2000 denarii. Labour cost: 20-30 denarii per day.

**Danger.** Moving machinery can catch clothing and hair. Repetitive work causes strain injuries.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Assembly line was systematized by Henry Ford in 1913. Principles are straightforward.

## Maritime

### sternpost_rudder - Sternpost Rudder

**What it is / why you want it.** A flat blade fastened vertically to the rear of the hull, pivoting on hinges, allowing the helmsman to steer. Replaces ancient side rudders.

**Why you would never guess this.** Side rudders work reasonably well for small vessels. A stern rudder is far more efficient because the stern moves faster through water.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry, hinges (iron or leather straps).

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or pine timber for the blade (2-3 metres tall, 1 metre wide, 5-10 cm thick). Iron straps and bolts. Iron rings. Leather strap for the tiller.

**Procedure.** (1) Shape wooden blade 2-3 metres tall, 1 metre wide. (2) Fasten iron straps near the top, creating hinges pinning to the sternpost. (3) Install iron rings on the sternpost. (4) Attach a long wooden lever (tiller) to the blade's top, extending horizontally into the ship. (5) Balance the rudder.

**How you know it worked.** Moving the tiller should cause immediate, smooth course changes without excessive force. The ship should hold a straight course with minimal adjustment.

**Failure modes.** Too-small rudder is ineffective. Too large creates excessive drag. Loose hinges cause flapping. Rot weakens the blade.

**Cost & labour.** For a large merchant ship: ESTIMATED 100-200 denarii in timber and iron. Labour: ESTIMATED 30-50 hours.

**Danger.** Loose parts can separate and leave the ship without steerage.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Stern rudders appear in Chinese ships by the 11th century. Roman ships could accommodate them.

### lateen_rig - Lateen Rig (Triangular Sail)

**What it is / why you want it.** A triangular sail suspended from a diagonal spar rather than square sail, allowing sailing much closer to the wind.

**Why you would never guess this.** A square sail seems more efficient. The lateen's advantage is aerodynamic: the triangle creates lift like an aerofoil. A square sail sails 60-75 degrees from downwind; a lateen rig manages 35-40 degrees.

**Prerequisites.** Sailmaking, rigging, understanding of wind and hydrodynamics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen cloth. Ropes of hemp or flax. The lateen yard is a long wooden spar.

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a triangular sail from linen, leading edge 12 metres, trailing edge 8 metres (creating aerodynamic shape), height 10 metres. (2) Attach leading edge to a wooden spar (lateen yard), 15-18 metres long. (3) The spar hangs from a line attached to the mast, roughly 1/3 of the way down. (4) Guy ropes to the deck control angle and tension. (5) When sailing, angle the forward end of the spar up 45 degrees.

**How you know it worked.** Sail on a course 40-45 degrees from the wind. A square sail would back; the lateen should fill with wind and pull the ship forward at 50-75% of downwind speed.

**Failure modes.** The lateen yard is heavy and difficult to manoeuvre in high winds. The sail can rip if wind suddenly shifts. The leading edge must remain smooth; wrinkles destroy lift.

**Cost & labour.** Sails and rigging: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 100-150 hours.

**Danger.** The heavy lateen yard can fall and injure sailors.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Lateen rigs are well-attested from the medieval Mediterranean. Aerodynamically sound.

### magnetic_compass - Magnetic Compass

**What it is / why you want it.** A magnetized iron needle floating in water, aligned by Earth's magnetic field to point north, providing constant reference direction at sea.

**Why you would never guess this.** Earth's magnetism is invisible. Iron becomes magnetized if aligned with Earth's field for extended time.

**Prerequisites.** Magnetized iron, understanding that iron can be aligned, watertight bowl.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron needle (thin, 5-10 cm long, sharpened). Lodestone to magnetize it. Cork or wood for a float. Bowl (glass, ceramic, or wood) with a window. Water.

**Procedure.** (1) Magnetize a steel needle by stroking repeatedly with lodestone in the same direction, 50-100 times. (2) Float the needle on cork in a bowl of water. (3) Observe that it aligns with a constant direction (roughly north-south). (4) Mark directions on the bowl rim: North, South, and cardinal and intercardinal directions. (5) Install a glass window on top. (6) Attach a gimbal so the bowl can swing freely.

**How you know it worked.** At a location where true north is known, verify the needle points north. Move the compass in a circle; the needle should maintain orientation. Bring large iron nearby; the needle should deflect. Remove iron; it should return to north.

**Failure modes.** Insufficiently magnetized needle points weakly. Sharp striking destroys magnetization. Salt water corrodes the needle. Needle can stick.

**Cost & labour.** Simple compass: ESTIMATED 5-10 denarii. Labour: ESTIMATED 3-5 hours. A ship's compass: ESTIMATED 20-30 denarii.

**Danger.** Minimal. Needle not hazardous unless bowl breaks.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Magnetic compasses are attested in Chinese ships by the 11th century. Principles are sound.

### marine_chronometer - Marine Chronometer

**What it is / why you want it.** A mechanical clock maintaining accurate time at sea, permitting determination of the ship's longitude by comparing local noon with the time at a known reference meridian.

**Why you would never guess this.** Determining longitude seems like an astronomical problem. It is a timekeeping problem. One hour of time difference equals roughly 15 degrees of longitude.

**Prerequisites.** Advanced clockmaking, understanding of longitude, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Brass and steel for gears and springs. A balance wheel. Copper or brass cases. Glass face.

**Procedure** (balance-wheel chronometer). (1) Construct a precision clock with a balance wheel oscillating at constant frequency, regulated by a spring. (2) The balance wheel oscillates roughly 2-3 times per second; gears count and translate into hours, minutes, seconds. (3) Enclose in a brass case for protection. (4) Mount in a wooden frame with low-friction pivots. (5) Include an escapement mechanism allowing gears to advance one tick per oscillation. (6) Adjust spring tension so the clock loses less than 5 seconds per day.

**How you know it worked.** Set to a known time and run for a week, recording time daily. Calculate rate of loss or gain. A good chronometer should lose less than 10 seconds per day.

**Failure modes.** Fast or slow running introduces longitude errors. Each second of error translates to 500 metres of position error at the equator. Temperature changes cause metal expansion. Salt spray corrodes and jams gears. Vibration can shock the balance wheel.

**Cost & labour.** A marine chronometer: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 300-500 hours. Precision required demands machine tools.

**Danger.** Minimal. Delicate and must be handled carefully.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Marine chronometers were perfected by John Harrison in the 1700s. Roman clockmaking was not advanced. Precision metalworking required exceeds Roman practice.

### screw_propeller - Screw Propeller

**What it is / why you want it.** A rotating propeller consisting of aerofoil-shaped blades submerged in water, driving a ship by pushing water backward. Superior to paddle wheels in efficiency and reliability.

**Why you would never guess this.** Paddle wheels are obvious and work. The screw propeller: (1) remains submerged regardless of ship loading or motion; (2) is immune to gunfire; (3) permits faster ship speeds and better control.

**Prerequisites.** Bronze or iron casting, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze (superior for salt water) or iron for hub and blades. Steel bolts and fasteners.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a propeller with 2-4 blades arranged around a central hub. Each blade is roughly an aerofoil (wing-like) in cross-section. (2) Cast the hub and blades as a single piece from bronze or iron. (3) The hub has a tapered hole for mounting on the ship's propeller shaft. (4) The pitch (angle) of the blades is typically 25-45 degrees from horizontal. (5) Mount on the ship's shaft behind the rudder. (6) The propeller rotates at 100-300 rpm.

**How you know it worked.** At propeller shaft running 200 rpm, the ship should move forward smoothly and controllably. Increasing shaft speed should increase ship speed roughly linearly.

**Failure modes.** Blades too thin flex and lose efficiency. Too thick creates excessive drag. Incorrect blade pitch causes poor efficiency. Fouling (seaweed, marine growth) reduces efficiency. Corrosion degrades performance.

**Cost & labour.** A cast bronze propeller 1 metre diameter: ESTIMATED 200-400 denarii in bronze. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours.

**Danger.** A rotating propeller is extremely hazardous; contact causes severe injury or death.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Screw propellers were developed in the 1830s-1840s. Roman metalworking can produce bronze propellers.

### iron_hull - Iron Hull

**What it is / why you want it.** A ship whose hull is constructed from riveted iron plates rather than wood, providing greater strength-to-weight ratio and longer lifespan.

**Why you would never guess this.** Iron sinks; an iron ship should sink. What matters is the average density of ship including enclosed air. An iron hull encloses air; average density of iron-plus-air is less than water.

**Prerequisites.** Iron smelting and rolling, riveting, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wrought iron rolled into sheets 2-3 mm thick, roughly 1-2 metres wide. Iron rivets. Caulking material (tar-soaked rope or lead sheet).

**Procedure.** (1) Design a hull shape using the same principles as a wooden ship. (2) Roll iron into plates (roughly 2 metres x 1 metre per plate). (3) Arrange plates in overlapping rows (strakes) like wooden planking. (4) Rivet each plate to its neighbor: align edges, drill holes through both plates, insert an iron rivet (short bolt with head), support rivet head on one side with a bucking bar (iron anvil), hammer the protruding end until it swells and pulls plates together. Repeat 10-20 cm along each seam. (5) Caulk seams: drive tar-soaked rope into the gap or solder with molten lead. (6) Paint hull with anti-corrosion paint.

**How you know it worked.** Float the ship and load it with cargo. The hull should remain watertight. The ship should show visible strength with no flexing or cracking of iron plates.

**Failure modes.** Poor riveting leaves gaps and the hull leaks. Overly tight rivets cause plate buckling. Corrosion (rust) gradually weakens the hull. Electrochemical corrosion (galvanic corrosion) can be severe. Improperly caulked seams leak. Strikes from rocks or other ships can dent or crack iron and shear rivets.

**Cost & labour.** An iron-hulled ship 30 metres long, 8 metres wide: ESTIMATED 5000-10000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 5000-10000 person-hours, spread over 3-6 months. Capital: a large shipyard with iron-rolling furnace.

**Danger.** Molten lead is extremely hazardous. Riveting creates noise and vibration causing long-term hearing damage.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Iron-hulled ships became common in the 1850s-1870s. Roman metalworking can produce plates and rivets, but quality would be lower. The biggest limitation is obtaining sufficient high-quality wrought iron.

### compound_engine - Compound Engine (Marine Steam Engine)

**What it is / why you want it.** A steam engine that expands steam through two or more cylinders in sequence, extracting more work per unit of fuel than a simple engine.

**Why you would never guess this.** Once steam has done work pushing a piston, the steam is still hot. Extracting more work seems impossible. Passing exhaust from the first cylinder into a larger second cylinder extracts much remaining energy.

**Prerequisites.** Steam engine design, high-pressure boiler technology, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron castings for cylinders and frame. Steel for pistons and rods. Copper or brass for fittings. Water for the boiler.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a compound engine with a high-pressure cylinder (roughly 30 cm diameter) and a low-pressure cylinder (roughly 60 cm diameter), connected so exhaust from the high-pressure feeds into the low-pressure cylinder. (2) Construct a powerful boiler maintaining steam pressure at 100-200 psi (roughly 7-14 atmospheres). (3) Superheating coils in the firebox heat steam above saturation temperature. (4) A governor (mechanical feedback device) maintains constant speed by adjusting the throttle valve. (5) Connect both cylinders to a common crankshaft.

**How you know it worked.** Compare fuel consumption to a single-cylinder engine of similar power output. A compound engine should require roughly 30-50% less fuel for the same power.

**Failure modes.** Poor steam superheating causes condensation and loss of efficiency. Unbalanced piston timing causes vibration. Boiler pressure fluctuations reduce efficiency.

**Cost & labour.** A compound marine engine producing 50 hp: ESTIMATED 2000-4000 denarii in iron and copper. Labour: ESTIMATED 1000-1500 hours.

**Danger.** High-pressure steam causes severe scalding or explosion. Boiler failure can destroy the engine. Flywheel imbalance causes violent shaking.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Compound steam engines were standard by the 1870s. Thermodynamics and mechanics are sound.

## Flight

### kite - Kite

**What it is / why you want it.** A lightweight frame covered with cloth, suspended by a string, that flies in the wind and demonstrates fundamental aerodynamic principles.

**Why you would never guess this.** Kites demonstrate that a flat or curved surface at an angle to the wind generates upward force (lift).

**Prerequisites.** Framework construction, cloth-working, string.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for frame (ash or birch). Linen cloth or paper. Hemp rope.

**Procedure.** (1) Build a simple kite: two sticks lashed in an X-shape, 1 metre tall and 1 metre wide. (2) Cover the frame with linen, leaving the bottom open for air to pass through. (3) Attach two string bridles from the top corners to a single point 10-15 cm in front of the frame's centre. (4) Attach the main tether line to the bridle point. (5) Add a tail of cloth 2-3 metres long to stabilize the kite. (6) Test in steady wind 3-5 m/s.

**How you know it worked.** The kite should rise against the tether and maintain a stable altitude, nose up at roughly 30-45 degrees to the horizontal. Pulling on the tether should cause climbing; releasing should cause diving or gliding.

**Failure modes.** An unstable kite (wobbling or spinning) requires a longer tail or different bridle geometry. Too much weight causes dropping. Too little area fails to generate lift in weak wind.

**Cost & labour.** A basic kite: ESTIMATED 1-2 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 2-3 hours to build.

**Danger.** Minimal if flown in open areas. Lightning can strike in a thunderstorm.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Kites have been flown for centuries. Physics is simple; construction is elementary.

### hydrogen_balloon - Hydrogen Balloon

**What it is / why you want it.** A bag of airtight cloth filled with hydrogen gas, carrying a basket suspended below, rising to great heights and carrying observers aloft.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hydrogen has density roughly 0.09 kg/m3, compared to air at 1.2 kg/m3 at sea level. A volume of hydrogen displaces its weight in air and provides buoyancy.

**Prerequisites.** Large quantities of hydrogen gas, airtight fabric, basketwork for gondola.

**Roman-available inputs.** Tightly-woven linen, coated with tallow or pitch to make it airtight. Wicker or basketwork for gondola. Rope for suspension lines. HYDROGEN: produced by passing steam over hot iron, or dissolving zinc in dilute acid. Production is labour-intensive (highly flammable).

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a large bag from airtight linen, roughly 10 metres diameter (roughly 500 m3 volume, providing 500+ kg of lift). This requires sewing many pieces and sealing all seams. (2) Attach suspension lines (rope) from the bag's envelope to a wooden frame. (3) Suspend a wicker gondola 10-20 metres below. (4) Produce hydrogen gas and fill the envelope through a filling valve. (5) Release from the ground; the balloon rises until the surrounding air density matches the average density of the balloon plus gondola.

**How you know it worked.** The balloon should rise steadily and maintain altitude with the gondola weight. From altitude, observers should see panoramic landscape views. Descend by releasing ballast (sand or water) or venting hydrogen.

**Failure modes.** Hydrogen leaks from seams reduce lift gradually. A large leak causes rapid descent. Hydrogen is highly flammable; any flame or spark ignites it. Wind at altitude can buffet and damage. Landing in trees can puncture the envelope.

**Cost & labour.** A 500 m3 hydrogen balloon: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in linen and pitch. Labour: ESTIMATED 200-300 hours to sew and seal. Hydrogen production (single fill): ESTIMATED 50-100 denarii in materials and 20-50 labour-hours.

**Danger.** Hydrogen is explosive. Filling near a flame is suicidal. Ascending to great altitude causes altitude sickness. Vulnerable to lightning.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Hydrogen balloons were flown in 1783 onward. Physics is sound. Roman chemistry can produce hydrogen.

### aerofoil_camber - Aerofoil Camber (Wing Curvature for Lift)

**What it is / why you want it.** A wing profile that is curved rather than flat, generating lift even at low angles of attack. A cambered wing provides far better lift-to-drag ratio than a flat plate.

**Why you would never guess this.** Camber accelerates air over the top of the wing, reducing pressure above and increasing lift by a factor of 2-3 compared to a flat plate.

**Prerequisites.** Understanding of lift and drag, wing design.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for frame, linen for covering.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a wing profile: the chord line (straight line from leading to trailing edge) remains roughly horizontal at cruising angle. (2) The upper surface is curved upward, creating an arc. (3) The lower surface can be flat or slightly curved downward. (4) Curvature is greatest near the leading edge, tapering toward the trailing edge. (5) A typical camber is roughly 1-2% of the chord length (a 1-metre wing has maximum height difference of 10-20 mm). (6) Construct the wing from wooden frame covered with tight linen, shaped to this profile.

**How you know it worked.** Test the wing by measuring the lift force perpendicular to the airflow. Compare to a flat plate of the same area and angle; the cambered wing should produce 50-100% more lift at the same angle.

**Failure modes.** Excessive camber (more than 3% of chord) increases drag and can cause the wing to stall at shallow angles. Insufficient camber provides little benefit. A damaged wing that is wrinkled or deformed loses aerodynamic advantage.

**Cost & labour.** A cambered wing is only slightly more difficult to construct than a flat wing. Labour: roughly 20-30% more.

**Danger.** Minimal.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Cambered wings are standard in aeronautical engineering. Principles were discovered through wind tunnel testing in the 1890s.

### wind_tunnel - Wind Tunnel

**What it is / why you want it.** A chamber where air is blown across a stationary model at controlled speed, allowing measurement of lift, drag, and other aerodynamic forces.

**Why you would never guess this.** Wind tunnels allow repeatable measurement with clean data and eliminate uncontrolled variables.

**Prerequisites.** Large fan (or water wheel driving a fan), ductwork, measurements of pressure and velocity.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wooden frame for the tunnel. Large wooden fan (similar to a mill wheel) driven by an animal or water wheel. Manometer (tube of water in U-shape). String or thread for smoke flow visualization. Model aircraft or wings from wood and linen.

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a wind tunnel: a rectangular duct roughly 2 metres x 2 metres x 5 metres long. (2) Mount a large fan at one end (driven by horse-powered shaft or water wheel); the fan pulls air through the tunnel. (3) Install a model aircraft or wing in the centre on a support stand. (4) Suspend the model from a balance arm or spring scale that measures vertical forces (lift). Measure horizontal forces with another balance perpendicular to airflow. (5) Adjust fan speed to achieve desired airspeed (measured using a manometer). (6) Record lift and drag forces at various angles of attack.

**How you know it worked.** Run the wind tunnel at a known airspeed (say 10 m/s). Measure lift on a flat plate at 10 degrees. Repeat with a cambered wing of the same area; the cambered wing should produce 50-100% more lift.

**Failure modes.** Turbulent airflow (caused by rough walls, sharp corners, or obstacles) produces unrealistic data. The model support stand creates drag and can interfere with flow. Temperature changes affect air density.

**Cost & labour.** A wood and canvas wind tunnel: ESTIMATED 200-500 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 100-200 hours to build and install.

**Danger.** Tunnel entrance can develop very high air velocity (100+ km/h) and can catch loose items or hair.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Wind tunnels were built by aviation pioneers in the 1890s. Principles are straightforward.

### three_axis_control - Three-Axis Control (Aircraft Control Surfaces)

**What it is / why you want it.** A system of three independently-controlled surfaces allowing the pilot to manage pitch (nose up/down), roll (wing up/down), and yaw (nose left/right) independently.

**Why you would never guess this.** Before the Wrights, designers focused on generating lift and power. In fact, all four elements (lift, drag, thrust, control) are independent problems requiring solution through engineering.

**Prerequisites.** Aircraft frame, control linkages, pilot control inputs.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wooden frame. Linen covering. Rope or leather straps for linkages. Pulley blocks.

**Procedure.** (1) Pitch control: attach a movable horizontal surface (elevator) at the rear of the fuselage. Connect a lever or cable from the elevator to the pilot's control stick. Forward stick pushes elevator down, pitching nose down; backward stick pulls elevator up, pitching nose up. (2) Roll control: attach movable surfaces (ailerons) on the rear of each wing. Connect one aileron to rise as the other drops. If the pilot pushes a stick to the right, the right aileron rises and the left drops, rolling the aircraft right. (3) Yaw control: attach a movable vertical surface (rudder) at the rear of the fuselage, operated by foot pedals. Pushing the right pedal deflects the rudder right, pivoting the nose right.

**How you know it worked.** Manually move the control levers; the control surfaces should move smoothly and completely without excessive force. Linkages should have no slack or binding.

**Failure modes.** Slack in linkages causes delayed response and makes the aircraft difficult to control. A control surface that binds or catches requires excessive force and can jam. Asymmetric response (one wing slower than the other) causes unwanted rolling.

**Cost & labour.** Three-axis control surfaces for a small aircraft: ESTIMATED 50-100 denarii in wood and linen. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours to fabricate and install.

**Danger.** Loose control linkages can cause uncontrolled motion of surfaces. Control reversals (where pushing the stick produces opposite motion) cause crashes.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Three-axis control was the key innovation of the Wright brothers. Mechanics are straightforward.

### propeller_aero - Propeller (Aeronautical Screw)

**What it is / why you want it.** A rotating propeller consisting of aerofoil-shaped blades, pulling the aircraft forward by accelerating air rearward.

**Why you would never guess this.** Early aviators treated aero-propellers as "screws" in air. Designing the blade shape and twist as a wing produces much higher efficiency.

**Prerequisites.** Aerofoil design, wood-working.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for propeller blades (ash, birch, or other strong, light wood). Iron or bronze for the hub and fasteners. Bearings.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a two-bladed propeller with each blade shaped as a cambered aerofoil. (2) The blade is twisted: the root (near hub) is pitched at a steeper angle (40-50 degrees) and the tip is pitched at a shallower angle (20-30 degrees). This variation is necessary because the tip moves much faster than the root; the twist compensates so all points see a similar effective angle of attack. (3) The blade is typically 2-4 metres long. (4) Cut the blades from solid wood, tapering and shaping the aerofoil profile. (5) Attach to a wooden or metal hub. (6) Balance carefully: any imbalance causes severe vibration.

**How you know it worked.** Spin the propeller at design rpm. Place a small flag 2-3 metres in front; it should be pushed backward strongly. Increase rpm; thrust should increase roughly with the square of rpm.

**Failure modes.** Imbalance causes violent vibration and can crack the engine or aircraft frame. Blade damage causes further imbalance. Incorrect blade twist reduces efficiency. Erosion of the blade leading edge gradually degrades performance.

**Cost & labour.** A two-bladed propeller 3 metres diameter: ESTIMATED 100-200 denarii in wood and metal. Labour: ESTIMATED 80-120 hours.

**Danger.** A spinning propeller is extremely hazardous; contact causes severe injury or death.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Aero-propellers were developed by aviation pioneers in the 1900s-1910s. Based on solid aerodynamic principles.

### powered_aeroplane - Powered Aeroplane

**What it is / why you want it.** A heavier-than-air aircraft with wings for lift, an engine and propeller for thrust, a tail for stability, and control surfaces for pitch, roll, and yaw. The aircraft lifts off, flies under power, and lands under pilot control.

**Why you would never guess this.** For millennia, humans attempted to fly by flapping artificial wings. The Wrights recognized that lift, drag, thrust, and control were independent problems solvable separately through engineering.

**Prerequisites.** Otto cycle engine, propeller design, three-axis control, wing design with camber, fuselage construction.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood frame (ash, birch, spruce). Linen covering for wings and tail. Iron or steel for engine and engine mounts. Rope and leather for control linkages. Ball bearings.

**Procedure.** (1) Design the aircraft: roughly 12 metres wingspan, 8 metres length, weighing 400-600 kg empty including a 10-15 kg engine. (2) Construct the fuselage: a wooden frame of longerons (long beams running front to back) and cross-bracing, strong enough to withstand flight forces. (3) Construct the wings: two large surfaces with cambered aerofoil profile, strut-braced to fuselage, covered with tight linen. Wing area roughly 30-50 m2 to generate sufficient lift. (4) Construct the tail: horizontal elevator for pitch control, vertical rudder for yaw control. (5) Mount the engine on the fuselage, with propeller on the crankshaft and a fuel tank (20-50 litres). (6) Install control linkages from pilot's position to elevator, ailerons, and rudder. (7) Install landing gear: bicycle-style wheels or skids. (8) Paint with dope (cellulose lacquer) to tighten linen and seal against moisture.

**How you know it worked.** The aircraft should take off when the engine is at full throttle and wheels are moving at roughly 30-40 km/h (the takeoff speed). Once airborne, it should climb at 1-2 metres per second. The pilot should be able to manoeuvre: pitch up/down, roll left/right, climb, descend, and turn. Landing should be controllable: throttle back to descend, flare (pitch up slightly) to increase drag and reduce landing speed, and touch down smoothly.

**Failure modes.** Insufficient engine power means the aircraft cannot reach takeoff speed. Excessive weight requires higher takeoff speed. Nose-heavy or tail-heavy imbalance makes the aircraft difficult or impossible to control. Control linkage failure during flight causes loss of control. Wing failure causes catastrophic crash. Engine failure causes forced landing.

**Cost & labour.** A complete aircraft including engine: ESTIMATED 5000-10000 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 2000-3000 hours for a team, spread over 3-6 months. Requires a dedicated aircraft factory.

**Danger.** Flight is inherently dangerous. Engine failure, structural failure, or loss of control causes fatal crashes. Spinning propellers, fuel leaks, and high-temperature engine parts are ground hazards.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - The Wright Flyer (1903) is well-documented proof of concept. Building a reliable aircraft requires precision metalworking, wood-working, and fabric skills available to Romans but at lower quality than industrial-era versions. A Roman aircraft would be heavier and less efficient than contemporary aircraft but should fly if carefully designed. Main limitations: (1) the engine is marginal (roughly 5-10 kg per horsepower is the requirement; Roman engines are bulkier); (2) precision wood-working and fabric construction require skill and care; (3) fuel supply (petrol or coal gas) is problematic.

## Sources and Confidence

The technologies described in this module reflect the state of knowledge as of roughly 1890-1910 AD, by which time most of these systems had been developed, tested, and proven in full-scale operation. The description of each technology includes a confidence rating (HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW) based on the maturity of the underlying physics and the feasibility of Roman-era construction.

The most significant constraint on implementing these technologies in the Roman world is not the fundamental physics, which is understood, but the industrial and material supply chain. Steam engines require high-quality iron and boilers; aircraft require precision metalworking and light, strong materials; internal combustion engines require petrol or other refined fuel; and many maritime innovations require industrial metallurgy or sustained engineering effort.

Despite these limitations, each individual technology is attainable by an intelligent, resource-rich civilization, given time and expertise. A Rome that prioritizes technological development and accepts the necessary capital investment could, over the course of a century or more, develop roads, railways, and even powered aircraft. The limiting factor would be energy: coal-fired steam engines would transform manufacturing and transportation, but Rome has access only to charcoal as a fuel, limiting the scale and efficiency of industrial processes.
