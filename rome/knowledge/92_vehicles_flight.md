# Module 92: Vehicles, Ships and Flight

This module covers the science and engineering of land transport, maritime vessels, and powered flight. It documents the technologies that will eventually render the Mediterranean a provincial backwater by reducing transport costs and opening global trade. The single most important fact is the freight arithmetic: **sea freight costs roughly one fortieth to one sixtieth of land freight per tonne-kilometre**. Everything else flows from this.

Rome already possesses an excellent paved trunk road network (the cursus publicus), carts, spoked wheels with iron tyres, very large merchant sailing ships with mortise-and-tenon hull construction and lead sheathing, artificial harbours built in hydraulic concrete, and the established monsoon route to India. The following technologies will extend and eventually supersede these capabilities.

## Land Transport

### suspension_springs - Suspension Springs

**What it is / why you want it.** A system of metal springs that isolate a vehicle's body from road shocks, greatly reducing vibration, wear on cargo, and fatigue for passengers. A cart using springs can carry heavier loads at higher speeds over poor roads without damage.

**Why you would never guess this.** Springs seem like they would make a cart bouncier, not quieter. The actual principle is that a spring defers impact over time rather than transmitting it instantly; the longer the force acts, the lower the peak stress. This requires understanding harmonic motion and damping, neither obvious to casual observation.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working (foundry for springs), geometry, empirical testing of steel hardness.

**Roman-available inputs.** High-carbon iron from a bloomery. Steel made by forge-hardening and tempering with water. Leaf springs are easiest: thin strips of spring steel, stacked.

**Procedure.** (1) Hammer-forge a strip of mild iron 50 mm wide, 8-12 mm thick, length proportional to cart weight. (2) Heat to orange-yellow (600-700 C), fold in half, and hammer-weld the fold so it becomes a loop. (3) Repeat step 2 with additional strips, stacking them atop one another, bound with iron straps. (4) Harden the assembly: heat to bright cherry red (around 800 C), quench in water, then temper at straw-yellow (around 200-220 C) by reheating in a low charcoal fire and watching the colour change. (5) Mount the spring assembly on axle brackets so the cart body rests on it, not on the axle itself.

**How you know it worked.** Roll an unladen cart with springs over a gravel road and watch a cup of water on the cart. A cart without springs will slosh and spill freely; a properly tuned spring system should lose only a few drops. Load the cart with 200 kg and test again; it should not sag to the ground.

**Failure modes.** Over-hardening leaves the spring brittle and prone to cracking. Under-hardening causes permanent deformation and loss of springback. Improper stacking causes the leaves to slide relative to one another and causes noise and eventual failure. Incorrect tempering temperature produces unpredictable hardness.

**Cost & labour.** A set of four springs for a two-axle cart: ESTIMATED 20-30 denarii in materials (high-carbon iron). Labour: ESTIMATED 40-60 personal hours for a skilled blacksmith, spread over multiple heating cycles. Calendar time: 2-3 weeks accounting for cooling. Capital: a large forge capable of handling 1-metre-long stock.

**Danger.** Quenching can cause steam explosions if water is not applied carefully. Tempering requires close attention to colour; overheating can ignite wood nearby. The springs, once under load, store enormous elastic energy and can snap without warning, becoming a projectile.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Leaf springs have been in continuous use since the 18th century and the physics is textbook. The main question is whether Roman metallurgy can achieve the necessary steel quality, and the answer is yes for small quantities, though the bloomery process is labour-intensive. Iron tyres (already in use) make this worthwhile.

### macadam_road - Macadam Road Surface

**What it is / why you want it.** A road built of small crushed stone compacted in layers, named for John McAdam (19th century) but practised in various forms long before. It drains water naturally, resists deformation under load, and can be repaired incrementally without rebuilding the entire surface.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive road surface is a hard, smooth monolith like Roman concrete or stone. Macadam works by allowing slight movement and drainage within a carefully graded structure. It is, counterintuitively, more resilient because it is slightly flexible.

**Prerequisites.** Understanding of drainage and geometry. Quarrying and stone-breaking capability. Existing Roman road infrastructure to improve.

**Roman-available inputs.** Crushed limestone or granite, sorted by size. Tallow or pitch as a light binder (optional but improves lifespan). Sand. Gravel.

**Procedure.** (1) Excavate a roadbed 40-60 cm deep and slope it slightly to shed water (1 in 50 is sufficient). (2) Lay a base course of large stones (fist-sized or larger) to a depth of 15 cm, slightly compacted. (3) Lay a middle course of medium stones (walnut-sized) to 15 cm, compacted more firmly by rolling a stone roller or driving laden carts over it repeatedly. (4) Top course: small stones (pea-sized) to 10-15 cm depth, also rolled. (5) Optional: spray thin tallow or pitch on the top surface and re-roll; this binds particles and reduces dust. (6) Compact everything by repeated rolling and traffic.

**How you know it worked.** A road in good condition should shed water within minutes of rain and show no puddles after half an hour. Press a stick into the surface; it should not penetrate more than 2-3 cm with moderate force. A loaded cart should leave no rut deeper than 1 cm.

**Failure modes.** Insufficient base layer causes the road to become muddy in wet weather. Stones that are too large in the top course create a rough ride and cause carts to bounce. Insufficient drainage leads to saturation and loss of bearing strength. Over-rolling compacts the surface so hard that water cannot drain, causing ponds to form.

**Cost & labour.** ESTIMATED cost per kilometre: 200-400 denarii in materials plus 5-10 days of skilled labour (road surveyor) and 20-50 unskilled labour-days for excavation and stone-breaking. Maintenance: 1-2 days per 10 km per year for raking and re-rolling. Capital: a large stone roller (concrete-filled drum on axle, about 500 kg) costs roughly 100 denarii to build.

**Danger.** Stone-breaking is hazardous; flying fragments can blind. Rolled stones can crush feet. The road, being permeable, permits contamination and does not shed concentrated sewage.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Crushed stone roads were built in Scotland in the 1780s-1790s and are well-documented. Roman stone-working and road engineering are more than adequate to the task. The main limitation is labour.

### wheelbarrow - Wheelbarrow

**What it is / why you want it.** A hand cart with a single front wheel and two handles, allowing one person to carry 30-50 kg at speeds faster than carrying. The wheel supports the weight; the handles guide and provide stability.

**Why you would never guess this.** It seems obvious once it exists, but the distribution of weight (most resting on the wheel, not evenly on all three supports) is geometrically non-obvious. Many cultures invented the wheelbarrow only after wheels themselves were routine.

**Prerequisites.** Wheel construction, iron working for the axle and hub, carpentry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood (ash or elm for the body; oak for the wheel rim). Iron rod for the axle. A completed wheel, 60-80 cm diameter.

**Procedure.** (1) Construct the barrow body from a single long piece of wood, curved up at both ends, roughly 100 cm long and 50 cm wide. Shape it slightly like a trough to prevent loads from sliding. (2) Install iron axle through holes near the front, 10-15 cm below the rim of the body. (3) Attach two wooden handles at the rear, extending to chest height when the barrow is tipped back on the wheel. (4) Test: a load of 40 kg should be easily lifted by pushing the handles down.

**How you know it worked.** A loaded wheelbarrow should balance on the wheel when the handles are lifted; this means the load is correctly distributed. A single person should be able to wheel 40 kg of sand 100 metres at a comfortable pace without excessive strain.

**Failure modes.** If the axle is mounted too far forward, the barrow is difficult to push and tips easily. Too far back, it becomes difficult to lift. The wooden body will split if not made from properly cured wood or if the wood is too thin. The wheel will slip on the axle if it is not firmly keyed.

**Cost & labour.** Materials: ESTIMATED 15-25 denarii (wood, iron rod, wheel). Labour: ESTIMATED 10-15 personal hours for a carpenter. A good wheelbarrow should last 3-5 years under normal use.

**Danger.** Minimal. Over-loading can cause the operator to tip backward.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The wheelbarrow is attested in Roman archaeology and medieval sources. It is a simple machine in the Archimedean sense.

### flanged_wheel - Flanged Wheel

**What it is / why you want it.** A wheel with an inward-pointing rim or lip (the flange) on one or both sides, allowing it to run on a rail or track without slipping sideways. This is the founding technology of railways.

**Why you would never guess this.** The obvious way to keep a wheel on a track is to put the track lip on the rail or road surface. This fails catastrophically: dirt accumulates on the lip, the lip becomes vulnerable to collision and bends, and it fouls with switch points. Putting the flange on the wheel itself means the wheel is always free to climb the lip only when the operator chooses, not from accidental misalignment.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working, wheel construction, understanding of geometry and stress.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast iron or wrought iron for the wheel rim. High-carbon steel for the flange.

**Procedure.** (1) Begin with a standard spoked wheel (oak or ash hub, iron-tyred rim). (2) Forge or cast an iron ring 4-6 cm tall, with an inward-pointing lip 2-3 cm deep. (3) Heat-shrink this ring onto the wheel rim so it is permanently fixed, or weld it in place if your blacksmith has the skill. (4) The lip should point inward and downward at roughly 30 degrees to the vertical, so it nestles onto a rail without binding.

**How you know it worked.** Mount the wheel on a rail of wrought iron or hardened wood, raised about 40 cm from the ground. A load applied to the axle should roll smoothly along the rail without binding or slipping sideways, even when the rail is wet.

**Failure modes.** If the flange is too tall or steep, the wheel binds on the rail and will not roll freely. If the flange points outward instead of inward, the wheel will fly off the track. If the flange is too thin, it will bend under load or shatter from impact.

**Cost & labour.** A flanged wheel 60-90 cm diameter: ESTIMATED 30-50 denarii in iron and casting. Labour: ESTIMATED 20-30 hours for an experienced iron-worker. A set of four flanged wheels for a small cart: 150-200 denarii.

**Danger.** Casting or forging iron on this scale requires a large forge and careful temperature control. Burned hands and eye damage from glare are common.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Flanged wheels are attested from the 16th century onward and are foundational to railways. The metallurgy is within Roman capability, though labour-intensive.

### permanent_way - Permanent Way (Railway Track)

**What it is / why you want it.** A system of parallel rails laid on rigid supports (sleepers or stone blocks), providing a fixed path for flanged wheels. It eliminates road damage, permits much heavier loads, and reduces friction dramatically compared to a cart on dirt.

**Why you would never guess this.** It seems to require advance planning and enormous capital. In fact, a primitive permanent way can be built incrementally using cast-off materials, and the savings in friction and animal labour pay back the investment in 2-3 years on a well-used route.

**Prerequisites.** Flanged wheels, iron working (casting or forging), surveying, carpentry, stone masonry.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast iron (if available from a larger foundry) or wrought iron. Timber (oak or pine for sleepers). Stone or concrete for the base. Level ground or graded earth.

**Procedure.** (1) Survey a level or gently sloped route for the railway. (2) Prepare a bed of compacted stone 30-40 cm thick, sloped to drain water. (3) Lay sleepers (wooden ties) across the bed at 1-metre intervals. (4) Forge or cast two parallel iron rails, I-shaped in cross-section, 2-3 cm tall. (5) Fasten the rails to the sleepers using iron spikes or straps, keeping them exactly parallel and level. (6) Test with a flanged wheel, adding shims if necessary.

**How you know it worked.** A single horse or two oxen should be able to pull a loaded cart (500 kg or more) along the rails with noticeably less effort than on a normal road. A cart that takes two oxen and extreme effort on a dirt road should move easily with one ox on rails.

**Failure modes.** If the rails are not parallel, the flanged wheel will climb one rail and tip over. If the sleepers are rotten or undersized, the rails will sag. If the base is not properly drained, frost heave will buckle the rails. Rust on iron rails increases friction and makes the wheels slip.

**Cost & labour.** A kilometre of permanent way: ESTIMATED 1000-2000 denarii in materials (rail steel or iron, timber, stone). Labour: ESTIMATED 200-400 person-days for a skilled crew of surveyors, metalworkers, and labourers. Capital: a rail-rolling operation requires a heavy hammer and a large forge. Calendar time: 2-3 months per kilometre.

**Danger.** Heavy objects can crush limbs. Iron railwork produces sharp edges. Transporting rails requires careful coordination.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Cast iron railways were built at scale in the 18th century. Roman metallurgy can produce the rails, but high-quality cast iron (as opposed to wrought iron) requires a blast furnace, which Rome does not have. Wrought iron railways work but are labour-intensive and heavier.

### railway_signalling - Railway Signalling

**What it is / why you want it.** A system of signals and rules that prevents two trains from occupying the same track section simultaneously. This is the fundamental safety device of railways.

**Why you would never guess this.** Railway crashes seem like a mechanical problem: drivers not paying attention, brakes failing, etc. In fact, with properly designed signals and rules, crashes become nearly impossible even if individual drivers make errors. Signalling is an information and communication problem, not a mechanical one. The key invention is the *block system*, which divides the track into sections and forbids any train from entering a section that is occupied.

**Prerequisites.** Permanent way, understanding of position and communication, reliable time-keeping.

**Roman-available inputs.** Semaphore arms (wooden poles with movable arms) for visual signals. Bells or gongs for audible signals. Iron or stone for signal posts. Paper and ink for timetables.

**Procedure.** (1) Divide the railway into blocks of 2-3 km each. (2) At the entrance to each block, mount a signal post with a movable arm or flag. (3) Paint or mark the arm clearly: "open" (pointing up at 45 degrees) means the block is clear and the train may enter; "stop" (pointing horizontally) means the block is occupied or a preceding train is slow. (4) Assign a signalman to each block. (5) When a train passes, the signalman sets the signal to "stop" until the train has completely left the block. (6) Only then does the signalman set the signal to "open" for the next train. (7) Trains travel to a strict timetable; the signalman's rule is to verify that no train has entered the block.

**How you know it worked.** Run two trains on the same track 30 minutes apart, strictly to timetable. Neither train should encounter the other or be forced to brake hard. If a train breaks down, the signalman should spot this immediately (the train does not exit the block in the expected time) and set the signal to "stop" to prevent a second train from entering.

**Failure modes.** Signalmen sleeping, drunk, or distracted can fail to set signals. Clocks that lose time cause schedule confusion. Ambiguous signals can be misread. Fog or darkness can obscure visual signals.

**Cost & labour.** A signal post: ESTIMATED 10-20 denarii in wood and iron. Staffing: one signalman per block per shift (or 3-4 per block with shift rotation), at roughly 20-30 denarii per month. Capital: a central office to coordinate timetables.

**Danger.** Minimal if signals are obeyed. Catastrophic if signals are ignored.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Railway signalling is attested from the 1830s onward and is mature technology. The principles are simple and do not require advanced materials.

### safety_bicycle - Safety Bicycle

**What it is / why you want it.** A pedal-driven two-wheeled vehicle where both wheels are nearly equal in size, the pedals drive the rear wheel via a chain, and the rider sits centred between the wheels. It is much faster and far safer than a "penny-farthing" bicycle where the front wheel is enormous.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive design for a pedal cycle is to make the wheel huge (where the pedal cranks attach directly to the wheel axle), giving one pedal revolution per wheel revolution, and thus one pedal revolution moves the bike a long distance. Making both wheels small and using a chain to reduce the pedal speed to the wheel speed seems wasteful. But the chain drive decouples pedalling speed from wheel speed, allowing a comfortable pedalling rate (50-80 rpm) regardless of the desired ground speed.

**Prerequisites.** Wheel construction, metalworking (chain forging or casting), bearings, understanding of ratios.

**Roman-available inputs.** Steel or iron for chain links (must be forged individually or cast in pairs). Wood or bentwood for the frame. Rubber for pneumatic tyres OR leather/iron for solid tyres. Ball bearings (see separate entry).

**Procedure.** (1) Construct two wheels, 60-70 cm diameter, with spoked rims and pneumatic or solid tyres. (2) Build a frame from bent steel tubing, joining at the rear axle and the steering head. (3) Mount the two wheels on the frame with ball bearings to reduce friction. (4) Forge or cast a chain of linked iron rings, each 15-20 mm diameter, connected by pins. This is labour-intensive; each link is forged separately. (5) Mount a toothed sprocket (8-12 teeth) on the rear wheel axle and a larger sprocket (20-30 teeth) on the pedal crank. (6) Thread the chain from the pedal crank to the rear wheel. The chain reduces the rotation rate, so one pedal revolution turns the rear wheel 1/3 to 1/2 revolution. (7) Mount the pedal cranks on the frame with a 17-20 cm radius (comfortable for the human body).

**How you know it worked.** The rider should be able to pedal at 60 rpm and move forward at 15-20 km/h, depending on gear ratio. Standing on the pedals (weight-bearing) should not cause the chain to slip or the frame to flex unduly.

**Failure modes.** A chain that is too tight will require excessive pedalling force. Too loose, it slips and jumps. Worn pins cause the chain to lengthen and slip. Rust on the chain increases friction dramatically and causes it to break. The frame will flex and crack if the tubing is too thin or weak.

**Cost & labour.** A complete safety bicycle: ESTIMATED 200-400 denarii in materials. Chain forging: ESTIMATED 30-50 labour-hours (very tedious). Frame building: ESTIMATED 20-30 labour-hours for a skilled craftsman. Total build time: 2-3 weeks.

**Danger.** Pedal striking the ground while turning can throw the rider. A chain that breaks suddenly can leave the rider coasting without braking. High speeds (over 25 km/h) increase injury risk from falls.

**Confidence: HIGH** - The safety bicycle was perfected in the 1880s and is well-documented. The critical innovations (chain drive and equal wheels) are mechanical and forging-within-Roman capability, though the chain is exceptionally labour-intensive. Pneumatic tyres are not available (see pneumatic_tyre entry).

## Precision and Rotation

### ball_bearing - Ball Bearing

**What it is / why you want it.** A bearing consisting of steel balls held in a cage and rolling between an inner and outer race, reducing friction in rotating axles to a fraction of the friction from sliding (bronze or iron on iron). A wheel on a ball bearing spins freely and quietly for minutes after being spun by hand.

**Why you would never guess this.** Bronze bushings (sleeves) were the standard Roman bearing, and they work reasonably well. The idea that tiny balls could somehow reduce friction more than a solid bearing surface seems backwards; in fact, rolling friction is far lower than sliding friction, and the ball bearing delivers nearly frictionless rotation.

**Prerequisites.** Steel working (hardening and tempering), precision grinding or lapping, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** High-carbon steel, hardened. Soft brass or bronze for the races (rings) if steel is difficult to machine.

**Procedure.** (1) Forge or machine an inner race, a steel ring roughly 5 cm diameter and 1-2 cm thick, slightly grooved on one face. (2) Forge or machine an outer race, slightly larger (6-7 cm diameter) with a matching groove. (3) Forge or machine steel balls, 8-12 mm diameter, to be as perfectly spherical as possible. (4) Harden and temper the balls and races to maximum hardness (bright cherry red, then cooled slowly in air). (5) Construct a cage of brass or soft iron, with holes spaced around a circle to hold the balls in position. (6) Assemble: place the balls in the cage, insert into the outer race, press the inner race into the centre. The balls should sit in the grooves and roll without binding.

**How you know it worked.** Spin the bearing by hand; it should coast for 10-20 seconds before friction stops it. Apply a small weight to the inner race; it should lift and lower freely with minimal resistance. Compare to a bronze bushing of the same size: the ball bearing spins far more freely.

**Failure modes.** Balls that are not perfectly spherical will create vibration and premature wear. Races that are too tight will bind the balls. Too loose, the bearing will clatter and the balls will skid sideways instead of rolling. Surface corrosion or dirt embedded in the race will cause grinding and rapid failure. Overload causes the balls to flatten (plastic deformation) and the bearing fails.

**Cost & labour.** A single ball bearing: ESTIMATED 15-40 denarii depending on size and precision. Four bearings for a cart (one per wheel): 60-160 denarii. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours for a skilled craftsman to hand-forge and lap four bearings. Precision requires a lathe (not available to Romans without significant development).

**Danger.** Hardening produces brittle balls prone to shattering if struck. Quenching is hazardous.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Ball bearings as described here are technically sound and appear in European clockwork by the 1400s. However, Roman precision metalworking lacks the lathe technology to produce truly spherical balls; hand-forged approximations will work but with more friction than ideal. True ball bearings require precision grinding, which demands industrial machine tools.

### pneumatic_tyre - Pneumatic Tyre (Filled with Air)

**What it is / why you want it.** A tyre consisting of a rubber bladder filled with air, mounted inside a stronger rubber casing, providing cushioning against impacts and dramatically improving traction and ride quality compared to iron tyres.

**Why you would never guess this.** Iron tyres are the proven standard, and they work. A hollow tyre filled with air seems fragile and subject to puncture. In fact, air pressure allows far lower contact pressure with the ground, reducing rolling resistance and wear on both wheel and road. Pneumatic tyres reduce road noise, provide excellent traction on wet surfaces, and absorb bumps without jarring the cargo.

**Prerequisites.** Rubber (raw latex), vulcanisation (sulphur cross-linking), precision moulding, metal rim work.

**Roman-available inputs.** RUBBER IS NOT AVAILABLE. Hevea brasiliensis (rubber tree) is native to Brazil and was not known to the Roman world. Latex was not traded across the Atlantic until the 16th century AD. The only Roman-accessible substitutes are:
- Solid leather tyres (tanned leather wrapped around the rim, laced with hemp cord for reinforcement). Durable for 500-1000 km, slightly cushioned but prone to rot in wet weather. Cost: ESTIMATED 5-10 denarii per wheel.
- Hardened leather over iron reinforcement. Lifespan similar to leather alone.
- Iron tyres with built-up leather pads. Cost: ESTIMATED 10-15 denarii per wheel.

These substitutes provide perhaps 30-50% of the benefits of pneumatic tyres: somewhat reduced road noise and vibration, but none of the superior traction or rolling resistance improvements.

**Cost & labour if rubber were available.** Vulcanised rubber tyre: ESTIMATED 200-300 denarii per wheel (rubber is expensive and the process is industrial). Lifespan: 5000-10000 km before cracking or wear requires replacement.

**Danger.** Pneumatic tyres can rupture suddenly due to puncture or over-inflation, potentially causing a crash. Overloading increases pressure and accelerates failure. The tyre is vulnerable to flame and heat.

**Confidence: LOW** - Pneumatic tyres were invented in 1888 and depend entirely on vulcanised rubber, which cannot be obtained in the Roman world. The concept is sound, but it is useless without the raw material. **Rome is not getting pneumatic tyres.** Building aircraft and vehicles without pneumatic tyres is possible but involves accepting significantly higher rolling resistance, lower maximum speeds, and greater maintenance requirements. Solid leather and iron tyres are the realistic option, and they impose a hard speed limit of around 20-25 km/h for a loaded cart.

## Internal Combustion

### otto_cycle_engine - Otto Cycle Engine (Spark-Ignition Internal Combustion)

**What it is / why you want it.** An internal combustion engine using a four-stroke cycle (intake, compression, combustion, exhaust), ignited by an electric spark. It burns petrol or coal gas and produces power on-demand without a separate furnace. A small engine produces 1-5 hp, sufficient for vehicles, pumps, and light machinery.

**Why you would never guess this.** The instinct is to say "of course combustion produces power; a fireplace heats water and a boiler produces steam." The non-obvious insight is that compressing a fuel-air mixture before ignition produces far more power per unit of fuel than burning at atmospheric pressure. Compression doubles the peak temperature and pressure and thus the power output. Achieving this requires tight-fitting pistons and valve timing, which demand precision.

**Prerequisites.** High-precision metalworking, understanding of thermodynamics and valve timing, spark generation (magneto), high-octane fuel (petrol).

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or brass castings for the cylinder and block. High-quality steel for valves and pistons. Copper or bronze for the spark plug electrodes. PETROL IS NOT AVAILABLE in the Roman world; it is refined from crude petroleum and requires fractional distillation, an 18th-century technology. Coal gas (produced by heating coal in an oxygen-free furnace) is accessible to Rome and will run an otto-cycle engine, but coal production at scale requires modern mining techniques. Liquid fuels available in Rome: vegetable oils (olive, linseed), animal fats, and tar. These burn, but produce far more smoke and soot and less power than petrol or coal gas.

**Procedure** (assuming coal gas is available). (1) Forge or cast a cylinder of iron, bore to a smooth finish 100 mm diameter, 150 mm tall. (2) Forge or cast a piston of tight-fitting steel (99.8 mm diameter) with rings grooved into its head. (3) Fit the piston rod to a crankshaft. (4) Machine two exhaust and two intake valves (mushroom-shaped) with springs. (5) Mount spark plug electrodes (1 mm gap) near the cylinder top. (6) Construct a manifold to supply coal gas into the cylinder. (7) Connect the intake valve to the manifold via a simple lever operated by a cam on the crankshaft. (8) Connect the exhaust valve similarly. (9) Time the valves so that: at cylinder-bottom position, intake valve opens and a fuel-air mixture is drawn in (1/4 rotation); at mid-rotation, intake valve closes and the piston rises, compressing the gas (1/4 rotation); at the top, a spark ignites the gas, driving the piston down (1/4 rotation, combustion stroke); at the bottom, exhaust valve opens and burned gas is expelled as the piston rises again (1/4 rotation).

**How you know it worked.** Run the engine at 100-300 rpm (hand-crank starts it). The engine should run smoothly without excessive vibration. Exhaust should be hot. Power output: ESTIMATED 2-4 hp at 200 rpm, enough to turn a dynamo or drive a lathe.

**Failure modes.** Imperfect sealing of the piston rings allows compression and combustion gas to escape, reducing power. Carbon buildup on the piston and cylinder head reduces compression and can cause pre-ignition (explosion before the spark). Valve timing errors cause power loss or backfiring (explosion in the manifold). Spark plug fouling (carbon deposit on electrodes) prevents ignition.

**Cost & labour.** An otto-cycle engine: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials (precision castings and machining). Labour: ESTIMATED 200-300 hours for a skilled metalworker with access to precision tools (lathe, boring bar). Obtaining coal gas requires a separate furnace and continuous supply.

**Danger.** Explosions inside the cylinder generate enormous pressure and can rupture the casting. Poorly timed valves cause backfires, which can ignite the fuel supply manifold. Running out of fuel while the engine is hot can cause spontaneous ignition of fuel vapour.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - The otto cycle is well-understood physics, and engines of this type were running by 1876. The main limitation is that Rome does not have petrol; coal gas is accessible but requires continuous production. The second limitation is precision: roman metalworking can produce the rough castings, but achieving the tight piston seals and accurate valve timing requires machine tools (lathes) that Rome would need to develop.

### diesel_cycle - Diesel Cycle Engine (Compression-Ignition Internal Combustion)

**What it is / why you want it.** An internal combustion engine using a four-stroke cycle where fuel is injected directly into the cylinder after the air has been compressed to high temperature, causing spontaneous ignition without a spark. Diesel engines are more efficient than spark-ignition engines and burn heavier, cheaper fuels.

**Why you would never guess this.** Using combustion without a spark seems impossible; the fuel must somehow be ignited by heat alone. In fact, compressing air to high temperature (above 600 C for diesel fuel ignition) is straightforward; injecting the fuel at exactly the right moment so that it ignites as it mixes with the hot air requires precision but no exotic technology.

**Prerequisites.** All the prerequisites of the otto engine, plus a fuel injection pump, precision valve timing, very high compression ratio.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron or brass castings. High-quality steel. Copper or brass for fuel injectors. DIESEL (a fraction of petroleum that boils around 200-350 C) is not available; however, heavy vegetable oils and tar-derived oils can run a diesel engine, though with more carbon buildup.

**Procedure.** (1) Forge or cast a cylinder identical to otto-cycle engine, but with thicker walls to withstand higher pressure. (2) Achieve a compression ratio of 15-20:1 (otto engines are 5-8:1); this requires precision in the piston and cylinder bore. (3) Use a fuel injection pump (similar to a high-pressure water pump) to spray diesel fuel into the cylinder during the compression stroke. (4) Omit spark plugs; instead, rely on the heat of compression. (5) The cylinder heats the incoming air to 600+ C, so the injected fuel ignites spontaneously. (6) Valves operate as in otto engines, but the injector valve timing is critical.

**How you know it worked.** Run the engine at 200-400 rpm. Exhaust will be cooler than an otto engine (due to better efficiency). Power output: ESTIMATED 3-6 hp for a similar displacement engine. Fuel efficiency: roughly 40% better than an otto engine.

**Failure modes.** Injection timing errors cause knock (pre-detonation) and power loss. Fuel that is too light (volatile) may ignite too early in the compression stroke. Carbon buildup from heavy oils fouls the injector and clogs the combustion chamber. Extremely high compression stresses the piston rod and crankshaft; overstress causes cracking.

**Cost & labour.** A diesel engine: ESTIMATED 800-1500 denarii in materials (heavier castings, precision injector pump). Labour: ESTIMATED 300-400 hours. The fuel injection pump adds significant complexity.

**Danger.** Diesel engines run at very high pressure and temperature. Failure of the casting or piston rod can cause explosion. Instantaneous combustion (no spark delay) creates extremely high pressure spikes.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - The diesel cycle is thermodynamically sound and engines of this type were running by 1897. Roman metallurgy can produce the basic components, but the precision required (compression ratio of 15:1, accurate injection timing) demands machine tools. The main advantage (better efficiency) is real, but not as critical as for otto engines.

### carburettor - Carburettor

**What it is / why you want it.** A device that mixes liquid fuel (petrol or volatile oil) with air in the correct proportion (roughly 1:14 by weight) for combustion in an otto-cycle engine.

**Why you would never guess this.** Pouring liquid fuel into an engine does not work; the fuel must vaporize and mix with air in precise ratio. A carburettor evaporates the liquid by passing hot air over it (using engine heat) and draws in the right amount of fuel via a jet (a small hole whose size controls flow).

**Prerequisites.** Otto or diesel engine, understanding of air/fuel ratios, precision machine shop to bore the jets.

**Roman-available inputs.** Brass or bronze for the main body (corrosion-resistant in the presence of oils). Precision-drilled iron for the jets. Copper tubing. Leather washers for sealing.

**Procedure.** (1) Machine a brass body resembling a barrel, 50 mm diameter and 30 mm tall, with a bore through the centre. (2) Drill a fine hole (jet) 1-2 mm diameter on one side, slightly above the centre. (3) Connect a fuel reservoir above the carburettor; fuel flows by gravity into a small chamber. (4) A needle valve controlled by a float (cork or hollow brass) maintains a constant fuel level in the chamber. (5) As the engine runs and draws air through the carburettor, the moving air reduces pressure slightly (Bernoulli principle), drawing fuel from the jet into the air stream. (6) The size of the jet controls the fuel-to-air ratio. (7) Engine heat evaporates the fuel, creating a combustible mixture.

**How you know it worked.** Start the engine and run at part throttle. The engine should run smoothly without hesitation or stumbling. Increase throttle; the engine should accelerate without coughing. The exhaust should be light grey or colourless; black exhaust means the mixture is too rich (too much fuel).

**Failure modes.** A jet that is too large allows excess fuel; the engine runs black smoke and fouls plugs. Too small, and the engine starves and dies under load. Dirt or varnish buildup clogs the jet. Fuel of insufficient volatility will not evaporate and the engine will not start in cold weather.

**Cost & labour.** A carburettor: ESTIMATED 30-50 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 15-20 hours for a machinist. The precision jet requires a fine drill or file work.

**Danger.** Carburettors are prone to leaking, and fuel leaks near a hot engine can ignite. Overflow fuel can flood the engine compartment.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Carburettors were standard on petrol engines by 1900 and the principles are well-understood. Roman metalworking can produce them, though precision is essential.

### magneto_ignition - Magneto Ignition (Spark Generation by Magnet)

**What it is / why you want it.** A device that generates high-voltage electrical pulses using a rotating permanent magnet and a coil of copper wire, producing sparks to ignite fuel in an otto-cycle engine.

**Why you would never guess this.** Generating electricity without a battery (chemical cells, which are unreliable and short-lived) seems impossible. A rotating magnet, however, induces current in a nearby coil; if the magnet rotates past the coil repeatedly, the coil generates alternating current. A secondary coil with many more turns can step up this current to very high voltage, sufficient to jump a spark gap.

**Prerequisites.** Permanent magnets (lodestone or magnetized iron), copper wire, understanding of electromagnetic induction and transformer action, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Lodestone (magnetite ore, naturally magnetized) from Egypt or elsewhere. Soft iron (to amplify the field). Copper wire, hand-drawn. Copper foil for capacitor plates. Leather or ceramic for insulation.

**Procedure.** (1) Obtain a piece of lodestone (natural magnet) roughly 5 cm x 2 cm x 1 cm, and magnetize a bar of soft iron by stroking it repeatedly with the lodestone (this is called magnetic induction). (2) Machine the iron bar into a cylindrical rotor 5 cm diameter. (3) Wind a coil of thin copper wire (primary coil) around the rotor, roughly 100 turns. (4) Mount this rotor on a shaft connected to a camshaft or crankshaft (rotating 1-3 times per engine revolution). (5) Position a stationary core (soft iron, shaped like a horseshoe) around the rotor, with another coil (secondary coil, 1000-2000 turns of very thin wire) wound tightly. (6) As the rotor rotates, the magnet's field sweeps past the secondary coil, inducing a voltage. (7) Connect the secondary coil output to the spark plug.

**How you know it worked.** Rotate the shaft by hand at moderate speed (300 rpm). Observe the spark plug; a small spark should jump the gap with each rotation. The engine should ignite consistently and run without misfiring.

**Failure modes.** Poor magnetic contact between the rotor and the stationary core reduces induced voltage. A broken wire in the secondary coil causes no spark. Fouling of the spark plug (carbon or oil deposit) prevents spark from jumping the gap. Timing errors (spark occurring at wrong point in cycle) cause backfiring or power loss.

**Cost & labour.** A magneto: ESTIMATED 100-150 denarii in copper, iron, and lodestone. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-80 hours (the secondary coil with thousands of turns is very tedious to wind by hand). The spark plug (electrodes spaced 1 mm apart) requires precision metalworking.

**Danger.** The secondary coil can deliver 10000+ volts, which is dangerous to touch. High-voltage arcing can cause burns or ignite fuel vapour.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Magnetos were the standard ignition system on early petrol engines (pre-1920) and are well-documented. Roman metalworking can produce them, though hand-winding a secondary coil is labour-intensive.

### gearbox_differential - Gearbox and Differential

**What it is / why you want it.** A mechanical transmission that allows an engine to operate at a fixed rpm while the wheels turn at different speeds. The gearbox steps down engine speed to wheel speed; the differential allows the two rear wheels to turn at different rates when cornering.

**Why you would never guess this.** Direct coupling of an engine to wheels means the engine stalls if the load is too high (wheels turn too slowly). The solution is intermediate gearing: small gears on the engine shaft mesh with large gears on the wheel shafts, reducing wheel speed and increasing torque. The differential is a set of four gears that split the torque equally between the two wheels but allow them to rotate at different speeds.

**Prerequisites.** Iron working (forging and casting of precision gears), understanding of gear ratios, tolerances, lubrication.

**Roman-available inputs.** Cast iron for gear blanks. Steel for precision cutting and finishing. Lubricating oil (animal fat or heavy mineral oil such as bitumen).

**Procedure for gearbox.** (1) Machine precision gears: engine pinion (small gear) 5 cm diameter with 20 teeth; wheel gears 20 cm diameter with 80 teeth. (2) Fit these gears in a steel housing with precisely-aligned shafts. (3) Select gears for a 4:1 reduction ratio (one engine revolution turns the wheels 1/4 revolution). (4) Lubricate all bearings and gear meshes with heavy oil. (5) For a multi-speed gearbox, add intermediate gears that can be engaged or disengaged via a sliding clutch on the shaft.

**Procedure for differential.** (1) In the rear axle assembly, replace the simple rigid connection between the two rear wheels with four small gears (spider gears) in a rotating case. (2) These gears allow the two wheels to turn at slightly different speeds while still sharing the engine torque. (3) When driving straight, the gears all turn together at the same speed. When turning a corner, the gears slip slightly, allowing the outer wheel to turn faster than the inner wheel.

**How you know it worked.** Start the engine and engage the gearbox. The wheels should turn at about 1/4 the engine rpm (for a 4:1 ratio). Increasing engine speed should increase wheel speed smoothly. Steer into a tight circle; the outside wheels should turn faster than the inside wheels, and the vehicle should maintain forward motion without slipping.

**Failure modes.** Gears cut with imprecise tooth spacing will vibrate and wear rapidly. Over-torque causes gears to strip (teeth break off). Insufficient lubrication causes gears to overheat and seize. Misaligned gears will bind and stall the engine.

**Cost & labour.** A complete gearbox and differential: ESTIMATED 300-500 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 100-150 hours to machine gears precisely. Precision gear-cutting requires a lathe and dividing head.

**Danger.** Running machinery with exposed gears can catch and crush fingers. Overheated lubricating oil can ignite.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Gear trains and differentials are well-understood mechanics. Roman metalworking can produce them, though precision is essential. The main limitation is the lathe; without a machine tool, gear-cutting by hand is extremely tedious and imprecise.

### assembly_line - Assembly Line Production

**What it is / why you want it.** A manufacturing process where a product (such as a motorcar) is assembled in stages by multiple workers, each performing a simple repetitive task as the product moves past their station. This reduces the time per unit from days to hours.

**Why you would never guess this.** The intuitive method is for each worker to build a complete vehicle from start to finish (or a small number of them). Assembly line production seems inefficient: how can breaking a job into tiny pieces and doing it in parallel be better than having skilled workers build complete units? In fact, specialization and parallel work reduce fatigue, improve speed, and eliminate rework because each station's output is checked immediately by the next station.

**Prerequisites.** Multiple identical products to build (economies of scale), standardized parts (all wheels are identical, all engines are identical), organization and labour discipline.

**Roman-available inputs.** Workshops with multiple stations. Carts or a continuously-moving platform (rope-driven, or human-powered) to carry the work between stations.

**Procedure.** (1) Analyse the complete build process and divide it into 10-20 stages, each taking 5-10 minutes. Example stages: frame assembly, axle and wheel installation, engine mounting, fuel system assembly, electrical wiring, painting. (2) Arrange workstations in sequence along a line. (3) Provide each station with the required tools and parts. (4) Move the product from station to station, either by hand (for lightweight products) or on a powered conveyor (rope and pulley, driven by a water wheel or animal power). (5) Train each worker to perform only their assigned task. (6) Measure the cycle time (time to move one product one station); the line rate is limited by the slowest station. Rebalance if a bottleneck appears.

**How you know it worked.** Measure the time to build one complete vehicle with traditional (one worker per vehicle) method: roughly 15-20 days per vehicle. With a 20-station assembly line, the cycle time per station should be 2-3 hours, so one vehicle exits every 2-3 hours; the same worker produces roughly 2-3 vehicles per 20-day period. Throughput increases by 5-10 fold for the same total labour.

**Failure modes.** If workers are trained poorly, they make errors. If stations are imbalanced (one station takes much longer than others), the line slows to the bottleneck. Supply chain disruptions (missing parts) cause the line to stall. Worker fatigue on repetitive tasks causes errors and slowdowns.

**Cost & labour.** For a 20-station assembly line producing motorcars: ESTIMATED 20-30 workers total, producing 2-3 complete vehicles per day. Capital cost for workspace, stations, and conveyor: ESTIMATED 1000-2000 denarii. Labour cost: 20-30 denarii per day (200-300 denarii per month).

**Danger.** Moving machinery (conveyors, rotating shafts) can catch clothing and hair. Workers standing in the same position for hours can develop repetitive-strain injuries.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Assembly line production was systematized by Henry Ford in 1913. The concept is straightforward and does not require advanced technology, only organization and discipline. Roman workshops can implement it.

### sternpost_rudder - Sternpost Rudder

**What it is / why you want it.** A flat blade of wood or leather fastened vertically to the rear post of a ship's hull, pivoting on hinges, allowing the helmsman to steer by changing the water pressure on the blade. This replaces the ancient side rudders (oars) mounted on the quarter (side) of the ship.

**Why you would never guess this.** Side rudders work reasonably well for small, light vessels and were the standard for millennia. A sternpost rudder seems like it would interfere with the ship's structure. In fact, a rudder mounted on the stern (rear) is far more efficient because the stern moves faster through the water (due to the ship's length creating a lever) and because the rudder is behind the centre of lateral resistance, making steering more stable.

**Prerequisites.** Carpentry, hinges (iron or leather straps), knowledge of hydrodynamics (informal).

**Roman-available inputs.** Oak or pine timber for the blade (roughly 2-3 metres tall, 1 metre wide, 5-10 cm thick). Iron straps and bolts for hinges. Iron rings for attachment to the sternpost. Leather strap for the tiller (the handle).

**Procedure.** (1) Shape a wooden blade 2-3 metres tall and 1 metre wide from a single piece of oak, planed smooth. (2) Fasten iron straps near the top of the blade, creating hinges that pin to the sternpost (the rear-most timber of the hull, running vertically). (3) Install iron rings or loops on the sternpost at the pivot points. (4) Attach a long wooden lever (tiller) to the top of the rudder blade (or to a crosspiece attached to the blade), extending horizontally into the ship. (5) Push the tiller to the right to steer right; the rudder blade swings left, pushing the stern left and the bow right. (6) Balance the rudder so it is neither too stiff nor too light to move.

**How you know it worked.** Sail the ship at cruising speed. Moving the tiller should cause immediate, smooth course changes without excessive force. The ship should hold a straight course with minimal tiller adjustment.

**Failure modes.** A rudder that is too small is ineffective. Too large, it creates excessive drag and requires excessive force to move. Loose hinges cause the rudder to flap noisily and lose effectiveness. Rot in the wooden blade (from salt water) weakens it and can cause failure in heavy seas.

**Cost & labour.** A sternpost rudder for a large merchant ship: ESTIMATED 100-200 denarii in timber and iron. Labour: ESTIMATED 30-50 hours for a carpenter and blacksmith. Installation is straightforward on a ship under construction or during maintenance.

**Danger.** Loose rudder parts can separate and leave the ship without steerage. Installation at sea is dangerous and requires climbing.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Sternpost rudders appear in Chinese ships by the 11th century and were widely adopted in European ships by the 14th-15th centuries. The engineering is simple. Roman ships are large and capable of accommodating a sternpost rudder. In fact, a few Roman ships appear to have had sternpost rudders, though side rudders were standard.

### lateen_rig - Lateen Rig (Triangular Sail)

**What it is / why you want it.** A sail rigged as a triangle, suspended from a diagonal spar (the lateen yard) rather than the traditional square sail. It allows sailing much closer to the wind direction, changing the wind-dependent routes and greatly expanding navigability.

**Why you would never guess this.** A square sail seems more efficient at first glance: it captures more wind area and is simpler to rig. The lateen's advantage is aerodynamic: the triangular shape creates lift (like an aerofoil) rather than just pushing the ship downwind, allowing the ship to sail at a more acute angle to the wind. A square sail can sail perhaps 60-75 degrees from the downwind direction (not very close); a lateen rig can manage 35-40 degrees from the downwind direction (much closer to the wind).

**Prerequisites.** Sailmaking, rigging, understanding of wind and hydrodynamics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen cloth (same as current sails). Ropes of hemp or flax (same as current rigging). The lateen yard is a long wooden spar, slightly curved, made from a single tree or spliced from multiple pieces.

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a triangular sail from linen, with the leading edge (perpendicular to the spar) roughly 12 metres, the trailing edge roughly 8 metres (creating the aerodynamic shape), and the height roughly 10 metres. (2) Attach the leading edge to a wooden spar (lateen yard), roughly 15-18 metres long. (3) The spar hangs from a line attached to the mast, roughly 1/3 of the way down the spar's length, creating a balance point. (4) Guy ropes (lines to the deck) control the angle and tension of the sail. (5) When sailing, the forward end of the spar is angled up 45 degrees, and the sail is trimmed to create a smooth aerodynamic surface from the leading edge.

**How you know it worked.** Sail the ship on a course that is close to the wind (40-45 degrees from the wind direction). A square sail would be backed and ineffective; the lateen should fill with wind and pull the ship forward at roughly 50-75% of the speed achieved sailing downwind.

**Failure modes.** The lateen yard is heavy and difficult to manoeuvre in high winds. The sail is prone to ripping if the wind suddenly shifts (an accidental jibe). The leading edge of the sail must be kept smooth and aerodynamic; wrinkles or folds destroy the lift. The yard can snap if the sail catches a gust in the wrong way.

**Cost & labour.** Sails and rigging for a ship with lateen rig: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials (more complex than square sails). Labour: ESTIMATED 100-150 hours for sailmakers and riggers.

**Danger.** The heavy lateen yard can fall and injure or kill a sailor. Manoeuvers involving the lateen rig (tacking, jibbing) require coordination and must be done in proper sequence or the ship is at risk of losing control.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Lateen rigs are well-attested from the Mediterranean in the medieval period (Arab dhows, European ships) and are aerodynamically sound. The principle is simple and the engineering is within Roman capability. Roman ships used square sails; introducing lateen rigs would require retraining sailors and modifying ships, but no new technologies.

### magnetic_compass - Magnetic Compass

**What it is / why you want it.** A floating magnetized iron needle suspended in a bowl of water, aligned by Earth's magnetic field to point toward the magnetic north pole. It provides a constant reference direction at sea, independent of sun or stars.

**Why you would never guess this.** Humans have navigated by sun and stars for millennia; Earth's magnetism is invisible and unmeasurable except by this one phenomenon. The idea that iron can be mysteriously aligned to a direction seems like sorcery. In fact, iron becomes magnetized by induction if aligned with Earth's field for an extended time (or if magnetized by stroking with a lodestone), and the magnetized iron then acts as a compass.

**Prerequisites.** Magnetized iron, understanding that iron can be aligned by Earth's field, water-tight bowl.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron needle (thin, 5-10 cm long, sharpened at one end). Lodestone (naturally magnetized magnetite ore) to magnetize it. Cork or wood for a float. Bowl made of glass, ceramic, or wood with a glass or mica window. Water (fresh or salt).

**Procedure.** (1) Take a steel needle and magnetize it by stroking repeatedly with a lodestone, always in the same direction, 50-100 times. (2) Float the needle on a piece of cork in a bowl of water. (3) Observe that the needle aligns with a constant direction (roughly north-south). (4) Mark this direction on the bowl: North, South, and the cardinal and intercardinal directions (East, West, Northeast, etc.) scratched or painted on the bowl rim. (5) Install a glass or mica window on top of the bowl to protect the needle from wind and spray. (6) Attach a gimbal (a system of rings) so the compass can swing freely without the bowl tilting.

**How you know it worked.** Take the compass to a location where the true north direction is known (using sun, stars, or landmarks) and verify that the needle points north. Move the compass in a circle; the needle should maintain its orientation. Bring a large piece of iron near the compass; the needle should deflect (demonstrating that it is magnetic). Remove the iron; the needle should return to north.

**Failure modes.** If the needle is not sufficiently magnetized, it will point weakly and be deflected by any nearby iron. If the needle is struck sharply, it can lose magnetization. Salt water can corrode the needle and destroy the magnetization. The needle can become stuck and not swing freely if the pivot is rough.

**Cost & labour.** A simple compass: ESTIMATED 5-10 denarii (bowl, cork, needle, window). Labour: ESTIMATED 3-5 hours for a craftsman. A ship's compass can be produced for ESTIMATED 20-30 denarii and would be valuable enough to carry on important voyages.

**Danger.** Minimal. The compass needle is not hazardous unless the bowl is broken and the needle becomes a splinter.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Magnetic compasses are attested in Chinese ships by the 11th century and in European ships by the 12th century. The principle is sound and straightforward. Roman metalworking can produce magnetized needles. The magnetic compass requires no new materials, only understanding that Earth's field can magnetize iron.

### marine_chronometer - Marine Chronometer

**What it is / why you want it.** A mechanical clock that maintains accurate time at sea, permitting a navigator to determine the ship's longitude by comparing local noon (the highest sun altitude) with the time at a known reference meridian (such as the home port).

**Why you would never guess this.** Determining longitude seems like an astronomical problem: measuring the positions of sun, moon, and stars and computing the ship's position. In fact, it is a *timekeeping* problem. If the navigator knows the time at a reference meridian (such as home) and compares it to local noon, the difference in time directly translates to longitude: one hour of time difference equals roughly 15 degrees of longitude. The hard part is keeping a clock running accurately on a pitching ship, with temperature changes, salt spray, and vibration all conspiring to degrade timekeeping.

**Prerequisites.** Advanced clockmaking, understanding of longitude, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Brass and steel for gears and springs. A pendulum (for large stationary clocks) or a balance wheel (for portable clocks). Copper or brass cases to protect the mechanism. A glass face to read time.

**Procedure** (simplified, for a balance-wheel chronometer). (1) Construct a precision mechanical clock with a balance wheel (an oscillating wheel) that beats at a constant frequency, regulated by a spring. (2) The balance wheel oscillates roughly 2-3 times per second; gears count these oscillations and translate them into hours, minutes, and seconds. (3) The clock must be enclosed in a brass case for protection. (4) Mount the clock in a wooden frame with low friction pivots so it can swing freely as the ship pitches and rolls (reducing the effect of ship motion on timekeeping). (5) Include an escapement mechanism (a lever that allows the gears to advance one tick per oscillation) to maintain constant rate. (6) Adjust the spring tension so the clock loses less than 5 seconds per day (ESTIMATED MEASURED for high-quality 18th-century chronometers; Roman clockmakers would struggle to achieve this).

**How you know it worked.** Set the chronometer to a known time and run it for a week, recording the time daily. Calculate the rate of loss or gain (seconds per day). A good chronometer should lose less than 10 seconds per day. On a ship, use the chronometer to determine longitude: at noon (when the sun is highest), observe the sun's altitude and note the chronometer time. Compare to a table of sun altitude versus time at a reference meridian; the difference gives the longitude to within roughly 1 degree (ESTIMATED, achievable with a 18th-century chronometer; Roman chronometers would be less precise).

**Failure modes.** A clock that runs too fast or slow introduces errors in longitude calculation. Each second of error in the chronometer translates to 500 metres of error in latitude (at the equator). Temperature changes cause the metal in the clock to expand and contract, changing the rate. Salt spray can corrode and jam the delicate gears. Vibration from the ship can shock the balance wheel and cause loss of accuracy.

**Cost & labour.** A marine chronometer: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in materials (precision gears, springs, case). Labour: ESTIMATED 300-500 hours for an expert clockmaker. The precision required (gear teeth must be cut accurately to fractions of a millimetre) demands machine tools. A ship that carries a chronometer is among the most advanced of its time.

**Danger.** Minimal. The chronometer is a delicate instrument and must be handled carefully.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Marine chronometers were perfected by John Harrison in the 1700s and are well-documented. The principle is sound. Roman clockmaking was not advanced; the largest Roman clocks (water clocks, or clepsydras) were stationary and not particularly accurate. Building a portable, accurate chronometer for a ship requires precision metalworking and clockmaking far beyond Roman practice. A Roman chronometer would be possible in principle but would require a major technological effort and would likely be less accurate than desired.

### screw_propeller - Screw Propeller (Submarine Propeller)

**What it is / why you want it.** A rotating propeller consisting of blades arranged around a central hub, submerged in water, that drives a ship by pushing water backward. It replaces paddle wheels (oars or rotating paddle boards) and is far superior in efficiency, stability, and reliability under heavy load.

**Why you would never guess this.** Paddle wheels are the obvious choice for a steam-powered ship: they are visible, intuitive, and work. The screw propeller seems like it would be slow and inefficient; it displaces less water per rotation. In fact, the screw's advantage is profound: (1) it remains submerged regardless of the ship's loading or motion, while paddle wheels lose effectiveness when the ship heaves or is heavily loaded; (2) it is immune to gunfire, whereas paddle wheels are vulnerable and their loss makes the ship uncontrollable; (3) it permits faster ship speeds and better control in rough water.

**Prerequisites.** Bronze or iron casting, precision metalworking, understanding of hydrodynamics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Bronze (superior for salt water) or iron for the hub and blades. Steel bolts and fasteners.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a propeller with 2-4 blades arranged around a central hub. Each blade is roughly an aerofoil (wing-like) in cross-section, not a simple flat paddle. (2) Cast the hub and blades as a single piece from bronze or iron. (3) The hub has a tapered hole for mounting on the ship's propeller shaft. (4) The pitch (angle) of the blades is typically 25-45 degrees from horizontal, creating a helical motion through the water. (5) Mount the propeller on the ship's shaft, behind the rudder if possible, so the rudder can operate in the propeller's wake. (6) The propeller rotates at 100-300 rpm (depending on the ship's speed and shaft gearing).

**How you know it worked.** With the propeller shaft running at 200 rpm, the ship should move forward smoothly and controllably. Increasing shaft speed should increase ship speed in a roughly linear fashion (doubled rpm roughly doubles speed, minus friction). The propeller should not cavitate (form bubbles) or vibrate excessively.

**Failure modes.** Blades that are too thin will flex excessively and lose efficiency or break. Blades that are too thick create excessive drag and require high power. Incorrect blade pitch causes poor efficiency at certain speeds. Fouling (seaweed, marine growth) on the blades reduces efficiency and causes vibration. Corrosion of bronze or iron blades degrades performance.

**Cost & labour.** A cast bronze propeller 1 metre diameter: ESTIMATED 200-400 denarii in bronze. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours including pattern-making, casting, and finishing. A ship requires one or two propellers (larger ships may have two, one per shaft).

**Danger.** A rotating propeller is extremely hazardous; contact causes severe injury or death. The propeller must be guarded or located underwater where no one can reach it accidentally.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Screw propellers were developed in the 1830s-1840s and are well-documented. The design is hydrodynamically sound. Roman metalworking can produce bronze propellers, though the pattern-making and casting requires skill. A Roman ship with a screw propeller would be a major achievement and would require steam engine development (not yet covered in this module).

### iron_hull - Iron Hull (Ship Built from Iron)

**What it is / why you want it.** A ship whose hull is constructed from riveted iron plates rather than wood, providing greater strength-to-weight ratio, longer lifespan, and immunity to shipworm and rot. An iron ship can be larger and faster than a wooden ship of the same material weight.

**Why you would never guess this.** Iron sinks in water, so an iron ship should sink. The counter-argument (which won out after considerable public debate) is that what matters is not the density of iron, but the average density of the ship including the air enclosed in it. An iron hull encloses air, and the average density of iron-plus-air is less than water, so the ship floats. Furthermore, iron's strength allows thinner plating, increasing the volume available for cargo and machinery.

**Prerequisites.** Iron smelting and rolling (to produce plates), riveting, precision metalworking, understanding of hydrostatics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wrought iron (from a bloomery or furnace), rolled into sheets 2-3 mm thick and roughly 1-2 metres wide. Iron rivets. Caulking material (tar-soaked rope or lead sheet) to seal seams.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a hull shape using the same principles as a wooden ship (underwater form matters). (2) Roll or forge iron into plates, sized to cover a section of the hull (roughly 2 metres x 1 metre per plate). (3) Arrange the plates in overlapping rows (strakes), similar to wooden planking. (4) Rivet each plate to its neighbor: (a) align two plate edges, (b) drill holes through both plates, (c) insert an iron rivet (a short bolt with a head), (d) support the rivet head on one side with a bucking bar (an iron anvil), (e) hammer the protruding end of the rivet until it swells and pulls the plates together, (f) repeat 10-20 cm along each seam. (5) Caulk the seams: drive tar-soaked rope into the gap between plates, or solder with molten lead (if available). (6) Paint the hull with anti-corrosion paint (tar or oil-based).

**How you know it worked.** Float the ship and load it with cargo. The hull should remain watertight and not leak significantly. The ship should have visible strength: no flexing or cracking of iron plates when the ship rocks in waves.

**Failure modes.** Poor riveting leaves gaps and the hull leaks. Rivets that are too tight cause the plates to buckle. Corrosion (rust) gradually weakens the hull and creates holes. Electrochemical corrosion (galvanic corrosion due to contact between different metals) can be severe, particularly near the waterline. Improperly caulked seams leak. If the hull strikes a rock or another ship, the iron can dent or crack, and rivets can shear.

**Cost & labour.** An iron-hulled ship 30 metres long, 8 metres wide: ESTIMATED 5000-10000 denarii in iron plate and rivets. Labour: ESTIMATED 5000-10000 person-hours for a shipyard team, spread over 3-6 months. Capital: a large shipyard with an iron-rolling furnace and riveting facilities.

**Danger.** Molten lead (if used for caulking) is extremely hazardous. Hammering rivets creates noise and vibration that can cause long-term hearing damage. Heavy work in confined spaces (inside the hull) is dangerous.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - Iron-hulled ships became common in the 1850s-1870s. The engineering is sound. Roman metalworking can produce iron plates and rivets, but the quality would be lower than industrial-era iron (wrought iron is variable in quality and contains slag). A Roman iron-hulled ship would be possible but would require a major industrial effort. The biggest limitation is obtaining sufficient quantities of high-quality wrought iron; a Roman bloomery produces limited quantities and the iron quality is inconsistent.

### compound_engine - Compound Engine (Marine Steam Engine)

**What it is / why you want it.** A steam engine that expands steam through two or more cylinders in sequence, each at a lower pressure and higher volume, extracting more work per unit of fuel than a simple (single-cylinder) steam engine.

**Why you would never guess this.** Once steam has done its work pushing a piston in one cylinder, the steam is still hot and contains much of its original energy. Venting it to the atmosphere seems wasteful, but extracting more work by expanding it further seems impossible. In fact, passing the exhaust from the first cylinder into a larger second cylinder, where the lower-pressure steam pushes a piston with a larger area, extracts much of this remaining energy.

**Prerequisites.** Steam engine design, high-pressure boiler technology, precision metalworking.

**Roman-available inputs.** Iron castings for cylinders and frame. Steel for pistons and rods. Copper or brass for fittings. Water for the boiler.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a compound engine with a high-pressure cylinder (roughly 30 cm diameter) and a low-pressure cylinder (roughly 60 cm diameter), connected so that the exhaust from the high-pressure cylinder feeds into the low-pressure cylinder. (2) Construct a powerful boiler that maintains steam pressure at 100-200 psi (roughly 7-14 atmospheres). (3) Superheating coils in the firebox heat the steam above the saturation temperature, reducing condensation and increasing efficiency. (4) A governor (a mechanical feedback device consisting of rotating weights) maintains constant speed by adjusting the throttle valve. (5) Connect both cylinders to a common crankshaft, phased so the pistons push alternately.

**How you know it worked.** Compare fuel consumption to a simple single-cylinder engine of similar power output. A compound engine should require roughly 30-50% less fuel for the same power output.

**Failure modes.** Poor steam superheating causes condensation and loss of efficiency. Unbalanced piston timing (wrong phasing) causes vibration. The governor can oscillate (hunting), causing power to surge and drop. Boiler pressure fluctuations caused by irregular fuel supply reduce efficiency.

**Cost & labour.** A compound marine engine producing 50 hp: ESTIMATED 2000-4000 denarii in iron and copper. Labour: ESTIMATED 1000-1500 hours for a specialized steam engine manufacturer.

**Danger.** High-pressure steam can cause severe scalding or explosion. Boiler failure can destroy the engine and damage the ship. Flywheel imbalance can cause the engine to shake violently.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Compound steam engines were standard in marine service by the 1870s. The thermodynamics and mechanics are sound. Roman metalworking can produce the castings and components, though the precision required for tight piston sealing demands machine tools.

## Flight

### kite - Kite

**What it is / why you want it.** A lightweight frame covered with cloth or paper, suspended by a string, that flies in the wind and can be made to climb, dive, or hover by adjusting the angle or tension on the string.

**Why you would never guess this.** A kite seems like a fragile toy; the idea that it could lead to powered flight is not obvious. In fact, kites demonstrate fundamental aerodynamic principles: a flat or curved surface at an angle to the wind generates an upward force (lift). Understanding kites is the first step to understanding wings.

**Prerequisites.** Framework construction, cloth-working, string (rope), empirical observation of wind and flight.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the frame (ash or birch for strength and lightness). Linen cloth or paper (if available) for the wing surface. Hemp rope for bridles and tether.

**Procedure.** (1) Build a simple kite: two sticks lashed in an X-shape, 1 metre tall and 1 metre wide. (2) Cover the frame with linen cloth, leaving the bottom open for air to pass through. (3) Attach two string bridles from the top corners to a single point 10-15 cm in front of the frame's centre. (4) Attach the main tether line to the bridle point. (5) Add a tail of cloth 2-3 metres long to stabilize the kite. (6) Test in steady wind 3-5 m/s; the kite should climb to a height limited by the string length.

**How you know it worked.** The kite should rise against the tether and maintain a stable altitude, nose up at roughly 30-45 degrees to the horizontal. Pulling sharply on the tether should cause the kite to climb; releasing should cause it to dive or glide.

**Failure modes.** A kite that is unstable (wobbling or spinning) requires a longer tail or a different bridle geometry. Too much weight causes the kite to drop. Too little material area (too small a wing) fails to generate lift in weak wind.

**Cost & labour.** A basic kite: ESTIMATED 1-2 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 2-3 hours to build.

**Danger.** Minimal if flown in an open area. Lightning can strike a kite in a thunderstorm.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Kites have been flown in China and other cultures for centuries. The physics is simple and the construction is elementary. Roman children would easily build kites if introduced to the concept.

### hydrogen_balloon - Hydrogen Balloon (Lighter-Than-Air Craft)

**What it is / why you want it.** A bag or envelope of airtight cloth filled with hydrogen gas (which is lighter than air), carrying a small basket or gondola suspended below. It rises to great heights and can carry observers or cargo aloft.

**Why you would never guess this.** Hydrogen is invisible, and the idea that an invisible gas could carry weight into the air seems absurd. In fact, hydrogen has a density of roughly 0.09 kg/m3, compared to air at 1.2 kg/m3 at sea level. A volume of hydrogen displaces its weight in air (by Archimedes' principle), and the difference in weight is a buoyancy force. A cubic metre of hydrogen provides roughly 1.1 kg of lift.

**Prerequisites.** Large quantities of hydrogen gas, airtight fabric (or rubber if available), basketwork for the gondola, understanding of buoyancy.

**Roman-available inputs.** Linen, tightly woven, coated with tallow or pitch to make it airtight, sewn into a large bag. Wicker or basketwork for the gondola. Rope for suspension lines. HYDROGEN: produced by passing steam over hot iron, or by dissolving zinc in dilute acid. Hydrogen production is labour-intensive and requires careful handling (highly flammable).

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a large bag from airtight linen, roughly 10 metres in diameter (roughly 500 m3 volume, providing 500+ kg of lift). This requires sewing many pieces together and sealing all seams with pitch or rubber. (2) Attach suspension lines (rope) from the bag's envelope to a wooden frame. (3) Suspend a wicker gondola 10-20 metres below the bag. (4) Produce hydrogen gas (see below) and fill the envelope through a filling valve. (5) Release from the ground; the balloon rises until the density of the surrounding air matches the average density of the balloon plus gondola.

**Hydrogen production procedure.** (1) Place iron filings or scrap iron in a vessel. (2) Add dilute acid (vinegar, or very dilute sulfuric acid if available). (3) The reaction produces hydrogen gas: Fe + 2H+ -> Fe2+ + H2. (4) Collect the gas over water (pass the gas through a tube into an inverted container filled with water; the gas bubbles up and displaces the water). (5) Dry the gas by passing it through a vessel of concentrated sulfuric acid or calcium chloride. (6) Transfer the gas to the balloon envelope via a flexible hose.

**How you know it worked.** The balloon should rise steadily and maintain altitude with the gondola weight. From altitude, observers should see a panoramic view of the landscape. Descend by releasing ballast (sand or water) or by venting hydrogen through a valve.

**Failure modes.** Hydrogen leaks from seams reduce lift gradually. A leak large enough to cause rapid descent requires emergency landing. Hydrogen is highly flammable; any flame or spark will ignite it and destroy the balloon. Wind at altitude can buffet the balloon and damage it. Landing in trees can puncture the envelope.

**Cost & labour.** A 500 m3 hydrogen balloon: ESTIMATED 500-1000 denarii in linen, pitch, and rope. Labour: ESTIMATED 200-300 hours to sew and seal the envelope. Hydrogen production (for a single fill): ESTIMATED 50-100 denarii in materials (zinc and acid) and 20-50 labour-hours.

**Danger.** Hydrogen is explosive. Filling the balloon near a flame is suicidal. Ascending to great altitude causes altitude sickness and hypoxia. The balloon is vulnerable to lightning. Landing can be uncontrolled and dangerous if the envelope is damaged.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Hydrogen balloons were flown by the Montgolfier brothers and others in 1783 onward. The physics is sound. Roman chemistry can produce hydrogen, though in small quantities. A Roman hydrogen balloon would be possible and would generate extraordinary interest, but it would not be practical for cargo or travel.

### aerofoil_camber - Aerofoil Camber (Wing Curvature for Lift)

**What it is / why you want it.** A wing profile that is curved (cambered) rather than flat, generating lift even at low angles of attack. A cambered wing provides far better lift-to-drag ratio than a flat plate at the same angle.

**Why you would never guess this.** A flat wing (or sail) at an angle to the wind generates some lift by pushing air downward. A curved wing seems like it would be more complicated without obvious benefits. In fact, the camber (upper surface curvature) accelerates air over the top of the wing, reducing pressure above and increasing lift by a factor of 2-3 compared to a flat plate at the same angle. This is not obvious from simple observation.

**Prerequisites.** Understanding of lift and drag, wind tunnel testing or empirical observation, wing design.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the frame, linen for the covering.

**Procedure.** (1) Design a wing profile: a chord line (the straight line from leading edge to trailing edge) remains roughly horizontal at cruising angle of attack. (2) The upper surface is curved (cambered) upward, creating an arc. (3) The lower surface can be flat or slightly curved downward. (4) The curvature is greatest near the leading edge, tapering toward the trailing edge. (5) A typical camber is roughly 1-2% of the chord length (a 1-metre-wide wing has a maximum height difference of 10-20 mm). (6) Construct the wing from a wooden frame covered with tight linen, shaped to this profile.

**How you know it worked.** Test the wing in a wind tunnel (see wind_tunnel entry) or by towing it through water or air. Measure the lift force perpendicular to the airflow. Compare to a flat plate of the same area and angle; the cambered wing should produce 50-100% more lift at the same angle of attack.

**Failure modes.** Excessive camber (more than 3% of chord) increases drag and can cause the wing to stall (lose lift suddenly) at shallow angles. Insufficient camber provides little benefit. A damaged wing that is wrinkled or deformed loses the aerodynamic advantage.

**Cost & labour.** A cambered wing is only slightly more difficult to construct than a flat wing. Labour: ESTIMATED 20-30% more time than a flat wing of the same area.

**Danger.** Minimal.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Cambered wings are standard in aeronautical engineering and are well-understood. The principle was discovered empirically through wind tunnel testing in the 1890s. Roman craftsmen could construct cambered wings if given the design.

### wind_tunnel - Wind Tunnel

**What it is / why you want it.** A chamber where air is blown across a stationary model at controlled speed, allowing measurement of lift, drag, and other aerodynamic forces without the complexity of building a full-scale aircraft.

**Why you would never guess this.** Wind tunnels seem like they would be crude and inaccurate compared to flight testing. In fact, a wind tunnel allows repeatable measurement with clean data, eliminates the uncontrolled variables of actual flight (turbulence, gusts, etc.), and allows rapid testing of design variations. Orville and Wilbur Wright's wind tunnel experiments were crucial to their success.

**Prerequisites.** Large fan (or water wheel driving a fan), ductwork, measurements of pressure and velocity, model building.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wooden frame for the tunnel. Large wooden fan (similar to a mill wheel) driven by an animal or water wheel. Manometer (a tube of water in a U-shape) to measure air pressure and thus velocity. String or thread for smoke flow visualization. Model aircraft or wings from wood and linen.

**Procedure.** (1) Construct a wind tunnel: a rectangular duct roughly 2 metres x 2 metres x 5 metres long. (2) Mount a large fan at one end (driven by a horse-powered shaft or water wheel); the fan pulls air through the tunnel. (3) Install a model aircraft or wing in the centre of the tunnel on a support stand. (4) Suspend the model from a balance arm or spring scale that measures vertical forces (lift). Measure horizontal forces with another balance perpendicular to the airflow. (5) Adjust fan speed to achieve desired airspeed (measured using a manometer: greater pressure difference indicates faster airflow). (6) Record lift and drag forces at various angles of attack (pitch of the model relative to the airflow).

**How you know it worked.** Run the wind tunnel at a known airspeed (say 10 m/s, equivalent to 36 km/h or a moderate breeze). Measure lift on a flat plate tilted at 10 degrees; record the force. Repeat with a cambered wing of the same area; the cambered wing should produce noticeably more lift (50-100% more, depending on camber).

**Failure modes.** Turbulent airflow in the tunnel (caused by rough walls, sharp corners, or obstacles) produces unrealistic data. The model support stand creates drag and can interfere with the flow. Temperature changes in the air (affecting density) require correction of measurements.

**Cost & labour.** A wood and canvas wind tunnel: ESTIMATED 200-500 denarii in materials. Labour: ESTIMATED 100-200 hours to build and install.

**Danger.** The tunnel entrance can develop high air velocity (100+ km/h) and can catch loose items or hair. The tunnel must be guarded to prevent accidents.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Wind tunnels were built and used by the Wrights and others in the 1890s. The principles are straightforward. Roman craftsmen and engineers could build a functional wind tunnel.

### three_axis_control - Three-Axis Control (Aircraft Control Surfaces)

**What it is / why you want it.** A system of three independently-controlled surfaces on an aircraft wing, allowing the pilot to control pitch (nose up/down), roll (wing up/down), and yaw (nose left/right) independently. This is the fundamental innovation that allowed the Wright brothers to fly controlled aircraft.

**Why you would never guess this.** Before the Wrights, aircraft designers focused on generating lift and power; control seemed secondary. In fact, control is the critical problem. A bird controls flight through subtle wing motions; a flying machine requires mechanical surfaces that can be adjusted by the pilot to maintain or change orientation. The three axes (pitch, roll, yaw) are independent, and controlling all three independently is necessary for controlled flight.

**Prerequisites.** Aircraft frame, control linkages, pilot control inputs (levers, cables, pulleys).

**Roman-available inputs.** Wooden frame for the aircraft. Linen covering for wings and control surfaces. Rope or leather straps for control linkages. Pulley blocks for mechanical advantage.

**Procedure.** (1) Pitch control: attach a movable horizontal surface (elevator) at the rear of the aircraft fuselage. Connect a lever or cable from the elevator to the pilot's control (e.g., a stick that the pilot pushes forward/backward). Forward stick pushes the elevator down, pitching the nose down; backward stick pulls the elevator up, pitching the nose up. (2) Roll control: attach movable surfaces (ailerons) on the rear of each wing. Connect one aileron to rise as the other drops, via a lever system. If the pilot pushes a stick to the right, the right aileron rises and the left aileron drops, rolling the aircraft to the right. (3) Yaw control: attach a movable vertical surface (rudder) at the rear of the fuselage, operated by foot pedals or a separate lever. Pushing the right pedal deflects the rudder right, pivoting the nose to the right.

**How you know it worked.** (Cannot fully test without flying the aircraft, but test components:) Manually move the control levers; the control surfaces should move smoothly and completely, responding to the pilot input without excessive force. The linkages should have no slack or binding.

**Failure modes.** Slack in the linkages causes delayed response and makes the aircraft difficult to control. A control surface that binds or catches requires excessive force and can jam during flight. Asymmetric response (one wing slower than the other) causes unwanted rolling.

**Cost & labour.** Three-axis control surfaces for a small aircraft: ESTIMATED 50-100 denarii in wood and linen. Labour: ESTIMATED 50-100 hours to fabricate and install.

**Danger.** Loose control linkages can cause uncontrolled motion of the control surfaces, making the aircraft unstable or uncontrollable. Control reversals (where pushing the stick produces the opposite motion) cause crashes.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Three-axis control was the key innovation of the Wright brothers and is well-documented. The mechanics are straightforward. Roman craftsmen could fabricate these systems.

### propeller_aero - Propeller (Aeronautical Screw)

**What it is / why you want it.** A rotating propeller consisting of blades designed as aerofoils (wings), pulling the aircraft forward by accelerating air rearward.

**Why you would never guess this.** Early aviators and engineers often treated the aero-propeller as a "screw" in air, similar to a ship's propeller in water. This approach produces inefficient propellers. The key insight (developed by the Wrights and others) is that a propeller blade is a rotating wing; designing the blade shape and twist as a wing (with camber, aspect ratio, and pitch varying along the blade radius) produces much higher efficiency.

**Prerequisites.** Aerofoil design, wood-working, understanding of rotating wing aerodynamics.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood for the propeller blades (ash, birch, or other strong, light wood). Iron or bronze for the hub and fasteners. Bearings (ball or plain).

**Procedure.** (1) Design a two-bladed propeller with each blade shaped as a cambered aerofoil. (2) The blade is twisted: the root (near the hub) is pitched at a steeper angle (40-50 degrees) and the tip is pitched at a shallower angle (20-30 degrees). This variation in pitch is necessary because the tip moves much faster than the root (tip speed = rpm x radius; root speed = rpm x smaller radius); the twist compensates so that all points along the blade see a similar effective angle of attack. (3) The blade is typically 2-4 metres long for an aircraft propeller. (4) Cut the blades from solid wood, tapering from root to tip and shaping the aerofoil profile. (5) Attach the blades to a wooden or metal hub (the centre piece that mounts on the engine crankshaft). (6) Balance the propeller carefully: any imbalance causes severe vibration and can destroy the engine.

**How you know it worked.** Spin the propeller at the design rpm. Place a small flag 2-3 metres in front of the propeller; the flag should be pushed backward strongly. Increase rpm; thrust should increase roughly with the square of rpm (doubling rpm roughly quadruples thrust). Measure current drawn by the engine (if it is electrically-powered) or fuel consumption (if gasoline-powered); the propeller should not require excessive power relative to the thrust produced. Specific power should be roughly 5-10 hp per meter of diameter at design speed.

**Failure modes.** Imbalance causes violent vibration and can crack the engine or aircraft frame. Blade damage (cracking or splintering) causes further imbalance and rough running. Incorrect blade twist (too steep or too shallow) reduces efficiency significantly. Erosion of the blade leading edge (from sand, insects, or water droplets) degrades performance gradually.

**Cost & labour.** A two-bladed propeller 3 metres diameter: ESTIMATED 100-200 denarii in wood and metal. Labour: ESTIMATED 80-120 hours to carve, shape, and balance. Carving the aerofoil shape and twist is labour-intensive and requires skilled craftsmen.

**Danger.** A spinning propeller is extremely dangerous; contact causes severe injury or death. The propeller must be fully guarded when the engine is running.

**Confidence: HIGH** - Aero-propellers were developed and refined by aviation pioneers in the 1900s-1910s. The design is based on solid aerodynamic principles. Roman craftsmen could carve propeller blades if given the design.

### powered_aeroplane - Powered Aeroplane

**What it is / why you want it.** A heavier-than-air aircraft with wings to generate lift, an engine and propeller to generate thrust, a tail for stability, and control surfaces to manage pitch, roll, and yaw. The aircraft lifts off, flies under power, and lands under the pilot's control.

**Why you would never guess this.** For millennia, humans observed birds and attempted to fly by flapping artificial wings. Birds do flap, but birds also have other features: cambered wings, tail control surfaces, high power-to-weight ratio, and instinctive 3-axis control. All of these elements were necessary; attempting to fly by flapping alone failed. The Wrights' contribution was recognizing that all four elements (lift, drag, thrust, control) were independent problems that could be solved separately through engineering rather than biomimicry.

**Prerequisites.** Otto cycle engine (or other power source), propeller design, three-axis control, wing design with camber, fuselage construction.

**Roman-available inputs.** Wood frame (ash, birch, spruce for strength and lightness). Linen covering for wings and tail. Iron or steel for engine and engine mounts. Rope and leather for control linkages. Ball bearings for rotating parts.

**Procedure.** (1) Design the aircraft: roughly 12 metres wingspan, 8 metres length, weighing 400-600 kg empty including a 10-15 kg engine. (2) Construct the fuselage (body): a wooden frame of longerons (long beams running front to back) and cross-bracing, strong enough to withstand forces during flight. (3) Construct the wings: two large surfaces with cambered aerofoil profile, strut-braced to the fuselage, covered with tight linen. Wing area should be roughly 30-50 m2 to generate sufficient lift. (4) Construct the tail: horizontal elevator surface for pitch control, vertical rudder for yaw control. (5) Mount the engine on the fuselage, with the propeller on the crankshaft and a large fuel tank (20-50 litres). (6) Install control linkages from the pilot's position to the elevator, ailerons, and rudder. (7) Install a landing gear: bicycle-style wheels on a simple frame, or skids that slide on the ground. (8) Paint the aircraft with dope (a cellulose lacquer) to tighten the linen and seal it against moisture.

**How you know it worked.** The aircraft should take off when the engine is full throttle and the wheels are moving at roughly 30-40 km/h (the takeoff speed, which depends on wing area, weight, and lift coefficient). Once airborne, the aircraft should climb at a rate of 1-2 metres per second (depending on engine power and weight). The pilot should be able to manoeuvre the aircraft: pitch up/down, roll left/right, climb, descend, and turn. Landing should be controllable: throttle back to descend, flare (pitch up slightly) to increase drag and reduce landing speed, and touch down smoothly.

**Failure modes.** Insufficient engine power means the aircraft cannot reach takeoff speed. Excessive weight (poorly designed structure or too much payload) requires higher takeoff speed. Nose-heavy or tail-heavy imbalance makes the aircraft difficult or impossible to control. Control linkage failure during flight causes loss of control. Wing failure (cracking, separation) causes catastrophic crash. Engine failure (usually carburetor ice or fuel starvation) causes loss of power and forced landing.

**Cost & labour.** A complete aircraft including engine, ESTIMATED 5000-10000 denarii in materials (wood, linen, iron, engine). Labour: ESTIMATED 2000-3000 hours for a team of aircraft builders, spread over 3-6 months. This requires a dedicated aircraft factory.

**Danger.** Flight is inherently dangerous. Engine failure, structural failure, or loss of control can cause fatal crashes. Spinning propellers, fuel leaks, and high-temperature engine parts are hazards on the ground. Landing in rough terrain can cause a crash.

**Confidence: MEDIUM** - The Wright Flyer (1903) is well-documented and is the proof of concept. However, building a reliable aircraft requires precision metalworking, wood-working, and fabric skills, all available to Romans, but at a lower quality than the industrial-era versions. A Roman-built aircraft would be heavier and less efficient than a contemporary (1903-era) aircraft, but it should be able to fly if designed carefully. The main limitations are: (1) the engine (otto or diesel cycle) is marginal for aviation (roughly 5-10 kg per horsepower is the requirement, and Roman engines are bulkier); (2) precision wood-working and fabric construction require skill and care; (3) fuel supply (petrol or coal gas) is problematic.

## Sources and Confidence

The technologies described in this module reflect the state of knowledge as of roughly 1890-1910 AD, by which time most of these systems had been developed, tested, and proven in full-scale operation. The description of each technology includes a confidence rating (HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW) based on the maturity of the underlying physics and the feasibility of Roman-era construction.

The most significant constraint on implementing these technologies in the Roman world is not the fundamental physics, which is understood, but the industrial and material supply chain. Steam engines require high-quality iron and boilers; aircraft require precision metalworking and light, strong materials (high-quality wood and steel); internal combustion engines require petrol or other refined fuel, which demands industrial chemical production; and many maritime innovations require either industrial metallurgy (for cast iron and steel) or sustained engineering effort (for iron hulls and complex mechanisms).

Despite these limitations, each individual technology is attainable by an intelligent, resource-rich civilization, given time and expertise. A Rome that prioritizes technological development and accepts the necessary capital investment could, over the course of a century or more, develop roads, railways, and even powered aircraft. The limiting factor would be energy: coal-fired steam engines (once developed) would transform manufacturing and transportation, but Rome has access only to charcoal (wood-derived) as a fuel, limiting the scale and efficiency of industrial processes.
