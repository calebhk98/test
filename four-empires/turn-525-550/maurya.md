# Part XIII: The Reckoning of the South (525 to 550 AD)

## The Ruler and the Inheritance

The exceptional ruler of the southern turn died in 525, and the crown passed to a career revenue official raised up by the treasury and the advisory council, a man who had spent the previous reign writing the objections that were overruled. The historians assess him at sixty-one: a superb keeper of accounts and a poor judge of when a ledger's logic stops being logic. He inherited the largest colonial venture any throne had attempted, a reserve more than half spent, a firearm his army did not know how to use, marine engines coming off the line, and one unanswered question. He did not slow the southern program, holding that a race half-run is worse than a race not begun.

## The Confirmation That the Fleet Sailed West

The answer assembled itself over six years. From 527 the realm's ships watched the arid northwest coast on the new engines, in every season, and no Egyptian sail appeared; the agents at Alexandria counted the fleet home from 531 with cargoes wrong for any eastern voyage. In 532 a Roman envoy at the Euphrates remarked in passing that Rome held a colony of its own across the western ocean, and asked what the realm knew of Egypt's venture there; the realm learned more from the question than from any answer it could have given. And in 533 a mate off an Egyptian hull was paid at Aden for his reckoning of the passage. The realm's navigators set it against the star tables and the new sea clocks: impossible eastward, unremarkable westward.

> **The finding.** The great fleet had sailed west, across the western ocean, to a land on its far side. It had never been bound for the southern continent, nor anywhere the realm's ships could reach. The court had read a tonnage, supplied a destination out of a hundred and fifty years of grievance, and spent three years of revenue against it.

The ruler forbade its suppression, had it lodged in three archives, and ordered the audit.

## The Southern Reckoning

Of the 6.25 billion paṇas spent, the treasury judged that some 2.2 billion would have bought the same three hundred and fifty founded places at a prudent pace across sixty years. The rest was the price of earliness. Of that four billion the auditors credited perhaps a billion and a half to assets the slow road would not have produced: a shipyard building ocean hulls twenty-five years early, two hundred thousand settlers, and the African drought grains proving out. The remaining two and a half billion bought earliness against a rival who was not coming. The arid northwest posts, fed by ship at a cost higher per post than any other line in the program, were entered as pure waste at their full figure and kept in service anyway, because a claim abandoned is a claim surrendered.

The court did not condemn the dead ruler. His reasoning held — coin spent on ground the realm wanted was recoverable, and the realm holds that ground whole and uncontested — and besides, the men doing the condemning had all voted for it. It did something worth more: it separated the gathering of intelligence from the judging of it, and laid down that every report to the throne must divide what was seen from what was reasoned, the inference marked as inference in the margin. It cost nothing, and it came six and a quarter billion paṇas late.

The consolation reached for was that the realm had wanted the continent anyway, and set against the figure it consoled no one. The deeper wound was not the silver. Egypt and Rome had both crossed the western ocean and planted ground on its far side, and the realm had not merely lost that race, it was never entered in it, because every gate to that ocean is Egypt's. Southern funding was cut from 250 million a year to 90 million from 535, and no further.

## The Islands Rise

In 536 the island peoples taken by force in the previous reign rose together: the northern groups, the eastern groups, and the cold southern island. They rose at the turn of the monsoon, when no relief could sail from home. This was the last season in which that was true, and they did not know it.

Six stations were overrun in the north and four in the east, and every farm on the cold island burned. Some two thousand one hundred died in the northern and eastern stations, eight hundred of them soldiers, and near fourteen hundred settlers on the cold island, where the fighting was worst because those settlers had taken not harbors but fields, and a people will tolerate a wharf where it will not tolerate a plough. The garrisons stood thinner than the establishment showed, for the ruler had trimmed seventy thousand men from the second line two years earlier to pay for the gun program, and the south is where the trimming fell. The relief sailed out of season on nine steam hulls, and arrived. That an engine could put soldiers on a hostile beach in a month the wind forbade was the most instructive fact of the reign, and it was learned in the worst way.

The realm answered three ways, because it could not afford one. The eastern groups sit astride the sea road to the Spice Islands and could not be given up; they were retaken in two seasons with eleven thousand men, firearm companies leading every landing, at three thousand four hundred dead, more than two thousand of them of fever. The northern groups it did not retake, the auditors putting the reconquest at four hundred million and the ground at less. It bought them instead, withdrawing from the interior, keeping three harbor stations, paying compensation for the land seized, and recognizing the island leaders as tributary lords under their own law. In return it took what it always takes: sole right of purchase on the islands' produce, the harbors and the water, pilots, tribute in kind, and the leaders' sons at Pataliputra. The court called it a settlement and not a defeat, and the treasury agreed. The cold southern island was neither retaken nor settled, and the fighting there runs on past 550 against a people who will not stand and cannot be bought, at thirty to forty million a year for nothing.

Rising and answer together cost near six hundred million. The lasting product was a rule under the advisory council's protection: no ground is taken in the south without a funded schedule of integration attached to the order that takes it. The realm had learned this in Mesopotamia in 250 and paid the tuition again three centuries later, which measures what a learned lesson is worth once the men who learned it are dead.

## Steam Under the Keel

By 550 the realm has near seventy sea-going steam hulls, forty transports and twenty-five with cannon, built in the Tamil yards, at Bengal, and on the southern continent itself, the Roman engineers gone home and the method now the realm's own. The southern road no longer waits on the monsoon. The seasonal convoy became a standing service, Ternate and Banda are reliably supplied for the first time since they were planted, and troops move between the subcontinent, the Strait, and the continent in any month, a capability the realm has not advertised. Inland, the same engines drive the great rivers.

The limit is fuel. The realm has opened coal in eastern Bengal and laid coaling depots down the southern road, each a fixed point to be garrisoned, so that its sea power is now a matter of ships and a chain of places, harder to defend and harder to keep secret. A hull that sets its own speed can also beat a current: the seas that turned the realm's ships back at the southern end of Africa a century ago were beaten in trial in 547, one of the two vessels lost with all hands.

## The Firearm and the Elephant

The realm makes its own firearms in the arsenals of the Gangetic plain, and its advantage is not skill but saltpeter: it sits on more than any power it knows, and powder was always the bottleneck. Output rose fourfold. By 550 some fifty-five thousand firearms are in the hands of drilled companies against a field infantry of four hundred and forty thousand — one man in eight, for each armed man costs what six spearmen cost.

What this does to an army built for seven centuries around elephants is not subtle. An elephant is a creature of nerve, caught and not bred, five thousand in the whole realm and no coin that makes more. Massed gunfire unmakes them precisely as elephants once unmade cavalry, and the corps' own trials at Ujjain settled it to everyone's satisfaction and no one's pleasure. The elephant ceases to be the arm that decides a battle and becomes what it always also was: the finest engine of haulage, bridging, and siege work in the world. Reorganization was ordered in 541, resisted, and is not complete. The corps' officers are the realm's military aristocracy, and they secured that every field army keep its elephant establishment entire while the gun companies rose alongside. So the realm pays for both.

## The Debasement

The reign's third failure was unforced. By 538 the reserve was near the bottom and the island war open, and the treasury proposed reducing the silver in the paṇa by one part in six. The ruler, who understood coin better than any man at court, signed it. He forgot that the paṇa's worth was never its metal but its sameness across seven centuries and four thousand miles, and that the merchant houses' bills, which carry the value of a caravan from the Strait to the Euphrates without metal moving at all, were every one of them written in paṇas.

The houses discounted the new coin within a season. Lenders would not take repayment in light silver on contracts struck in heavy, and the credit that carried the long-distance trade seized. Caravans that ran on paper stopped running, and traffic on the Rome road fell near a third across 540 and 541. The debasement raised some hundred and eighty million and cost better than four hundred million in trade revenue and collection. The reversal ran from 543 to 545: full weight restored, the light coin recoined at the state's expense at near two hundred and fifty million, and the standard published under seal on printed sheets in every provincial market, the presses turning for the first time to the defence of the currency. The revenue minister was dismissed, which repaired nothing; the ruler had signed the order himself. The houses now keep a discount against the chance of a next time, and that is the permanent price.

## The Galvanic Work

The wire-path theory matured. The realm's researchers hold a general understanding of the galvanic force, make reliable batteries, and can send a current down a wire to swing a needle at the far end, at two hundred paces. They cannot do it across a province. The signal dies over distance for reasons they can describe but not defeat, their insulation fails in the first rain, and copper enough to line a road is copper committed to the coinage. The plant path was closed in 534 and recorded as Egyptian sabotage, of which Egypt was told nothing. So the window ends with the theory and no device of consequence, while Egypt has bound an empire together with the thing for a century. The semaphore towers already carry word from coast to capital in a day, which is good enough to make the funding argument hard, and being good enough is how such gaps stay open.

## Rome, Egypt, China

Rome remains the steadiest tie. It let fall that it holds a colony across the western ocean, and would say nothing of its size, its strength, or where it sits; the realm did not press, having nothing it cared to trade for the answer. Rome asked in turn after the realm's firearm manufacture, and was refused. Egypt trades as it always has, takes the realm's silver at its ports, and grants it not one foot of ground beyond them.

China has not moved, and the realm's silence toward China has held. The Silk Road runs at twelve parts in the hundred and Chinese hulls use the Strait on the terms of 480. The realm has quietly rebuilt an eastern fleet to some forty-five cannon-armed vessels, twenty-five under steam, and has put not one of them east of the Strait. The arithmetic that broke its fleet in 478 has changed in its favor on every term the court can measure, and the court has resolved to leave it unmeasured.

## The Realm at the Close of the Reign

> **By the numbers, around 550 AD.** The realm's registered hundred and five million people hold all they long have, the southern register of two hundred and sixty thousand settlers and the tributary islanders kept apart. Yearly revenue is near twenty-five hundred million paṇas, some eleven hundred and twenty million the land tax and near seven hundred and eighty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls, with the Kolar gold at half its old peak. The standing army is near a million and fifty thousand and costs more than the larger army before it: four hundred and forty thousand field infantry, four hundred and thirty thousand in garrison and second line, ninety thousand horse, eighty thousand navy crew. Fifty-five thousand firearms are in service, the elephant corps at five thousand, and seventy sea-going steam hulls in commission. A thousand million goes to the army, two hundred and fifty million to weapons research at the doctrinal tenth, and two hundred and sixty million to roads and works, with twenty-two thousand researchers. The reserve stands under a third of a year's revenue, the thinnest since the third century. Reading and writing remain with about one person in ten, higher among officials and merchants since cheap paper.

## The Unresolved Matters of the Reign

The southern continent is the realm's, uncontested and unpaid for, and will not pay for itself in the lifetime of anyone now living. The northern islands are bought and the eastern held; the cold southern island is a war without a plan. The currency is restored and not trusted. The elephant reform is written and not done. The galvanic force is understood and does nothing. Four hulls sent in 549 to round the African cape under steam had not returned by the close of 550. The realm holds steam, the firearm, and a monsoon-free ocean, and has aimed none of it at anyone. And across a western ocean it cannot enter, Egypt has planted a land and Rome has planted a land, and of either it knows nothing but that they are there.
