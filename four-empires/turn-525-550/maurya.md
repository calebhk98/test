# Part XIII: The Reckoning of the South (525 to 550 AD)

## The Ruler and the Inheritance

The exceptional ruler of the southern turn died in 525, and the crown passed without quarrel to a man the treasury and the advisory council had both had a hand in raising up: a career revenue official who had governed Gujarat and then the Tigris capital, and who had spent the previous reign writing the objections that were overruled. The historians assess him at sixty-one. He was a superb keeper of accounts, a patient administrator, and a poor judge of when a ledger's logic stops being logic. He inherited the largest colonial venture any throne had attempted, a reserve drawn past half a year's revenue, a fielded firearm his army did not know how to use, marine engines coming off the line, and one question the realm was waiting on. He did not slow the southern program, holding that a race half-run is worse than a race not begun.

## The Confirmation That the Fleet Sailed West

The answer assembled itself over six years, from four directions at once. From 527 the realm's ships worked the arid northwest coast on the new engines, no longer waiting on the season, and in eleven years of watching the shore where an Egyptian sail must appear, none appeared. The agents at Alexandria and the East African harbors, keeping the tally of hulls the previous reign had taught them to keep, counted the great fleet coming home from 531 with cargoes wrong for any eastern voyage and crews longer gone than any eastern voyage requires. In 532 a Roman envoy at the Euphrates remarked in passing that Rome held a colony of its own across the western ocean, and asked the realm, as a man making conversation, what it knew of Egypt's western venture; the realm learned more from the question than from any answer it could have given. And in 533 a mate off an Egyptian hull was paid at Aden for his reckoning of the passage, and the realm's navigators set his days and headings against the star tables and the new sea clocks and found the voyage impossible in any eastern direction and unremarkable in a western one.

> **The finding.** The great fleet had sailed west, across the western ocean, to a land on its far side. It had never been bound for the southern continent, nor anywhere the realm's ships could reach. The court had read a tonnage, supplied a destination out of a hundred and fifty years of grievance, and spent three years of the realm's revenue against it.

The ruler forbade the finding's suppression and ordered it printed, sealed, and lodged in three archives, holding that a state which lies to its own record loses the one instrument by which it corrects itself. Then he ordered the audit.

## The Southern Reckoning

Of the 6.25 billion paṇas spent, the treasury judged that some 2.2 billion would have bought the same three hundred and fifty founded places at a prudent pace across sixty years, as the realm had settled Kalinga and the Deccan and every other ground it took. The remaining four billion was the price of earliness. Of that, the auditors credited perhaps a billion and a half to assets the slow road would not have produced: a shipyard on the continent building ocean hulls from local timber twenty-five years ahead of schedule, a transplanted population near two hundred thousand already reproducing the realm's order, and the African drought grains proving out on ground they suited better than any at home. The rest, near two and a half billion, bought earliness against a rival who was not coming. The chain of cheap posts along the arid northwest, watered and fed by ship at a running cost higher than any other line in the program, was entered as pure waste at its full figure and kept in service anyway, because a claim abandoned is a claim surrendered and the ocean had proved it could deliver strangers.

The court did not condemn the dead ruler, and the record is honest about why. Partly his reasoning held: coin spent on ground the realm wanted was recoverable, the realm did want the continent, and it holds it whole and uncontested, which is more than it holds of anything else. Partly a court that arraigns an exceptional reign teaches every future ruler to hide his ledgers. And partly the men doing the condemning had all voted for it. What the court did instead was quieter and worth more: it separated the gathering of intelligence from the judging of it, so that the men who count hulls no longer write what the hulls mean, and it laid down that every report to the throne must divide plainly what was seen from what was reasoned, the inference marked as inference in the margin. The realm's chronicle has carried that mark ever since. It cost nothing, and it came twenty-five years and six and a quarter billion paṇas late.

The consolation reached for was that the realm had wanted the continent anyway. Set against the figure, it consoled no one. The deeper wound was not the silver. Egypt and Rome had both crossed the western ocean and planted ground on its far side, and the realm had not merely lost that race, it was never entered in it, because every gate to that ocean is Egypt's and always has been. Southern funding was cut from 250 million a year to 90 million from 535, held to consolidating what stood, and the ruler refused all pressure to cut further.

## The Islands Rise

In 536 the island peoples taken by force in the previous reign rose together: the northern groups, the eastern groups, and the cold southern island. They rose at the turn of the monsoon, in the weeks when no relief could sail from home. This was the last season in which that was true, and they did not know it.

Six stations were overrun in the north and four in the east, and every farm on the cold island burned. Some two thousand one hundred died in the northern and eastern stations, eight hundred of them soldiers, and near fourteen hundred settlers on the cold island, where the fighting was worst because the settlers there had taken not harbors but fields, and a people will tolerate a wharf where it will not tolerate a plough. Fourteen ships were burned or taken at their moorings. The garrisons stood thinner than the establishment showed, for the ruler had trimmed seventy thousand men from the second line two years earlier to pay for the gun program, and the south is where the trimming fell.

The relief sailed out of season on nine steam hulls, and arrived. That an engine could put soldiers on a hostile beach in a month the wind forbade was the most instructive fact of the reign, and it was learned in the worst way.

The realm then answered three ways, because it could not afford one. The eastern groups sit astride the sea road to the Spice Islands and could not be given up; they were retaken across two seasons with eleven thousand men, firearm companies leading every landing, at three thousand four hundred dead, of whom rather more than two thousand died of fever and not of wounds. The northern groups the realm did not retake, the auditors putting the reconquest at four hundred million and the ground at less. It bought them instead: withdrawal from the interior, three harbor stations kept, compensation in iron and cloth for the land seized, and the island leaders recognized as tributary lords with their own courts and law. In return the realm took what it always takes — sole right of purchase on the islands' produce, the harbors and the water, pilots for its ships, tribute in kind, and the leaders' sons at Pataliputra for their education. The court called this a settlement and not a defeat, and the treasury, which had priced both, agreed. The cold southern island was neither retaken nor settled; the fighting there runs on past 550, a grinding war in cold forest against a people who will not stand and cannot be bought on terms the realm will offer, costing thirty to forty million a year and yielding nothing.

Rising and answer together cost near six hundred million between 536 and 545. The lasting product was a Southern Office at the Tigris capital and a standing rule under the advisory council's protection, as the research budget is: no ground is taken in the south without a funded schedule of integration attached to the order that takes it. The realm learned this in Mesopotamia in 250 and paid the tuition again on fresh coasts three centuries later, which measures how little a learned lesson is worth once the men who learned it are dead.

## Steam Under the Keel

By 550 the realm has near seventy sea-going steam hulls, forty transports and twenty-five with cannon, built in the Tamil yards, at Bengal, and on the southern continent itself, the Roman engineers long gone home and the method wholly the realm's own.

The southern road no longer waits on the monsoon. The seasonal convoy became three sailings a year and then a standing service; Ternate and Banda are reliably supplied for the first time since they were planted; and troops move between the subcontinent, the Strait, and the continent in any month, a capability no rival is known to hold and one the realm has been careful not to advertise. Inland, engines in shallow river hulls work the Ganges, the Indus, and the Tigris against the current, and the cost of moving grain and soldiers within the realm has fallen further in twenty years than in the previous three hundred.

The limit is fuel. An engine eats, and only where it is told to eat. The realm has opened coal in the eastern hills of Bengal as a state working and laid coaling depots down the southern road, each of them a fixed point to be held, watered, and garrisoned. The realm's sea power has ceased to be a matter of ships and become a matter of ships and a chain of places, which is harder to defend and far harder to keep secret.

## The Firearm and the Elephant

The realm makes its own firearms in the arsenals of the Gangetic plain, and its advantage is not skill but saltpeter: it sits on more than any power it knows, and powder was always the bottleneck. Output rose fourfold across the reign. By 550 some fifty-five thousand firearms are in the hands of drilled companies against a field infantry of four hundred and forty thousand — one man in eight, no more, for each armed man costs what six spearmen cost and the ruler would not raise the army's budget faster than the revenue.

What this does to an army built for seven centuries around elephants is not subtle. An elephant is a creature of nerve, caught and not bred, five thousand in the whole realm and no coin that makes more. Massed gunfire unmakes them precisely as elephants once unmade cavalry, and the corps' own trials at Ujjain settled it to everyone's satisfaction and no one's pleasure. The elephant ceases to be the arm that decides a battle and becomes what it always also was: the finest engine of haulage, bridging, and siege work in the world, and still decisive against any enemy without guns, of whom the realm faces many. The corps was ordered reorganized on that basis in 541. The order was resisted and is not complete. Its officers are the realm's military aristocracy, and they secured in the drafting that every field army retain its elephant establishment entire while the gun companies were raised alongside. So the realm pays for both.

## The Debasement

The reign's third failure was its own, unforced, and worse in what it touched than either of the others. By 538 the reserve was near the bottom and the island war open, and the treasury proposed reducing the silver in the paṇa by one part in six. The ruler, who understood coin better than any man at court, signed it, and forgot what his own career should have taught him: that the paṇa's worth was never its metal but its sameness across seven centuries and four thousand miles, and that above the coin sat the merchant houses' bills and letters, the paper that carries the value of a caravan from the Strait to the Euphrates without the metal moving at all — and every one of those instruments is written in paṇas.

The houses discounted the new coin within a season. Lenders would not take repayment in light silver on contracts struck in heavy, the courts filled with the question, and the credit that carried the long-distance trade seized. Caravans that ran on paper stopped running. Traffic on the Rome road fell near a third across 540 and 541, and the transit revenue with it. The debasement raised some hundred and eighty million and cost better than four hundred million in trade revenue and collection.

The reversal ran from 543 to 545: full weight restored, the light coin recalled and recoined at the state's expense at near two hundred and fifty million, and the standard published under seal on printed sheets in every provincial market and trading city, the presses the Brahmins had been given turning for the first time to the defence of the currency. The revenue minister was dismissed. The record notes that the ruler signed the order and that dismissing the minister repaired nothing. Confidence was not fully back by 550, and the houses now keep a discount against the possibility of a next time, which is the permanent price.

## The Galvanic Work, and What It Did Not Reach

The wire-path theory matured. The realm's researchers hold a real and general understanding of the galvanic force, make reliable batteries, and can send a current down a wire and swing a needle at the far end. They can do this across a room, and a courtyard, and at two hundred paces. They cannot do it across a province. The signal dies over distance for reasons they can describe but not defeat, their insulation fails in the first rain, and copper enough to line a road is copper committed to the coinage and the bronze. The plant path was closed in 534 and recorded formally as Egyptian sabotage, and the realm has said nothing of this to Egypt.

So the realm ends the window with the theory and no device of consequence, while Egypt has bound an empire together with the thing for a century. The semaphore towers carry word from the coast to the capital in a day when the weather is clear, which is good enough to make the funding argument hard, and being good enough is how gaps like this one stay open.

## Rome, Egypt, China

Rome remains the steadiest tie. It let fall that it holds a colony across the western ocean and would say nothing of its size, its strength, or where it sits, and the realm did not press, having nothing to trade for it that it was willing to spend. Rome asked in turn after the realm's own firearm manufacture; the realm declined, having sold that account once, and offered its materia medica and antiseptics on commercial terms instead. Rome bought some of it. Egypt trades as it always has, takes the realm's silver at its ports, and grants it not one foot of ground beyond them.

China has not moved, and the realm's silence toward China has held. The Silk Road runs at twelve parts in the hundred, Chinese hulls use the Strait on the terms of 480, and the realm has sent no probe and made no complaint. It has quietly rebuilt an eastern fleet in home and southern yards to some forty-five cannon-armed vessels, twenty-five under steam, and has put not one of them east of the Strait. The arithmetic that broke the realm's fleet in 478 has changed in its favor on every term the court can measure, and the court has resolved to leave it unmeasured.

## The Cape

A hull that sets its own speed can beat a current, and the current that turned the realm's ships back at the cold southern end of Africa a century ago was beaten in trial in 547 by two steam vessels, of which one was lost with all hands in the seas past it. A second attempt of four hulls, provisioned for a year and ordered to round the cape, learn what lies beyond, and come home without landing or provoking anyone, sailed in 549. It had not returned by the close of 550.

> **By the numbers, around 550 AD.** The realm's registered hundred and five million people hold all they long have, with the southern continent's own register — some two hundred and sixty thousand of the realm's people, and the tributary island populations — kept separately. Yearly revenue is near twenty-five hundred million paṇas, some eleven hundred and twenty million the land tax, near seven hundred and eighty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls, the Kolar gold at half its old peak and the Silk Road still ebbing. The standing army is near a million and fifty thousand, fifty thousand fewer than at the century's turn and costing more: four hundred and forty thousand field infantry, four hundred and thirty thousand in garrison and second line, ninety thousand horse, and eighty thousand navy crew. Fifty-five thousand firearms are in service, the elephant corps at five thousand and reorganized on paper only. About a thousand million goes to the army, two hundred and fifty million to weapons research and armament at the doctrinal tenth, and two hundred and sixty million to roads and works, with some twenty-two thousand researchers. Seventy sea-going steam hulls are in service. The reserve, after the south, the rising, and the recoinage, stands under a third of a year's revenue, the thinnest since the third century. Reading and writing remain with about one person in ten, though cheap paper and the presses have carried it far higher among officials and merchants than any earlier reign could show.

## The Unresolved Matters of the Reign

The southern continent is the realm's, uncontested and unpaid for, and will not pay for itself in the lifetime of anyone now living. The northern islands are bought and the eastern islands held; the cold southern island is a war without a plan. The currency is restored and not trusted. The elephant reform is written and not done. The galvanic force is understood and does nothing. The realm holds steam, the firearm, and a monsoon-free ocean, and has aimed none of it at anyone. And somewhere across a western ocean the realm cannot enter, Egypt has planted a land and Rome has planted a land, and of either the realm knows nothing but that they are there.
