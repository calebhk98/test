# Turn: 525–550 AD

## Standing Situation at Turn Open

- Revenue near 132,000 talents, reserve near 263,000, and the slack thin before this turn spends a coin.
- The Western Enterprise is a thin unbroken coastal perimeter with almost nothing behind it. The Plata is the only ground filling with people at a rate worth reporting.
- Rome has not attacked anywhere, has not answered the Atlantic posture, and has not repeated the 524 reconnaissance. The council does not read silence as absence. Silence is a policy, and Rome is disciplined enough to keep one.

## The Settled Charge, 532

The oldest open problem in Egyptian chemistry closes this turn. Worked since the 320s, through two centuries in which the assay houses could make a compound that would tear a rock face apart and could not make one a man could carry there without dying on the way.

The answer is not a new compound but a way of holding an old one. The unstable oil is soaked into an inert porous earth, a fine siliceous silt dredged from Rift lake beds, which carries it like a sponge and will not pass a shock through it. Soaked cartridges can be dropped, struck, carried on a mule, stacked through a Nubian summer, and set alight, and they burn sullenly rather than detonating. What detonates them is a separate initiating charge, a mercury compound long known and avoided, packed in a copper cap and fired electrically or by fuse. The art is the separation: the mass is stupid, the cap is clever, and the two meet only at the moment of use.

Adopted in 532 as the settled charge, third formulation. It is the most consequential result of this turn and it is not, mostly, a weapon.

**Mining.** Rock that was cut is now broken. Bulk blasting reaches the Copperbelt, Hwange, the Zimbabwe plateau and the Nubian fields inside four years, with overburden removal at a scale that makes working an ore body from the surface a real alternative to a shaft. Copper output rises by a third without one additional bonded worker: the mechanization argument of the slave reform arriving by a door nobody was watching. Gold is not ramped, since a known output invites attention, though the cap is quietly lifted (see the treasury note).

**Engineering.** Harbor deepening at the Plata and Trinidad, cuttings for mine railways that took a decade of picks, a straightened alignment on the canal's worst bends. The Sinai engineers rewrite their demolition doctrine: every bridge, sluice, cistern and pass in the fortress system now has prepared chambers, and the corridor can be made impassable on a signal rather than over a fortnight.

**Weapons.** Moored mines gain a charge that keeps for years instead of months, and aerial munitions become bombs rather than incendiary cans. A self-propelled underwater charge on compressed air, begun 536, is undelivered at 550: it runs but will not hold a depth, and four hulls' worth of funding has bought weapons that swim under their targets or broach in front of them.

What the settled charge does not give is any means of throwing a charge at a distance. Egypt can break rock and drop a bomb from an aircraft. Nothing in its chemistry suggests that a charge might be pushed down a tube, and the gap is not identified as a gap.

## The Nitrogen Ceiling, and the Failure at the Falls

The success creates the crisis. Demand across the mines, the engineers, the Sinai works, the fleet and the colonies rises roughly fivefold in eight years. Every grain of it is nitrogen, and every grain of Egyptian nitrogen comes from the niter beds: turned, watered, leached, four to five years from building a bed to drawing from it. They cannot be hurried.

Twenty-five further years at the Livingstone Falls have not broken that ceiling and will not. The line was kept undirected on the reasoning that Egypt had more concentrated current in one place than had ever existed, and chemists who run current through everything, so something might fall out. Nothing did. Currents through nitrate solutions gave back the nitrate that went in; molten salts gave metals already known. The one result that looked like one was an arc in open air, which does yield a trace of nitrous fume that can be washed down into acid. The assayers reported that the falls, running every generator into arcs and making nothing else, would yield a fraction of one year's niter-bed output at a current cost that beggars the product. Closed as a directed program in 543.

Reported as what it is: a sink. Roughly 3,000 talents and four hundred chemists across twenty-five years, byproducts nowhere near covering it. The money was not badly spent, since the prize would have been decisive. The method was, because nobody was ever told what to look for.

An allocation board sits in Alexandria from 544 dividing the empire's explosive between mining, engineering and the arsenals: the first body in three centuries that rations a thing Egypt cannot buy, make faster, or conquer.

## The Lower Congo Navigation Works, 534–545: A Failure

With rock now breakable, the engineers proposed cutting a navigable channel through the Livingstone cataracts to join the Atlantic directly to the industrial city and the upper river. The council approved it in 534 largely on the strength of the new tool and the pleasure of having one.

It failed. The river's discharge is beyond anything Egyptian hydraulics had modeled; blasted rock does not vanish but resettles, and each cleared drop reformed as a new rapid below itself. In 541 a bank charge undercut a slope carrying the portage railway, which came down with two hundred and ten men on and under it. Work continued four more years on sunk-cost reasoning before abandonment in 545. Roughly 19,000 talents and eleven years, against a doubled portage railway, the cheap answer in 534 and the answer now.

The council's review found the deeper error. The falls city is Egypt's most advanced industrial base and its value is that it sits a thousand miles up a river no fleet can ascend. The project's success would have destroyed the reason for the project. Nobody said this aloud for seven years.

A smaller disaster: the initiating-cap works outside Berenice detonated in 539, killing three hundred and eighty-one, most of them in the town. Cap manufacture now sits on desert ground and two Red Sea islands.

## Aircraft

Operational service in small numbers, metal-framed on falls aluminum. Two types: armed reconnaissance carrying pilot, observer, radio set and a light load; and a larger incendiary type carrying settled-charge bombs. By 550, something over two hundred airframes built and perhaps ninety serviceable on a given day, in flights of six to twelve at the canal, the Sinai approaches, Cyprus, Aden, the Cape, the Plata and Trinidad.

Attrition is the story nobody expected: eighty-one machines and fifty-three fliers lost, four in anything resembling an operation and the rest to engines, weather and ground. Training fliers is now harder than building aircraft, and the school at Memphis is the bottleneck under the arm. What growth there is comes from the industrial turn, not the aircraft program: series production of engines, instruments and radio sets makes ninety machines possible where a workshop tradition would have delivered fifteen. None of this stays classified much longer, an aircraft over the canal being visible to every hull in the roads. Standing instruction: never flown over foreign ground or hulls, so that Rome may hear of them and not measure them.

## American Contact with Rome, 534

Survey parties working north from Trinidad found Roman-held ground in the spring of 534: cleared land, a stockade, a jetty, boats drawn up, a standard. Neither power held fortified home ground within a thousand miles. Squadron commander Senuthios of Berenice had four hulls, two aircraft, no orders covering this, and radio that reached his own ships and nobody else. He did not fire and did not withdraw. He anchored in sight, sent a boat under a plain flag with a Latin-speaking officer, and asked for the officer commanding.

From his log: each named himself and his crown, and each stated that the ground his flag stood on was his crown's. Neither asked where the other's claim ended and neither volunteered it. They agreed, on no authority whatever, to three things: neither lands where the other's flag already stands; vessels meeting at sea here pass and do not close; either may send a boat to the other's nearest post, and it will be received. Senuthios then did the thing the council commended most. He let the Roman believe the southern coast was long held and settled deep inland, without saying so, by speaking of harbors and provinces as a man speaks of home. It is a bluff Egypt can support at the shoreline and nowhere behind it.

Report reached Alexandria in thirty-four days by packet and heliograph. The council ratified everything in 536: a war begun at the end of the longest supply line in the world, over an island neither crown can reinforce, against a peer, is the worst war Egypt could choose, and two captains' understanding commits neither crown while a treaty would commit both. Egypt will not propose a treaty, because a treaty must name latitudes, and naming latitudes tells Rome the size of the claim and hands it a document to hold up later.

Egypt took no ground and gave none. What it extracted was measurement: hull counts, the state of the post, the health of the men in it, four place-names. The northward advance stops at the southern arc of the chain, the effort going into fortifying Trinidad, worth more than three more islands.

The note to Rome, sent 537, asserts no latitude. It proposes mutual advance notice of squadron movements in Atlantic waters and names two ports where each may address the other. Rome's reply in 541 acknowledges receipt and answers nothing, which is all Egypt has had from Rome on the Atlantic in twenty-five years: a decision neither to be provoked nor to concede, which is what Egypt would do. Spy tasking returns partial. Roman naval circles now hold that Egypt is across the ocean, and the lost-ship question has hardened into grievance in some quarters and not others, with no sign of a crown position.

## The Dying

The collapse of contacted coastal populations is now undeniable in scale. The physicians' reports from the Guianas, the Brazil coast, the Orinoco and the islands are consistent and careful: a spotted fever, then a coughing sickness, then a flux, within two to four years of contact, killing across all ages rather than the young and old, and running inland along native trade paths faster than any Egyptian has walked them, so that villages sixty miles inland have died without seeing one. Where Egypt returns after ten years it finds a third of the houses standing and a tenth of the people. Egyptian and African-provincial settlers are barely touched.

The observation is excellent. The explanation is wrong where it matters. Egypt's physicians hold that specific organisms cause specific diseases, which is right, and conclude that these peoples' constitutions were never seasoned to the organisms of the wider world, which is close enough to be dangerous. From there, two false steps: that the cause is a weakness in the peoples rather than a carriage by Egyptians, since Egyptians are not sick; and that the dying is spent once it has passed through a district.

The policy is worse than the error. Contact parties are pushed ahead of settlement deliberately, on the reasoning that the dying will happen anyway and had better happen before Egyptian towns need the ground. Quarantine is not imposed, the physicians who argued that Egyptian bodies carry the sickness having been overruled because Egyptian bodies are visibly well. Nine headmen brought to Alexandria in 538 were dead within the year, recorded as evidence for the theory.

The council's entry is a labor entry. Egypt went to that coast expecting to trade with its inhabitants, recruit from them and feed garrisons from their fields, as it did in Africa for five centuries. There is nobody to trade with. Every colony must import every person and every sack of grain across forty days of ocean, and the Enterprise costs about twice what was budgeted in 525.

## The Rest of the Turn, Briefly

- Shipyards on standardized components reach seven to nine steam hulls a year against three in 500. The most valuable number in the industrial program, and still not an industry.
- Radio to a further two hundred and forty hulls, though no set Egypt can build reaches across the ocean and the colonies stay a packet problem.
- The American crops mature into the generational wave, cassava and maize now carrying whole districts of the Congo, the West African lowlands and the Great Lakes. Population near twenty-eight million.
- The China exchange runs at twenty scholars each way, Egypt's germ and tropical-disease body opened in full. Delayed report suggests China is moving on its southern frontier. Egypt priced that knowledge at optics, mathematics and a consultation clause, and records without pleasure that it may have sold a wall. India: nothing given, nothing taken.

## Open Questions Carried Forward (550–575)

1. The niter ceiling is now the binding constraint on Egyptian power: beds, guano, or a chemistry nobody has found. Directed program this time, or none.
2. Whether the captains' understanding survives its captains. It is written in two logs and no treaty. What happens the first time a Roman officer arrives who has not read his.
3. Whether to tell Rome plainly that Egypt is in the New World, in exchange for something, before a concession already inferred is worth nothing.
4. Whether aircraft should be shown deliberately, as the Cape fleet never was.
5. Whether the dying can be slowed, and whether Egypt wishes it slowed, now that empty ground has proved to cost more than peopled ground.

## Treasury Note

Revenue reaches roughly 141,000 talents by 550, the growth coming from copper, colonial customs and the crop-fed provinces rather than from anything new. Against that: the Western Enterprise at double its budgeted cost, the abandoned Congo works at 19,000 talents, the aircraft arm, the explosive plants and niter-bed expansion, the Trinidad fortifications, and the falls city still absorbing capital. The reserve stands at roughly 205,000 talents at turn close, down from 263,000, below the two-year target for the first time since the tsunami and above the one-year floor.

Two measures were taken that no easier decade would have produced. Gold output was lifted about a quarter, quietly, breaking a century-old restraint on visibility. And the crown accepted the first true state loan in its history against its own name, 12,000 talents from the Alexandrian banking houses, who were delighted to make it, and which the council regards as a door it would rather not have opened.

Egypt is not poor. Egypt is, for the first time in four hundred years, spending faster than it earns, in a quarter-century when Rome did nothing at all.
