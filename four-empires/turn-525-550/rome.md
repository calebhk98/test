## 525 to 550 AD: Powder, Coal, and the Meeting in the West

### The Account from Pataliputra

The Maurya keep the bargain, and they keep it whole. The firearm account reaches Rome in the twenty-eighth year, and it is not the padded and directional thing Rome had braced for. It is written to a maker's standard: the construction of the tube and how it is closed at the breech, the ignition and how fire is brought to the charge at the instant the man wills it, dimensions with tolerances, the loading sequence, the rate a trained soldier sustains, the range at which the ball tells against armor, and the failure modes, on which it is unusually frank. Rome tests it against what it already holds, exactly as it warned it would, and it proves out at every point Rome can check.

The account also does what a hundred and eighty years of Roman work could not. The turn from pressure to combustion at the close of the last century was correct, but it was made in the abstract, and the weaponization program that opened this turn had begun, as its own directors predicted, by designing a powder-charged vessel: a heavy emplaced launcher on the two-century pattern of Roman steam-weapon doctrine, a chamber, a crew, a carriage, a thing to be sited and served. The Indian account describes a weapon one man carries on his shoulder, loads standing, and fires on the instant, and gives its dimensions in inches. Rome had stopped looking for the wrong mechanism and had then gone on looking for the wrong scale, and only a rival's honesty caught it.

What Rome does not do is thank India in any way that costs something. The swap was closed. Rome's engineers are already in the Indian yards under the steam half of it, and Rome's read is that the debt now runs the other way, India having bought a navy where Rome bought a category. When the Maurya court asks, twice across the turn, what progress Rome has made, Rome answers that its engineers continue to study the account.

### The Ratio and the Weapon

The powder search arrives at a stable ratio in the thirty-first year, found the Roman way: a great many men burning a great many measured mixtures and printing the results until the proportions of saltpeter, charcoal and sulfur that give the most violent and most repeatable expansion are bracketed and fixed. There is no theory under it. Rome cannot say why saltpeter is necessary, only that nothing substitutes for it and that without it the mixture merely burns. Corning into grains follows within two years by the same brute method, and the grain sizes go under the specification discipline that governed the launcher valves.

With the ratio in hand and the Indian account on the bench, the working firearm comes fast, because every remaining problem is a metal problem and metal is what Rome is. A serviceable matchlock exists in the thirty-fourth year, proofed in the thirty-sixth, in the hands of two demonstration cohorts on the Rhine by the thirty-ninth. Cast ordnance follows with less difficulty still, a naval gun being a short thick cylinder closed at one end, and Rome has bored cylinders for two hundred and fifty years. The first powder guns go to sea in the forty-third year in the iron-plated hulls.

### What the Factories Could and Could Not Do

Rome discovers within five years that it has two separate industries to build, and that it is superbly equipped for one and hopeless at the other.

The barrel is not a difficulty. Rome bores engine cylinders to a tolerance, rolls iron in quantity, proof-tests boilers by pressure because a boiler that fails at sea kills the ship, and has an administrative habit of standard specifications for critical components dating to the compromise of 480. Barrel-making moves into the Rhine and British works with less disruption than the guilds' objections implied, and the proof house, testing every barrel to destruction before issue, is the boiler yard's practice carried across. The lock is solved by the clockmakers, whose escapement work is the nearest thing in Roman industry to a small precise mechanism made in numbers. By 550 sixty to eighty thousand serviceable arms exist, and the works could make three times that a year if asked.

They are not asked, because the powder is not there.

### The Nitre Failure

Charcoal is free. Sulfur is a Sicilian and Campanian commodity Rome can lift in any quantity. Saltpeter is the whole war, and Rome does not have it. Europe holds no natural deposit worth working, and the only way Rome knows to make it is the nitre bed: dung, urine, ash and lime layered under cover, turned, watered, and left eighteen months to two years before leaching. It is an agricultural process with a two-year lag and an enormous appetite for ground, labor and filth, and it goes under the state because no private venture builds a thing that returns nothing for two years.

Rome underestimates the requirement by roughly an order of magnitude. The error is instructive rather than stupid: the planners costed the beds against the demonstration cohorts in exercise, and real consumption is set by battle, by damp spoiling stock in a frontier magazine, and by the guns, which eat in one broadside what a hundred infantrymen eat in a day. The first twelve imperial works are sited in 535, yield in 538, and produce a fraction of specification, partly because contractors paid by the acre sited beds on wet ground, partly because the method went out in print to men who had never done it. Two procurement officials are prosecuted in 541 for certifying beds that did not exist, and the trial is printed in full, which does the program more damage than the theft did.

The second error is political. To feed the beds Rome imposes a collection levy on stable sweepings and the contents of the public latrines, contracted out and enforced, on a genuinely literate public with a competitive gazette culture. The administration cannot say what the collection is for, the program being classified, so it defends a foul and intrusive levy on no stated grounds for four years, and the pamphlet war is the sharpest domestic embarrassment of the turn.

At 550 the army holds tens of thousands of firearms and a powder reserve adequate to perhaps three to six weeks of one theater at real expenditure, with no depth behind it. Rome has bought a weapon and not yet bought a war. The only large and reliable saltpeter Rome knows of lies on the Ganges plain, in the hands of the power that gave Rome the account, and buying it in the quantities wanted would announce the program as loudly as firing it. Rome buys small through merchant intermediaries for testing and builds forty further works, which will not yield until the next turn.

### Coal Behind the Carolina Coast

The prospecting parties sent up the navigable rivers behind the northern seaboard find coal in the thirty-seventh year, and by 545 it is confirmed at three reaches in workable quantity and depth, close enough to water that a barge loads at the pithead. This is the most consequential western finding of the turn. Its first value is that a Roman steam warship can now live in western waters; coaling stations are raised at the river mouths and stocked, and steam hulls are stationed permanently across the ocean rather than sent as a gesture. Its second is that the Carolina coast, dismissed a generation ago for holding no gold, becomes the western industrial keystone the last turn's reasoning predicted. Engines, boilers, rail and men must all be carried three thousand miles at punishing freight cost, but by 550 there are pumps and hoists and a rolling mill on the ground, a short iron tramway from pit to landing, and a yard beginning to repair hulls without sending them home. Mortality among the first works crews is severe, and volunteers are drawn with land grants rather than wages, land being the one thing Rome can spend freely there.

### The Yucatan Measured, and the Column That Went Inland

The reconnaissance completes and the answer is the awkward one. The interior is neither a fractured handful of towns nor a single empire. It is a densely settled country of dozens of cities of real size, each with its own lord, its own writing and its own quarrels, running inland and southward past where the balloons could see, with a population Rome's surveyors put in the millions and cannot bound. There is no capital to take, no king whose defeat ends the matter, no metal anywhere in it, and no army in it that could stand ten minutes against a Roman line even before powder.

Rome tests the country rather than the enemy, and the test goes badly. A column of some four thousand marches inland in 543 to fix the road to the nearest three cities. It is not beaten in battle; it fights three small actions and wins them all. It is beaten by limestone that holds no surface water for days, by forest that must be cut a road's width at a time, by the absence of any navigable river to carry supply, by the uselessness of horses, and by fever. Something over a third does not come back, and almost none of the loss is to Maya arms. The filed report is correct and cold: the enemy is the country, and the country cannot be beaten with more infantry.

The decision of 546 is therefore not the conquest that had been loaded. Rome takes the coast and the near cities, which sea can reach and supply, breaks those that resist, and sets the rest under tributary terms rather than administration, extracting cacao, cotton, jade, gold and labor through their own lords, whom it leaves in place because it cannot staff what it cannot reach. The deep interior is deferred for stated reasons: no water road, no fuel, and a tax base shrinking for causes Rome cannot name.

### The Dying

The physicians can no longer file it as coincidental flu, and to their credit they stop trying. Across the islands and along every coast Rome has touched, local populations are collapsing at rates the tallies put between a third and two thirds within a decade of sustained contact, in villages under Roman labor obligation and in villages under none, in the worked gold creeks and in fishing settlements a hundred miles from any Roman. The correlations are drawn honestly: mortality tracks the recency and closeness of contact and nothing else, does not track the labor regime or hunger, does not spare the young and strong as famine does, and does not touch Romans standing in the same village.

The explanation is wrong. Rome has no theory of contagion beyond the practical quarantine rules its printed protocols carry, and what it reaches for is the air: the exhalations of European men, harmless among themselves through long habituation, corrupt an air these constitutions were never tempered against, and the remedy is separation, settlements sited apart and upwind, contact confined to appointed markets. In two or three places the rule slows the thing slightly, which the physicians take as confirmation and which hardens a wrong mechanism into printed doctrine. A minority hold that Egypt has poisoned wells, and are not taken seriously.

What Rome does about it is governmental. The island economy was built on subjugated labor and the labor is dying faster than it can be worked; gold output on Hispaniola falls by half across the turn. Rome imports no substitute, having no slave trade and no cheap source. It pours colonists onto the emptying ground, which is what the veteran-settlement engine is for and which the deaths make easier, and it accelerates the coal coast, where an engine does what dead men cannot. The conclusion, written plainly in the fiscal papers and nowhere argued morally, is that the islands are becoming a settlement colony rather than an extraction one, and that the transition is being made for Rome by a cause it does not understand.

### The Meeting South of the Islands

In the thirty-fourth year a Roman squadron working south of the islands raises harbor works on a mainland coast, and the men who see them know them at once, half the officers in the western fleet having read the printed African survey: cut stone laid to a plan, engineered quays, a shrine of expensive stone, and ships. Egypt is in the west, on ground south of Rome's, and has been there long enough to build in stone.

Neither side holds fortified home ground and both know it. The Roman commander has no orders covering the case, no one in Rome having imagined it, and his nearest authority is an ocean and eight months away. He does what a Roman officer carrying five centuries of institutional memory of the Delta and Carrhae does: he does not fire, does not close, counts, and then sends a boat under parley with a written notification in Latin and Greek, resting it on the only precedent in the record, the mutual naval notification arrangement Egypt itself proposed for the Mediterranean a century ago. He states Rome's presence, states the islands as Roman ground, asks nothing, concedes nothing, and withdraws north. Then he sends his fastest hull home and spends the intervening year fortifying, because a man who has just met a peer at the edge of the world does not wait for permission to dig.

Rome ratifies him in 537, having no better option: repudiating a notification already delivered would cost more than accepting it, and the alternative to a de facto line is war with the greatest naval power in the world at the end of a three-thousand-mile supply route. The Senate's reading, held loosely as the last turn's discipline requires, is that the western ocean is now the one theater where Rome and Egypt stand on comparable terms, both newcomers, neither at home, and that Rome's advantage there is not its fleet but its coal, its settlers and now its powder, none of which Egypt is to learn of.

### Accounts, and the Cost of Doing Three Things

Rome attempts the rearmament, the western program, and the continuing iron-hull and screw work in the same twenty-five years, and the margin does not stretch. By 542 it is gone. Rome debases the silver in 543, the oldest instrument in the box, used against a public that reads: within eleven weeks the gazettes have printed assays of the new coin beside the old, merchants in three cities are pricing openly in the old standard, and the measure is reversed in 545 at a cost greater than it raised.

What replaces it matters more. Rome levies an emergency assessment on the great industrial, mining and shipping companies and takes forced advances from the banking houses against future customs, and the Industrial Council, asked to fund a program whose purpose it is not told, extracts a price: a formal right of prior consultation on any new levy falling on industry. The emperor grants it because he needs the money in the year he needs it. It is a small clause and the largest constitutional change of the turn. The Dnieper fortification program is slowed to pay for the rest, which is noted at the frontier and resented there.

### Standing Programs

The screw propeller reaches fleet-reliable service late in the turn and becomes standard on new construction, the paddle wheel retired from the building yards though not from the water. Roman engineers remain in the Indian yards under the steam bargain and prove, as predicted, worth more than any bought agent. The Han junction reply is at last sent on the terms held from the previous turn: transshipment yard on the Roman bank, Roman gauge unbroken to the Dnieper, Han factors under Roman law, sheds and cranes at Roman cost. The Alexandria network holds, its quarantined branch still untouched and still silent. The relay timing observed off the African coast, one ship handing the watch to another with no messenger that could have carried word, remains unexplained; the pneumatic theory is the one Rome can build and has built, and nothing this turn has moved it.

**State of the empire, c. 550 AD.** Population ~122M; revenue ~4,600M HS (military ~66%, industry ~27%); technical workforce ~510,000; literacy ~71%. Technology: a working matchlock and cast powder ordnance held in strict secrecy, corned powder to a fixed ratio and no powder supply worth the name, screw propulsion in service, iron hulls, western coal. Territory: the Caribbean islands settled, the Carolina rivers held for coal, the Yucatan coast taken and its interior deferred. Rome has acquired the weapon it spent a hundred and eighty years failing to imagine, and has learned that owning a weapon and being able to fight with it are two separate purchases.
