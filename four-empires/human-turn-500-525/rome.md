## 500 to 525 AD: The Ocean on Both Sides

### The Second Voyage and the Decision to Commit

The turn opens with the second western fleet already home. Four ships had gone out where one went before, provisioned for eight months, guided by the two surviving pilots of the first crossing, and they returned with a report Rome could no longer file as a curiosity. The islands were not the edge of anything. Beyond them lay a coast that ran for more than a thousand miles without closing or ending, holding at least three distinct kinds of society: island farmers with fine pottery and traded gold; a walled city of cut stone on the Yucatan shore with its own writing and its own merchant class; and, further north, smaller scattered fishing communities with none of the wealth of either. The fleet brought back cacao in quantity, fine cotton cloth, worked jade, gold ornaments, alluvial gold noted by exact position on Hispaniola and Cuba, and a ship's carpenter's note, entered into the formal report though it concerned neither gold nor trade, that the oak and pine of the northern coast were superior shipbuilding timber.

Rome resolves the internal argument that the previous generation left open, and resolves it toward commitment rather than caution. The fiscal conservatives, who had wished to treat the west as a matter to revisit later, lose. The determining facts are three: the land is real and rich, the veterans owed land for generations now have somewhere to be sent, and Egypt learned of the discovery within months and cannot be assumed to be idle. No new institution is required to carry it. Rome's existing fleets are already large enough for the task, hundreds of ocean-going sailing warships and bulk transports beyond them, and the venture is run on those hulls under imperial command, with the route and charts held as an imperial secret.

### The Conquest of the Islands

The islands present no military problem worth the name. Stone tools and canoes against steel, mail, torsion artillery, and shield-wall infantry is not a war but a conquest with a foregone result. A first expedition on the order of fifteen to twenty-five thousand troops, small by Roman standards and overwhelming by local ones, lands on Hispaniola first, where the second voyage had marked the gold creeks and the farming communities were organized enough to be worth subjugating. Cuba follows in the same window, larger and therefore slower, but no harder. Chiefdoms that resist are broken, those that submit are enslaved or set to labor, and the gold-bearing ground is seized and worked. By the fifth year both major islands are Roman ground, with the first garrison forts raised and the first veteran allotments laid out.

Once the islands are held, the shipping pivots from carrying soldiers to carrying colonists, and here the true scale of Roman capacity tells. Dozens of ocean-going sailing hulls dedicated to the run move colonists in the tens of thousands per year, and across the quarter century the western migration runs into the hundreds of thousands: veterans and their families onto the taken land, subjugated islanders as the labor force beneath them, gold and cassava and cotton and the first cacao flowing east in the returning holds. The land era, which had closed in Europe two centuries before when the frontier stopped growing, does not end. It crosses an ocean. Rome trumpets this loudly and largely truthfully, and the veteran pressure that might have fed a usurpation is poured onto a continent instead. The mortality of the crossing and the fevers of the islands are soft-pedalled in the telling but are not, in fact, severe; the fevers that had thinned the second voyage's crews are determined by Rome's physicians to have been common flu, coincidental to the voyage rather than native to the new land.

### The Mainland Foothold and the Measuring of the Maya

In parallel with the island settlement, once the islands form a secure base, Rome establishes a fortified position on the Yucatan coast near where the second voyage's crews had met the stone city's traders. This is a garrisoned military foothold, not a trading station, and its true purpose is reconnaissance. Rome trades in the near term as a courtesy that doubles as intelligence-gathering, sends observation balloons up along the coast for elevated sightlines over the treeline, and probes cautiously inland, building the one piece of knowledge Rome lacks and needs: how large the civilization behind that coast is, how many cities it holds, how much wealth, and how far it runs.

The restraint is not respect for the city's statehood. Rome does not respect statehood; it respects the capacity to do it harm, and a walled city of people who work no metal has no such capacity against Roman arms. Rome would butcher a Maya army and could raid the city's wealth close to at will. The single genuine unknown is the extent of the interior for the purpose of holding and administering it, and that unknown, not any principle, is what defers the conquest. The interior conquest is loaded as a next-turn program, to be built on this turn's reconnaissance: if the hinterland proves a fractured landscape of city-states, Rome takes them one at a time; if it proves larger or more unified, Rome will know before it commits, exactly as the Delta and Carrhae taught it to know before it strikes.

Florida and the northern seaboard are charted but not conquered this turn. The second voyage had already shown them poor in gold and thinly peopled, and there is nothing there worth a legion while Cuba holds gold and the Yucatan holds a wealthy city. Late in the turn, however, the northern coast is reassessed upward and given real survey effort, for a reason set out below.

### Coal, and the Reassessment of the Northern Coast

A correction of doctrine occurs within the turn. Rome had reasoned that the New World would be a hand-labor and slave-extraction economy, on the inherited belief that steam machinery only ever became profitable because slaves grew expensive, and that where slaves are cheap the machine does not follow. This is recognized as a slogan concealing a more precise truth. The steam engine of this era is no longer the marginal machine of the first factories; two and a half centuries of empirical improvement have made it out-produce hand labor by a margin so wide that the cost of the human, slave or free, no longer decides. A slave cannot compete with a mature engine in any mechanizable trade, even a slave who costs nothing.

The true gate on New World industry is therefore not labor but fuel. The islands and the Yucatan coast hold no coal Rome has found, and an engine without coal is dead iron. But Rome's steam warships themselves cannot operate across the ocean without coaling stations, so Rome is compelled to prospect for coal regardless of any industrial ambition, simply to keep a steam fleet alive in western waters. Fuel for the ships is a basic requirement of presence, not an afterthought of industry, and where coal is found for ships it serves engines equally.

This reorders the value of the "poor" northern seaboard entirely. The mid-Atlantic and Carolina coast, dismissed at first for holding no gold, is the one stretch that can fuel itself: good shipbuilding timber, navigable rivers, and coal country reachable up those rivers not far inland. Gold is a prize carried home; coal is a prize that lets a presence stay and sustain itself. Rome accordingly redirects prospecting parties up the navigable rivers behind the northern coast to hunt fuel, and this coast, rather than the gold islands, begins to look like the eventual industrial keystone of a Roman New World. The economy that results splits along the seam of what steam can and cannot mechanize: un-mechanizable extraction, gold, cacao, field cotton, worked by slaves where there is no coal, and genuine steam industry growing wherever coal is found.

### Crops

The voyages return not only luxuries but staples, and one staple matters greatly. Maize, a calorie-dense grain that yields where wheat struggles and adapts to warm Europe and the Mediterranean, is the strategic prize, with cassava and sweet potato adding drought-tolerant calories beneath it. Adoption is a bottom-up and generational process rather than an imposed one: farmers take up a crop that returns more food for the same labor because it pays them to, and Rome's print network and its habit of distributing agronomic method spread a working technique far faster than the word-of-mouth crawl of the older world. The gate is agronomic, the generational selection work of adapting a warm-country grain to shorter northern seasons, which no quantity of engineers can compress, but once adapted strains exist the knowledge propagates quickly.

Rome keeps the staples to itself as a domestic strategic good, stacking maize atop the Ukrainian grain that had already been eroding Egypt's oldest weapon, and declines to trade either the food or its seed to the peers, who are grain-rich already and would gain a permanent new staple from a one-time sale. What Rome sends east instead are the New World luxuries, cacao, tobacco, chili, dyes, and fine cotton, the high-value low-weight goods that survive the long routes and sell, carried in Roman hulls to the Euphrates and the Dnieper on the standing terms.

### The Han Junction (planned, not yet sent)

Rome prepares, but does not this turn send, a reply to the Han proposal for a rail junction at the Dnieper, the single message of the turn having gone instead to Pataliputra. The intended terms, held for a future exchange, are these. Rome would accept the joining of the lines under the existing auto-renewing agreement, but as a transshipment yard rather than a true joining: Rome's single gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and would not be relaid to match the Han line, so Han cars would come to the eastern bank and stop, Roman cars would stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods would cross where wheels do not. The reason is honest on both sides, that a rail running from one empire onto the other is a road for an army, and neither would lay the other's gauge inside its own territory. The yard would stand on the Roman bank under Roman garrison, with Han factors working in it under Roman law as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory, and Rome would build the sheds and cranes at its own cost, wanting the volume of direct rail-to-rail silk more than the argument. Of the offered Han gifts, Rome would receive the mechanical clock and escapement with thanks for comparison against Roman work, return the offered paper method with the note that Rome has made linen-and-flax paper for five hundred years and printed with cast type for four hundred, and receive the measurement of the sphere as the true gift, along with the Han's stated reason for having asked four times about the west: that they had measured the earth themselves, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned the western land into existence before Rome ever sailed to it.

Underneath this sits a piece of intelligence Rome already held for a century and had never properly weighed. The Han trade directly with Egypt, and the Han had told Rome, at the early Dnieper contacts, that their ships had reached the waters of India. Rome, holding to the four-hundred-year Ptolemaic doctrine that the Indian Ocean is an enclosed sea ringed about by land and joined to nothing, had filed this and never asked the obvious question: if the eastern sea is closed, how did the Han ships enter it. The answer, unexamined until now, is that the sea is not closed. If it opens to the east to admit Han vessels, and if the Han and Egypt trade across it, then the enclosed-sea belief is simply wrong, and a road to India exists that need not pass Bab-el-Mandeb, the canal, or Alexandria, and need pay Egypt nothing. This reasoning, drawn from evidence a century old and joined to the printed heliocentric question of whether a Ptolemy wrong about the sky can be trusted about the sea, is what licenses the southern survey that follows.

### The Message to Pataliputra

Rome's single message of the turn goes to the Maurya court, and its posture is cold and transactional, India being filed already as a commercial partner engaged in systematic impression management, having concealed its Syr Darya cannon while pressing Rome four times for intelligence on Chinese firearms. India had offered a trade: the full account of China's hand-firearm, everything its Tidore survivors saw and its engineers judged, against steam that moves a hull, which India had deduced Rome possessed and had concealed.

Rome accepts, but as a single clean swap rather than the installment scheme first considered, the installment logic being fatal at a distance where one message crosses per generation. Rome offers the complete working method of the hull engine, boiler through transmission, seals and their failure points, fuel figures, and Roman engineers in Indian yards to build the first beside Indian engineers, delivered whole in one exchange, against the complete firearm account delivered whole in return. Rome names nothing that it withholds, having recognized that to list the screw and the iron plate as things kept back would be to hand a rival desperate to rebuild its navy a targeting list for its own effort and its spies. Rome states plainly only that it will test what India sends against what it already knows, and that a padded account will show its seams as fast as a true one will prove out.

### The Southern Survey Returns

The three ships sent south down the African coast hold their orders, standing well off, entering no surf, attempting no contact, closing the land only to look through the glass and then standing off again. All three return, and what they carry reorders Rome's map of the world.

The African Atlantic coast below the edge of Roman reach is not the empty Saharan shore the charts and the traders had promised. It is built. First, half-built harbor works, jetties, and foundations laid in rows on a coast supposed to hold no one; then, at intervals, stone signal towers throwing hard flashes of mirrored light, plainly a coastal semaphore net akin to Rome's own; then, past the towers, cities that do not stop for three thousand miles. They are built in the Egyptian manner, the sloped pylon gateways, the columned halls, a temple of expensive stone at the heart of each town, recognized on sight by men who knew Egypt and the Egyptian shrines in Rome's own cities. The richest stretch, from the green coast to the equator, holds a true planned city in cut stone, with engineered quays and breakwaters, ranked warehouses, a grid of streets, halls of administration, and along its waterfront the smoke and heat of foundries and machine-work, with warships among a crowd of heavy cargo hulls in its harbor and, by the weight and guard of the work, gold moving through it in quantity. Town after town of real size follows, a dozen and more, each with its harbor works and its shrine and its craft offshore, and behind and between them native towns and villages beyond counting, plainly under Egyptian rule, their fishing canoes on the water in thousands, thickest at the mouths of a great river.

Further south stands a fortified city guarding a river mouth, then a kinder coast of scattered harbor towns, then a long terrible stretch of desert falling into a cold and fog-bound sea where the ships saw little but a few hardened refuge harbors. And at the bottom, the corner: Africa ends, and open sea runs east beyond it. At that corner stands the greatest city of the whole coast, larger than any passed, planned in stone, holding in its harbor the largest gathering of ships seen anywhere. The captains held one to two miles off, close enough to count and far enough to run, and did not close further before turning for home.

Two observations weigh above the rest. The first is that a single ship shadowed the survey its entire length in relay, coming out from down the coast to hold off the beam, matching course, never closing, never firing, never answering a signal, and being replaced by another from further south when the survey ran past the length of one, the relieved ship falling back the way it came. Egypt was never in doubt where the Roman ships were, watched them the whole three thousand miles, and chose to let them go. The second is that throughout the voyage great Egyptian ships, larger than anything in Rome's fleets and loaded deep, passed northward up the coast by the dozen and the score, day after day, a column that never truly ended, until the count ran past two hundred and the men stopped counting. They were never seen returning south, and never seen more than one at a time in any port.

### The Reassessment of Egypt

Rome treats its own ships' observations as reliable and holds the interpretations loosely, but even the reliable core is a reordering. The West African power that destroyed Rome's ships two generations before was almost certainly Egypt or Egyptian subjects; Rome had lost those ships to an established, defended Egyptian coast, and Egypt had spent a century ensuring Rome blamed a fiction of reef, current, and aggressive canoe-peoples. Rome's Egypt, held for six centuries to be Egypt, Cyprus, the Levant, and an Indian Ocean toll, is revealed as a continental two-ocean power: stone cities to a plan for three thousand miles, dozens of ports each with a fleet, a coastal signal net, subject populations beyond counting, and the greatest city and fleet of all at the Cape. The concealment was never of a coastline but of the true size and nature of the whole civilization, held all along at the line of Alexandria. The scale of the stonework and the flawless naval relay imply decades at the least, and Egypt's wealth is reassessed not merely upward but very likely above Rome's own, since Egypt sits on the source of the African gold rather than taxing it as a Berber middleman, and runs it north in guarded quantity atop the canal and the Indian Ocean monopoly already known. Egypt is a far greater naval power than Rome, its ships beyond counting against Rome's twenty to thirty steam warships and few hundred sailing hulls. This is the largest single military reassessment in the record.

Two standing Roman assumptions die with the survey. The first is the four-hundred-year Ptolemaic belief in an enclosed Indian Ocean: Africa is confirmed by Roman eyes to end, with open sea running east around the Cape, so a sea road to India exists that skips Bab-el-Mandeb and the canal, but it is Egyptian end to end and guarded at the corner by the largest fleet on the coast. The second is the value of Gibraltar as a chokepoint on Egyptian Atlantic access, reinforced only the previous generation on the belief that it bottled Egypt out of the ocean. It does not; Egypt reaches the Atlantic down the length of the African coast without passing the strait, and the northbound column of Egyptian ships is already outside it, in the Atlantic, moving up toward the latitude from which a fleet crosses west. Rome's chokepoint closes a Mediterranean door while Egypt's Atlantic door stands open on the far side of it.

The convergence unsettles the Senate: Egypt learned of the Americas within months, its home yards went to a tenfold overdrive of shipbuilding and resource-buying that its own public could sense without understanding, and a column of its largest ships now streams north toward a crossing latitude. Rome holds the reading open rather than closed. The pieces fit a race to the Americas, but they fit equally a centuries-old Atlantic gold artery carrying metal north, invisible before only because no Roman had sailed the coast, with the return leg run offshore out of Roman sight exactly as Rome's own survey ran its own return. Rome logs the tenfold surge and the northbound column as unresolved rather than as a confirmed armada, holding to the discipline its predecessors were warned to keep and had so often failed to keep: not to draw a confident conclusion from a telescope sighting.

What Rome takes as settled enough to shape thinking, without overclaiming intent: its five-year head start to the Americas may be no head start at all; Gibraltar closes a door Egypt walked around before Rome conceived of it; attacking Egypt is now harder than ever and impossible anywhere near its own waters, the one theater where Rome could contest Egypt at all being the New World, where both are newcomers and neither holds fortified home ground; and the response to discovering Egypt is twice the feared empire is emphatically not to reach for the army, there being nothing here an army of Rome's can reach, but to move faster to the reachable rich ground of the near new world before Egypt closes its hand around it, and to find the coal that lets a Roman presence there fuel and sustain itself.

### Intelligence, Research, and Standing Programs

The Alexandria network, whose long silence from one agent had prompted a moment's fear of a rollup, is kept standing rather than abandoned on the evidence of a single vanished man; the silent branch is quarantined and left untouched, no replacement walked into it, with the network watched for whether the silence spreads and tasked above all to learn the size and heading of Egypt's shipbuilding surge and any sign of westward provisioning. The Dnieper frontier watch continues, redirected from chasing the Chinese firearm, which India is about to hand Rome for free, toward counting how much the Han rail can carry to the river and how often, a supply line to a frontier being a supply line to an army. Rome does not spy on India this turn. The intelligence doctrine begins a deliberate shift from espionage toward presence, the Roman engineers going openly into Indian yards under the steam trade being worth more as long-term eyes than any bribed agent.

The gunpowder program's twenty-year search stands where the prior turn left it, a stable ratio some six years off and weaponization to begin the following turn; when it begins, Rome's engineers are expected to reach first for a powder-charged pressure vessel, an emplaced launcher in the two-century tradition of Roman steam-weapon doctrine, rather than for a handheld tube no Roman has yet any reason to conceive, and India's incoming firearm account is the one thing likely to pull them off that path before a generation is spent on the larger vessel. The screw-propeller and iron-hull program continues toward fleet-reliable deployment. The natural-philosophy patronage bears its fruit within the turn: the observatories built the prior generation confirm the phases of Venus and the moving lights of Jupiter, and the heliocentric case is settled among the men who look through the glass whatever the schoolmen say, and with it comes the printed and widely circulated question that licensed the southern survey in the first place, that if Ptolemy was wrong about the sky there is no reason to keep believing him about the sea.
