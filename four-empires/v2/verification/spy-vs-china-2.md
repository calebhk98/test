# ADJUDICATION — Maurya operation against Han China, 525–550
## The Sogdian caravan channel (Nanaivandak of Samarkand)

*Neutral adjudication. Sources: `03-han-china.md`, `human-turn-500-525/china.md`, `v2/turn-525-550/china.md`. Nothing here is drawn from any Maurya file.*

---

## Standing of the asset

There is no cover to fail. Nanaivandak is a silk broker asked to report on caravans, which is the content of his working day. Samarkand sits on the Maurya bank of the standoff, on the road itself, and the Chinese record independently establishes that Sogdians have interpreted for the Han since first contact at the Syr Darya (~325 years) and have been a formally tasked Chinese network run from the Office of Frontier Interpretation at Dunhuang for ~220 of those years, currently under **Kang Mi**. The Maurya are therefore retaining a man whose family is simultaneously on the Han payroll. That is not a flaw in the operation; it is the reason it works, and it is also the reason its product must be read with the grain of his interest.

**Outcome: full and correct information on the tasked question, delivered with a systematic bias toward alarm, plus a large volume of untasked material of greater strategic value than the tasking.**

---

## I. WHAT THE MAURYA OBTAIN

### The tasked question — the northern steppe road

He answers it well, because it is his trade and because the Chinese themselves publish most of it at the crossing.

- **The road exists as a rail road now.** Chinese rail from **Anhe** was completed to the eastern bank of the Dnieper in **541**, with a transfer yard, sidings, cranes and a gauge-matching bogie shop built on the Chinese bank. Any factor on the platform sees this; China's own file records that Chinese frontier officials at Anhe openly collect Roman gauge, sleeper spacing, wheel-flange profile and published freight rates as declared overt collection, and the traffic runs the other way just as freely.
- **The growth number, and it is the correct one.** The Anhe customs returns record **outbound silk tonnage from the Chinese bank rising by about half between 541 and 550**. This is the single figure the tasking asks for and he can get it, because it is a customs return at a market a Sogdian house works.
- **A junction has been offered and is not yet made.** China's letter of 548 offers Rome rail-to-rail transfer at the crossing, expressly so that "your transit tariff takes its tenth of a larger flow." The Chinese-built apparatus stands finished on the eastern bank; whether iron meets iron on the western bank is not settled at the close of the period. A broker in the trade would know the offer was made — such an offer is a commercial fact merchants discuss before couriers arrive.
- **The southern road is unchanged.** China pays the Maurya toll at **twelve parts in a hundred, as since 477**, and no change has been recorded at any Chinese post on the road. Nanaivandak, who helps assess that toll, knows this to the coin.

**Verdict on the tasking: substantially correct, and the answer to "is the loss still growing" is yes.**

### Where his report will support a wrong conclusion

He measures tonnage at a crossing. He is paid a share of a toll. Both push the same way, and the Maurya court should discount for it.

1. **Tonnage on the Chinese bank is not throughput to Rome.** The +50% is what Chinese rail *delivered to the water*. China's own file states flatly that the figure "reflects what the Chinese rail delivered to the crossing, not what happened west of it," and declines to claim anything about the western bank. A report of a surging bypass may be a report of goods stacking up in a transfer yard.
2. **The whole overland line is nearly flat in money.** Chinese revenue from **Silk Road tolls and silk export moved 365 → 420 tonnes** across the twenty-five years, against total revenue moving 4,050 → 4,390. Trade tolls have sat at roughly nine per cent of Chinese revenue for two centuries and did not break out. A broker's tonnage curve reads like a transformation; the ledger says the overland trade is a modest and structurally stagnant line for both empires.
3. **China has already decided the frontier is not a business.** In 500–525 the court formally declared "the frontier pays for itself" **false**, halted new offensive conquest, and judged the westward expansion a security project that lost money. The northern road is not an economic weapon aimed at Samarkand; it is a supply line that carries freight as a by-product. A Maurya court reading Nanaivandak's file as commercial aggression would be reading a correct number into a wrong intent.

The characteristic error available here is precise: **the Maurya may conclude China is deliberately strangling the transit toll, when China is deliberately building a military rail and pocketing the freight.**

## II. WHAT ELSE CROSSES HIS DESK

None of this is tasked. All of it is what a carter mentions without understanding it, and it is worth more than the caravan counts.

### The powder reform — the largest item, and China knows he has it

The Chinese file contains a leak assessment written for exactly this man. In **534** the standing powder allowance was replaced: rail-served posts from five days to **fifteen**; posts beyond rail from five days to **sixty**, with hardened magazines. The court's own estimate, entered by the Shangfang:

> "A twelvefold rise in beyond-rail powder issue is visible to every arsenal clerk who fills the requisition, every quartermaster who moves it, **every carter who counts the wagons**, and every post commander who now holds a standing order permitting withdrawal. **The Sogdian carters on the western line are Maurya subjects, and the empire's own western intelligence is bought from the same trade.**"

The court estimated the *fact* of a large increase leaves the empire within **one to two seasons** and cannot be concealed at all; the *shape* — sixty days beyond rail against fifteen on it, which is the sealed arithmetic in disguise — takes **five to ten years to assemble from convoy sizes**. Sixteen years have elapsed by 550. A network of this reach and age can have the shape by the 540s. Whether it does depends on whether anyone in Samarkand troubles to compare two convoy manifests, which is not a broker's habit but is exactly a toll-assessor's.

### The withdrawal clause — the sharpest thing available on the road

The 535 doctrine amendment reclassified everything beyond rail — the Irtysh posts, the Ob approaches, Minusinsk, the Amur stations, the northern-forest fur posts, the thin Pontic scatter — as **observation**, and permits those commanders **to withdraw on contact rather than stand**. The Chinese assessment of this is unsparing: it "is read aloud to garrisons, and a regulation that permits a post to leave is discussed in **every frontier market within a season**." Frontier markets are Nanaivandak's premises. He will have this, in the garrison's own words, dressed in the euphemism the Shangfang drafted for it (*preservation of force for counter-concentration*) — and a broker who has spent thirty years listening to soldiers will know what a euphemism is for.

What he does **not** get is the reasoning: the **Jinyang Report** is sealed to three offices. He does not get the two posts lost in Bing province in 356, the finding that a post with powder cannot be held against an attacker with powder, or the naval side-finding that four fifths of the fleet's carried powder went in one afternoon at Tidore. He gets the symptom, not the proof.

### China told the Maurya court itself, in 547

Materially reducing the value of the coup: the Han letter of the summer of 547, by the Zhenxi crossing, states plainly that the empire has "this reign increased the powder held at our frontier posts, substantially, and reorganised how those posts are classified," and gives the reason as **"because you will hear a version of it from the carters if we do not."** The Maurya court thus receives the same fact twice, from its broker first and its counterpart second. The adjudicable consequence is that China's disclosure will read as candour rather than as warning, because the broker's version arrived years earlier and matches — which is precisely the effect China purchased.

### What the state is buying, and at what price

- **Rail.** The **Datong–Jinyang–Luoyang** trunk completed **543**; **Luoyang–Shouchun** two thirds built at 550; the Yangtze crossing surveyed and not begun. Datong coal reaches the Luoyang and Chang'an arsenals at roughly **a third of its former carriage cost**. Carters know carriage costs the way brokers know silk.
- **The refusal that defines the north.** The Ministry of Works costed a line from the Mongolian coal country toward the Irtysh in 539 at **over 900 tonnes**, and it was **refused in 541 without a second reading**. A surveyed line abandoned is public in the trade.
- **Iron.** The **535 re-rating** doubled the monopoly price of iron sold to licensed works — shafting, gearing, bored cylinders, plate. Returned ~30 tonnes in 537 and ~95 by 549. A merchant sees the second schedule.
- **Saltpeter did not rise.** The court refused to raise the monopoly price, because the largest buyer of monopoly saltpeter is the Shangfang. Note the mirror: China's own tasking against the Maurya asks whether saltpeter has risen in *Maurya* civilian markets as a sign of state offtake. The same test run against China gives a negative — correctly, and misleadingly, since the offtake happened at a price the state simply paid itself.

### Are the soldiers being paid — and in what

Yes, and **in paper**. The **changping chao** was authorised in the eighth month of **538**: issued only by the Da Sinong against certified ever-normal granary stock, redeemable at any commandery granary in grain or any commandery treasury in bronze cash, receivable for the land tax, capped at one third of certified stock. Use is **restricted by edict to frontier military pay, rail contracts and magazine stores** — which is to say, restricted to precisely the transactions a frontier merchant witnesses. By 550 it carries about **290 tonnes** of annual payments against a statutory ceiling near 615.

A broker who takes these notes at a market is running a daily credit test on the Han treasury, and he has more standing to judge the instrument than any Maurya official. Two readings are available to him, and the honest one is the harder:

- The easy reading — *China is short of silver.* Supported by what he can see: a fifth of the escort details on the western remittance road **stood down by 546**; twenty proposed warships refused; a third Northern Road squadron refused in 545; the Irtysh line refused on cost.
- The true reading — China is grain-rich and cash-poor and has just built a machine for converting immobile grain into mobile silver. **Genuinely free revenue at 550 is about 45 tonnes on revenue of 4,390 — one per cent.** He cannot know that figure. He can arrive at its flavour from a decade of refusals.

Either reading tells the Maurya something they can act on. The second, if reached, is worth more than every caravan count in the file.

### The trade in his own hands is changing

The item a silk broker of all men would notice and report unprompted: **thirty-one licensed works** in Wu, Kuaiji and the lower Yangtze by 550, the **Gu house at Wu** running somewhat over three thousand hands under one roof; **fourteen of the thirty-one** now making hemp and ramie rather than silk; goods more uniform than any hand's work. And the **riot of the ninth month of 545** — some four thousand displaced household weavers, largely women, burning two outbuildings and a dyeing shed at the Gu works, dispersed in a day and a half, eleven dead. The court's noted relief that "nobody at Wu had powder" is an internal minute; the riot is market talk from Panyu to Samarkand.

### The question he was asked is itself intelligence

In **546** the Dunhuang office extended these same Sogdian factors south, to ask **whether the Maurya court had noticed Bamo**, what Maurya presence exists on the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal, and whether the Irrawaddy is navigable below Bamo and at what draught. A factor who is asked those three questions learns, without being told anything, that the Han have something on the upper Irrawaddy, that they are frightened of it being seen, and that they are contemplating the Bay of Bengal — which is to say, a Chinese port on the eastern shore and the **Malacca toll made irrelevant**. That is the Maurya's central commercial interest, and it arrives free, in the form of a question.

### What he cannot reach

Rifling and effective range (900–1,100 m), the copper obturating ring, steel practice, the two steam hulls (coastal, warm-water, never shown abroad), the electrical work, Fusang and the eastern crossings (disclosed to Rome and to no one else), the sealed Jinyang arithmetic, and any true total of powder stocks. He may hear at Zhenxi that the breech guns stopped burning their crews around 541 — a garrison rumour, unquantified, and easy to mistake for a new weapon when it is a new gasket.

## III. WHAT FLOWS BACK THE OTHER WAY

This is the crux, and the Chinese record concedes it in a single sentence entered in the empire's own spy file:

> "*Known weakness, entered in the file:* the Sogdians are Maurya subjects and sell in both directions. **The court has never priced what they carry out of the empire, and this reign did not price it either.**"

The channel is not one-way and never has been. Its content in each direction:

**Historically, Maurya → China, via this same trade.** The Sogdians interpreted the first contact at the Syr Darya in 200–225. Through them China learned that Parthia did not exist, that Bactria had been absorbed, that Ferghana had been Maurya for two centuries, and that a single continental empire ran from the Syr Darya to the Indian subcontinent. Every foundational fact China holds about the Maurya arrived down this pipe. The Maurya's own use of the channel is what taught China the size and shape of the Maurya realm.

**Now, China → Maurya.** Everything in section II, and one thing more valuable than any of it: **the shape of Chinese tasking.** The Dunhuang office asks its Sogdian factors how many Maurya hulls carry cannon and of what bore; whether any are rifled; whether saltpeter has risen in Maurya civilian markets, indicating production drawn into state hands; whether naval crews remain short-rationed in drills; and whether Bamo has been noticed. Any factor who sells that question list southward hands the Maurya court the Chinese estimate of the Maurya, in the Chinese court's own priorities: that China believes the Maurya hold the powder formula but not the production volume, that China is watching specifically for rifling, and that China's naval range advantage is understood by China itself to be on a clock of two generations and not three.

**Neither court has closed the channel, and both have tried to route around it.** China hired a Maurya interpreter in the 320s and immediately set him to teaching junior officials the language, so as not to leave the Maurya a window into China–Rome correspondence; it then learned Latin outright in the 340s and cut the Maurya out of that correspondence entirely. The Maurya, from their side, learned Chinese and wrote to China in it, which China noted as evidence of long deliberate study. Both empires have spent two centuries reducing dependence on the Sogdians without once being willing to do without them, because the trade is the only thing either can use.

**Has the Maurya court priced it?** Nothing in the Chinese record can say. What the record does establish is that the *Chinese* court has not, twice explicitly, and that the loyalty in question runs to neither empire. A man retained against a share of the transit toll he helps assess has a direct pecuniary interest in the northern road being found alarming. He is the ideal instrument for measuring it and the worst possible one for judging whether it matters.

## IV. WAS IT NOTICED

**Noticed as a category, at the highest level, in writing, and deliberately not acted upon. Not detected as an operation, because there is nothing to detect.**

Three separate acknowledgements stand in the Chinese record of this period:

1. The 537 leak assessment naming Sogdian carters as Maurya subjects and the empire's western intelligence as bought from the same trade — an estimate of *when* the leak lands, not a plan to stop it.
2. The spy-tasking file's known-weakness entry: sells in both directions, never priced.
3. The letter to the Maurya of 547, disclosing the powder increase pre-emptively because "you will hear a version of it from the carters if we do not."

China's counter-measure is not counter-intelligence. It is **cover rather than concealment** — publishing the reform as strengthening, announcing the rail-served increase first and loudest so the beyond-rail figure lands as an afterthought, drafting the withdrawal clause as a manoeuvre, and giving observation posts a fur-and-survey return so a post not expected to fight has a visible reason to exist. And it is **pre-emption**: tell the Maurya the fact before the carters do, so a secret discovered is not read as a preparation.

The emperor's own minute is the fairest statement of the outcome for both sides:

> "The empire is not hiding the conclusion, it is buying the years between the conclusion becoming visible and the conclusion becoming actionable, and those years are what the rail is for."

**Status of the asset at 550: intact, unsuspected as an individual, structurally anticipated as a class, and unimpeded.** No Chinese action in this period is directed at the channel. The empire has decided it is cheaper to be read than to be silent, and has priced the reading at zero — which the file admits is a price nobody has ever calculated.
