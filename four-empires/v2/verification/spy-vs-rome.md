# ADJUDICATION: FOREIGN INTELLIGENCE OPERATIONS AGAINST ROME, 525–550 AD

**Adjudicator's standing.** Neutral. Judged solely from Rome's own three files: `02-rome-alternate.md`, `human-turn-500-525/rome.md`, `v2/turn-525-550/rome.md`. The operators' hopes and claims were withheld from me deliberately and I have not inferred them. What follows is what the target's own reality permits to be taken from it.

---

## PART 0: THE TARGET'S COUNTER-INTELLIGENCE, ASSESSED HONESTLY

Any judgement of an operation against Rome in this period turns on one fact that Rome states about itself in plain language, and it is the most important paragraph in the file for this purpose.

**Rome's standing counter-intelligence instrument is the *officium custodiae*, founded 536.** It is fourteen years old at the moment of judgement. Rome's own declaration says: *"the first standing counter-intelligence instrument in Roman history; the record before 536 does not contain the concept."* For the first eleven years of the twenty-five-year window under review, Rome had no such body at all.

**What it actually does.** Its listed instruments are, without exception, domestic and western-facing:

- crew and passenger registers at the *named western ports* (Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, Portus Namnetum, Londinium, Dubris);
- licensing of chart-makers and chart-sellers;
- sealed sailing orders opened at sea;
- classification of western latitudes, soundings and coal reports;
- prior notification before publication touching the western voyages;
- from 547, an audit of the books of the *Societas Oceani Occidentalis* at Londinium.

**What it is unable to do.** It has no eastern desk. It has no register of foreign travellers. It has no watch on Roman Syria, no watch on the Euphrates crossing, no interest in the overland trade at all, and no counter-espionage function of any kind directed at a foreign network operating inside Roman territory. Its entire object is the *outward* leak of one specific category of information — western positions, soundings, charts and coal — and it lost even that fight in part: the prior-notification rule was withdrawn in 540 for prices, cargoes and losses after the *Acta Rhenana*, the *Diurna Londiniensia* and eleven other sheets refused registration and the Industrial Council and the Patriarch's men backed them. What was retained — positions, soundings, charts, coal — Rome's own record calls **unenforceable**.

**And Rome knows this.** The *officium*'s unpublished survey reached the conclusion that governs everything below:

> *an empire like this one cannot be sealed, because Rome's information does not leak through traitors but through the machinery that makes Rome work — competing newspapers, share markets, insurance underwriters who must be told what they insure, crews paid off in six ports, and an open foreign legation in the capital. Do not seal. Feed.*

This is a target that has audited its own porosity, found it structural, and abandoned suppression in favour of deception. But the deception is aimed at **one address only** — the turned Alexandria branch. There is no deception programme, no chaff, no controlled feed, and no watch of any kind pointed at Roman Syria or the Euphrates frontier.

**The environment being penetrated.** Rome at 550 is 72 percent literate empire-wide, with competing commercial gazettes in every major city, printed law, printed price sheets, printed proceedings of the Industrial Council entered under its right of prior consultation, printed medical tallies, printed clerical polemic, an insurance market that must be told what it insures, and a chartered monopoly company keeping open books. Rome's final marginal note in the file lists what it hands the press voluntarily: *"The gazettes get the coal, the bombards, the twenty-two-day passage, and rather more maize than the agronomists signed for."*

The practical consequence, which governs both operations: **against Rome in this period, the marginal value of a spy is very low, and the marginal value of a literate resident with a market stall is very high.** Nearly everything a foreign power wants to know about Rome is either printed or inferable from printed material, and the small residue that is not printed lives in places no cover of the kind described can reach.

# OPERATION A (MAURYA) — THE CTESIPHON CHURCH NETWORK

**Composition.** Mar Isḥaq, deacon of the Christian community of Ctesiphon; two men of his congregation; from 546, two Sogdian merchant factors on the Syrian road. Founded 542. Eight years old at most, four for the commercial half. Untested.

**Cover, assessed.** Sound, and better than the operators may realise. Christian traffic between the Mesopotamian sees and the congregations of Roman Syria is lawful and constant, and Rome has been a Christian empire in the imperial-patronage sense since 440–450. There is nothing anomalous to detect. But cover determines access, and this cover buys exactly one kind of access: **the road, the market, the garrison town seen from outside its gate, the congregation, and the printed sheet.** It does not buy the arsenal, the Consilium, the proof-house, or a chart.

One friction the operators may not have priced. Rome deliberately seated the Patriarchate at Rome rather than in the east, *because Rome controls that appointment and Alexandria does not*. A deacon of an eastern see circulating among Syrian congregations is therefore a figure of mild ecclesiastical interest — of interest to bishops, on a question of doctrinal regularity, not to any organ of the Roman state. This produces friction and correspondence, not exposure. It is noted here because it is the only Roman attention the network attracts at all, and it is the wrong kind.

## TARGET 1 — NOTICE TERMINATING THE OVERLAND TRADE TREATY

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

The network returns, across 547–550, a body of material of mixed value. In the words it would be received:

> *From the deacon, by way of the congregation at Antioch and the caravan factors of the crossing:*
>
> *The Roman market talk of the eastern road is bad and has been bad for a generation. At the Euphrates stations the caravans are fewer than the old men remember; the merchants say the eastern goods now go north to the river of the Scythians and south to the Egyptian sea, and that a man who trades on the Euphrates road trades on the remnant. They lay this to the doubling of the tariff, which they speak of with anger and name as the Indian's act, done without word given.*
>
> *In the summer following [546] a letter of the Emperor went east by this road with a courier of the post and an escort. It is spoken of at the stations and it was afterwards printed in the sheets of the great cities, and the deacon has had the substance of it read to him. In it the Emperor sets out that Rome gave the whole method of the fire-engine that moves a ship, that fourteen of his engineers went and built it, and that Rome has received nothing back that was promised; and he says — and this is the sentence that is repeated everywhere, and it is repeated as though it were the whole letter — that **Rome will open no second exchange until the first is closed**.*
>
> *The merchants of Antioch and Seleucia take this to mean that the Emperor has done with the eastern road. Several say plainly that notice has been given or will be given at the next term, and the factors of two houses say they will not renew their standing arrangements at the crossing on that account.*
>
> *The deacon adds, being careful: he has seen no such notice, and the customs men at the crossing, when asked in the ordinary way, know of none.*

That last line is the network's single best moment and it should be credited. The deacon reports the rumour as rumour and separates it from what he could check.

### WHAT IS TRUE

**Rome has given no notice and intends to give none.** The opposite is true, and demonstrably so from Rome's own record:

- The Maurya agreement, like the Han one, **renews itself automatically unless one party moves to change it**, so that continuation is the default and the burden of disruption falls on whoever wishes to break a working arrangement. Rome has not moved.
- Rome's letter of 546 asks for **a fixed-term tariff schedule by agreement** — *"at whatever rate"* — on the express ground that *"a merchant can trade against a high tariff. He cannot trade against an uncertain one."* This is an application to stabilise the treaty relationship, not to end it.
- Rome states the unbalanced ledger *"plainly and without threat, because a plain statement of an unbalanced ledger is worth more at this distance than a grievance."*
- The sentence the market has seized on — no second exchange until the first is closed — is a suspension of **new technology transfer**, not of trade. It is a restriction on a class of exchange the treaty does not govern.
- Rome simultaneously asks the Maurya for two large favours (the mathematics and astronomy entire; the apparatus of the elementary electrical demonstration) and *sends payment in advance without waiting for an answer* — telescope construction, the Venus and Jupiter observations, the pneumatic record, four hundred years of boiler metallurgy. A state does not send unrequited gifts down a road it is closing.

**The gap.** The operators receive a market inference — *Rome is finished with the eastern road* — attached to a correctly reported real sentence. The inference is wrong. The correct reading of Rome's posture is: cold, aggrieved, transactionally suspended on technology, and **committed to the trade**.

**The adjudicator's principal note on this target.** The Maurya court holds, or will hold within months of these reports, **the primary document itself**, carried by Rome's own courier to Pataliputra in 546–547. The network's product on Target 1 is therefore not intelligence but noise laid over a source the principal already possesses in the original. Where the report and the letter disagree, the letter is right. An operation that spends its access confirming, degrading, or contradicting a document its own government has been handed in full has spent its access badly. This is not a failure of the deacon. It is a failure of tasking.

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No, and Rome possesses no instrument that could detect it.** Asking a customs clerk at a frontier crossing whether a notice has been lodged is an ordinary merchant's question, asked daily by men with a genuine interest, at a station whose staff exist to answer exactly that. The *officium custodiae* has no eastern desk and would not receive the question even if it were reported. Nothing happens.

## TARGET 2 — THE LOCATION AND PRODUCTS OF THE OVERSEAS COLONY

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

Substantial, and almost none of it by espionage. Four channels feed it, and the largest is the Church.

> *From the deacon, in the third and fourth years:*
>
> *That the Romans have found lands across the western ocean is not a secret in any degree; it is in the sheets, it is in the customs law, and it is in the churches. I set down what can be established.*
>
> ***That it is west, and across the outer ocean.*** *The Emperor has laid a duty of one part in eight — since raised to one in five, and now settled again at one in eight for registered ships — on all goods of the western ocean at landing, and the law names the ports at which such goods may be landed and makes landing anywhere else contraband. Every port named is on the outer ocean or the northern sea: Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, Portus Namnetum, Londinium, Dubris, and lately two more in Hibernia and the north of Britain. Not one is on our sea. From the law alone, without any man's word, it follows that the thing lies west beyond the Pillars and is carried home to the Atlantic coasts and to Britain.*
>
> ***What it produces.*** *The same law names the goods: gold, cacao, cotton, hides, dyes, the burning leaf, and new dyewoods. To these the merchants add a bitter bean drunk as a hot drink, a fruit, and a pod of fire that is not pepper. Of these I have seen cacao and the burning leaf and a dyewood in the market at Antioch, at prices that mark them as new and dear. Gold is the thing spoken of most.*
>
> ***How far.*** *Six weeks' sail west from certain islands of the Emperor called the Fortunate Isles, on a wind and a current that carry a ship whether she will or no; and the return not by the same road but far to the north. This is common sailors' talk in every port and I do not think it false, but I have it from no Roman officer.*
>
> ***That there are more lands than islands.*** *The churches are the best witness and I have this at first hand. There is a bishop at a place called Ozama, on the greater island, and his letter of the year before last was read in the basilicas at Rome and printed in four cities, and it has been read aloud in Antioch. It accuses the Emperor's own economy of killing the people it employs. The subject peoples of the islands are dying of a sickness the Roman physicians cannot name and their quarantines cannot stop; the letter says the villages empty; the numbers are printed by the Roman officials themselves and the western clergy publish them. From above four hundred thousand at the conquest to some tens of thousands now.*
>
> ***And therefore this, which I judge the most useful thing I have.*** *The gold works are stopping for want of hands. Rome has licensed a house of Ostia to buy men through Alexandria and has written to Egypt to ask leave. The Roman labour in the west is failing and Rome has gone to the Egyptian to be saved from it.*

> *From the Sogdian factors, 547–550, out of the Alexandria shipping talk that reaches every Syrian port:*
>
> *The Roman islands are the whole of the matter and the rest is little. There is a northern coast the Romans have been at, but the reports of it are disappointing and the coal there is poor; the place they have built on that coast is a timber station and no more. The Romans were to give up the fort on the stone shore and the port has taken it as settled these several years.*

### WHAT IS TRUE

**The deacon's product is largely correct, and the Sogdians' product is Roman disinformation manufactured for Egypt and received sideways.**

True:

- The possessions are Insula Aurea (Hispaniola, reduced 531), Insula Longa (Cuba, 536), Insula Sancti Iohannis (533), Insula Fontium (537); Castra Cuzamil, the fortified island off the Yucatan shore; Statio Chetumalis, the walled trading post; the Insulae Fortunatae and Insulae Accipitrum as waypoints; and **Portus Severianus and Colonia Carbonaria on the Flumen Novum**.
- Products: gold, cacao, cotton, hides, dyes, tobacco, dyewoods — the deacon has the customs schedule verbatim and it is accurate.
- The dying is real, worse than reported, and Rome prints it: above 400,000 to under 70,000 between conquest and 550. The bishop of Ozama's letter of 546 is real, was read in three basilicas at Rome and printed in four cities, and *changed no policy*.
- The African labour application through Alexandria is real, is Rome's declared intent, and is stated in Rome's own letter to Egypt of 545. The gold works are stopping for want of hands. This is accurately obtained and it is the most valuable single item the network takes.

False or inverted, and knowably so:

- **The northern coast is the opposite of disappointing.** Bituminous coal was found in workable seams within eight miles of navigable water above the fall line of the Flumen Novum in autumn 543. Rome's own file calls that report *"the most important document produced in the turn."* Pits from 545; 11,000 tons raised in 549; the first stationary steam engine west of the ocean turning at Colonia Carbonaria in 550. Rome has moved the centre of the entire western project off the gold islands and onto that coal.
- **Portus Severianus is not a timber station.** It is a walled fleet coaling and repair station with a shipyard from 547, and it is the reason a Roman steam warship can now take coal in western waters at all.
- **Rome is not abandoning Castra Cuzamil.** That item was the deliberate false test-item passed into the compromised Alexandria branch in 537 to establish whether the branch was turned. Rome held Cuzamil throughout and ran the whole Yucatan reconnaissance out of it, 528–542.

**The mechanism of the contamination, which the operators cannot see and their principal should be told.** Rome runs a turned branch of its own Alexandria network as a **deliberate feed** from 540. The items passed 539–550 are, verbatim from Rome's declaration: *the intended abandonment of Castra Cuzamil; island commitment overstated; northern-coast commitment understated; coal reports called disappointing; Portus Severianus called a timber station.* Every false element in the Sogdians' product is an item off that list. The feed was manufactured for Egypt. It entered the Alexandrian port rumour, and Alexandrian port rumour is the common water of every eastern Mediterranean harbour. It has reached a Mauryan network in Syria that Rome does not know exists, aimed at a power Rome was not addressing, and it will be believed in Pataliputra for the same reason it was designed to be believed in Alexandria.

Rome cannot model this and does not try. Its own memorandum concedes it does not know whether any of the feed is believed even by its intended target. Third-party contamination is beyond the instrument entirely.

**Not obtained, and not obtainable by this cover:** positions, latitudes, soundings, charts. These are the one category Rome does classify. A churchman on the Syrian road has no route to them, and would have nothing to do with them if he had — the distance from Antioch to the Fortunate Isles alone forecloses verification. What he brings is direction, distance in weeks, product list, and political condition. That is a great deal, and it is all open-source.

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No.** Every element the deacon obtained was published by Rome or by Rome's own clergy. The *officium custodiae* fought the gazettes over precisely this and lost in 540. Rome's own conclusion — *do not seal, feed* — is an admission that this class of loss is not detectable because it is not a breach.

**Would Rome notice and not act?** Yes, in a specific and instructive sense. Rome *has already noticed the category*. The entire *officium* exists because of the 523 conclusion that word of the western islands reached Alexandria within months. Rome audited how such a thing could travel, found the answer was its own machinery, and elected not to act, because the acts available — licensing printers, sealing crews, silencing bishops — cost more than the leak. The Britannic customs crisis of 542–547 taught the same lesson in fiscal form. Rome tolerates the leak knowingly. A deacon reading a printed customs schedule is inside a hole Rome measured, priced, and decided to leave open.

## TARGET 3 — THE ONE-MAN FIREARM

This is the target on which the operation's whole character is decided, and it must be adjudicated in three movements: what Rome has, where it physically is, and what the man will therefore mistake.

### WHAT ROME ACTUALLY POSSESSES IN 550

**Rome has no hand firearm. Rome has entered a formal finding that a hand firearm is impossible.**

The chronology from Rome's own file:

- The gunpowder programme reached a stable ratio in 531 and became a weapons programme in 532.
- It went where two centuries of doctrine pointed — an emplaced vessel — because Rome has built fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers since 301 and its engineers reached for the shape they knew.
- First cast-bronze bombard at Argentoratum, 538.
- Grained powder established at the Argentoratum proof-house 541–544 by chasing an anomaly nobody could explain; a third to a half more range and disproportionately more against masonry. This is what turns a gate-breaker into a wall-breaker.
- **A hand weapon was attempted once.** A *fabricator* of the Argentoratum works, Vibius Crispinus, argued in 543 that a small tube would do what a large one did, and made three. The second burst on its ninth firing and killed him. **The finding entered in 544: the metal cannot be thin enough to carry and strong enough to hold; the hand weapon is a blind alley.**
- Rome's own record says the finding is wrong and says why it will stand anyway: the Han told Rome in the 480s that their infantry carry the thing, and Rome believes them and has reoriented an entire military assessment around it — *but Rome has no basis of its own for believing the metallurgy possible, the one man who might have persisted is dead, and the programme has a battery of successful bombards to point at instead.*

**The specific irony, which the adjudicator records because it is the true shape of this target.** The construction the operators believe was given to Rome in the previous generation was not given. The exchange of 528 ran the other way and in a different subject: Rome sent fourteen engineers and the complete working method of the marine steam engine to Barygaza, and **Rome has entered no receipt of the firearm account promised against it.** Rome's letter of 546 says so in writing, plainly and without threat. Rome does not have the firearm because nobody ever sent it the firearm.

### WHERE THE WEAPONS PHYSICALLY ARE

**Artillery.** Emplaced batteries of cast-bronze bombards at seven positions by 550: **Borysthenia** (the Dnieper), **the Dacian corridor forts**, **Gades**, **Gibraltar**, **Cyrenaica**, and **Portus Severianus** (across the western ocean). The guns stand on stone.

**Not one of these is in Syria. Not one is on the Euphrates. Not one is anywhere a traveller between Ctesiphon and Antioch will pass.** The nearest position to the network's entire operating area is Cyrenaica, across the sea and half the length of North Africa away.

**At sea: nothing.** Not one gun has gone to sea, by decision minuted in 547. The fifty-two warships carry ram, ballista, steam grenade and the old bow launcher, exactly as in 525. The reason is the oxidiser: seven fortress batteries consume a few score tons a year, while the *magister classis* put a single hard season of a ten-ship gunned squadron above Rome's entire annual refined output of about 1,100 tons of nitre yielding some 1,500 tons of grained powder. **Rome's guns are limited by dung, not by founding.**

**Powder and nitre.** The proof-house is at Argentoratum on the Rhine. The *cura nitri* sits there. The 280 nitre-beds are at the remount depots and great post stations of **Gaul, the Rhine and Pannonia**. All of it is a thousand miles and more from the Syrian road.

### WHO WOULD EVER SEE ONE

In Roman Syria and on the Euphrates frontier: **nobody**. There is no gun there to see. There is no powder mill, no proof-house, no bed, no battery.

What there *is* on that frontier, and has been for a hundred and fifty years, is the older Roman kit that gunpowder has not yet replaced anywhere: **cart-mounted steam launchers, placed at major river crossings and fortified positions** since the fourth century; **steam grenades delivered by catapult**; ballistae; and, standard since about 430, **tethered observation balloons at frontier and harbour stations**.

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

A man told to look for a weapon he cannot describe will report something. He reports three things: one true and public, one true and unasked-for, and one false.

> *From the deacon, 548–550:*
>
> ***First, what is printed and not denied.*** *The Romans have a powder that burns with great violence and they have made great guns of cast bronze to throw stone by it. This is in the sheets and is boasted of. The guns are said to break a wall in hours where the old engines took weeks. They are set in fixed batteries at the great places of the frontiers and the straits, and I am told they stand upon stone and are not moved. I could name four or five such places from what is printed. None is in Syria and none is on this frontier, and I have asked and looked and there is nothing of the kind here.*
>
> ***Second, and I did not go looking for it.*** *There is a law of the Emperor of some ten years' standing over the salt that gathers on the walls of old stables and cellars. Licensed men may enter any stable, cellar, byre, dovecote or post-house and scrape the floor, paying a fixed price, and the owner may not refuse and may not floor it over. Every man who keeps horses complains of it and they complain freely. And all such salt when refined must be sold to the treasury at a set price, and to sell it, move it or carry it out of the empire by any other hand is death. The factors confirm that it is asked after at the crossing among the forbidden goods. I do not know the use of this salt. I set it down because the Romans have made a capital matter of one substance and of no other, and because a state does not hang men over a stable sweeping for nothing.*
>
> ***Third, on the weapon your lordships named.*** *I cannot say I have seen it and I will not say I have. But at [a fortified crossing of the Euphrates] I was kept at the gate some hours in the year 549 and saw the soldiers about a tube of metal upon a cart, with a fire beneath it and men feeding the fire a long while before anything was done, and afterwards a great roar and a rush of white smoke and a thing thrown out of it further than a bow. The soldiers spoke of it as a common thing of the station. It is not carried by one man; four could not lift it and it goes upon a cart. If the Romans have a smaller kind that a man carries, I have not found a soldier who has seen one, and I have asked in three garrison towns. One optio, in drink, said the fire-tubes were all of them useless in the field because of the long waiting for the fire, which I take to be a soldier's complaint and not a secret.*

### WHAT IS TRUE

**On the first item: correct.** Rome has powder and bombards, the bombards are emplaced on stone at seven positions, none in Syria, and the grained powder of 544 is what makes them wall-breakers. Rome published this. The deacon's report is accurate because Rome handed the gazettes the bombards on purpose.

**On the second item: correct, unasked for, and the most valuable thing either operation obtains.** The *cura nitri* is real, dated 539, and its three limbs are exactly as reported — the right of the scrape at a fixed price per weight of raw earth with the owner obliged to permit and forbidden to floor over; the beds; and the **imperial pre-emption**, all refined nitre to the fisc, private sale, movement or export a capital matter. Rome's own summary: *"Rome nationalised nitre and nothing else."* Rome's closing note names the nitre establishment as **"the only secret in this file that anyone could act on,"** and withholds it from the gazettes.

The deacon did not penetrate that secret. He walked into its edge, which was never concealable, because a right of entry exercised against every stable-keeper in the western provinces and an export prohibition enforced at every customs post are laws that must be published to function. What he cannot obtain, and did not: **the output figure of about 1,100 tons refined, the 1,500 tons of grained powder, the two-year war reserve, and the *praefectus nitri*'s estimate that the beds laid down since 539 will not carry a fleet before the 570s.** Those numbers are the actionable part and they live in a Consilium minute.

To a principal that already fields land cannon on the Syr Darya, the nitre law is legible on sight, and it says: *Rome has powder, Rome's constraint is oxidiser, and Rome is building against that constraint on a multi-year cycle.* That is a strategically true and correctly dated finding, obtained by accident, off the commercial cover, on a question nobody asked.

**On the third item: a true observation, correctly described, and a wrong category.** What the deacon saw is a **cart-mounted steam launcher** — Roman equipment since 301, matured toward cart mounting across the fourth century, and explicitly *placed at major river crossings and fortified positions*. Every detail he reports is diagnostic of it and against gunpowder: **the fire beneath**, **the long pre-heating**, and the optio's complaint that the long wait makes them useless in the field. Rome's own file says the same thing in its own words — *its own launchers are slow and must be pre-heated*. A bombard has no fire beneath it and no waiting. The deacon described a steam weapon with complete accuracy and had no framework to name it.

To the deacon's real credit, he refused to convert it into the thing he was sent for. He recorded that four men could not lift it, that it goes on a cart, and that no soldier in three garrison towns had seen a smaller kind. A less disciplined man, or a man under pressure to produce, turns that same sighting into *the Romans have the weapon and I have seen it fire.*

**The truth that will not be obtained, at any price, by this operation or any operation of this shape:**

1. Rome has no hand firearm.
2. Rome tried once, in 543, and killed Vibius Crispinus doing it.
3. Rome entered a **formal finding in 544 that the hand weapon is impossible**, and that finding will stand — not because it is true, but because the one man who might have persisted is dead and the programme has successful bombards to point at.
4. Rome believes the Han carry the weapon, has reoriented its entire military assessment around that belief, and has no expectation whatever of building one.

This is a proof-house file at Argentoratum on the Rhine, held by a programme whose whole political interest is in pointing at its bombards. It is not in the gazettes, it is not in a Syrian market, and it is not in a congregation. **No cover of the kind described reaches it.**

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No.** A traveller held at a gate and watching a routine drill is not an intelligence event. Questions in garrison towns about whether the soldiers have a smaller fire-tube are the kind of question a curious civilian asks in a literate empire whose newspapers print the bombards. There is no organ of the Roman state that collects such a report, and the *officium custodiae* would not have jurisdiction if there were.

**One genuine hazard, and its correct resolution.** The single act in this operation that could kill a man is any attempt by the Sogdian factors to **buy, carry, or procure refined nitre**, for which the penalty is capital and the enforcement point is the frontier customs at the crossing, which is real and staffed. On the record before me the factors asked after it among the forbidden goods and did not attempt to acquire it. That distinction is the whole of their survival. Asking a customs clerk what may not be exported is a merchant's legitimate business; carrying an ounce of the one nationalised substance in the empire is a hanging. **Not caught, narrowly, and by a margin the operators may not know they had.**

# OPERATION B (MAURYA, PASSIVE) — RESIDENT SUBJECTS AND TRAVELLERS, 525–550

**Scope as put to me.** Not the fourteen Roman engineers in the Maurya yards — that is the reverse direction and not adjudicated here. The question is the reciprocal: what a foreign power's own resident subjects, merchants and travellers in Roman Syria and at the Euphrates frontier could observe of Rome **incidentally, without tasking, over twenty-five years**.

The answer is: **more than Operation A obtained, on every axis except the one that was tasked.**

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

**1. The eastern frontier is static, and this is a twenty-five-year measurement.**

The single most valuable intelligence product available anywhere in this file, and it requires no agent, no cover and no risk — only a merchant who uses the same road for a working lifetime and remembers.

Observable: garrison strengths at the Euphrates stations, unchanged; no new fortification programme; no siege train moving east; no rail built toward Syria (Rome's military rail runs Dacia-to-Dnieper and Dnieper-to-Rhine, and stops there); no massing, no depots, no requisition of transport, no unusual grain movement. Twenty-five years of nothing.

True: *"Not one mile taken and not one lost. No Roman soldier has crossed the Dnieper or the Euphrates in the twenty-five years."* Rome's military line is 62 percent of revenue and is climbing — but it is climbing into the Han arms race, the iron-hull and screw programme, and the western garrisons. Rome's free margin is 7.7 percent. Rome cannot borrow, has no bond market and no conception of one, and holds reserves of a few months' revenue that one serious war erases. Rome's standing strategic arithmetic, unchanged for a century and a half, is that it cannot concentrate against any one peer without baring itself to the other two.

**A traveller cannot see the ledger. He can see the absence of an army, for twenty-five years, and that is the same conclusion by a road he can walk.**

**2. Rome's finances, constitution and internal politics, in print.**

A literate resident with a subscription obtains, without a single illicit act: the *vectigal occidentale* and its named ports; the *indictio maritima*; the sale of tax-farming advances; the three-year public fight over the *collatio fabricarum*; the **Concordia Fabricarum of 534** entire — chartered companies with legal personality and bounded liability, the twenty-year *privilegium* on registered methods held in a printed register at Rome, and the Industrial Council's converted right of prior consultation with its objections *entered in the record and printed*; the collegia settlement and the inspectorate; the accident fund; the Patriarch's homily; the emperor's public undertaking not to touch the coin.

He further obtains, because the parties fought it in print: the Britannic customs crisis. The petition of the Londinium houses on the argument that produced a usurpation in 355; the raise from one-eighth to one-fifth in 542; the seizure of three hulls at Portus Magnus in 546; a crowd taking the crews off while the port magistrates declined to disperse it; a provincial governor declining to send troops against his own province's shipping and writing to Rome to say so; and the **Constitutio Oceanica of 547**, which puts the whole of Rome's ocean commerce into one chartered monopoly, seated at Londinium, with its own capital, its own ships and its own men.

The true reading is available to any attentive reader and Rome's own court has not made it: *Rome cannot enforce a fiscal law against Britannia without a naval action against its own province, at a moment when every ocean-capable hull it owns is committed to the western run.* Rome's file states flatly that **not one paper in the Consilium's record connects the Constitutio Oceanica to the events of 355.** A foreign reader is under no such handicap.

**3. Rome's technical position, from published law and standing orders.**

- Bombards and grained powder: handed to the gazettes.
- The *cura nitri*: legible from the right of scrape and the export prohibition.
- **The compass deviation table**: from 548 a printed, per-hull, sworn document required by the *magister classis* before an iron hull is cleared for ocean service. It tells any reader that Rome has iron-framed hulls in ocean service, in enough numbers to need a fleet regulation, and that Rome has **no theory of why iron pulls a needle**.
- The twenty-two-day passage of *Aquila Occidentalis* in 550, printed — and printed with Rome's own correction that it is a sailing passage with an engine aboard and **not** a steam crossing. The correction is more informative than the boast: it discloses that Rome's steam range does not cross an ocean.
- The maize claim in the gazettes, and the *curatores agrorum* declining to sign it. A merchant who watches the warm provinces and the Danube trial stations learns the truth the gazettes obscured: warm provinces yes, Pannonia and Dacia not for a generation to three.
- The medical failure: printed tallies of a subject population falling by five-sixths in eighteen years, with no physician able to say why, published by Rome's own officials and amplified by its own clergy.

**4. Roman market and price data, continuously, for twenty-five years.**

Prices, cargoes and losses were the categories the *officium* was forced to release in 540 after losing to the gazettes. Insurance underwriters must be told what they insure. Crews are paid off in six ports. A merchant house on the Syrian road reading Roman price sheets tracks the western trade's volume, the shift of eastern goods from the Euphrates to the Dnieper and the Red Sea, the coming of cacao and tobacco and dyewoods, the state of the grain supply, and the cost of the Han arms race as it shows up in metal prices.

**5. What the untasked observer cannot get.**

The same residue that defeated Operation A, and for the same reason — it lives in files, not in the world:

- The hand-firearm finding of 544.
- The nitre output figures and the 570s date.
- The decision not to arm the fleet, and its stated reason.
- Western positions, soundings, charts, and the coal reports — classified, and the one classification Rome retained.
- The Alexandria feed and the fact that Rome believes a branch turned.
- The Consilium's own strategic self-assessments.

### WHAT IS TRUE

Everything in items 1 through 4 above is true as stated, and it is true because Rome published it, argued it in public, or could not conceal it without ceasing to function. The gap between the passive observer's picture and Rome's real position is **narrow on politics, finance and posture, and wide only on the specific negative findings Rome itself has buried in proof-house and Consilium minutes.**

The one systematic distortion available to a passive observer is the same one that caught Operation A: the Alexandria feed, which enters the general port rumour of the eastern Mediterranean and is indistinguishable from ordinary commercial talk. A passive observer with twenty-five years of continuity is, however, better placed than the deacon to catch it, because he can notice that the volume of shipping, the yard activity, and the price of western goods do not behave as a disappointing colony would make them behave.

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No, and the concept of detecting it does not exist in the Roman record.**

There is no register of foreign residents. There is no restriction on foreign subjects travelling the Syrian road or trading at the Euphrates crossing — the crossing exists to be traded across, under a treaty with waystations and merchant protections that Rome is actively trying to stabilise. There is no censorship of the gazettes except the withdrawn western rule. The *officium custodiae* watches western ports, chart-sellers, and one chartered company in Londinium.

**Would Rome notice and not act? Yes — and this is the sharpest point in the whole adjudication.**

Rome has noticed. The *officium*'s own unpublished survey of 536–540 **is** the finding that Operation B describes: that Rome's information leaks through competing newspapers, share markets, insurance underwriters, crews paid off in six ports, and an open foreign legation in the capital. Rome examined its own transparency, understood it precisely, and **declined to close it**, for three reasons all present in the record:

1. **It tried and lost.** The prior-notification rule of 536 was beaten in four years by thirteen sheets, the Industrial Council and the Patriarch's men, on the argument that a magistrate who can suppress a shipwreck can suppress a peculating governor.
2. **The machinery is the empire.** The printed law, the printed price sheet, the open books, the insurable cargo and the literate workforce are not incidental to Roman power; they are the mechanism of it. The *collatio* produced Rome's first true industrial census precisely because Rome counts and prints. Sealing the leak means dismantling the instrument.
3. **It chose the cheaper answer.** *Do not seal. Feed.* Rome's whole counter-intelligence doctrine at 550 is one deception channel aimed at one address, run on the explicit reasoning that *a feed costs Rome nothing but ink.*

So Rome sees the hole, has measured it, has priced closing it at more than it will pay, and has left it open on purpose. **A foreign power's untasked residents are exploiting a vulnerability that Rome has formally studied, formally accepted, and formally decided to live with.** There is nothing to detect because Rome is not looking, and Rome is not looking because it decided that looking was not worth what looking costs.

# THE ADJUDICATOR'S SUMMARY

**Neither operation was caught. Neither could have been, by any instrument Rome possesses at 550.**

**Operation A** returned three things: a true negative wrapped in a wrong market inference, on a question its own government could answer better from a letter in its own archive; a substantially true open-source picture of the western possessions, poisoned in its commercial half by disinformation Rome manufactured for a different power entirely; and, on the firearm, a correctly-described steam launcher it could not name, a correctly-reported public bombard programme, and — by accident, on no tasking — the nitre monopoly, which is the most strategically diagnostic thing anyone obtained. The decisive truth, that Rome has no hand firearm and has formally ruled it impossible, was never within reach of a churchman on the Syrian road and never will be.

**Operation B**, which cost nothing and was tasked with nothing, outperformed it on every axis but one: Rome's finances, its constitutional settlement with industry, its labour bargain, its Britannic weakness, its published technical state, and — above all — twenty-five continuous years of an entirely static eastern frontier.

The lesson the target's own files teach is the one Rome wrote down about itself in 540 and then failed to draw the full consequence of: **against a printing, insuring, litigating, gazette-reading empire, presence beats espionage, and the only things worth spying for are the things that were never written down for anyone — a dead *fabricator*'s finding, a nitre clerk's estimate, and a line on a chart.**

*Adjudicated on the target's files alone. Where Rome's record is silent, this adjudication is silent.*
