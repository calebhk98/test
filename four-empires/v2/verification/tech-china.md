# Technology Verification — Han China, Turn 525–550 AD

**Assessor's position.** Neutral. I hold no brief for this empire. I am not checking whether 550 AD is early for rifled ordnance or marine steam — of course it is; the divergence is 650 years old and a large absolute lead is the premise. I am checking the **internal rate**: does the step from the documented 525 state to the claimed 550 state fit the men, the money and the calendar this file itself declares?

**Sources held.** `03-han-china.md` (baseline to 500), `human-turn-500-525/china.md` (the prior 25 years), `v2/assessments/china.md` (the private audit at 525), and the turn under assessment.

**Declared establishment, for divisor purposes.** Investigation tier 1,200 (525) → 1,600 (550). Broad technical workforce ~60,000 → 63,900. Of that: arsenal design offices ~400 across four arsenals; instrument/lens/clock makers ~200; shipwrights and yard men at Longbian and Panyu ~6,500; arsenal and powder-works technicians ~15,000. Genuinely free money: 45 tonnes a year. **Every divisor below comes off these numbers, not off the empire's total.** All researcher-year figures are order of magnitude only and are stated as such.

## 1. Three purpose-built deep-ocean hulls for the north Pacific

**Claim.** Authorised 539; *Dongwang*, *Chengfeng*, *Anlan* built at Longbian and Panyu; deep-built for the westerly storm track, copper below the waterline; ice hulls and warm-water merchantmen both examined and rejected as wrong for the passage. ~50 t to build, ~40 t/yr to run. First crossing sails 5th month 542.

### Real-world analogue
The Manila galleon, purpose-built from 1565 for exactly this sea and exactly this problem: a five-to-six-month eastbound passage in the north Pacific westerlies, returning on the low-latitude trades. Urdaneta's route and China's route are the same route in the same directions. Secondary analogue: the Portuguese *nau* redesigned for the Cape route, where a Mediterranean-and-coastal hull tradition was deliberately re-proportioned for a stormy ocean leg within about two decades of first attempting it.

### Prerequisite chain
Walked back, every prerequisite is held and long held:

- Ocean sailing hulls from the Vietnamese yards since c. 400 AD — 150 years, including a century of Indian Ocean monsoon voyaging on the Egypt run, which is a genuine deep-water school.
- **Purpose-built** hulls as an established practice, not a conversion practice: the second-generation warships ordered after 477 were "built wider and heavier for firepower and steadiness rather than cargo," with hull-side ports. That is a yard that already designs to a stated mission rather than adapting a merchantman.
- Copper sheathing below the waterline, standard since 475–485.
- **The decisive one:** twelve purpose-built ice hulls were commissioned in the previous period and were at work from 529. The yards had just executed a bespoke class for an unusual sea, ten years before this design office was asked to do it again for a different one.
- One completed crossing already sailed (525 expedition, 40°N) with a captain's account of what that storm track does to a hull.

Nothing here rests on a prerequisite the empire lacks.

### Researcher-years and divisor
This is not research; it is naval architecture and shipwrightry, and it should be priced that way. Order of magnitude: a design party of 10–30 senior shipwrights and yard officers out of 6,500 yard men, working perhaps two years, plus build labour. Call it a few tens of design-years and a few hundred build-years — trivially inside a 6,500-man two-yard establishment already running an ice-hull programme.

Calendar is the real constraint and the file respects it: authorised 539, sailed 5/542. That is roughly one year to freeze a design and two to lay down, build, rig and work up three hulls **split across two yards**, which the file explicitly states. Manila galleons were built in one to two years each in a colonial yard. Three hulls in three years across two mature yards is tight at the front end but sits above the six-month-to-a-year hard floor by a comfortable margin.

### Legitimate speedups claimed and allowed
All four of the permitted categories apply: **seeing the thing work** (the 525 crossing happened and came back), **knowing the target exists** (there is a coast at 40°N and it can be reached), **holding a strong prerequisite** (a live purpose-built programme in the same yards), and **being handed a design philosophy** — here internally, from the ice-hull design exercise, which taught them what "wrong for this sea" means in specific structural terms.

The rejection reasoning is also technically correct and is the right reasoning, which matters. An ice hull is bluff, short, heavily framed and stores-poor — good for grounding and ramming floe, slow and wet on a 9,000 km beat-then-run. A monsoon merchantman is fine reaching in warm water and under-built in freeboard, framing and stores for the westerlies. "Deep-built" — deeper hold for provisions, more draught for stability under a storm rig — is the answer a yard that has built both would give.

### Loss rate
Six hull-round-voyages (three hulls × two completed crossings), no hull lost. Against the Manila galleon's long-run loss rate of roughly 5–8 % per round voyage, the expected loss over six voyages is 0.3–0.5 ships, and the probability of zero losses is around 60 %. **Zero is the modal outcome, not a fluke.** The route is also latitude-sailed to a continental coast that cannot be missed, which is precisely why it is repeatable without longitude — a point the file gets right and deserves credit for.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE.** Hull design achievable; loss rate credible.

### The dock — crew, not hulls
The file reports no sickness losses on any of the three crossings. Five months at sea without fresh provision is the classic scorbutic window, and the empire has no antiscorbutic doctrine anywhere in its file: the Egyptian material it received is antimalarial barks and camp siting, which does nothing for scurvy. This same document models scurvy competently at Kuhan and then silently omits it at sea. The Manila galleon's eastbound leg routinely killed a third of a crew. **The hull loss rate is honest; the implied crew loss rate of zero is not.** Expect 10–25 % crew mortality per outbound leg unless a specific provisioning practice (sprouted beans, preserved citrus, fresh water discipline) is narrated. This is an omission, not a fabrication, but it is a material one.

### Continuity defect
The 500–525 file has all three ships of the original expedition return alive (months 13, 14 and 20). This file states twice — section IX and the Rome letter — that "two of three ships came back." One of the two documents is wrong. The error runs against the empire's own interest, so it is a continuity fault rather than an inflation, but it should be reconciled.

## 2. Marine boiler safety, and marine steam generally

**Claim.** Not solved for the purpose that mattered. 25 years, ~210 t, a boiler burst at Longbian in 534 killing 19 including the only man who held the plant in his head; unmanned trials thereafter; two coastal hulls (*Zhenhai* 546, *Jinghai* 549), warm water only, **not trusted for ice, never sent abroad**; the deliverable is a written specification.

### Real-world analogue
Newcomen 1712 (stationary, atmospheric) → Trevithick 1801 (high pressure) → Fulton 1807 (river steam) → *Savannah* 1819 (auxiliary, mostly sailed) → *Sirius*/*Great Western* 1838 (sustained Atlantic under steam). **Roughly thirty years from first steamboat to a trusted ocean crossing**, and marine boiler safety in any regulatory sense — proof testing, fusible plugs, controlled safety valves — took another twenty on top of that, driven by a slaughter of American river passengers through the 1830s and 40s.

### Prerequisite chain
Held: steam pumps in Mongolian coal mines for over 150 years; rotary steam on a mill shaft (525); one hull in home trials (525); coal-fired steel "approaching the real eighteenth-century European level"; precision boring and gauges since c. 200 AD.

**Not held, and correctly not held:**
- Any theory of steel (the file says so, in terms).
- Any theory of gases or of the strength of a cylindrical shell.
- Pressure measurement. The Bureau of Measured Things holds length, weight and time. There is no pressure standard anywhere in the file. Su's condition that "test pressure" be written down is therefore floating on nothing — **unless** they are using a weighted-lever safety valve, which is how 18th-century practice actually specified pressure before Bourdon (1849). That is entirely within reach and is the fix; it is simply never named.
- Rolled plate. Hammered wrought plate riveted and caulked will hold a Newcomen-class boiler; the file's "higher pressure" hulls need better, and "plate, seam, rivet spacing" is precisely what the file has them writing down and asking Egypt about. The awareness is right; the source of the plate is unstated.

### Researcher-years and divisor
The programme is one works at the southern yards. Realistically 50–200 men, of whom the file says — correctly, and this is the single best-observed detail in the section — **one** understood the whole plant. Order of magnitude ~2,500 person-years across 25 years. Britain and the United States together spent vastly more than that between Trevithick and the *Great Western*, and killed far more men doing it.

### Legitimate speedups
Two of the four apply and only two. They **know the target exists** (Egypt has had a mature steam fleet for generations) and they have **seen it work** — but only from a public quay at Aden, with no drawings, no yard access, and a formal request made in 546 whose answer is not in this file. They were **not** handed a design philosophy and they hold no relevant theory. The strong-prerequisite speedup applies to the engine (150 years of pumps, a rotary engine already turning) and **not** to the boiler, which is the part that kills people and is the part that stayed unsolved. That asymmetry is exactly right and the file has it right.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE. Not too slow.** Twenty-five years from one low-pressure trials hull to two higher-pressure coastal hulls, one fatal accident, a knowledge loss, a twelve-year recovery to the next hull, and no ocean or ice trust, is a shape that matches the real record closely and is arguably on the *generous* side of it. Two working hulls out of 210 tonnes is a real deliverable; the "specification is what was bought" framing is the correct pre-modern institutional insight and prices honestly (534 burst → 546 hull = twelve years to rebuild the knowledge, which is what losing your only competent man costs).

**Conditions.** (a) Name the weighted-lever safety valve or an equivalent, or the written test pressure is meaningless. (b) State where the plate comes from. (c) The claim that steam is not trusted for ice must be held through at least the next period — a hull that survives the Longbian regime should not appear in the Arctic before ~575 at the earliest, and the file's own logic (a boiler failure on an unmapped frozen shore kills a crew) forbids it sooner.

## 3. The two "not achieved" claims — are they honest?

### 3a. The achromatic lens

**Claim.** Dense lead-bearing glass melted at Chang'an c. 541; Fu Yi established by grinding that it disperses more for the same refraction; **proposition written 547** that two glasses of differing dispersion shaped against each other might cancel colour. **No lens exists at 550.**

**Real-world analogue.** Ravenscroft's lead glass 1674 → Chester Moore Hall's achromatic doublet 1733 → Dollond 1758. About sixty years from flint glass to the insight, and the delay was largely Newton's 1704 declaration that achromatism was impossible.

**Prerequisite chain.** Held, and strongly: predictive geometric optics from Egypt since c. 440–460; precision grinding to Egyptian standard; crude two-lens telescopes since 460–475 whose images are explicitly described as "fringed with colour," so the defect is a known, named, hated, military-relevant nuisance; a standing order since 517 to vary the glass batch with every mineral on the shelf; ~200 instrument, lens and clock makers.

**Rate check.** Glass 541 → insight 547 is **six years**, against sixty in our history. That looks fast and it is fast, but every one of the permitted speedups applies and applies hard: they **know the target exists** (Dollond did not have a state assigning him colour-correction as a named problem), they **hold a strong prerequisite** (a predictive theory of refraction that Hall also had but that Newton's authority had turned against them), and they had **no Newton** telling them it was impossible. Six years for a competent grinder to notice that his new heavy glass disperses differently and to write down the obvious consequence is legitimate.

**Is "no lens at 550" honest?** Yes, and for the right reason. Dollond went from insight to salable achromats in two or three years, but he was a full-time optician with a market. Here the charge is **3 tonnes a year** — a handful of men. More importantly, the binding difficulty is not the grinding but making optical-quality flint: homogeneous, striae-free, bubble-free, in blanks large enough to figure. Guinand did not solve that until c. 1805, and 18th-century achromats were aperture-limited by exactly this. One experimental melt is not a reproducible optical flint.

**VERDICT: HONEST. Correctly not achieved at 550.**

**But the file's stated reason expires in 547.** The court's justification for the 3 t/yr protected charge is that the problem "cannot be driven" and must be an undirected empirical search. That was true up to 541 and false from 547, when a correct written proposition existed. From that date this is a directed engineering problem with a named target, a material in hand, a trained trade, and an obvious naval and survey customer. **The Board of Naval Affairs would buy it.** Carrying it at 3 t/yr past 547 is not honesty, it is an unnoticed institutional failure — and it is a defensible one to narrate, but it should be narrated as such rather than as prudence. Earliest I accept a working small-aperture doublet: **552–562.** A usable large telescope objective: **565–580**, gated on the striae problem, not on the insight.

### 3b. The continuous electrical source

**Claim.** Not achieved. Capability unchanged from 525: hand-cranked machine and storing jar. A **bounded search of "every material known to carry the force, in every arrangement, wetted and dry, paired and stacked,"** >9,000 recorded failures. One entry, 11th month 547: **paired copper and zinc plates in brine, persistent rather than momentary deflection of the indicator.** Unreplicated, unexamined by any senior man, claimed as nothing. The 549 estimate directs that the charge is not to be reported on.

**Real-world analogue.** Volta's pile, 1800. Prerequisite chain there: Leyden jar 1745 → fifty years of electrostatics with no continuous source → Galvani 1780 → Volta's insight that the metals, not the frog, were the source → pile within about a year of asking the right question.

**Prerequisite chain here.** Held: electrostatic generator and Leyden-jar equivalent (525); a conductor/insulator taxonomy (525); copper and zinc in quantity; brine; a funded, protected, failure-logging search.

**This is the sharpest claim in the file and it is on the edge.**

The problem is that **the stated search protocol is a description of how to build a voltaic pile.** "Paired and stacked, wetted, dissimilar metals, all arrangements" is not adjacent to Volta's construction — it *is* Volta's construction. Run that protocol for twenty-five years across 9,000 entries and you will stack wetted copper-zinc pairs, and a stack of twenty pairs is not a subtle result: it gives a shock a man feels in his arms and a spark he can see. It is not deniable and it does not need a senior man to notice it.

What saves the claim, narrowly, is two things the file does supply:

1. **The 547 entry is specified as a single pair, not a stack.** One cell is about a volt. That is genuinely below the threshold of an electrostatic detector, which responds to charge and potential in the hundreds or thousands of volts. A "persistent rather than momentary deflection" from one pair actually implies a *more* sensitive instrument than they should have — Volta needed his own condensing electroscope and his own tongue — so the file is if anything conceding the empire more instrumentation than it has earned, while denying it the result.
2. **The interpretive frame is genuinely wrong for the observation.** Their entire model of the force is spark-and-jar: big, brief, violent. A feeble persistent deflection reads as a leak, a damp bench, or a bad instrument. Volta's contemporaries argued for twenty years about what the pile even was.

**VERDICT: HONEST, BUT ONLY JUST — and the protocol as written contradicts the result as written.** The words "and stacked" are doing damage the file did not intend. Either the search stacked dry pairs only (say so), or the empire has had a pile since roughly 540 and does not know it, which is a different and much bigger claim.

**Ruling.** Acceptable at 550. **Not carryable past 565.** A funded, protected, failure-logging bounded search over dissimilar metals in brine that has already produced one persistent deflection, in an empire that has named this as its most important unsolved problem and keeps twenty scholars standing at Alexandria against it, cannot plausibly go another fifteen years without a junior man stacking two of what he built once. If it is to stay open past 565, the file must either wind the search down, narrate the abandonment of the wetted-pair line, or lose Lou Zhen. Otherwise "not achieved" stops being honesty and becomes a thumb on the scale in the other direction.

## 4. Powder, rifled ordnance, breech-loading

### 4a. Powder allowance and output

**Claim.** Rail-served posts 5 → 15 days; beyond-rail 5 → 60 days with hardened magazine. ~135 t/yr rising to ~160. Total arsenals/powder/magazines line 490 t at 550.

**Arithmetic.** Base arsenal-and-powder line was therefore ~330 t; the reform adds ~48 % to it and ~3.6 % of total revenue. Against 1,760 t/yr on the garrison system, this is a real but proportionate charge. Consistent.

**Physical deliverability.** The beyond-rail establishment is a scatter of very small posts — Beihai ~50, Yanhe ~45, Kuhan ~20 — so twelvefold on a small base is a modest absolute tonnage of powder. Saltpeter supply is not a constraint: northern beds, Central Asian beds since 120–150 AD, a monopoly, 15,000 powder-works technicians. **Carriage is the constraint**, and the reform's perversity is that it moves twelve times the powder to precisely the places six weeks to five months from anywhere. The file sees this. 135–160 t/yr is defensible and slightly light.

**The dock.** "Sixty days of continuous engagement" is doctrinally over-specified. Nothing in pre-modern siege history involves sixty days of continuous firing; no fifty-man post could physically sustain it, and no magazine of the size implied could be built at the Yana mouth. The number is an arithmetic answer to "how long until relief," dressed as a rate of fire. Read as sixty days of *siege consumption at a low daily rate* it is fine and the money supports it. As written it overstates. **VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE as policy and cost; the "continuous engagement" phrasing should be corrected.**

Credit where due: the leak analysis — a twelvefold requisition rise being visible to every arsenal clerk, quartermaster and Sogdian carter, the *fact* out in one to two seasons and the *shape* in five to ten years — is the best piece of second-order reasoning in the document and prices correctly.

### 4b. Rifled naval cannon

**Claim.** 525: a few pieces in trial, 700–900 m. 550: fleet standard on new build from 539, all refits complete 549, 900–1,100 m effective against a hull. ~190 t.

**Analogue.** Cavalli and Wahrendorff 1840s, Armstrong 1855, Parrott 1861; the Royal Navy took roughly 1858 to the 1870s to standardise, with a humiliating partial reversion to smoothbore in between.

**Prerequisite chain.** Rifling known on land since the late 300s — 150 years. Naval rifled pieces in trial since 490–495 — thirty years of trials before this period opens. Precision boring and gauges since c. 200 AD. Coal-fired steel strong enough to hold grooves. All held, all long held.

**Rate.** This is a **production and refit** claim, not a research claim, and should be divided accordingly: it is arsenal capacity and dockyard time against a fleet of perhaps twenty to forty hulls. Trials → new-build standard in fourteen years, → all refits complete in twenty-four, is unhurried. The range gain, 700–900 → 900–1,100 m, is about 20 % over 25 years with no metallurgical change and no theory — attributable to twist, projectile fit and powder consistency. Appropriately unspectacular. **1,100 m "effective against a hull" from a rolling deck without a rangefinder is a conservative and correct number**, not an optimistic one.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE, if anything conservative.**

**The dock.** The file never says **how the projectile takes the rifling**, and that is the entire engineering content of muzzle-loading rifled artillery. Historically it required studded shot machined to the grooves (Blakely, Whitworth) or a soft-metal base cup that expands into them. Both are within reach — they have gauges, boring machines and Kazakh copper in bulk — but the capability is asserted rather than shown, and it is the part that would actually consume the 190 tonnes.

### 4c. Breech-loading — **the weakest block in the file**

**Claim.** 525 baseline: "Crude working form, judged 10–20 years from field." 550: "Wedge closure and cartridge seal settled 541; general issue to holding-position garrisons by 549; about three times the rate of fire." ~140 t.

**Problem one: the baseline contradicts two other documents.**

- `human-turn-500-525/china.md`: "**By year five to eight the breech loader reaches the field**, the paper cartridge seal already identified meaning this was refinement rather than invention." That is 505–508.
- `v2/assessments/china.md` (the private audit at 525), listing what is in hand at 525: "**field breech-loaders sealed by the paper cartridge**."
- This file: at 525, crude, not fielded, 10–20 years away.

Two of three sources say fielded before 525; this file's own baseline column says not. They cannot both stand. **Against its own stated baseline the 525–550 claim is unremarkable and passes. Against the corrected baseline it is TOO SLOW** — forty-plus years from a fielded weapon to a settled closure and general issue, in an empire whose declared doctrinal priority after 500 is "breech loading rate of fire." This needs reconciling before the block can be scored at all.

**Problem two: the seal mechanism is the one thing our history says does not work.** A sliding wedge closure is Wahrendorff/Krupp, 1840s–60s — fine, and reachable for an empire with 350 years of precision boring. But **gas-tight obturation by a paper cartridge swelling under pressure was never made to work.** The Dreyse and Chassepot leaked at the breech and burned their crews; the problem was solved only by a metallic cartridge case (Pauly 1812 onward) or by de Bange's plastic pad and Broadwell's ring (1870s), and both of those are *soft-metal or compressible* seals, not paper. Declaring the paper seal "settled" in 541 asserts a result the real dependency chain does not deliver.

**The fix is cheap and is sitting in their inventory.** They have Kazakh copper in industrial quantity and have been cladding hulls with it for seventy years. A copper cup, a copper-based cartridge, or a copper obturating ring in the wedge is the historically correct answer and is fully within their metalworking. It is simply not named.

**VERDICT: FAIL AS WRITTEN — two faults.**
1. Reconcile the 525 baseline against the two prior documents. If breech-loaders were fielded c. 507, this reign's breech-loading claim is an under-claim and the reign should be further along (a soft-metal cartridge and a much higher issue rate).
2. Replace or supplement "paper cartridge seal" with a soft-metal obturator. If the paper seal is retained as written, the weapon must carry a persistent gas-leak and crew-injury cost through 550 and cannot honestly be described as "settled," and "general issue" should be to a narrower class of position than every holding garrison.

The 3× rate-of-fire figure is fine and conservative — the Dreyse gave roughly 5× a muzzle-loading musket.

## 5. Rail rates, and "900 km charted, 0 km posted"

### 5a. Rail

**Claim.** Datong–Jinyang–Luoyang complete 543 (surveyed and begun in the previous period, so c. 515–525). Luoyang–Shouchun begun 544, ~2/3 built at 550. ~285 t/yr on the southern trunk out of 417 t/yr total rail.

**Distances and rates.** Datong→Taiyuan ~250 km; Taiyuan→Luoyang ~350 km; call it ~600 km, begun c. 520, complete 543 = **~26 km/yr**. Luoyang→Shouchun ~400 km, two thirds in six years = **~44 km/yr**.

**Is that right?** Yes, and the *ordering* is what makes it right. The Datong line crosses the Shanxi loess plateau, the Taihang range and the Yellow River — the hardest rail terrain in China proper. The Shouchun line runs plain country. The file has the mountain line at 60 % of the plain line's rate, which is the correct direction and about the correct magnitude. Both sit below the Pontic steppe rate of roughly 50–60 km/yr implied by the 450s–490s construction, which is again correct, because the Pontic is the easiest ground the empire has ever laid on.

**Cost check.** 285 t/yr for ~44 km/yr is ~6.5 t of silver-equivalent per km, against ~5–6 t/km implied by the appendix's 305–320 t/yr rail line in 450–500. Consistent, marginally higher, appropriate for harder ground.

**Carriage claim.** Datong coal to the arsenals at ~1/3 former cost. Iron rail with animal traction cuts rolling resistance by roughly an order of magnitude; a 3× delivered-cost reduction after transshipment and amortisation is **conservative and correct**.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE, and internally well-reasoned.**

**Credit, unprompted.** The 500 baseline named the missing industrial trigger precisely: "the coal of the north sits far from the people of the Yangtze, with the rail running west to the frontier rather than south to the population." This reign completes exactly that line — and then the file **declines to cash it in**: the mills still run on water, rotary steam is achieved but installed in no works, and the Yangtze crossing is surveyed and not begun. That restraint is the single most disciplined judgement in the technology block. A less honest file would have industrialised on the spot.

### 5b. The Northern Road ratio — **the file under-claims and contradicts itself**

**Claim.** "Twenty-one years, four hulls after Kuhan, 62 tonnes a year: **900 km charted, 0 km of that coast posted.**"

**Pace check.** 900 km / 21 years = **43 km/yr**, which is *below* the 50–75 km/season floor the 525 memorandum set. The file justifies the shortfall well: four hulls crushed in 542, a rebuild to four rather than six, the 544 edict barring operations east of the Yana after the first of the ninth month (self-assessed as a one-third slowdown), and a season that ice restricts to six to ten weeks.

**Against reality this is if anything generous-to-correct.** The Great Northern Expedition needed 1733–43 and thousands of men for the Siberian arctic coast, and Dmitry Laptev's detachment took 1739–41 to get from the Lena to the Kolyma alone. The geography also checks: Yana (~136°E) to east of the Kolyma (~162°E) at ~71°N is roughly 900 km great-circle and more along the coast. **The eastern squadron's number is honest.**

**Kuhan.** 238 dead of ~329 ashore (72 %) wintering unprepared on a treeless coast with scurvy and no antiscorbutic doctrine. Barentsz lost 5 of 17 on Novaya Zemlya *with* a built house and driftwood; De Long's party lost about 20 of 33 in the Lena delta; Franklin lost everyone. **72 % is credible and the survival of 91 is the generous part.** Fuel is recoverable — East Siberian driftwood from the great rivers is abundant, though the file says "no timber" and should say "no standing timber."

**But the ratio as presented is wrong in the empire's disfavour, twice over.**

1. **"0 km posted" is contradicted by this same document.** Section VIII and declaration D1 both record **Yanhuo**, established 549 on the eastern shore of Kanchajia, garrison 40, "the easternmost position on the empire's returns on this side of the ocean." That is a posted position on newly surveyed coast, inside the period, funded by the same programme.
2. **The 900 km is the eastern squadron's chart only, presented as the Northern Road's.** The southern squadron in the same section charts Kuye (Sakhalin) and rounds its northern cape by 534, surveys the Qiandao (Kuriles) chain through the 530s and 540s, and reaches Kanchajia (Kamchatka) by 543. That is on the order of **3,000–4,000 km of charted coast**, omitted from the headline ratio entirely.

The file's own stated intent is to avoid the previous version's fault of "running the two together," which "flattered the programme by several hundred kilometres." In fixing that it has over-corrected into a different error: it now presents one squadron's worst number as the programme's total.

**VERDICT: TOO SLOW / MIS-STATED — an under-claim.** Fix by stating the ratio per squadron: eastern squadron 900 km charted / 0 posted / 238 dead / 4 hulls; southern squadron order-3,000 km charted / one post at Yanhuo / no losses recorded. The self-critical framing survives — the eastern squadron genuinely is the disaster — and the empire stops throwing away a real achievement.

**Minor internal tension.** At the file's own 43 km/yr, the ~1,500 km of Chukotkan coast from the Kolyma to the strait is a 35-year chart, reaching the strait around 585 — not the "three or four periods, a hundred years" of Su Jian's 546 recalculation. The hundred-year figure is defensible only if it refers to the **posted** line, which moves at ~28 km/yr and does then land near 625–650. The file should say which line the estimate is about, since its whole rhetorical point in section VIII is that the two are different.

## 8. The three most serious findings

**I. The voltaic pile is described and then denied.** The search is characterised as "every material known to carry the force, in every arrangement, wetted and dry, **paired and stacked**." That is not adjacent to Volta's pile; it is Volta's pile. Twenty-five years and 9,000 entries of that protocol, in copper-and-zinc-rich hands, with brine, cannot fail to produce a stack — and a twenty-pair stack shocks a man's arms and throws a visible spark. The claim survives at 550 only on the narrow reading that the 547 result was a **single** pair (below any electrostatic detector's threshold) and that a spark-and-jar frame renders a feeble persistent deflection uninteresting. Both defences are real, and both are thinner than the file's confident "claimed as nothing" implies. **Ruling: acceptable at 550; not carryable past 565.** Either strike "and stacked," or accept that the empire has held the answer since roughly 540 without recognising it — which is a legitimate and interesting story, but a different one.

**II. Breech-loading fails twice — on baseline and on physics.** The turn's own 525 column ("crude working form, judged 10–20 years from field") contradicts the previous turn, which puts the breech-loader **in the field by 505–508**, and contradicts the 525 private audit, which lists "field breech-loaders sealed by the paper cartridge" as in hand. Against the corrected baseline, "settled 541, general issue 549" is forty years to standardise a fielded weapon and is **too slow** for an empire whose declared post-500 doctrine is breech-loading rate of fire. Separately, and independently: a **paper** cartridge seal in a metal wedge breech is the one solution the real dependency chain never delivered — Dreyse and Chassepot leaked and burned crews, and obturation was solved only by metallic cases and soft-metal rings. The fix is sitting in their own stores: they have been cladding hulls in Kazakh copper for seventy years. Name a copper obturator, or carry a permanent gas-leak and crew-injury cost and stop calling it settled.

**III. The Northern Road ratio over-corrects into a false headline.** "900 km charted, 0 km posted" was written to avoid the previous version's flattery, and it succeeds in flattering the programme in reverse. **"0 km posted" is contradicted forty lines earlier in the same document** by Yanhuo — established 549 on Kanchajia, garrison 40, explicitly "the easternmost position on the empire's returns." And the 900 km is the **eastern squadron's** chart alone, silently standing in for a programme whose southern squadron charted Sakhalin, the Kuriles and Kamchatka in the same twenty-one years — order 3,000–4,000 km, omitted entirely. The 43 km/yr eastern rate is itself honest and defensible against the real Siberian record; the disaster at Kuhan is honest; the mistake is arithmetic housekeeping, not nerve. State the ratio per squadron and the self-criticism survives intact while the empire stops discarding a genuine achievement. Related: at the file's own charting rate the strait is reached around 585, not 650 — Su Jian's hundred-year figure only works for the **posted** line, and the file should say which line it means, since the difference between the two is the entire point of section VIII.

## 9. Overall

This is a conservative file. Its dominant failure mode is **under-claiming**, not over-claiming: it discards the southern squadron's survey, it declines to industrialise off a coal rail it has just completed, it refuses to cash in a proven ocean route, and it keeps three refusals (steel theory, longitude, ice-trusted steam) that a less disciplined document would have quietly granted itself. Nothing in the technology block is impossible on prerequisites. Nothing compresses below the six-month-to-a-year floor.

Two claims require correction before the turn stands: **breech-loading** (baseline conflict plus an unsupported seal mechanism) and **the Northern Road ratio** (self-contradicted and under-stated). One claim is on probation: **the continuous electrical source**, honest at 550 and indefensible past 565. Three claims need a named mechanism rather than an assertion: the rifled projectile's engagement with the grooves, the boiler's pressure standard, and the antimalarial's actual plant and preparation. And one omission should be repaired: men die on five-month ocean crossings even when hulls do not.
