# Adjudication: Two Foreign Intelligence Operations Against the Maurya Realm, 525–550

**Adjudicator's note.** I have read only the Maurya files: `04-maurya-realm.md`, `human-turn-500-525/maurya.md`, and `v2/turn-525-550/maurya.md`. I have not been told what either operating power hoped to find or claims to have found. Every ruling below is derived from the target's own record. Where I state what the operators receive, I state it in the form it would reach them; where I state what is true, I state it from the Maurya files; and where the two diverge I say so explicitly.

---

## PART I — THE TARGET'S COUNTER-INTELLIGENCE, ASSESSED FROM ITS OWN FILES

Before either operation can be judged, the realm's actual capacity to catch a foreign merchant network must be established from the record rather than assumed from its reputation.

### 1. The informer apparatus is real, is old, and points the wrong way

The realm is "governed as it has long been, by the science of the *Arthashastra*, through appointed governors watched by overlapping networks of informers." Its own 550 file calls those networks "the single reason this realm can govern a hundred million people." This is not a hollow claim. It is the most developed internal surveillance instrument any power in this world holds.

It is also, on the record's own account, an instrument for watching **Maurya officials**, not foreigners. Its function throughout eight centuries is counter-corruption and counter-sedition: it watches governors, catches strongmen gathering authority under weak reigns, and reports on provincial administration. There is not one instance anywhere in the record of the informer networks detecting a foreign agent.

### 2. The networks were never extended to the newer territories — and the realm has written this down

The 550 file states it without evasion, in the tasking for its own failed colonial audit:

> "There has never been an intelligence establishment on the southern continent — the informer networks that watch every governor in the old dominion, and that are the single reason this realm can govern a hundred million people, were never extended across the ocean, because the settlements were founded as a programme and not as provinces. Twenty-five years on, they are provinces, and nobody has changed the classification."

This matters for both operations, but with an important qualification that cuts **against** the operators as much as for them. Bharuch (Bharukaccha), the Kalinga coast, the Bengal ports, **and Sindhudvāra at the Strait** are all listed at 550 under "Unchanged and entire in the old dominion." Operation A and the coastal half of Operation B are therefore working inside the best-watched ground the realm holds — not in the ungoverned south.

The qualification that saves them is narrower and more precise: Sindhudvāra's establishment was built to be "a customs house, a garrison and a berth." In twenty-five years it became a town of fourteen thousand with ropewalks, a chandlery quarter, godowns, a stone dry dock and a coaling stage, thick with Malay, Sumatran, Bugis and Chinese shipping. The watching apparatus there was sized for a toll post and was never resized. Nobody reclassified it either.

### 3. The record on noticing foreigners is bad, and the realm knows it

The evidence is uniform and damning:

- **Trikūṭa, spring 543.** Two ships of unfamiliar build stood off a Maurya watch station "the better part of a day," observed through a spyglass. The report reached Pataliputra **fourteen months later**. A survey in 545 worked eight hundred kilometres of coast and found nothing. The honest entry: "two ships of a build we could not name looked at our coast and went away."
- **The Strait bypass.** "Chinese hulls continue to work the passage south of the Strait into the archipelago east of Sumatra, unseen by our facility and paying nothing, as since the 470s; we have made no attempt to stop them and will not." Eighty years of traffic passing a chokepoint the realm garrisons, unseen and unchallenged by policy.
- **Oman, 531.** The shore party "was taken by men who were not the local people and who appeared to be waiting." The realm's own record notes that "the obvious reading of it is that our preparations at Oman were known before the hulls sailed, and that reading points at our own harbour" — and that the court "has never wanted to examine" it. The realm has an unexamined leak in its own port and declined to look for it.
- **Kannan, 547.** The one inspector the throne sent anywhere new was "housed, married into the harbour interest, given a share of the dues, ceased reporting after his second season. Nobody was sent after him."
- **By contrast**, Egypt's counter-intelligence works: the realm's own factor at Alexandria "reports harbour traffic, prices, and that he is watched and knows it," and the yards "remain closed to any foreigner." The realm has a working model of counter-intelligence in its files and has never built one.

### 4. Where the realm *is* genuinely hard

This must be stated as plainly as the weaknesses, because it determines what both operations cannot have.

- **Arsenals and powder works.** The realm's own experience against a peer is explicit: of its two-decade effort against the Han arsenals, "Many other agents were taken, presumably killed, and many were turned in by the very people they had tried to bribe," and the court "drew the lesson that bribery against a peer's frontier arsenals is murderously costly and had succeeded once only by the chance of one desperate man in a bad year." A peer's arsenal is the one place where a network of this kind reliably dies. The six new powder works (535–549) are built as separated mill-house compounds with earth banks and minimum-manning discipline — closed industrial sites, not quays. After Bhadrapura killed a hundred and eighty-four men in 541, access control at such places tightens as a matter of course and not of policy.
- **Proofing and gauge inspection (543).** Barrels are gauged, charged to standard and returned in writing. This is an arsenal-internal regime. Nothing about it is visible from outside a works.

### 5. The dual-loyalty point, and whether the realm has ever priced it

The realm has **named** the problem for two centuries and has **never** costed it.

It knows: the Sogdians' "interest lies in the trade's continuance rather than in any one power's favor" (Appendix I). It says of its own oldest network, in this very reign, "its loyalty runs to the trade, not to us." In the 300s it founded the Chinese-reading scholar programme expressly "to loosen the realm's dependence on go-betweens whose first loyalty ran to the trade rather than to the realm," and in the 350s it made a point of writing to China in its own hand as "a deliberate sign that it was investing in the tie and leaning less on the Sogdians."

And then, in 525–550, it did all of the following:

- Ran its measurement of its own Silk Road revenue loss through Nanaivandak of Samarkand, with **"Cover: none required."**
- Carried its 548 letter to the Emperor of the Han "by the Sogdian caravan channel," receipted by Nanaivandak's house.
- Added "two Sogdian factors travelling the Syrian road on ordinary business" to its Rome network in 546.
- Used "a Sogdian and a Yemeni interpreter together" for its 534 herald's letter to Egypt at Aden.
- Set its commercial policy — the 549 toll reduction, "the one piece of intelligence this reign both bought and used" — on Sogdian reporting, and then **proclaimed that policy at the Sogdian cities**.

Nowhere in eight centuries of file does the realm ask what these houses carry outward. There is no compartment, no vetting note, no counter-flow assessment, no price. The realm has converted a known loyalty problem into an operational dependency and has written the dependency down as a virtue ("The tasking asks it to do only what it does anyway, which is why a mediocre reign approved it").

**Compounding this: the Sogdians of Operation A are Maurya subjects.** Sogdia is Maurya ground to the Syr Darya; Sogdian is named as a working administrative language of the realm. A Sogdian factor at Bharuch or the Strait is a subject of the throne going about lawful business in his own sovereign's ports. There is no border to cross, no alien registration to evade, no anomalous presence to explain. The realm's counter-intelligence has **no category** for a legally loyal subject working for a foreign frontier office, and its files contain no instance of it ever having looked for one.

### 6. The realm's named characteristic failure, which governs both verdicts

The 550 file states its own diagnosis, and I adopt it as the operative rule for detection:

> "The realm's characteristic failure is not that it lacks a thing. It is that it already has the thing, in its own hands, filed under another heading, and no one is paid to notice."

Three worked examples in the same file: Bhūtiśarman's memorandum of 541, the only serious intelligence work of the reign, "filed under commercial correspondence" and never read to the council; the Kāntitīra surveyor's report that the Agnikāra's burning was husbandry, which "went into the geographic archive and was not circulated"; and the wire men and the glass shop, four hundred paces apart in one school, whose returns "go up two different chains to two different councils and meet, if they meet at all, in a summary written by a clerk who understands neither."

**Ruling:** against this target, a trace is not a detection. Both operations will leave recoverable traces. Neither trace will be read by anyone whose advancement turns on reading it.

## PART II — OPERATION A

**Configuration.** Sogdian merchant factors and hired local dockyard labour; run from a frontier interpretation office by a named foreign official; no officer of the operating power travels. Cover: silk and lacquer factors and their agents, plus hired dockyard labour, at Bharuch, the Kalinga coast yards, and the Strait ports. Standing instruction forbids any approach to military facilities, arsenals or powder works. Network age: 325 years of interpretation, ~220 years as a tasked network. The Sogdians are Maurya subjects trading in both directions.

### Cover-to-target match, target by target

| Target | Cover fit | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| Number of hulls carrying cannon | Good — hulls at quays are the factor's daily sight | Partial, undifferentiated, undercounted |
| Bore of those cannon | Poor — a bore is measured, not seen | Not obtained; a bad eyeball estimate reported without error bar |
| Whether any are rifled | None — indistinguishable from a quay | Not obtained; a true observation feeds a false inference |
| Saltpeter price in civilian markets | Excellent as an act; **wrong geography** | Obtained, true, and answers the analytic question backwards |
| Naval crews short-rationed in gunnery drill | Excellent — powder issue and live practice are public events | Obtained cleanly and correctly. The operation's best product |

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

Rendered as the frontier office would receive it, accumulated across the years to 550:

> **On hulls and their guns.** Our factors at Bharukaccha have counted, over the seasons, guns mounted upon vessels of the Maurya war establishment lying in home water, and our men at the Kalinga yards have seen ocean hulls building and fitting out with ports cut for guns. At the Strait our house has kept a tally of every armed hull that has entered. Taken together and setting aside those we believe counted twice, we can name between forty and sixty hulls seen to carry cannon. We cannot say this is all of them; we are told at Bharukaccha that hulls lie also in the Gulf and on the Tamil shore, where we have no house.
>
> **On the guns themselves.** Two sizes are commonly seen, the greater about a man's forearm at the muzzle and the lesser somewhat more than a hand's breadth. We cannot give a true measure. We have not been near an arsenal and have not tried, per our instruction.
>
> **On whether any are cut within.** We cannot say. We have heard, from men who labour at the yards, that the Maurya arsenals now measure every barrel to a fixed standard with gauges and will not pass one that fails, and that a written return goes with each piece, which is a thing new in the last seven or eight years. What that standard governs we could not learn.
>
> **On the white salt in the markets.** At Bharukaccha, at the Kalinga ports and at the Strait the price of saltpeter is unchanged in any degree worth reporting. Our factors have bought and priced it these several seasons and find no scarcity and no state buyer in these markets.
>
> **But on powder itself.** Powder moves through the Bengal and Kalinga wharves and through the Strait in quantities these ports have never seen, upon the state's account, going south with soldiers, iron and officials in a convoy that from about eight years ago runs twice in the year instead of once. Powder is also now landed at the island garrisons in the eastern sea in far greater store than formerly.
>
> **On gunnery drill.** The answer is no, and it has changed within these years. Maurya gun crews at their vessels now practise with live powder, which men at the yards say they had not done before in their fathers' time. The practice is regular, the charges are issued, and the noise of it is heard from the wharves. We judge their aim still indifferent and their crews new to the work, but they are no longer kept from firing.
>
> **Volunteered and not asked for.** The Maurya now work a great number of hulls that move without wind or oar, driven by fire and burning black stone. At the Strait every such hull must take on that stone at a stage the Maurya built about five years ago, and there is no other place between the subcontinent and the far east where it may be had. We have counted them there and make them something near seventy. The Maurya have also built at that port a stone dock that may be emptied of water, so that a hull sits dry and whole to be worked upon.
>
> **From the country, not from any works.** In the year we reckon 541 the greatest of the Maurya powder manufactories, on the Ganges, burned and was destroyed to the ground, killing near two hundred men, and the making of powder in that country was halved for above a year while it was rebuilt. This is common talk on the wharves and we did not go near the place.

### WHAT IS TRUE

**Hulls and guns.** True figure at 550: **about seventy-five cannon-armed vessels**, up from thirty to forty at 525. Also **about seventy engined hulls**, "including the first engined warships" — the two categories overlap, and the realm's own record does not separate them cleanly. Naval cannon vessels were as of 525 held to "defensive growth and to quality over visible numbers... kept in home and Gulf waters," and nothing in 525–550 moves them. The operators' 40–60 against a true 75 is therefore a **fair undercount produced by honest geography**: Bharuch is home water and shows a share; the Gulf squadron and the Tamil coast are outside the network's reach; the Kalinga yards show building, not establishment.

**Bore.** No bore figure exists anywhere in the Maurya record — not in the arsenal returns, not in the 550 technology schedule. The operators' "a man's forearm" is a quayside guess and should carry no weight whatever. Its danger is that it arrives in the same despatch as the gunnery-drill finding, which is excellent, and inherits that despatch's credibility.

**Rifling.** **None. Not one Maurya barrel is rifled.** The realm has no rifling, is not working on it, and does not name it as a problem. What the yard labourers described is entirely real and entirely different: **gauge-and-limit inspection at the arsenals, reached in 543**, funded from the 190M metallurgy-and-gauges line, and recorded by the realm as "our first step toward made parts that interchange" — together with the proofing standard of gauged charges and a written return imposed on every barrel. **This is the operation's sharpest true-observation-supporting-a-false-inference.** A gauge applied to a bore is precisely what an analyst hunting for rifling will read as evidence of a bore being worked to a specification. The realm's genuine achievement here is standardisation and quality control, which is strategically more important than rifling would be and will be read as its opposite.

**Saltpeter — the central finding, and the one the tasking's own indicator gets backwards.**

The operators report, truthfully, **no rise in the civilian saltpeter price at the three ports**. An analyst applying his own stated rule — "a rise would indicate production being drawn into state hands" — concludes that Maurya saltpeter is *not* being drawn into state hands.

He is exactly, catastrophically wrong. The truth:

- Powder output at 550 stands at **about four times the rate of 525**.
- **Six new powder works** were built, 535–549, of which **four are at Gangetic saltpeter sites** — that is, the state built its refining capacity *on top of the beds*, drawing from ground it already controls, and never entered a coastal commodity market to buy.
- Saltpeter is "abundant on the soil of the Gangetic plain, long gathered and known, and the realm sat on more of it than any rival possessed" — a renewable surface efflorescence, not a mined scarcity. Quadrupling offtake from an abundance the state sits on does not produce a coastal price signal.
- Bharuch is in Gujarat; the Kalinga yards are on the Bay; Sindhudvāra is fifteen hundred miles beyond. **None of the three ports is a market in which Gangetic saltpeter is priced.** The operation was sent to take a temperature at three points on a body that does not run a fever there.

The indicator fails not through Maurya deception — the realm has done nothing to conceal this and is not aware anyone is asking — but through the **design of the indicator against the target's actual industrial geography.** A power that refines at the source never bids at the port.

Two secondary risks on the same question:

1. **The white-salt conflation.** The realm's own record names saltpeter "the white salt." Hired local labour asked in the vernacular about "the white salt" may well return the price of the **state salt monopoly**, whose revenue rose from 260 to 320 million paṇas across the reign — a genuine rise driven, in the treasury's own words, by the fact that the monopoly "grows with the economy and the coin," and having nothing whatever to do with powder. Where this branch is taken, the operation returns a **false positive** on the same question where the primary evidence returns a **false negative**. Both readings are wrong; the second is wrong in the direction that happens to point at the truth, for no valid reason.
2. **The 541 dislocation.** Bhadrapura's destruction took the works *and the saltpeter refinery beside it*, halving Gangetic output for fourteen months, followed by a rebuild. Any Gangetic price series across 541–543 therefore swings violently for reasons no analyst reading a "state absorption" story would ever reconstruct. The operators do not hold that series and are better off for it.

**Powder movement — the true answer, obtained by an untasked route.** Everything the operators report about powder in transit is correct and is the *right* evidence for the question they were sent to ask. The convoy carries "soldiers, iron, powder and officials"; it runs twice a year from about 542 because engined hulls do not wait on the monsoon; the convoy rose from forty ocean hulls to fifty-two; and harbour powder at Ternate stands at **a hundred and fifty shots against forty at 525**, Banda likewise. Production *is* being drawn into state hands at four times the previous rate, and the operators have the proof in their hands — filed under the wrong heading, because they were told to look at prices.

**Gunnery drill — clean, correct, and decisive.** At 500 the realm's naval cannon vessels had "green crews and powder always short," "a few years' training and poor aim, their powder always short." At 550 the naval cannon line (500M across the reign) delivered "cannon-armed vessels from 30–40 to about 75; **crews drilled with live powder for the first time**." The operators' answer — no, they are no longer short-rationed, and the change is recent, and the crews are still poor — is **accurate in every particular including the qualification**. This is the single best product of either operation, and it is the one target where the merchant cover and the intelligence requirement match exactly: powder issued for live practice is a public, audible, chandlery-visible event on a working waterfront.

**The volunteered material is the most valuable thing the operation produces and nobody asked for it.** Engined hulls went from **19 at 525 to about 70 at 550**. The Strait coaling stage (545) bunkers Bengal coal and is, in the realm's own words, "the only place between the subcontinent and China where an engine can be filled" — which means **every engined hull on that road must present itself, in daylight, at a counting station a foreign factor can watch for the price of a berth.** The realm has additionally noted that "no minister has yet written down that a coaling stage is a stronger hold on a strait than a fort is." A foreign merchant house has now, in effect, written it down for them. The stone dry dock (543) compounds this: a hull in dry dock shows its underwater form, its mountings and its engine bed entire, to anyone hired to work on it — and the operation hires dockyard labour.

**Bhadrapura is correctly obtained and correctly interpreted.** A hundred and eighty-four dead, a works destroyed to the foundations, the saltpeter refinery lost, Gangetic output halved for fourteen months, and a rebuilt town — this is not a secret and cannot be one. It travels as market news and carter's talk to a Bengal or Kalinga wharf within a season. The inference an analyst will draw, that Maurya powder capacity is large but fragile and is the binding constraint on the realm's armament, is **true**: the realm's own file names powder capacity as "the wall, as it has for 275 years," and records thirty-four thousand firearms against a standing army of 1,119,000 — "three parts in a hundred... a real force and not an armed realm." The operation obtains the powder programme's most important truth **precisely because** its standing instruction kept it away from the powder programme.

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No. Not detected, and not detectable as configured — with two traces created and unread, and one commercial notice taken that goes nowhere.**

The reasoning, in order of weight:

1. **The standing instruction is what saves the operation, and I weight this above everything else.** The one behaviour that this target reliably punishes is approach to arsenals and powder works, and the record proves it with the realm's own dead: agents "taken, presumably killed," others "turned in by the very people they had tried to bribe." An operation forbidden to approach those places is forbidden the only ground where it would have died. It is also thereby forbidden the bore and the rifling, permanently. **The instruction bought survival at the exact price of two of the four targets, and the trade was correct.**
2. **The operators are Maurya subjects in Maurya ports.** No status anomaly exists to be noticed.
3. **The apparatus points at governors, not foreigners.** Nothing in eight centuries of file records the informer networks detecting a foreign agent.
4. **The demonstrated record in the maritime east is poor** — fourteen months for the Trikūṭa report, eighty years of unseen Chinese hulls south of the Strait, an unexamined leak at Oman.
5. **A port is made of foreigners asking about prices.** The behavioural signature of Operation A is invisible against its own background at Bharuch, the Kalinga yards and Sindhudvāra in a way it would not be in a Gangetic market town.

**The traces that exist and are not read** (adjudicated, consistent with the file's stated failure mode):

- **At Sindhudvāra**, the customs establishment records lading against declared trade as a matter of routine — that is what a customs house is. A silk-and-lacquer house whose declared business is thin against the size of its establishment and the frequency of its enquiries appears in those returns as an anomaly of *revenue*, not of security, and the Strait's watching apparatus was sized for a toll post in 385 and never resized for a town of fourteen thousand. The trace exists in the customs book. Nobody is paid to read it that way.
- **At Bharuch**, the Gujarati merchant houses are commercially alert — this is the community whose insurers, on their own judgement, killed the entire African run by raising a premium, and which fields the realm's own Alexandria factor. I rule that a rival house does lodge a complaint about a Sogdian factor's inquiries around the naval yard, and that it is disposed of **as a licensing and harbour-dues matter** — the house is assessed at a higher rate — and never leaves the revenue chain. **This is the realm noticing and doing nothing**, in the precise institutional style its own record describes. It gives the operators a cost signal they will probably misread as harassment or suspicion when it is ordinary extraction.

**What would have caught it.** Only two things, neither of which the realm possesses in 525–550: an officer whose duty was to read the Sindhudvāra customs returns against the harbour-master's book for houses whose trade does not match their presence; or any assessment, ever written, of what the Sogdian houses carry outward. The realm has named the second problem repeatedly since the fourth century and has never once costed it. On this operation's own terms, **the 325-year network's greatest asset is not its access. It is that the target has spent two centuries knowing exactly what it is and never writing down the consequence.**

## PART III — OPERATION B (new, 546)

**Configuration.** The same Sogdian factors extended south, plus two traders of highland peoples recruited in 546 and unproven, working the upper reaches of a great river from the north-east. Cover: medicinal herb and resin trade out of the highlands; ordinary coastal factoring at the Bengal ports. Four seasons at most before 550, of which the first is orientation.

### A preliminary ruling: which river, and why it matters more than anything else here

The tasking speaks of "a great river from the north-east," "the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal," and highland traders on the upper reaches. I take the intended river to be the eastern one, beyond the realm's frontier — not the Brahmaputra system, which drains into Maurya Bengal.

**This distinction is the operation's largest hidden hazard, and the frontier office has no way to resolve it.** The two answers are opposite:

- A **Bengal-port factor** asked how far Maurya power reaches up the great river will answer about the Ganges–Brahmaputra system: **totally**. Assessed, taxed, garrisoned, roaded, granaried, carrying printed tax rolls and relief registers from sixty provincial presses, with a wire running forty kilometres from Pataliputra toward Kāśī from 548, and a registered population of 103 million on the roll.
- A **highland recruit** asked the same question about the eastern river will answer: **nothing. Not one post, not one marker, not one boat.**

Both men will be telling the truth. The reports will contradict each other flatly, they will arrive in the same pouch, and there is nothing in the network — no officer of the operating power travels, and the frontier office has never been to either river — capable of telling the analyst that his two sources are describing two different waters. **I rate this the most likely single cause of the operation's product being misread, ahead of any Maurya action.**

### WHAT IS OBTAINED

> **From the Bengal ports (Sogdian factors, reliable).** The Maurya move in the Bay in strength and in growing strength. Their hulls out of the Bengal and Kalinga ports go south twice in the year now and no longer wait upon the season. Black burning stone is brought down by river barge from new workings in the eastern hills and laded for the south. Two years ago they set a new station upon the islands of the northern approach, where hulls water and take on the stone, with a small garrison, near a hundred men. They carry soldiers, iron, powder and officials. They print tables of the stars and the tides for their masters. Their charts of these waters are good and are getting better.
>
> **On Maurya presence upon the eastern shore of the Bay.** Our factors know of no Maurya post, garrison, factory or officer upon that shore. We have asked at Tāmralipti and along the Bengal wharves and no master claims to have landed men there for the state. We hold this lightly, having asked for two seasons only.
>
> **From the highlands (two men, recruited 546, unproven).** No Maurya official has been seen upon the upper river, nor any soldier, nor any tax taken in the Maurya name, nor any Maurya boat. The people of those reaches do not know the name. Of the outpost you name: the traders of the country speak of a foreign station far up, and say the Maurya do not come there and take no notice of it.
>
> **On the river's navigability.** The boats of the country come up as high as [a named place] while the water is high after the rains, and no higher; in the dry season they stop well below it, and the goods are broken to porters and beasts from there. One of our men gives the depth below the station as sufficient for a sea-going hull at any season. [ADJUDICATOR: **this figure is fabricated.** See below.]

### WHAT IS TRUE

**1. Has the Maurya court noticed the foreign river outpost? No — and the deeper truth is worse than "no."**

There is not one line, in eight centuries of Maurya record, about the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal, about any river running into it, or about any foreign presence on either. The court's eastern attention in 525–550 consists entirely of: the Strait, the Spice Islands, the southern continent, and China across the Syr Darya. The reign sent **no embassy to anyone in twenty-five years except three letters**, and the file's own summary of its failures reads "No answer to China at sea. No use made of a full treasury. No audit of a continent... Nobody was sent to find out."

The court has not noticed. It has no instrument that would notice. And the record establishes what would happen if a frontier officer *did* report it:

- The Trikūṭa sighting of 543 — a live, visual, spyglass observation of unknown foreign hulls at a Maurya watch station — took **fourteen months** to reach Pataliputra, produced one inconclusive survey, and ended in a written shrug.
- The Kāntitīra surveyor's report on the Agnikāra "went into the geographic archive and was not circulated."
- Bhūtiśarman's memorandum, the reign's only serious intelligence product, was "filed under commercial correspondence."

**A report of a foreign outpost up an eastern river would be entered in the geographic archive and would not be circulated.** The operators' finding is therefore right, and right for stronger reasons than they can evidence.

**One material qualification the operators will not capture, and it is the operation's real risk.** *Not noticed by the court* is not the same as *not known in the realm*. Bengal port merchants, Bay coasting masters and the realm's own hill traders will carry knowledge of an upriver foreign station as ordinary commercial rumour — the realm's subjects trade that country. The realm holds the information, distributed, and has never collected it. That is precisely its named characteristic failure, and it means the finding "the Maurya have not noticed" is **stable only so long as nobody at Pataliputra is paid to ask.** It is a fact about an administration, not a fact about a frontier, and it could reverse in a single competent reign without anything on the river changing. The file makes the point itself: the next ruler inherits a treasury of 2,750 million paṇas and "would have paid for a hundred inspectors."

**2. What Maurya presence exists on the eastern shore of the Bay, and how far up the river?**

**None, and zero.** The 550 territorial schedule is exhaustive and I take it as authoritative:

> "Unchanged and entire in the old dominion: the subcontinent and Sri Lanka; Afghanistan; Bactria and Sogdia to the Syr Darya; the Iranian plateau; Armenia and Azerbaijan; Mesopotamia to the Euphrates; Oman; the tributary ports of the Gulf's Arabian shore; eastern Yemen; the Strait of Malacca and our port at its eastern mouth. **Nothing gained, nothing lost, no frontier moved.**"

The only new Maurya thing anywhere in the Bay is the **"Bay coaling and watering stage, established 547 on the islands of the northern approach to the Strait. Garrison 90"** — on islands, not the eastern shore, and its purpose is stated in the file as fuelling the southern road. Maurya presence on the eastern shore: nil. Maurya presence up the river: nil kilometres. No post, no marker, no survey, no claim, no chart, no sounding.

The operators' answer on this target is **correct**, and it is the operation's clean product.

**3. The great trap: everything the Bengal reporting says is true, and it points at the wrong continent.**

The operators will observe, correctly, a large and rapidly compounding Maurya maritime effort in the Bay: convoys doubled in frequency from 542, engined hulls in numbers, coal barged from newly opened eastern seams, a new island coaling and watering station in 547 with a garrison, soldiers, iron and powder moving, printed star and tide tables from 541, hulls assigned to nothing but charting.

An analyst holding this, and asking about the eastern shore of the Bay, will very reasonably conclude that **Maurya power is being projected east**, toward the shore and the river he is interested in. It is not. Every item on that list feeds a **settled continent of four hundred thousand Maurya subjects roughly six thousand kilometres to the south** — a place whose population tripled in this reign, which "keeps accounts that do not reconcile with Pataliputra's, builds its own hulls, feeds itself, mines its own coal, grants its own land," and of which the realm's own record says "the next ruler will inherit a province of four hundred thousand people that has never seen a minister."

The operating power almost certainly does not have a name for that continent. The realm told Rome of it by letter in 539 — "we tell you so directly rather than let you hear it the way we heard of yours" — and told nobody else. **The southern continent is the missing variable that makes every Bay observation legible, and it is invisible to this operation.** Without it, a true and well-collected body of shipping observation resolves into precisely the wrong strategic inference. I rate this the single largest gap between what is obtained and what is true across both operations.

**4. Navigability and draught: partial, qualitatively right, and one fabricated number.**

The realm holds no chart, no sounding and no pilotage of that river — it has never been there. But the realm's *general* navigational competence is high and rising (charts, monsoon, currents, lodestone, mechanical clock at sea from about 500, printed tide tables from 541, and a demonstrated willingness to spend two hulls for five years charting the northeastern reef, 544–549). It could survey that river whenever it decided to. It has not decided to.

What the two highland recruits can honestly deliver is a **break-of-bulk answer, not a hydrographic one**: how far the country's boats come in the wet and in the dry, and where cargo transfers to porters. That is genuinely useful and roughly right, and it is what herb-and-resin traders actually know.

What they cannot deliver is a **draught**. A draught comes from a sounding or from a pilot; these men are neither, they were recruited in 546, they are explicitly unproven, and they are paid by the report. **Unproven agents paid by the report produce reports.** I rule that one of the two supplies a confident figure he does not have, that it is wrong, and that **there is nothing in the network capable of catching it** — no second source on that river, no officer of the operating power travelling, and a frontier office that has never seen the water. The Maurya files offer the exact parallel from the target's own side: Kannan, sent openly with every advantage, was co-opted in two seasons and nobody was sent after him.

Any operational plan built on that draught figure is built on nothing.

### WAS IT DETECTED

**No — and the finding worth reporting is that the Maurya are not the relevant counter-intelligence threat to this operation at all.**

- **The Bengal-ports leg** is Sogdian factors, Maurya subjects, in old-dominion ports, doing exactly what they appear to be doing. Same ruling as Operation A, and lower risk, because the questions asked at Bengal are about shipping and coasts — the common currency of every wharf conversation in the world — rather than about powder and guns.
- **The highland leg lies outside Maurya administration entirely.** The realm's frontier is its own edge; its informer networks reach the provinces it governs and, by its own written admission, were not even extended across the ocean to provinces it founded. They certainly do not reach the upper reaches of a river the court has never heard of. **The Maurya cannot detect this leg because the Maurya are not there.** That is the same fact as the operation's principal finding, seen from the other side.
- The real exposure on the highland leg is to the local highland polities and to the foreign outpost itself, neither of which is a Maurya matter, and neither of which I am positioned to judge from the target's files. I note the boundary rather than guess past it.

**One Maurya-side thread that could pull later, and is worth flagging now.** The herb-and-resin cover is unusually good for 546–550 for a reason the operators did not plan: the realm **re-founded its medical programme in 538 at 250 million paṇas across the reign, under the Pataliputra and Taxila houses**, built on "distillation-grade extract consistency" and a "systematic, empirically rigorous materia medica." The realm's own state-funded buyers are therefore in the market for exactly these goods, in quantity, with treasury money. This makes the cover commercially convincing beyond anything the operators arranged — and it simultaneously creates the one plausible route by which a Maurya official ever travels up that trail: **a procurement inquiry into provenance, not a security investigation.** That is a 550s risk, not a 546–550 risk, and it would arrive wearing the wrong uniform, which is why it would work.

**And an opportunity the operation did not take.** The same channel that carries resin down-country carries a factor's man into the supply chain of the best-funded scientific establishment in the world — a programme that by 550 employs some thirty-three thousand funded researchers and has, in the last six years, put a current down a wire, seen small bodies moving in water through a bead lens, and opened coalfields. Operation B was pointed at a river. It was standing in a doorway.

### The single most important thing in this adjudication

Neither operation is caught, and that is not generosity. It follows from three findings in the target's own hand:

1. **The standing instruction against arsenals and powder works is the whole reason Operation A survives.** The realm's own agents died in Han arsenals — "taken, presumably killed... turned in by the very people they had tried to bribe" — and the court wrote the lesson down. An operation forbidden that ground is forbidden the only place it would have died, at the cost of two of its four targets. Correct trade, correctly made.
2. **The target's informer networks watch its own governors, were never extended to its newer territories, and have never in eight centuries detected a foreign agent.** Against foreigners the realm's demonstrated performance is fourteen months for a visual sighting, eighty years of unseen hulls past its own chokepoint, and an unexamined leak in its own harbour.
3. **The realm has known for two centuries that the Sogdians' loyalty runs to the trade and not to the throne, has written it down repeatedly, and has never once priced the reverse flow** — while in this very reign routing its China correspondence, its Silk Road revenue assessment and part of its Rome network through Sogdian hands, and proclaiming the resulting policy at the Sogdian cities. The oldest network in the world is not valuable here because it has access. It is valuable because the target has spent two hundred years naming the risk and never converting the name into a countermeasure.

**And the sharpest gap is not a secret kept. It is an indicator that lies.** Operation A's saltpeter question is answered truthfully at three ports and answered backwards, because the realm built four powder works on top of the beds and never entered a market the operators could watch. The operators will hold, in the same despatch, the actual proof they were sent for — state powder moving south in unprecedented bulk, twice a year — filed under the wrong heading, because they were told to look at a price.
