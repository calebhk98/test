## §1. THE POWDER WORK — THREE STEPS PRICED SEPARATELY

### 1a. Stable ratio, 531

**Analogue.** Convergence on ~75/15/10 saltpetre/charcoal/sulphur. In our history the useful explosive proportion emerged c. 1000–1300 AD from a starting point (Chinese alchemical incendiaries, c. 850) that had no idea what it was looking for.

**Prerequisite chain.** Ratio ← knowing the three ingredients ← identifying saltpetre as an oxidiser-bearing distinct substance ← the concept that a solid can supply its own combustion air (Rome does **not** hold this; it has no chemistry and the turn says so) ← a purification method for nitre (crystallisation from lixiviated ash-and-earth liquor — well within Roman salt and dye practice, but unstated). Rome **does** hold: sulphur in bulk from the Italian and mining districts, charcoal in industrial quantity, and — critically — the *category*, "something that burns and expands violently as it ignites," reasoned out around 490 from the Han firearms account.

**Researcher-years.** Given the ingredients and a known target, proportioning is cheap: on the order of **a few hundred person-years** of systematic burning, weighing and tabulating. Without the ingredients it is unbounded.

**Divide.** 6 years × ~500 staff ≈ 3,000 person-years. Ten-fold sufficient for the proportioning alone.

**Accelerators.** Legitimate and large: a confirmed target (China demonstrated a firing to a Roman envoy at forty paces in 515 — see the superseded draft; the canonical line has the 480s account and the 290 incident); a print network that circulates trial results empire-wide in weeks; two centuries of proof-testing culture.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE — but resting on an unstated prerequisite.** The six years of proportioning are, if anything, slow for Rome's swarm. The claim is not too fast; it is **under-derived**. Fix costs one sentence: state that nitre was identified from stable- and latrine-wall efflorescence already gathered by Roman farmers as a meat-curing salt, and that it is purified by ash-lye lixiviation and crystallisation. That is how it happened here and Rome has every input for it.

### 1b. A proofed hand weapon — CLAIMED AS FAILED

**Analogue.** The hand gonne, c. 1350s: a short iron or bronze tube on a stick. Metallurgically it is *far* below a Roman steam cylinder — Rome has bored true cylinders under working pressure for three centuries and hydraulically proofs naval boilers where failure is fatal.

**Assessment of the failure.** The turn enters a formal finding (544) that a hand tube is impossible, from **n = 3 pieces and one burst on a ninth firing**, and the document itself says the finding is wrong and explains institutionally why it will stand. That is a legitimate and well-constructed narrative failure, and an empire under-claiming is not something I will penalise as such.

**But the stated technical reason does not survive contact with Rome's own practice.** A burst *on the ninth firing* is fatigue or overcharge, not a strength ceiling — and Rome is the civilisation that invented the discipline of counting boiler failures to find a safe working pressure. An arsenal with standardised specifications for critical components since 480 and a proof-testing tradition would proof a tube to burst, derate it, and file a *working pressure*, not an impossibility. The document needs Vibius Crispinus's death to close the question, and one death closing a question in an empire of 60,000 designers is thin.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE AS A FAILURE, with a soft flag on the mechanism.** Keep the outcome; the institutional argument in §IX is the best-written passage in the turn. Strengthen the cause: the finding should rest on Crispinus being the sole advocate and the programme having bombards to point at (which the turn already says), and *not* on a metallurgical claim that Rome's own proof-house would have refuted in a fortnight. As written, the false negative is slightly too convenient.

### 1c. Cast ordnance — AND THE NAVAL CASE THAT IS NOT MADE

**What is claimed.** First cast-bronze bombard at Argentoratum 538; **emplaced** batteries at seven positions by 550 (Borysthenia, the Dacian corridor forts, Gades, Gibraltar, Cyrenaica, Portus Severianus); "against stone, a bombard does in hours what a siege train did in weeks."

**Analogue.** First European guns c. 1320s; wall-reducing bombards c. 1420s–1453 (Mehmed II at Constantinople). ~130 years, a few thousand founders across Europe: order **20,000–50,000 person-years**.

**Prerequisite chain, and Rome's genuine discount.** That 130 years was spent on two things: (i) casting and boring a large tube that does not burst, and (ii) **corning** the powder. Rome holds (i) outright — bronze founding at boiler and cylinder scale, boring machines, gauges, hydraulic proofing, standardised critical specifications since 480. A defensible discount on (i) is 3–5×. Rome does **not** hold (ii), and the turn never mentions it.

**Divide.** 538→550 is 12 years × ~1,000 staff ≈ 12,000 person-years, against a prerequisite-discounted requirement of maybe 5,000–15,000. It closes.

**VERDICT ON THE BOMBARD: PLAUSIBLE, at the fast edge.**
**VERDICT ON "STONE REDUCED IN HOURS": TOO FAST AS WRITTEN — conditional, and cheaply fixed.** Serpentine meal powder separates in transport and burns too slowly to do that to a curtain wall; the "hours not weeks" performance is a *corned*-powder result. Corning is an accident-grade discovery (damp-cake the powder, break and sieve it) that Rome's empirical swarm would find within a few years of making powder in bulk. Add one clause dating corned powder to c. 540–545 and the claim stands as written. Without it, I would hold "hours" back to c. 555 and allow only "days rather than weeks" at 550.

**AND THE OMISSION: no gun goes to sea. TOO SLOW.** The prompt asked me to price "cast naval ordnance at sea." **The turn does not claim it.** Rome ends 550 with fifty-two steam warships — twelve iron-framed, nineteen screw — armed, so far as the record runs, as they were in 400 AD: ram, ballistae, steam grenades by catapult. This is the weakest point in the technology block, because:

- Rome's declared central strategic problem is Egyptian naval superiority ("its ships beyond counting against Rome's twenty to thirty steam warships").
- Rome will ship a bombard across an ocean to emplace it at Portus Severianus, but will not bolt one to a deck.
- Rome sites batteries at Gades, Gibraltar and Cyrenaica — i.e. it has already decided bombards are the answer to ships. It has simply put them ashore.
- In our history shipboard guns followed land guns within one to two decades (English ships carrying guns from the 1330s–40s).

The genuine engineering objections — recoil on a deck, cooling time, breeching — are real and are exactly the sort of thing Rome's arsenal solves in three years, not never. **Earliest I would require: experimental deck-mounted pieces on one or two hulls by 546–548, a small armed squadron by 550.** As it stands the turn buys Rome a siege train and leaves its fleet unable to hurt anything it meets.

### 1d. THE SALTPETRE SUPPLY — NOT DERIVED, NOT MENTIONED, NOT CONSTRAINED

I was asked to check whether the claimed nitre shortfall is derived from Rome's own agriculture and nitre-bed practice rather than assumed.

**Finding: there is no such claim in the turn to check.** The strings *nitre*, *niter*, *saltpetre*, *saltpeter* and *sal petrae* appear **nowhere** in `/home/user/test/four-empires/v2/turn-525-550/rome.md`. Rome spends 310M HS, stands up a bombard programme, and emplaces seven batteries without one word about where the potassium nitrate comes from. The proposition that "the works could produce arms far faster than they are asked to because nitre is the binding constraint" is **not present in the record and therefore neither derived nor assumed — it is absent.**

This matters more than any dating question in the block, because nitrogen supply is the *actual* ceiling on a pre-synthetic gunpowder power, and every other empire in this world is explicitly costed against it: China runs a state **Saltpeter Monopoly Office** with prosecutions for unlicensed working; the Maurya name Gangetic-plain nitre as a heartland resource; Egypt's own assessment names nitrogen as its number-one constraint and spends 45,000 talents on an electric-nitrate line to escape it. Rome alone is silent.

**What Rome's own agriculture would actually yield.** Rome holds the strongest nitre-bed base in the world and has not noticed: ~127M people; a cavalry and draught-horse economy on rail-fed forage; industrial-scale urban stabling in the Rhine and British cities; centuries of latrine and byre infrastructure; and — decisively — a state that already runs licensed, inspected, output-returning industrial works under the *curatores fabricarum*, which is exactly the administrative machinery a nitre-bed monopoly needs. European powers with a fraction of that base supported continental artillery trains on nitraries and imported Indian nitre. Rome would be nitre-rich by the standards of any real gunpowder state and nitre-poor by the standards of what a 52-ship fleet and seven batteries would want to shoot.

**VERDICT: A MISSING PREREQUISITE — the most serious in the block.** Not impossible, not too fast: **unpriced.** Required addition: a stated nitre supply — licensed nitraries and byre-scrapings under the *curatores*, with a stated annual tonnage and an explicit statement of what it does and does not support. My recommendation, which I think is also to Rome's dramatic advantage: derive the constraint honestly and let it bind. A fixed battery firing occasionally is cheap in powder; a fleet action is not. The correct Roman finding for 550 is *we can arm fortresses and we cannot yet arm a fleet* — which would, incidentally, supply the missing reason why no gun went to sea, and convert my §1c under-claim into a derived result.

## §2. THE NAVAL PROGRAMME

### 2a. Iron framing — *Constantia* 541, twelve in service by 550

**Analogue.** Iron-framed and iron-hulled ships: *Aaron Manby* 1822; *Great Britain* 1843; iron-framed composite construction and *Warrior* 1860. Roughly 20–40 years, hundreds of yards.

**Prerequisite chain.** Rolling mills for consistent plate (held, from before 500); rivets and riveted pressure joints (held, four centuries of boilers); structural wrought iron in bulk (held); frame-first naval architecture in iron rather than sawn timber (new this turn); an ocean requirement to force it (present — the western run and the loss of *Fulmen*).

**Researcher-years / divide.** Rome's own 525 forecast was 25–30 years; it delivered in 16. With ~2,000–3,000 on the naval line, several established Atlantic yards (Isca, Londinium, Gesoriacum), and a hard operational forcing function, beating one's own forecast by ~40% is defensible. Twelve hulls in nine years at ~1.3/yr is a modest build rate for that yard base.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE, at the fast edge.** Two prerequisites go unaddressed, and one of them is serious:

1. **Compass deviation. This is a real missing prerequisite.** An iron-framed hull deflects the magnetic compass — in our history this cost ships until Barlow and Airy worked it out in the 1820s–40s and Flinders bars and corrector magnets went into service. Rome's entire oceanic method is compass bearing + clock + telescope, and Rome has **no theory of magnetism whatsoever** and has explicitly declined an electrical programme. Twelve iron-framed hulls navigating 2,700 nm of open ocean on an uncorrected compass is not a small hand-wave; it is the failure mode that would have killed *Fulmen* more plausibly than weather. **Required:** either state that Rome discovered the deviation empirically (a swinging-ship compass-adjustment procedure is findable by exactly Rome's bookkeeping-applied-to-instruments habit, and would be a lovely small win), or accept that iron hulls stay on coastal and Mediterranean service and the ocean run stays composite.
2. **Fouling and corrosion.** Iron hulls foul and corrode badly in warm water — the reason the Royal Navy clung to copper-sheathed wood. Rome has no anti-fouling and no chemistry. Not disqualifying (docking and scraping works), but it should cost the western run something in availability.

### 2b. Screw propulsion — ordered for all ocean construction 544, nineteen hulls by 550

**Analogue.** Ressel 1827; Smith and Ericsson 1836; *Rattler* v. *Alecto* 1845; Royal Navy fleet adoption late 1840s–1850s. Roughly 15–20 years from working demonstrator to committed procurement, with a few hundred engineers: order **5,000 person-years**.

**Prerequisite chain.** Blade and shaft (Rome had a crude screw at 30–40% by 525, after fifty years' work); a **stern gland** keeping the sea out around a rotating shaft below the waterline (Rome holds this — greased leather and hemp packing, and the turn's own steam-exchange letter names "seals and their failure points" as a Roman deliverable; no rubber needed, which is fortunate since Rome still cannot source it); step-up gearing from a slow marine engine to screw revolutions (held — Antikythera-tradition precision gearwork plus mechanical clocks plus rolling mills).

**Divide.** ~200–300 of the naval line on the screw × 25 years ≈ 5,000–7,500 person-years, against a historical ~5,000. It closes almost exactly.

**Accelerators.** Strong and legitimate: Rome has watched wakeless Egyptian hulls in Alexandria harbour for decades and knows with certainty that the thing works and roughly where it sits. That is the textbook "chasing a target it knows exists."

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE — the best-calibrated claim in the block.** The performance statement ("above half a paddle wheel in flat water and superior in weather") is physically correct in both directions and exactly the right size of claim: paddles emerge and race in a seaway, screws do not. If anything this is mildly *under*-claimed for fifty years of prior work.

### 2c. Ocean range 600–700 → 900–1,000 nm

**Analogue.** Compound-engine and boiler-pressure gains, 1840s–1860s. A ~45% range gain over 25 years from incremental boiler pressure, better firing practice, hull form and the screw's efficiency at sea.

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE, mildly conservative.** Rome already holds compound engines at 150–400 hp. Our own 1840–1865 did better than 45%. No objection.

### 2d. The crossing — *Aquila Occidentalis*, spring 550, twenty-two days. **THE ARITHMETIC DOES NOT CLOSE.**

**The problem.** The declared road is Insulae Fortunatae (Canaries, 28°N) → the trades and the North Equatorial Current → the Antilles. That leg is ~2,700 nm to the Lesser Antilles, ~3,300 nm to Hispaniola, and more to Portus Severianus on the Chesapeake. **Declared range is 900–1,000 nm on a full load.** The shortfall is a factor of roughly three.

The declared waypoints do not fix it. Insulae Accipitrum is stated as "mid-ocean **on the return road**" — the Azores, on the northern Gulf-Stream-and-westerlies leg. It is not on the outbound road and cannot be reached from the Canaries except by beating northwest against the trades. Stocking Portus Severianus in 549 provisions the *far* end; it puts no coal in the middle of the outbound ocean. And Rome has already established, at the cost of *Fulmen* and 120 men in 543, that at-sea coaling from depot ships does not work — the turn makes that failure the emotional hinge of the whole coal programme.

22 days over ~2,700 nm is ~5.1 knots, which is a fine speed for the era. The speed is right. The fuel is not there.

**VERDICT: TOO FAST / INTERNALLY UNCLOSED.** Three fixes, any one sufficient:
1. **State that she crossed under sail and steam together** — every real ocean steamer of the 1840s–60s was auxiliary-rigged and used coal selectively for calms, the Sargasso and the approaches. This is the honest and cheapest fix, but it means the headline must read *first steam-assisted crossing*, not "first steam crossing of the western ocean," and 22 days becomes a wind-dependent figure rather than an engineering one.
2. **Name a westward mid-ocean depot** — but there is no land on that road, so this means a stationed collier hulk, which re-opens the *Fulmen* problem.
3. **Raise the range** to ~1,400–1,800 nm and accept two long legs with a Cape Verde staging point off the Mauretanian coast, which Rome already holds. This is the most conservative and requires moving the range claim in §2c, which I have otherwise passed.

I would accept (1) without argument. As written, the single most trumpeted naval result of the turn rests on a fuel budget the turn's own *Fulmen* episode proves Rome does not have.

## §3. COAL WEST OF THE OCEAN

**Claim.** Prospecting 541–542; bituminous seams above the fall line of the Flumen Novum within eight miles of navigable water, autumn 543; pits 545–547; first coal 549 at 11,000 tons in the first full year; four miles of iron rail 548–549; first western stationary engine 550. 240M.

**Analogue.** The Richmond Basin at Midlothian, Virginia — Triassic bituminous, ~13 miles west of the James River fall line, worked from c. 1701, the first commercially mined coalfield in North America, shipped down the James. The geology is real, correctly placed, and correctly characterised.

**One geographic error.** The turn puts the coal on "the third river from the south" of a 200-mile drowned estuary with four rivers entering from the west. That is Chesapeake Bay — James, York, Rappahannock, Potomac — so the third from the south is the Rappahannock, which has no workable bituminous seams. The coal is on the **first** river from the south, the James. One-word fix.

**Rate.** 543 discovery → 549 first coal is six years, through a colony burned in 546 with 410 dead and refounded behind three thousand troops. Given Roman deep-mining, steam pumps, rail and organised labour applied to a shallow outcrop field, six years is if anything **slow**, and the turn earns that slowness honestly with the 546 disaster. 11,000 tons in the first full year is aggressive but supportable for a pit colony with rail to tidewater, and the turn correctly calls it "a rounding error against the Rhine's millions."

**VERDICT: PLAUSIBLE, and the best-derived programme in the block.** The chain — steam warships cannot cross an ocean → therefore fuel is a condition of *presence*, not an industrial ambition → therefore the "poor" northern coast outranks the gold islands — is causally clean and was already set up in the prior turn. Fix the river.

## §4. AGRONOMY — MAIZE INTO PANNONIA AND DACIA

**Claim.** At 525, ripening no further north than Baetica (~37°N). At 550, "strains ripening reliably in Pannonia and on the Dacian lowland" (~46–47°N). 55M, six trial stations, printed method sheets.

**Analogue.** Maize reached Europe 1493 and became a reliable peasant staple in Hungary and Romania — precisely Pannonia and the Dacian lowland — over roughly **150–250 years**, i.e. the late 17th and 18th centuries.

**Why headcount cannot fix this.** This is the one item in the block where the hard floor is biological rather than organisational, and the turn's own prior generation says so in terms: "the generational selection work of adapting a warm-country grain to shorter northern seasons, which no quantity of engineers can compress." One year, one generation. Twenty-five years is **twenty-five generations**. Directed mass selection with six trial stations and printed protocol is a genuine and large accelerator over undirected peasant seed-saving — call it 3–5× — but 150–250 generations discounted 4× is still 40–60 generations. Twenty-five is short.

**The fix is free, and it is sitting in the turn already.** Rome is not confined to tropical Caribbean maize. From 541 it is running prospecting parties up the rivers of the mid-Atlantic seaboard at 37–39°N, among "scattered fishing and farming peoples" who in our own history grew **Northern Flint maize** — already selected for short seasons and high latitudes, and the exact germplasm that made maize viable in northern Europe and New England. Rome has boats, balloons, interpreters, a printed grammar habit and *curatores agrorum*. Collecting northern landraces is a thing Rome would obviously do and has obviously had the opportunity to do.

**VERDICT: TOO FAST AS WRITTEN; PLAUSIBLE IF RE-SOURCED.**
- From island stock only: earliest acceptable for reliable Pannonia/Dacia is **c. 570–590**.
- From northern-seaboard flint collected 541 onward: **550 stands**, and 9 years × ~9 generations of directed selection on already-cold-adapted stock is a fair claim.

Required change: one clause stating the Pannonian and Dacian strains descend from short-season maize collected on the northern seaboard, not from the Antilles. This also tightens the turn's own argument, since it makes the "worthless" northern coast pay twice — coal and grain — which is the reassessment the turn is built around.

## §7. THE THREE MOST SERIOUS FINDINGS

**1. The nitre supply is absent from a turn whose headline result is artillery.** Not under-derived — *absent*. The words saltpetre and nitre do not occur in the document. Rome funds 310M, stands up seven batteries, and never states where the oxidiser comes from, in a world where China polices a Saltpeter Monopoly Office, the Maurya name Gangetic nitre as a heartland asset, and Egypt calls nitrogen its number-one constraint and spends 45,000 talents trying to escape it. Rome would in fact be nitre-rich — 127M people, an enormous stabled-horse and draught economy, and a licensed-and-inspected works regime under the *curatores* that is exactly the apparatus a nitrary monopoly needs — but rich is not unlimited, and the difference between arming seven fortresses and arming a fifty-two-ship fleet is precisely a nitrogen question. Derive it, state a tonnage, and let it bind.

**2. The first steam crossing does not close on its own fuel budget.** ~2,700 nautical miles from the Canaries to the Antilles against a declared range of 900–1,000. The one named mid-ocean waypoint, Insulae Accipitrum, is explicitly on the *return* road and unreachable outbound against the trades. Stocking Portus Severianus provisions the far end, not the middle. And the turn itself establishes, at the cost of *Fulmen* and 120 men, that at-sea coaling does not work. The 22-day passage is a good speed and the wrong claim: it is a sail-and-steam passage, and calling it "the first steam crossing of the western ocean" is the single most quotable line in the turn resting on a factor-of-three shortfall.

**3. Two prerequisites go unmentioned where our own history found them expensive — compass deviation, and corning.** Twelve iron-framed hulls navigate an ocean on a compass that iron deflects, in an empire with no theory of magnetism that has just declined an electrical programme; in our world that error killed ships until the 1820s–40s. And "stone reduced in hours" is a corned-powder performance claimed by a programme that never mentions granulating its powder. Both are cheap to fix and both are *findable by exactly the habit the turn says Rome has* — bookkeeping applied to a physical effect. Neither costs Rome a date. But as written the turn's two flagship capabilities each skip the step that made them hard here.

## §8. ONE NOTE IN ROME'S FAVOUR

The block should not be read as over-claiming on balance. It enters six explicit failures — the hand firearm, electricity, chemistry, predictive natural philosophy, medicine (marked *failed*), and any theory of heat or steel — and the naval and range figures are conservative rather than generous. The Yucatan geography, the Richmond Basin geology, the Taino demographic collapse, the screw's weather advantage, and the entire fiscal and research arithmetic are all independently correct. The problems above are three unpriced prerequisites and one crossing that does not fuel, in a turn that is otherwise unusually disciplined about saying what it did not achieve.
