# INBOX — THE MAURYA REALM, 525–550

## FROM EGYPT
## MESSAGE OUT — TO THE MAURYA

**Sent from Alexandria, summer 544, through the Aden commercial office to the Court of the Mauryas, in Greek and in the court's own script.**

> From the Council of the Pharaoh Ptolemy, at Alexandria, to the Court of the Mauryas, greeting.
>
> There has been little between us but trade for four hundred years, and what there has been besides trade has not been good on either side. We are not writing to mend that, which cannot be mended by a letter, and we will not pretend otherwise.
>
> We are writing because our two frontiers touch along the Euphrates and the Syrian desert edge, and because a frontier that touches without a procedure is a frontier where a misreading becomes a war that neither court chose.
>
> We therefore propose three things, none of which costs either of us a soldier or a coin.
>
> First: notice of movements. Let each court inform the other of any body of troops moving toward that border in strength, before it moves. The number and the season, no more. We will give it first and without waiting to receive it.
>
> Second: a standing channel. Let there be one man at Aden and one at whatever port you name who is known to both courts and empowered to carry a question and return an answer within a season, so that a question can be asked before an army is raised to ask it.
>
> Third, and this is the one that matters: that neither court enter into any arrangement with a third power directed against the other without saying so. We do not ask you to refuse such arrangements. We ask only that they not be secret from us, and we bind ourselves in the same words.
>
> Our published toll at the Red Sea entry stands at thirty parts in the hundred, assessed once, the same for your hulls as for every hull afloat, and it will not be raised against you nor lowered for anyone. We say so plainly because the certainty is worth more to a merchant than the rate.
>
> We offer no knowledge and we ask for none. We think you will understand why, and we would rather be plain about it than courteous about it.
>
> The Council of the Pharaoh, at Alexandria.

*(Chancery note, not sent: the third article is the whole letter. The first two are there so that the third does not stand alone. The council's expectation is refusal or silence; the purpose is served either way, since a court that refuses to say it has no arrangement has told us something.)*

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. The Atlantic Question (the priority carried forward from 525).**
*Target:* whether Rome now believes Egypt is across the western ocean, and whether Rome's account of its five vanished vessels has hardened into a belief that Egypt destroyed them.
*Who goes:* four officers of the Greek-Egyptian establishment — men indistinguishable by speech and manner from Hellenes of the Roman east — placed as timber and cordage factors and as a shipwright's clerk.
*Cover and stations:* the naval contracting yards at **Ostia** and **Ravenna** (the old Roman-east Greek network, roughly four hundred years old, deep and reliable), and the Atlantic-facing yards and victualling houses at **Gades**, which is the station that actually matters and where the network is about twenty years old, thin, and run through two merchants only.
*Return to 550:* fragmentary and internally contradictory on the question asked. The council records no finding and treats the return as a floor and not an answer, per standing discipline. Nothing in it is firm enough to plan on and nothing in it has been allowed to change a plan.

**2. The Western Ocean (new, and not run).**
*Target:* whether any other power is present in the western ocean, and where.
*Who goes:* nobody. There is no station, no cover, and no hull with leave to go north of the Orinoco.

## FROM ROME
### 3. TO THE MAURYA
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus, carried by the Euphrates route and by sea from Berenice to Barygaza, 546.**

To the Emperor at Pataliputra and to his ministers, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

*On the exchange agreed in the last generation.* Rome performed. Fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus sailed to Barygaza in 528 with the complete working method of the marine steam engine — boiler, transmission, seals and their failure points, fuel figures — and built the first beside your engineers over four years. Three remain resident with you. Nothing was withheld and nothing was padded, and your own engineers who worked beside ours are the witnesses of that, not we.

Against it we were to receive the full account of the Chinese hand-firearm. Rome has entered no receipt of it. We do not accuse you of bad faith; a letter takes a year and an empire is slow. We state the ledger as it stands because at this distance an unspoken grievance becomes a policy before either of us has noticed, and we would rather write it down. Rome will open no second exchange with you until the first is closed.

*On the tariff.* Your transit rate on Chinese goods through the Euphrates route was doubled without word to us, and Roman merchants have paid for a quarrel they had no part in; the trade has gone to the Dnieper and the Red Sea, which serves neither of us. We ask for a schedule fixed by agreement for a term, at whatever rate, rather than a rate that may move without notice. A merchant can trade against a high tariff. He cannot trade against an uncertain one.

*On what we ask, first.* Your mathematics and your astronomy, entire, such as you are willing to send: the method of calculation and the works of your calculators. Rome has used your figures in its ledgers for a hundred and fifty years and has never asked for what is done with them. Our engineers reckon; they do not derive. We know the difference now.

*On what we ask, second, and why we are ashamed to ask it.* A generation ago your ministers told ours that the copper strips on the walls at Alexandria carry a natural force that travels through metal near-instantaneously, and that your own researchers had found the same force at elementary scale. Rome refused it. We judged it a planted story or a misreading, on the ground that a power which had not grasped the steam engine from watching working machines could not be trusted to interpret an invisible thing — and we built tubes of compressed air instead, because tubes and pressure are what Rome had mastered.

We were wrong, and we are able to say how we were wrong, because we have done it three times. We spent a hundred and eighty years believing the Chinese weapon must be a stored pressure, because pressure is what we understood. We judged Egypt's fast messages to be mirrors, because optics is what we understood. And we told you the force in the copper was compressed air, because air in a pipe is what we understood. Three times we named the wrong category by taking our own competence for the shape of the world.

Therefore we ask for the elementary demonstration: the apparatus, the materials, the method, and what your researchers have observed of it, however small the effect and however uncertain the explanation. We will test it and we will tell you what we find, whether it confirms your account or contradicts it.

We offer in return, and send with this letter without waiting for an answer: the construction of our telescopes complete, glass, figure and mounting; the observations of the phases of Venus and the lights that move about Jupiter; our record of the pneumatic tube installations, which is a record of the wrong answer and therefore of some value; and the metallurgy of our naval boilers, which is four hundred years of failures written down.

*On residence.* We ask that not more than ten Roman men of learning reside at Pataliputra, and offer the same number of yours the same at Rome, on the same terms we have offered the Han.

Severianus Augustus.

## INTENTS DECLARED, OUTCOMES NOT WRITTEN

Rome declares these as intents and does not record any other power's response to them. What each power does is theirs.

1. **Purchase of African labour in quantity at Alexandria**, through the licensed house of Vibulenus of Ostia, for the western possessions. Rome's contingency if refused or priced beyond bearing: abandon the labour-intensive island economy as the centre of the western project and rebuild it around the mechanizable coal coast.
2. **Extension of the naval notification arrangement with Egypt** to the Atlantic and the western ocean.
3. **Request to Egypt for fuller sailing detail** on the hazard of the coast south of Mauretania.

## FROM CHINA
### E3. To the Maurya

**To the Court of the Maurya, from the Emperor of the Han, by the Zhenxi crossing and the ordinary frontier channel, summer of the twenty-first year of the reign (547 AD).**

Greeting.

We write about one thing only, and we write about it because it is cheap now and will not be cheap later.

Both our fleets now carry cannon at sea. Ours are laid to open at long range and our captains have standing orders not to close. Yours, by what our own captains report seeing, are shorter pieces and newer aboard. We say nothing about your gunnery and make no claim about your intentions; we do not know them.

Here is the difficulty. Two fleets that both shoot, in weather, at distances neither crew can judge well, with merchants under escort between them, can begin a war that neither of our courts has decided to fight. In 474 something very like that happened and it cost both of us a great deal. Our own losses that year and the state of our magazines afterward are matters we have not previously discussed with you and do not propose to now.

We therefore propose a convention for encounters at sea, to bind both of us and to be renounceable by either on a year's notice:

- That an armed vessel of either empire approaching a convoy of the other shows its flag and holds beyond two thousand metres unless it intends interception, and that interception is signalled before it is begun.
- That neither empire's ships fire on a vessel that is showing its flag and is neither closing nor interposing.
- That each of us name a port — we propose Panyu and any port you choose — where a commander of the other may send a written account of an incident within ninety days, and that a written account so sent be answered.
- That an incident so reported and answered is not treated by either court as an act of the other's state until both accounts have been read.

We propose no change to the toll, which we pay at twelve parts in a hundred as we have since 477, and we ask no change in what you administer. This is not a treaty about the sea lanes. It is a rule about what our lieutenants may do on a bad morning.

We add one thing plainly, because you will hear a version of it from the carters if we do not. We have this reign increased the powder held at our frontier posts, substantially, and reorganised how those posts are classified. We did this after auditing our own records and finding our arrangements older than they should be. We tell you because you will learn the fact within a season and would otherwise learn it as a secret, and a secret discovered is read as a preparation.

Your cannon stand on the south bank of the Syr Darya where our observers have reported them since the 450s. Ours stand on the north bank. We raised the question of yours in 477 and we are not raising it now. Our position is that the river has held for three hundred years because both of us have found it cheaper than the alternative, and that nothing about the present makes it dearer.

If you will not have the convention, say so and we will not press it. Our escorts' orders stand either way.
