# INBOX — ROME, 525–550

## FROM EGYPT
## MESSAGE OUT — TO ROME

**Sent from Alexandria, autumn 542, by the formal channel through the Roman legation at Alexandria; carried in Greek and in Latin, sealed.**

> From the Council of the Pharaoh Ptolemy, at Alexandria, to the Senate and the Princeps of the Romans, greeting.
>
> We write in a matter that is small now and will not remain small if neither of us speaks of it.
>
> Some years past, three vessels flying your colours came south along the Atlantic face of Africa, kept station off our coast, and returned north. Our ships were present the whole way and did not close, did not signal, and issued no threat. That was by our standing order, given years ago and unchanged, and we tell you of it plainly so that you may know it was not accident, nor weakness, nor the judgment of one captain, but the policy of this court: our vessels will not fire upon yours except upon being fired upon first. We ask nothing in return for telling you this. We think it better that you know it.
>
> We say to you also that the coasts of the western ocean from the mouth of the great northern river of that shore southward to the River of Silver, and the islands lying off them, are within the sphere of Alexandria and have been within the sphere of Alexandria's ships for longer than the present reign. Our harbours are there, our garrisons are there, and our people farm there. We do not set out their number or their places, as you do not set out yours, and we do not think either of us expects the other to.
>
> We make no claim northward of that river, we have entered nothing northward of it, and we do not propose to discuss what lies there.
>
> Two peoples whose ships may begin to meet on an ocean that neither of them can see the whole of will meet again, and the second meeting is always worse than the first if nothing has been agreed between them. We therefore propose a thing that costs neither of us anything and may spare us both a great deal. Let each court give the other notice of voyages passing westward beyond the Fortunate Isles: the number of hulls, the season, and no more than that. Not the purpose. Not the destination. Merely that they sail. A ship expected is a ship that is not mistaken for something else, and most of what has gone wrong between distant powers has gone wrong because someone appeared where no one was looking for him.
>
> We would receive your envoys at Alexandria in any season, and our ships would carry them safely and swiftly, which is a thing we can offer that few can.
>
> The Council of the Pharaoh, at Alexandria.

*(Chancery notes, not sent: the phrase "longer than the present reign" is to be preferred to any statement of years — the reign began in 528 and our first crossing was in 402, so it is true and is meant to be read as recent. This is the house style: we lie by true statement wherever we can, because a true statement survives investigation. No mention is to be made of the vessels lost in Atlantic waters between 398 and the 440s, in any draft, for any reason; no faction wanted it raised and none will.)*

No reply had reached Alexandria by the close of 550. Eight years is not long for that ocean, and the council has drawn no conclusion from the silence.


## FROM CHINA
### E1. To Rome

**To the Emperor of Rome, from the Emperor of the Han, by the Dnieper road, spring of the twenty-second year of the reign (548 AD). Under the standing Agreement, which continues.**

To the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

Twenty-three years ago your court told mine of a crossing of the western ocean: islands six weeks west of the Canaries on a one-way wind, a coast beyond them running more than a thousand miles without a found end, a walled white city of merchants who read and write. You told us before we could have learned it, and told us plainly that you would keep the currents and the reckoning to yourselves rather than let us discover it. You asked nothing.

We owe you a fact of the same kind and send it before you learn it elsewhere.

Before your letter reached us we sent three ships east into our own ocean to learn whether land lay on the far side. Two returned. From ten degrees south to twenty degrees north the wind blows steadily westward and an eastbound ship makes no way against it; that band is closed. At forty degrees north the wind blows the other way, and a ship crossing there reaches a far coast in about five months and returns by descending south and riding the low-latitude wind home. There is land east of us. It is reachable, and only by the high northern latitudes.

We tell you also what we are doing about it, in full, because a court that asks for a rule of restraint while quietly holding ground on the far shore has asked for nothing.

We are surveying the northern coast eastward from our Siberian rivers and northward from the islands beyond Wa. It is slow: we have lost four ships and two hundred and thirty-eight men on that coast, our furthest chart lies six hundred li beyond our furthest post, and our reckoning is that the survey will not reach the far strait for a hundred years, and that we could put no weight on any far shore for a generation after that.

We have also crossed the ocean twice more since, by the forty-degree road, in the years we count 542 to 544 and 546 to 548. Three ships were built for it and all three have come home each time. We have charted some seven hundred li of the far coast about our landfall, which we call Fusang. We have built there a cache of stores in stone, above the tide, with a written board saying what it is and who left it. And in 548 we left twelve men to keep it through the cycle. Our relief sailed this spring and has not yet returned, so at the hour of writing we do not know whether those twelve are living.

That is the whole of it. Twelve men, a store of iron and grain, and a chart. We have refused a settlement, because our treasurer showed the court that a thousand souls fed across that ocean would cost more each year than our entire northern survey. We have forbidden our captains to treat with the peoples of that coast, to settle, to garrison, or to sell iron or powder there on any account, and those orders are in writing and we will show them to your envoy at the Dnieper if you wish to read them.

So we propose nothing about the present beyond what we have just told you. We propose something about a time when neither of us will be alive.

Our two courts have kept a river between us for two hundred years, and it held not because either of us is virtuous but because it was written down early, when neither wanted the far bank badly enough to lie. We propose the same again, before either of us is there at all: that neither empire settle, garrison, or claim ground within some stated distance of the other's established settlements on that continent; that first arrival gives no claim beyond what is actually held and worked; that neither approach the other's positions in force without prior notice by the ordinary channel; and that neither arm the peoples found there against the other. We do not propose a line, since neither of us knows the shape of the land well enough to draw one honestly. We propose the rule by which a line will later be drawn.

We say frankly why. Our court has one old fear, older than anything else in it, of a power growing strong on ground nobody is watching. Seven hundred years ago our founder was surrounded on the northern grass and had to talk his way out; every emperor since has been raised on that story, and we crossed eight thousand li of grassland at ruinous cost so it could not happen again. We know what that fear costs and that it is sometimes foolish. We would rather spend a letter on it than an army, and rather do that with you than with anyone, because you have not once lied to us in two hundred years and we have not forgotten which of us disclosed first.

We have also completed our iron road to the eastern bank of the Dnieper at Anhe, with the yard, cranes and transfer works a junction would require, all of it on our own bank. If your rail is brought to the western bank, silk moves from our wagons to yours without touching the ground, at greater volume and lower cost, and your transit tariff takes its tenth of a larger flow. If you would rather not, the crossing serves our carts as it always has and we shall say no more about it.

If you will not agree to the far-continent rule, say so and we will not press; if you would rather something narrower, propose it. The river between us stands regardless.

We send with this letter, as gifts and not as trade: the pendulum-regulated clock and its method complete, with the caution, learned at our own cost, that it keeps excellent time ashore and none at all on a moving deck; our tables of the sun's angle from our northernmost and southernmost posts, with the circumference we derive from them, near forty-five thousand li in our measure; and our method for making paper from bast and rag, which will cost your presses less than what you use now.

We do not send the tube of two lenses that lets a man see a banner across a valley. We tell you we have one and are not sending it, because we would rather you knew we had kept something than believed we had kept nothing.

We want nothing west of the Dnieper. We have said this for two hundred years and say it again.


## FROM THE MAURYA

**Sent 539 by the overland road through Ctesiphon to the Roman frontier command in Syria, in Latin and Sanskrit, delivered to the Roman transit officials named in the treaty. Drafted by the advisory council; the throne signed it.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Emperor and Senate of Rome, greeting, and to the officers of the road.
>
> Our two empires have kept the overland agreement for two hundred years — twelve parts in the hundred through each empire's ground, fixed for twenty-five years and renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gives notice. It has been the surest revenue either of us draws from the other and the surest friendship. We write about the clause and not about the rate.
>
> We find, on searching our own registers, that we have never established when notice must be given, to whom it must be delivered, or by what channel, and we suspect we have been trusting to good luck and to your good faith. Both have held. Neither is a procedure. We propose, therefore, and would sign at once:
>
> That notice under the agreement be given not less than three years before the end of a span; that it be delivered in writing to the transit officers of the other empire at the Euphrates crossing and simultaneously to the other's court, and be effective only when both have acknowledged in writing; that each empire's transit officers exchange, at each midsummer, a signed statement that no notice has been given, so that silence is recorded as silence and not merely assumed; and that the span now running be confirmed as renewed for a further twenty-five years upon the existing terms.
>
> We ask this because our own habit has bitten us before. We once read a treaty of the eastern sea as implying a toll its words did not contain, and we were wrong, and we paid for the reading with a fleet. We would rather write down what we mean than discover in twenty years what we assumed.
>
> Three further matters, plainly.
>
> On the road itself: your merchants complained in the last generation of robbers on the Mesopotamian stretch. We have built eleven new waystations, kept the patrols at strength, and from two years ago we offer a mounted escort to any convoy that wants one at a charge we do not profit by. We believe the road is safer now than at any time since the tariff was set. If your merchants say otherwise we would hear it from you and not from rumour.
>
> On the firearm: we sent you, in the last reign, a complete and honest maker's account of the hand-weapon we had met in the eastern sea, and you gave us the marine engine, and both sides kept the bargain. We ask now whether you have made the weapon and put it in your soldiers' hands, and in what numbers, and what you have found. We ask because we have done so ourselves and have learned that the hard part is not the weapon. It is the powder — bulk manufacture of even quality, which is a question of works built and not of cleverness, and which has held this realm back for two hundred and seventy-five years. We will send you, if you want it, our proofing standard, our gauged charges, and the design of our incorporating mills, which are the whole of what we have learned in twenty-five years of building six of them. We ask nothing for it that you do not wish to give. We have concluded that on this one matter neither of us gains by the other's ignorance, since the power that has this weapon in numbers already is neither of us.
>
> On the western ocean: we know that you have found land beyond it and have settled it, and we learned that by accident from a merchant, which does neither of us credit. We have settled a great southern land of our own, reached from our eastern seas, and we tell you so directly rather than let you hear it the way we heard of yours. We do not propose to trade charts. We propose only that neither of us should again learn a thing of this size from a third man in a market.

*Receipt was acknowledged by the Roman transit officers at the crossing in 540 and the letter forwarded. No reply on the notice clause had reached Pataliputra by 550.*

*One note on the sixth paragraph. We offered Rome our proofing standard, our gauged charges and our mill designs in 539, and in 541 the largest of those mills killed a hundred and eighty-four of our own men. Whether anything of ours was ever built to it we do not know. Nothing was sent to correct it. The council considered the question in 546 and concluded that to write again would be to advertise the accident. That is the reasoning; the record does not endorse it.*

**And in 546 Rome wrote to us, and we refused it.** The offer, brought to the Euphrates by Rome's own hand, was of exchanged engineers again as in the steam bargain, and with them the making of telescopes, Rome's record of what happens to air and vapour under pressure and heat, and — set out first in the letter, as the thing Rome evidently thought most valuable — **its metallurgy of boilers**: which metals, worked how, fail where and at what heat and pressure, and what may safely stand beside a fire.

The realm declined. Not by argument and not after a debate; by clerk, under the standing instruction of 535 that no technical guidance offered by a foreign power is to be accepted, acted upon or carried home. That instruction was written in temper against Egypt over a plant. Nobody had ever reviewed it, extended it, or noticed that it was worded generally, and so by 546 it applied to Rome, to China, and to anyone else who might ever offer this realm anything, and it was executed by a man in the diplomatic office doing his job correctly.

**Five years earlier a steam engine standing beside an incorporating mill had killed a hundred and eighty-four of our men, and the discipline that governs whether an engine belongs beside anything is the metallurgy of boilers.** The men who buried the mill-workers at Bhadrapura sit in the ordnance establishment. The men who decide what foreign correspondence may be accepted sit in the diplomatic office. Their returns go up two chains and meet in a clerk's summary. Neither had any reason to know what the other was about.

This is the third instance in this file — Bhūtiśarman's memorandum, the glass shop four hundred paces from the wire men, and now this — and it is time the record stated the pattern as a finding rather than an anecdote. **The realm does not fail for want of knowledge, money, or men. It fails because it holds the answer in another building, under another heading, in the hands of someone with no cause to speak, and it has never once paid anybody to walk between rooms.** Every other defect named in this reign is affordable. This one is free to fix and has cost more than all of them.

*Our frontier officials report that no notice has been given by Rome, and this record states that as what our officials report and not as a fact about Rome's intentions.*
