# INBOX — HAN CHINA, 525–550

## FROM EGYPT
## MESSAGE OUT — TO CHINA

**Sent from Alexandria, spring 543, by the Aden commercial office and the Quanzhou representation, in Coptic and in Chinese.**

> From the Council of the Pharaoh Ptolemy, at Alexandria, to the Court of the Emperor, greeting.
>
> The exchange holds and we have no complaint in it. Your twenty have had our physicians' whole body of practice, on no schedule and with nothing kept back, as we said they would. Your paper and your presses have done more for our schools than we told you at the time.
>
> We write for three reasons, and we ask for nothing you have said you will not give.
>
> First, we tell you before you hear it elsewhere, because you told us plainly of the western land when you had no obligation to. Egypt has settled the western ocean's far shore. Our harbours run from the mouth of the great northern river of that coast southward to a river we call the River of Silver, and our people farm there. We claim nothing north of that river. We have written to Rome saying the same, in the same words, and we enclose the text so that you hold what they hold.
>
> Second, we ask that the consultation clause be read to cover that ocean as it covers this one: that if a third power brings either of us a proposal touching the western shore, each tells the other that it was brought. You granted consultation in both directions and openly, and said you would tell Rome whatever you tell us. We ask nothing better than that, and we hold you to nothing more.
>
> Third, we propose the same procedure we proposed to Rome, which costs nothing: notice of voyages, the number of hulls and the season and no more, for your ships passing west of Socotra and ours passing east of it. Not the purpose. Not the destination. Merely that they sail.
>
> We ask you for no knowledge in this letter. Our standing instruction since the last exchange is to look first at where we already lead before requesting anything, and on this occasion we found nothing to ask for that we do not have.
>
> The Council of the Pharaoh, at Alexandria.


## FROM ROME
### 1. TO THE HAN
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus. Carried from Statio Borysthenia on the Dnieper, spring of 548.**

To the Emperor of the Han and to his ministers, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

*On the junction of the rails.* We accept the joining of our lines at the Dnieper, under the agreement standing between us, which renews itself and which we do not ask to change — as a yard of transfer, not a joining of roads. Our gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and we will not relay it; we do not ask you to relay yours. Your cars will come to the eastern bank and stop, ours will stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods will cross where wheels do not. The reason is honest and we state it rather than dress it: a rail running from one empire onto the other is a road for an army, and neither of us would lay the other's gauge inside his own ground. The yard will stand on the Roman bank under Roman garrison at Statio Borysthenia; your factors will work in it under Roman law exactly as our merchants are protected in your territory; and we will build the sheds, cranes and sidings at our own cost, because we want the silk carried rail to rail more than we want the argument over who pays.

*On the gifts offered.* We receive the mechanical clock and its escapement with thanks and will set it beside our own work, which has reached the accuracy of navigation and not yet beyond. We return the method of paper with respect and a plain statement: Rome has made paper from linen and flax for five hundred and fifty years and printed with cast type for four hundred and fifty. We say so not to diminish the gift but because a man who accepts a gift he does not need has told his friend a small lie for politeness, and small lies compound at this distance.

*On the question you have asked four times.* We answer in full, and you will not have to ask a fifth.

Rome has sailed west. Ships leave the islands we call the Fortunate Isles at twenty-eight degrees north, where the wind blows steadily west and a great current runs west beneath the hull, and together they make a road that carries a ship whether she wishes it or not. Six weeks on that road brings her to a chain of green mountainous islands, and west of them a coast, which our ships have followed more than a thousand miles without finding its end either way.

The lands hold peoples: island farmers of cassava and maize, skilled at sea in canoes cut from single trees; on a stone shore to the west, walled cities with their own writing, calendar, priesthood and merchants, forty and more, at war among themselves and under no single king; further north, scattered fishing and farming peoples of no great wealth, on a coast of good timber and, we have found, of coal. In all that country we have found no metal worked at all — not iron, not bronze, not copper, not as tool or weapon or ornament. Rome has taken the two largest islands and holds them, holds a fortified island off the stone coast, and has planted a coaling port and a colony on a northern river. We tell you because you will hear it, and because a man who conceals what his friend already knows has concealed nothing and revealed himself.

We do not send a chart of our positions, and we tell you why rather than let you infer it. Two hundred and fifty years ago Rome gave your envoys at this same river a full survey of Roman roads, cities, forts and supply lines, and received a sketch without scale. Rome recorded that as its own error and has not repeated it with any power since. We hold to the rule with you not because we distrust you more than others but because we distrust ourselves in this particular way, having been foolish once in exactly this place.

*On the thing we ask.* Your envoys told ours that you had measured the sphere of the earth, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned the western land into existence before any ship of ours touched it. We ask for that — not the number, which is a curiosity, but the method and the mathematics beneath it: how the measurement is made, what is assumed, and how one reasons from a measured sphere to a place no man has seen. We ask further for the works of your astronomers and calculators, such as you are willing to send.

We offer what we have of the same kind: the construction of our telescopes, glass, figure and mounting, complete; the observations made with them, that Venus shows phases as the moon does and that small lights move about Jupiter and keep to it; and the argument these have raised among us, that the inherited model of a fixed earth cannot hold them and that the sun and not the earth may stand at the centre. Our schoolmen resist it. The men who look through the glass do not. We send you the dispute along with the observations, because a dispute is worth more than a conclusion.

*On residence.* We ask that not more than ten Roman men of learning reside at your capital, and offer the same number of yours the same at Rome. A man who lives among you twenty years will understand you and a man who visits twenty days will not, and every misunderstanding between us for two hundred and fifty years was born in that gap.

*On the boundary.* The Dnieper stands. We want nothing east of it, have moved no soldier across it, and will not without prior word to you. Your people on the eastern bank have grown into settled towns as ours have on the western, and we take that as two peoples farming and nothing else — and we say so plainly, so that no clerk on either bank has to guess what we take it as.

Severianus Augustus.

## FROM THE MAURYA

**Sent 548 by the Sogdian caravan channel to the frontier command across the Syr Darya, written in Chinese in our own hand. Proposed by the treasury, not the throne.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Emperor of the Han, greeting.
>
> It is fifty years since our two realms settled the matter of the eastern sea, and the settlement has held on both sides without a word of complaint from either. We write on a matter of trade and on nothing else.
>
> Our transit charge upon the overland road stands at twelve parts in the hundred, where it has stood since we returned it to that figure under our agreement with you. We observe that a growing share of your western trade now goes by the northern grasslands and not by our road. We do not complain of it; a merchant takes the cheaper road and that is the whole of the science.
>
> We propose to lower our charge to ten parts in the hundred upon goods carried the whole length of our ground, and to hold it there for twenty-five years, and we tell you so in advance rather than let your merchants discover it. Our road is watered, walled, patrolled, and repaired; the northern grass is none of these. We would rather take ten parts of a great traffic than twelve of a shrinking one, and we say so plainly because there is no advantage to us in your not knowing it.
>
> If your merchants find our waystations wanting in any particular we will hear it and mend it. Our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait remains open to your hulls on the terms agreed, and the berth worked by your own harbour men stands as it was set.
>
> We ask nothing in return and we raise no other matter.

*Carried by Nanaivandak's house and receipted at the frontier. No reply had reached Pataliputra by 550. The toll reduction was proclaimed at the Sogdian cities and along the road in 549 whether or not any reply ever comes, the treasury holding that a rate set for a reason should not wait on a courtesy.*
