# INBOX — EGYPT, 525–550

## FROM ROME
### 2. TO EGYPT
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus, through the Roman legation at Alexandria, 545.**

To the Throne of Egypt, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

Our two states have kept permanent legations for a hundred and thirty years and have not in that time exchanged a blow. We write on three matters, plainly, and ask nothing that is not stated.

*First.* Rome holds islands and a coast beyond the western ocean. Our gazettes print it, our shipping houses insure it, and our ports register the crews, so we will not affect a secrecy that our own printers abandoned twenty years ago. We hold two great islands, a fortified island off a stone shore, a walled trading post, and a coaling port and colony on a northern river. We have taken no ground on any coast either of us has previously touched, and we do not intend to.

*Second, and the substance of this letter.* Since the reign of the Augustus who opened these legations, our two navies have notified one another of movements in the Mediterranean, and in a hundred and thirty years that arrangement has never once been broken by either of us. It is the single most useful thing the two states have made together. We propose to extend it: that the same notification apply in the Atlantic beyond the strait, and in the western ocean, on the same terms and by the same channel. Neither of us gives up anything by it. Both of us are relieved of the necessity of guessing, and guessing between fleets is how two states that do not want a war acquire one. We hold this offer open whatever answer is given to the third matter.

*Third.* Rome asks licence for Roman houses to purchase labour in quantity through your factors at Alexandria, for the western possessions, at whatever rate and under whatever conditions of registration you set. We have licensed the house of Vibulenus of Ostia to make the application. We state the reason without ornament, because you will infer it and an inferred reason is worse than a stated one: the peoples of the islands we hold are dying, of something our physicians cannot name and our quarantines have not stopped, and the works there have no hands.

*A fourth thing, smaller.* Twenty-five years ago your officers told ours that the coast south of our Mauretanian provinces is held by maritime peoples who have destroyed outside vessels for as long as your African trade has existed, and that the current there carries a disabled ship away so that no wreckage returns. We have lost five ships to that coast and have closed it. We ask for whatever fuller sailing detail you are willing to give — the latitudes, the season, the manner of approach — for the safety of our merchantmen on the Mauretanian run. We do not ask what lies behind that coast.

Severianus Augustus.

## FROM CHINA
### E2. To Egypt

**To the Court of Egypt, from the Emperor of the Han, by the hand of the senior scholar at Aden, autumn of the twentieth year of the reign (546 AD).**

Greeting.

Four years ago our physicians finished the work your scholars began for us. What you gave us was method — the siting of camps, the managing of water, the timing of movement against the season, the closing of a port — and one proposition our physicians had not held before, that particular fevers answer to particular plants and not to regimen alone. What we had to do with it took ten years, because not one of the plants you named grows in our south and none could be carried in any useful quantity. We had to run your principle against our own herbs and find our own answers.

We tell you what we found, since you gave freely. Our agent is the sweet wormwood we call qinghao, and the thing that decided the whole matter is its preparation: boiled, as any physician would prepare a herb, it does nearly nothing, and we nearly abandoned the work on that result. A physician of ours working away from a fire steeped it cold instead, and cold it works. We cannot tell you why. We have forbidden our surgeons to heat it, in the same words we would use to forbid them to poison a man, because we are afraid a cook will improve it. Our second agent is changshan root, decocted in the ordinary way, and it will empty a man violently above the dose so the dose is written down.

It works. In the fourth year of the trial we lost nine men in a hundred where we had always lost fifty or more in a single season, and we now hold a post through the wet season in country that stopped our armies for four hundred years. We tell you this for two reasons. The first is that you gave the knowledge freely and at once, asking only continued access to our optics and our mathematics, and a court that takes a gift of that size and then reports nothing is not a partner but a pocket. The second is that you should know what your gift did, since you may not have intended to hand us a frontier.

We will not pretend to you that it was only a medical matter. It was, for us, the removal of a wall. What we do with the ground behind that wall is not yet decided and we do not undertake to tell you when it is.

We continue to send you what we said we would: our lens geometry and our grinding, our tables and our measured circumference, our clock and its escapement, and now our method for the dense glass we melted at Chang'an in 541, which spreads colour further than ordinary glass for the same bending of light. We have not made a lens of it that corrects colour. If your workshops have, we would rather be told than left to spend another twenty years.

We ask one thing and name it plainly rather than let our scholars circle it. We are building hulls that carry fire under pressure. In 534 a boiler burst at our southern yard and killed nineteen men, among them the only man who understood the whole of it. We have rebuilt the knowledge on paper and we have two vessels working our own coast, and we are certain we are doing several things badly that your yards stopped doing badly generations ago. We ask for what you judge you can give of plate and seam and rivet, of the pressures your yards hold, and of how you test a vessel before you put men in it. We ask for the testing above all. If the answer is no, we will take the no without complaint and our scholars will continue as they are.

And we tell you what we withhold, since you have not asked and will find it out eventually: we do not send you our work on the electrical force, which would show you only how far behind you we are; we do not send our steam hulls or their drawings; and we send nothing of powder, which you have never requested.

The standing question our scholars have carried since the year 425 is unanswered and we put it once more, without expectation: what is it that produces the force continuously, rather than in a single stored burst. Nine thousand recorded failures at Chang'an say we will not find it by looking.


## FROM THE MAURYA

**Sent 534. Carried by sea from Oman to Aden under a herald's flag, offered to the Egyptian officer commanding at the strait, translated by a Sogdian and a Yemeni interpreter together.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Sovereign of Egypt, greeting.
>
> A vessel bearing our flag put men ashore upon your coast in the spring of the year before last, below the harbours where our ships have traded by your permission for five hundred years. Those men went inland without your leave, and they have not come back to us. If they are in your hands we ask for them.
>
> We say plainly what our own inquiry found. The master sailed upon an old standing order of our station at Oman which no living minister had reviewed, and exceeded any warrant of ours; the officer who let that order stand has been removed. We did not send those men to take your ground and we do not claim it. Eleven men walked inland to buy a plant. That is the whole of the offence, and we dress it neither more nor less.
>
> We ask two things.
>
> The first is our men — seamen, a physician's assistant, one interpreter. Return them and we count the matter closed, and we will set a heavier hand upon our own captains than you could set upon them.
>
> The second is the trade, and here we ask you first for information and only then for a favour, because we find we do not know what has happened. Since last summer our masters have been turned from harbours on that coast where they have watered for four hundred years, and our factors have closed their houses. We do not know whether this is done by your order, by the order of your officers on that shore, or for some cause that has nothing to do with us at all. We would rather be told than guess, and we have guessed enough already. If it is your answer to the eleven men, we say that it is a heavier answer than the fault, and we ask that the old permission stand again upon your own old terms — our merchants trade through your ports, hold no ground, claim no ground, which we have kept for five hundred years and kept even while we were angry.
>
> In return we offer what is ours to offer, and it is not small. Egyptian shipping entering the Gulf at our station of Oman, and Egyptian shipping using our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait between the peninsula and the great island, shall pay no port due and no transit charge for twenty-five years, on the same footing as our own hulls. We have never made that offer to any power, and we do not make it to China.
>
> One further matter, set down so there is no misunderstanding later. In past years your people have offered our scholars guidance upon questions of natural philosophy, and ours were grateful for it. Our court has determined that we will henceforth pursue such questions by our own scholars, in our own houses, upon our own reasoning, and our envoys are instructed to accept no such guidance in future. We say it directly rather than let your people learn it from a closed door. It touches nothing of the trade, and we would not have you read into it more than we mean.
>
> Send us your answer by the same hand.

*Our herald was received at the strait and the letter taken from him. Nothing had come back to Pataliputra by 550, by any hand, in any form. The eleven men have not returned. We do not know whether the letter travelled beyond the officer who took it.*

*A note on the third paragraph of that letter, which the council fought over for a month. The envoy's draft asserted that Egypt had closed the coast. It was struck out and replaced with a question, over the objection of most of the room, by the one councillor who pointed out that we did not know it and that a great power which accuses another of an act it did not commit gets an answer it will deserve. He was right and he was resented for it, and the letter went as it stands.*
