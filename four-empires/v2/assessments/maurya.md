# The Maurya Realm — Internal Assessment at the Close of 525 AD

*Compiled at Pataliputra for the throne and the advisory council. This is not the chronicle. The chronicle is what we tell ourselves. This is the audit.*

---

## 1. What we actually hold

The largest continuous governed dominion any throne has held, and it is four different things stapled together.

**The heartland.** The subcontinent whole, Hindu Kush to Tamil cape, plus Sri Lanka. Seven and a half centuries of roads, canals, granaries, standard coin and weights, mingled custom. The only part of the realm that would not come apart if the court did. Roughly three-quarters of our people.

**The old marches.** Afghanistan (eight hundred years held), Bactria, Sogdia with the Silk Road cities, the Iranian plateau, Armenia and Azerbaijan. Thin population, high strategic cost, moderate revenue.

**The western dominion.** Mesopotamia to the Euphrates, from the second capital on the Tigris. Revolted once, drifted twice, bound now by prosperity rather than love — it is the richest transit ground on the Rome road, and a province that revolts burns its own purse. A real bond, and one that dissolves the day the road stops paying.

**The sea positions and the new continent.** Oman at the Gulf mouth; the tributary pearl ports of the Arabian shore; eastern Yemen; the Strait of Malacca with our port at its eastern mouth; the shrunken garrisons of Ternate and Banda; and now fifty major settlements and three hundred lesser stations on the southern continent, plus island groups north and east of it and a cold island off its south coast — the latter three taken by force.

Frontiers: the Syr Darya (China beyond), the Euphrates and Greater Caucasus (Rome), the Levantine approaches and the Red Sea strait (Egypt), open ocean east and south. We say there is no neutral march anywhere on our border. That is doctrine, not geography — the Arabian desert is one, the ocean is one, and the whole southern hemisphere was one until we filled it in. We say "no buffers" because the *Arthashastra* wants us encircled and vigilant.

**How we expanded.** In twenty-five-year bites, by the Rolling Frontier Doctrine: conquer only what you can hold and supply, then spend a generation turning it into the platform for the next push. Ashoka conquered nothing after Kalinga; he built forward granaries so his son could reach farther. Bactria, Sogdia, Khorasan, Iran, Mesopotamia, Armenia — each from a platform the previous reign built, each against a divided or distracted enemy, none by lunge. We halted at the Euphrates because the ground gave a holdable line and Rome had a hard name we never tested.

**Tributaries.** Extensively, and the record is worse than we admit. Tamil tribute was *entered in our own registers during years when nothing was collected* — the founding precedent of this realm's bookkeeping, that we write down what we are owed and call it revenue. We conquered the Tamil south outright around 25 BC because tribute is cheap to demand and dear to keep. The Gulf ports, eastern Yemen, and the Malay and Sumatran kingdoms remain tributary, and every tributary tie we have held decayed the moment the visible threat of our arms lapsed.

**Are we nice?** No, nor cruel; we are *transactional*. Our discovery, made in Mesopotamia and repeated in the Spice Islands, is that occupation is expensive and cooperation cheap, so we make submission pay better than resistance. We tax conduct and not belief — Zoroastrians, Jews, Christians, Buddhists, Jains all keep their rites under a Sanskrit administration. The tolerance is genuine and it is the cheapest instrument in the box. Where cooperation could not be bought we took: the Tamil kingdoms, Sri Lanka, the Spice Islands, now the southern islands.

That is not why our population is what it is. Ninety-eight million live under this throne because the Gangetic plain, the Deccan, the Tamil south, and the Mesopotamian valley are among the most productive watered farmland on earth, and because we have spent seven centuries on wells, reservoirs, canals, and granaries. Governance adds at the margin. Rivers and rice do the work.

## 2. People, letters, faith, bondage

Ninety-eight million *registered* — and that word is doing quiet work. Growth flat since 400 (95M to 98M across a century). We blame plague, famine, flood, and war. I would blame part of it on a tax register evaded faster than it is corrected, in provinces whose informer networks were gutted in the bad reigns and only partly restored.

Literacy: "about one person in ten," a figure appearing in every numbers block from 250 BC to 500 AD without moving. That is not a measurement; it is a constant copied forward for seven hundred and fifty years, through the arrival of administrative printing, paper, and a Brahmin-sealed press. Stop quoting it.

Language: Sanskrit for administration everywhere, with Iranian, Aramaic, Tamil, Sogdian, Malay in their regions; scholars who read Chinese without Sogdians; Latin through the Rome-road officials. The most multilingual court in the world, treated as clerical convenience rather than the asset it is.

Faith: Brahmanical order at the summit, Buddhism and Jainism regional and unsupported, Zoroastrianism embraced in the west, everything else tolerated. Ashoka's refusal to give Buddhism the imperial embrace is the hinge of our religious history, and the consequence we never state is that the caste order was never disrupted — which is why literacy sits at a tenth and our researcher pool is capped with it. We have now handed the Brahmins the press itself and called it a victory.

Slavery: one in ten to one in twenty, five to ten million people — mines, great construction, skilled crafts, rich households, and now shipped south for the labour of founding. Protections are "kept unevenly, least of all for the enslaved of foreign birth," our polite way of saying they are not kept. We have planted a colonial society on another continent whose labour base is bonded and whose neighbours we conquered.

## 3. Money — and the hole in the middle of this reign

Revenue in 500: 2,280 million paṇas — land tax 980M, trade near 750M, the rest mines, monopolies, tolls. Flat and tilting down, its composition shifting from the glamorous trades to farm and salt. Silk Road tolls down from 320M to 265M as China's steppe route bleeds them; maritime tolls collapsed from 170M to 110M when we lost the eastern sea; Kolar gold at three-fifths of peak. Against those, the Rome road climbing to 155M and the land tax grinding upward. Reserve: 1,400M, six-tenths of a year against a prudent target of one. Surplus: 112M a year.

This reign then committed ~250M a year to southern colonization (6,250M over the quarter century) and restored armament from 150M to ~230M. Neither the thirty-to-forty new cannon vessels nor the colonial garrisons and seasonal convoys are costed anywhere.

The chronicle says the money came "from the surplus, from a hard draw on the reserve over the treasury's loud objection, and from trimming the household and temple lines." Household and temple together are 138M; a hard trim might yield 40M. So: 250 + 80 − 112 − 40 = **178 million paṇas of unfunded spending per year.** Against a 1,400M reserve, that is empty in roughly eight years. The reign ran twenty-five.

**This is the most important fact in our situation, and our own turn record does not state it.** There is no "By the numbers, around 525" block. Every other quarter-century for seven hundred and eighty-five years has one. Either revenue rose far beyond anything our documents suggest — it did not; the south produces nothing yet, the Silk Road still ebbs, Kolar still fails — or we cut lines we have not admitted cutting, or we debased the paṇa, or the colonial ledgers are fiction, or all four. Working assumption: **the reserve is at or near zero, infrastructure and famine-relief have been quietly trimmed, and the treasury knows it.** The Tigris canals that broke in the 440s broke exactly this way, from a slackened line nobody discussed.

**Genuinely free money: none.** Perhaps 50 to 100 million a year of true discretion, and negative if the audit is honest. The only large reversible line is the southern program, and reversing it means confessing that the defining act of the reign was pride dressed as strategy — near-impossible while the Egyptian fleet's destination stays unconfirmed. Also unpriced: we took ocean hulls out of the Tamil and Bengal ports for twenty-five years, and those shipowners are exactly the men whose capital we need to rebuild a navy.

## 4. What we can build, who builds it, what stops us

**In hand at 525:** wootz steel unmatched anywhere. Mathematics beyond any rival — decimal notation with a working zero, algebra, number theory, trigonometry, now reaching into continuous change — and astronomers among whom some argue the sun sits at the centre. Ayurvedic medicine, surgery, pharmacology, inoculation against the pox, the Alexandrian washing protocols, a systematic materia medica with real antiseptics and analgesics. Ocean shipwrightry, monsoon and current navigation, the mechanical clock with escapement giving east-west reckoning at sea, the spyglass, optical signal relays carrying word across the realm in a day. Distillation, ground lenses, zinc and brass, spinning aids. Paper since about 513; printing past its administrative confinement. Fifty to eighty fixed frontier cannon, thirty to forty naval cannon vessels, a crew-served two-man piece since about 506, a one-man firearm since about 518, a marine steam engine since about 506, and a functional theory of the galvanic force four years matured.

**Researchers.** The armament budget at 230M supports, on our own reckoning of one part in four to wages at 2,000 paṇas, some **28,000 to 29,000 trained researchers**, with perhaps 50,000 craftsmen and bonded labourers behind them, inside a technical class of 55,000–110,000 and an educated professional class of 650,000–1.1 million. So far as we know, the largest concentration of funded investigation in the world.

**What blocks us.**

1. *Powder.* Two hundred and seventy-five years of consistent testimony: the bottleneck is never the idea, it is bulk manufacture of gunpowder of even quality. We have the one-man firearm; we almost certainly have a few thousand of them, not an armed million-man army.
2. *Fine optics.* We grind lenses and build spyglasses; we cannot grind well enough to see the small — and without that there is no route to why the washing works.
3. *Chemistry.* Combustion, distillation, accidental electrochemistry, and no theory of matter beneath any of it. Our medicine advances by watching what works without knowing why, and that is now its binding ceiling.
4. *Precision.* Egypt had interchangeable fittings across a whole city in 367. We do not. Every engine and gun is a craftsman's individual work — exactly why armament lags wealth.
5. *Fuel.* Nobody has asked what our steam engines burn at scale. Egypt has oil of the earth; we have charcoal and forests. Never costed.
6. *Wire, insulation, and a code.* We have galvanic theory and not Egypt's network, and the gap is drawn wire, insulation, relays, and a signalling scheme — none begun.
7. *Caste and letters.* One in ten literate caps the researcher pool, and the cap is social, not fiscal. We could pay for four times as many investigators; we could not recruit them.
8. *The purge.* Every research catastrophe we suffered was a purge, not a shortage of ideas — 275, 425. This reign built friction against the next one, and that friction is untested.

Our Appendix K argues money and staff buy speed. Broadly right, and self-serving. Two of our most valuable holdings — the galvanic force and the African drought-grains — came out of an underfunded program and a *failed* one. Our best returns are accidents of breadth, not payoffs of concentration.

## 5. The other powers, honestly

**China.** Across the Syr Darya since 225. Cannon, ship-borne guns, bursting shells, the one-man firearm, warships that set their own speed regardless of wind. Two engagements: eleven of ours lost for one of theirs; then ninety of a hundred and ten for *none*. Our total naval record against a peer is a hundred and one hulls lost and one enemy destroyed. China took Tidore with a few hundred men, offered terms as a victor, took the commerce it wanted, and demanded we strip our northern cannon. We refused that one point and it let the matter lie. We have since concluded China's aims are "commercial rather than bent on conquering the realm's lands" — a settled conclusion that stopped being examined. A demand to remove fixed artillery from a land frontier a thousand miles from the disputed islands is not commercial. It was a probe of whether we would disarm under pressure, and we filed the conclusion anyway.

**Egypt.** For four hundred years we called it a keeper of gateways. In the 440s we found it holds the entire eastern coast of Africa unbroken — every harbour and river mouth — a continental empire whose African provinces alone dwarf what we had reckoned the whole of Egypt to be. It rebuilt Alexandria from an earthquake so completely in two years that our engineers found nothing to mend. It has printing, steam at sea, petroleum lighting, canal locks, interchangeable fittings, medical hygiene, a mixed-nationality administration doing effortlessly what we failed at in the west for a century, and a copper network carrying messages across distance. It gives function and never construction, roasts every exported bean so none can be planted, and spends spies in Rome to guard a plant. Our estimate of Egypt has been revised drastically upward twice. That is a pattern, and the correct posture is to assume it is still too low.

**Rome.** Our "steadiest tie." The ledger: it concealed that it had steam until it slipped a hint only a builder could have known; it subsidized Egypt's earthquake recovery at charity prices and told us nothing while sharing a front with us against Egypt's tolls; it extracted a *maker's* account of the Chinese hand-firearm, complete in one delivery, under threat to find the seams; and it colonized a new continent, which we learned of by accident while pulling a thread on Egypt. Rome has out-negotiated us in every exchange. It is steady in the sense that it has never lied outright. It is not our friend. And the trade treaty — twelve parts in the hundred each way, fixed twenty-five years and **renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gives notice** — is the most consequential document we hold and nobody at court reads it. It has rolled over by default three or four times. Establish exactly when notice must be given, because Rome certainly has.

**The Sogdians.** Not a state. Our translators, messengers, and first channel to China, whose loyalty runs to the trade. We reduced our dependence; we did not remove it.

**How far ahead or behind?** Ahead of everyone in mathematics, steel, land war, medicine, and in the scale of funded research. Behind China at sea and in the breadth of gunpowder. Behind Egypt by an unknown span in the telegraph, standardization, engineering throughput, and whatever we have not seen — which, on precedent, is most of it. On land none of this matters; nobody can reach our heartland. At sea it matters entirely, and at sea is where everything new is happening.

**How much land do they have?** We do not know, and that is the honest answer for all three. Egypt: the Nile, the Levant, the Red Sea, the strait, and the whole east African coast below the tropic — depth unmeasured. China: a landmass wide enough that its armies crossed it westward to reach our river, plus a steppe route and an ocean fleet built on a far southeastern coast. Rome: an Italian heart, a reach "far to the north and northeast," an overseas colony, a northern river frontier where it has met gunpowder. Every one is a shape, not a survey.

## 6. Philosophy, temperament, fears

**Philosophy.** The *Arthashastra*. A state that stops growing invites attack. Your neighbour is your enemy, his neighbour your friend. Tend all seven pillars — territory, treasury, army, forts, allies, counsel, king — together or lose all of them. Tax conduct, not belief. Buy the enemy's isolated allies before fighting him. On top of it, the Rolling Frontier Doctrine. Cold, patient, materially rational, indifferent to glory, deeply interested in the harvest.

**Governing style.** Bureaucratic, survival-minded, forgiving where forgiveness is cheaper than force and ruthless where it is not, comfortable with informers and bribery. Not backstabbing — we have kept our word to Rome and China with unusual consistency, partly by policy and partly because we keep getting caught when we do not. Our characteristic vice is not treachery but *earnestness*.

**Are we comfortable lying? Yes.** Our registers carried Tamil tribute never collected. Our ledgers counted Spice Island garrisons at establishment strength for fifty years while disease emptied them, "counting the establishment and not the graves." We painted Egypt to China in the darkest honest colours we could find. We sent Rome a true account of the firearm while concealing that we held one, that we had a channel into the Han arsenals, and that we had become a maker of the weapon rather than its victim. Our lies are omissions and framings, and the most dangerous are the ones we tell ourselves in our own account books.

**Rational worries.** The treasury. China at sea. Egypt's compounding lead. A thin northern frontier. A west governed more than embraced. The next bad reign and the next contested succession. Powder capacity. The Silk Road bleeding north. Kolar running out.

**Irrational fears — which drive us harder than the rational ones.**

- *Being walled out.* The master neurosis. For a century and a half Egypt has told our captains, harbour after harbour, "trade if you must and then go home," and the humiliation of it — not the lost profit, the humiliation — is what our own turn record admits set this reign in motion. We spent nearly three years of the realm's entire revenue, more than the reserve has ever held, on a continent we might not have needed, because we could not bear being made the fool of the sea twice. We wrote the minutes in the calm language of platforms and supply. The decision was made by a court miffed to the point of grief.
- *Monopoly as the shape of power.* Because Egypt's coffee monopoly stung, we came to believe holding a chokepoint is what greatness looks like. So we squeezed the Spice Islanders into a single buyer, garrisoned three islands, and tried to enforce a Strait toll by force against China. That belief cost us a fleet and an island. We still hold it.
- *The north.* Five hundred thousand garrison troops, six thousand elephants, and fifty to eighty fixed cannon oriented at a river where nothing has happened in three hundred years. Every loss we have actually suffered came at sea or in the ledger.
- *Egyptian poison.* We have formally concluded that Egypt's advice to seek the galvanic force in plants was sabotage. I do not believe it. Egypt said it began with the torpedo fish and that much of its path ran through living things — and our own plant program produced tissue twitching under live current, confirming the wire thread from another angle. That is what an honest hint from a power that shares function and not construction looks like. We ran the wrong experiment for twenty-five years and blamed the one rival that ever handed us a thread. Believing this makes us refuse the next one.
- *Centrality.* We believe we sit necessarily between east and west. We have been routed around twice: by China's northern steppe route, and by the Latin interpreter we sold China, who taught China's own scholars Latin and made us unnecessary within a year.

**How logical are we?** Logical in method, emotional in choice. We reason beautifully about decisions already made for other reasons. The habit that should worry us most is the one after the interpreter loss: we made a large error, felt alarm, then *talked ourselves down*, groping for the good in it until we had constructed a listening post that never existed. We do that often.

## 7. Why we are still here, and what we got wrong

**No collapse in seven hundred and eighty-five years** because of a heartland integrated by roads, coin, measures, canals, and granaries rather than garrisons; a salaried bureaucracy that draws pay and orders from the state and cares little whose name sits above it; an officer corps raised for centuries to regard marching for one claimant against another as the ruin of the thing it serves; tolerance that gives nobody a banner; and a tax base so large that a very bad reign reaches only the edges. And, unmentioned but decisive: **no rival has ever had the logistics to reach the Gangetic plain.** We have never been tested.

**Rebellions.** One serious — Mesopotamia in the 230s, crushed easily, instructive out of proportion because it taught us that a century of garrisons produces a population that obeys without belonging. Two contested successions, 300 and 475, resolved among great men without provincial war, the second costing two years at the worst moment. Standing structural resentment in the Spice Islands. New, unmeasured resentment on the southern islands taken by force. That is a suspiciously clean record for five thousand kilometres over seven and a half centuries — either extraordinary fortune, or a history written by the winners of every quarrel.

**Mistakes.** Selling China a Latin interpreter and then congratulating ourselves. Trading Egypt our eastern charts *including the location of the southern continent* for one sentence about copper wires — the single trade that created the race we have spent 6,250 million paṇas running. Monopolizing the Spice Islands, converting willing partners into a resentful captive market and giving China a cause. Enforcing the Strait toll by force against a power with better ships. Fighting both naval battles by closing with a longer-ranged enemy, the second time knowing that was the problem. Treating research as discretionary in bad reigns, twice. Letting the Tigris canals decay.

**The elephants.** Six thousand beasts, capped by the supply of animals that can be caught and tamed. Their entire military value rests on one fact: horses untrained to them panic. That fact broke the Bactrian Greeks, the Yuezhi, the Parthians, and panicked Chinese cavalry three centuries ago. **Elephants also panic under gunfire, every rival now has gunpowder, and we have just given Rome the plans for the infantry firearm.** The corps is the oldest pride in the realm, the mahouts are hereditary families, and nobody at court will say this aloud. It should be said.

## 8. War: who, whether, could we win

**Do we want to attack anyone?** Institutionally yes, and the target is Egypt. The *Arthashastra* names it an enemy, we have meant to move against it whenever we could, and the grievance is a hundred and fifty years old and personal. We cannot reach it — every route is a sea route and our navy has never beaten a peer. So: we want to attack Egypt and will instead go on spending money to get around it. We do not want China; we learned that in blood. We do not want Rome; the road is worth more than the ground beyond it. What we will actually attack, without calling it war, is the peoples of the southern continent and its islands — already happening, recorded as founding.

**Will others attack us?** China can take Ternate and Banda whenever it pleases — twelve hundred men on one, seven hundred on the other, powder for forty to sixty shots, no resupply through waters its fleet watches — and has declined, having what it wanted. Egypt may contest the southern continent; if its fleet reaches that arid northwest coast it finds fifty-man posts at two-hundred-and-fifty-kilometre intervals, enough to plant a claim and not to defend one. Rome is the sleeper: a shared river border, a treaty renewing by default, a new faith, a demonstrated willingness to keep warm hands with Egypt behind our back, and now our maker's account of the infantry firearm.

**One-on-one?** On our own ground, almost certainly — a million-man standing army, interior lines, seven centuries of forward granaries, the finest steel, the deepest treasury. Nobody can supply an invasion of the Gangetic plain. But can we *defeat* any of them? No. Not one. We cannot reach Egypt at all; we cannot cross the Syr Darya into a continent-sized power with better guns; we could cross the Euphrates and would be doing precisely what our doctrine forbids. **We are undefeatable at home and incapable of offense abroad, and we have not admitted that to ourselves.**

**Two together?** If two could reach us at once we would lose the sea, the islands, and the southern continent, and hold the subcontinent. The heartland survives almost anything. The empire as a great power would not.

## 9. Spies, secrets, and what we are bad at

We have had a permanent service since the founding: the *Arthashastra*'s overlapping informer networks watching governors, provinces, and each other, inside the 240-million administration line. Internally it is good — it is how one throne governs ninety-eight million people — though gutted in the purge reigns and only partly restored.

Externally the record is poor. Nearly two decades against the Han arsenals produced exactly one hand-cannon, smuggled out by a debt-ridden armorer paid fifteen kilograms of silver, while many agents were taken, presumably killed, and many turned in by the very people they tried to bribe. We took four hundred years to learn Egypt's true extent, learned of Rome's colony by accident, and learned of Egypt's fleet only because the provisioning was too large to hide.

**Counter-intelligence: we have no record of ever catching a foreign agent.** Egypt runs watchers in Rome to guard a coffee plant; it would be remarkable if it ran none here. Either our counter-intelligence is superb and was never once written down, or we are blind. I assume the second. Our real weakness is not collection but *valuation*: we have been out-traded in information in almost every exchange.

**Army and navy, tested.** The army is the largest and most experienced land force in the world, undefeated in open battle for seven hundred and eighty-five years against Greeks, Yuezhi, Parthians, and rebels. Its weakness is irregular war: it bled for a century in Iran and Mesopotamia against men who would not stand, and the cure was economic, not military. The navy is a logistics service with a combat record of one enemy ship destroyed against a hundred and one lost.

## 10. Is the founding divergence realistic?

The divergence itself — Ashoka reading Kalinga as the price of rule rather than a summons to renounce it — is entirely plausible. It turns on one man's temperament in one year; he was trained as a provincial governor and understood both armies and account books, and conquerors have gone either way.

What strains belief is the *persistence*: seven hundred and eighty-five years of one continuous empire across five thousand kilometres, through several changes of ruling house, half a dozen destructive reigns, two contested successions, plague, famine, flood, and a lost naval war, without one successful secession or one period of fragmentation. Meanwhile the Yuezhi were pre-emptively shattered, no other steppe confederation ever formed on our northern border, Parthia was digested whole, and the Arabian peninsula sat empty of any power worth naming for eight centuries. Any one of those is fortunate. All together is a chronicle written by an institution that has never lost.

## 11. Where this document is most likely wrong, or lying

Ranked by what it costs us to be wrong.

1. **The missing 525 accounts.** Every quarter-century for seven hundred and eighty-five years has a numbers block. This one does not, and the southern program's arithmetic fails to close by roughly 178 million paṇas a year. Assume the reserve is gone, infrastructure and relief quietly cut, and someone discovering it at the worst moment. Our own record shows this realm entering fictions into its registers whenever the truth is unwelcome.

2. **The Egyptian fleet.** The entire reign rests on an inference drawn from provisioning rumours by agents who could not say where it was bound. It has not sailed. It may never have been for the south — it could have been a war elsewhere, a western ocean, consolidation in Africa, or theatre fed to agents Egypt had identified. We may have spent three years' revenue racing a ghost, and the court's ability to consider this is impaired because the alternative is unbearable.

3. **"The mainland was empty coast."** The record names the northern, eastern, and cold southern islands as populated and says they were taken by force. About the mainland it says nothing — fifty settlements and three hundred stations "raised from nothing," "on ground no Maurya hand had worked before." Silence is not emptiness. Either there are peoples there whose displacement our ledgers omit, or we have not met them yet and will. And we will bring the pox to them, holding inoculation and no germ theory.

4. **The eastern-coast placements.** The 415 survey charted the *northwest* shore. Forty-odd major anchors were then placed "along the eastern coast and the northern belt the survey had called fertile." The survey never saw the eastern coast. Those placements are extrapolation dressed as chart, and some fraction sit on ground that will not carry what we built on it.

5. **"Steam closed much of the gap with China at sea."** No. We have an engine; China has an engine, longer guns, a rate of fire we could not account for, sails that would not burn, and a fleet that destroyed a hundred and ten ships without a scratch. Our first engines went to the southern convoy by standing directive, not to the navy. Anyone who plans on our fleet contesting Chinese warships will lose another fleet.

6. **The colonial ledgers.** We have documented proof that our capital counted Spice Island garrisons at establishment strength for fifty years while disease emptied them. We have now planted three hundred and fifty places in a tropical belt five thousand miles away, with founders who defied orders and were ratified after the fact. Treat every colonial number before the court as an establishment figure, not a count — and treat 6,250 million paṇas spent at that distance without audit as the largest peculation opportunity in our history.

7. **Three settled conclusions.** *"Egypt's plant guidance was sabotage"* — almost certainly false, and expensive, because it will make us refuse the next thread. *"China's intentions are commercial"* — unexamined for a century, contradicted by China's own demand that we disarm a land frontier. *"Rome is our steadiest tie"* — Rome is our most disciplined negotiator, which is not the same thing.

8. **The firearm's scale.** "In the hands of the realm's soldiers" is not "the army is armed." Two hundred and seventy-five years of testimony says powder production is the wall. Assume thousands of pieces, not hundreds of thousands.

9. **"One in ten can read"** and **"ninety-eight million registered."** The first is a constant copied forward for seven and a half centuries; the second is flat for a century, counted in provinces whose informer networks were gutted.

10. **"The southern road is a route China has no reason to watch."** China's ships already work south of the Strait and reach the Spice Islands from the east; secrecy was formally downgraded for speed. Assume China and Egypt both know.

11. **"The largest, wealthiest, most populous dominion in the world."** Our estimates of the others are our own, and twice we have found a rival several times what we thought. No reason to think that pattern has finished.

## 12. What we should do that is illogical, but that we will do

Because you asked for the true institution and not the clever one, this court will:

- **Keep funding the south past the point the ledger justifies,** because stopping means confessing the reign was pride. If the Egyptian fleet never appears we will call that vindication of the deterrent rather than proof of waste.
- **Refuse Egypt's next gift, or take it and assume poison,** because we have written the sabotage finding down.
- **Keep the elephant corps at six thousand and doctrinally central.** No minister will propose otherwise in open council.
- **Keep the cannon on the Syr Darya** and the enormous garrison facing a river where nothing happens, because that refusal is the only thing we won in the eastern war.
- **Not abandon Ternate and Banda,** though they are indefensible and we know it, because leaving would make the defeat total.
- **Go looking for the coffee plant again.** It is not strategic. It never was. We cannot let it go.
- **Not audit the colonial ledgers,** because auditing means finding out.
- **Look for one more chokepoint to hold,** because after Egypt this court cannot conceive of power in any other shape.

*Submitted for the council. The chronicle may stand as written. This should not leave the room.*
