# ROME — SELF-ASSESSMENT, 525 AD
### Prepared for the Consilium and the Industrial Council. Not for the gazettes.

---

## I. The Ground We Hold

We hold nearly every habitable acre of Europe west of the Dnieper. Ireland's western cliffs to the Dnieper forts; the north of Scotland and the workable coasts of Norway and Sweden down to the old African provinces. Britain, Ireland, Gaul, Iberia, Italy, the Alps, the Danube lands, Dacia, Poland, the Baltic lands, western Belarus, western Ukraine and its black earth, Greece, Macedon, Denmark, southern Sweden, the Norwegian strip. Roughly two and a quarter million square miles — six million square kilometres by the surveyors' new reckoning — of which the overwhelming share is arable, forested, or mineral-bearing rather than desert. That clause matters more than the total. Our predecessors held a comparable acreage that was half sea and half sand. We hold a solid contiguous block of the best-watered farmland on the continent, and rail crosses it.

Added this generation: Hispaniola and Cuba, taken outright; a garrisoned foothold on the Yucatan shore; and charted but unheld, the Lesser Antilles, Florida, the northern seaboard, the Faroes and Iceland.

Our borders touch three powers and one wall. The Dnieper, where Han China stands and Han civilian towns thicken yearly on the far bank. The Euphrates, where the Maurya stand. The Mediterranean and the Sinai, where Egypt has stood since before we were an empire. Everywhere else, nothing worth taking: tundra north, Sahara south, ocean west. We finished Europe two hundred and twenty-five years ago and have had nowhere to point the appetite since.

**Are we nice?** No, and the question is badly framed. We are not cruel by temperament and not merciful by principle. We are *assimilative*, which is different and more dangerous. We conquered the Germanic, Celtic, Baltic and Dacian peoples, settled discharged soldiers on their land in layers, taught their grandchildren Latin because the army and the market required it, and let their languages die of economic irrelevance rather than by edict. Ten generations on, the descendants of the men who ambushed our columns in the German forests are Roman farmers who would fight to stay that way. That is why we number one hundred and fifteen million where our historical counterpart's Europe held thirty-five. Not kindness. Absorption, continuity, and the absence of collapse.

We put down the British coal strikes with troops and executions and changed nothing afterward. We are, this generation, breaking island chiefdoms with steel against stone and setting the survivors to the gold creeks — while patronizing a faith that argues human dignity. That will cost us, and I return to it.

---

## II. How We Expanded, and How Fast

Slowly, systematically, and without a tributary if we could avoid it. The engine was veteran settlement: forts forward into new ground, veteran farming colonies a step behind on ground already secured, established towns behind those. Each layer fed the one in front and defended land it owned outright. Roughly a province a generation for three centuries — not fast by the standard of a horde, unstoppable by the standard of anything that had to be *held*.

We annexed rather than clientized because the engine required owning the ground: a client king gives tribute and no land to settle citizens on, and tribal Europe had few monarchies worth co-opting. Where we genuinely could not conquer we used intermediaries and hated it — Berber traders for Saharan gold, Egypt as the unavoidable middleman for everything east and south. We have never made a peer a client. Clientage is imposed on the weak.

Expansion stopped around 300 AD because Europe ran out, and that date is the central fiscal and political fact of our history. Plunder vanished permanently from the revenue table, the slave supply collapsed and forced us to mechanize, and the veterans owed land began to be owed land there was none of. We have spent two centuries managing the consequences with usurpations, arms races, and now an ocean.

---

## III. The People

One hundred and ten to one hundred and twenty million. Literacy near seventy percent empire-wide — eighty-five to ninety in the Rhine cities, Britain and Rome; thirty-five to fifty in the frontier countryside. Not one denarius of it was spent by the state on schools. It was pulled into being by demand: printed primers made town schools cheap, the army taught every recruit to read a roster and discharged him onto a colony, and no factory or railway hires a foreman who cannot read a schedule.

Language: Latin, and Latin did not fracture. Continuous administration, print, literacy, rail and the churn of soldiers and officials held it as one tongue from Ireland to the Dnieper. Greek circulates among the educated. The tongues we displaced died faster than they had any right to.

Religion: the civic cult still stands, and over it since about 445 the dynasty's Christian patronage, the Patriarch seated in Rome because we appoint him and Alexandria does not. Identical printed scripture from near the time of composition kept the faith textually unified where a manuscript faith would have splintered. It is congregational, charitable by doctrine, and it argues that a man's worth is not what he produces. We adopted it for the legitimacy and the charity. We have not thought carefully about the last clause.

Slaves: residual at home; mass productive slavery ended two centuries ago when the machine out-produced the man. And we are, this turn, re-founding chattel slavery across an ocean on gold, cacao and cotton — the trades steam cannot mechanize because there is no coal there to run it — in a literate empire with newspapers and a dignity theology. Write that sentence down and look at it.

Culture: engineering, law, and the gazette. Gas-lit streets, factory whistles at all hours, coffee-houses, rail yards, balloon platforms, winter smog that dims the midday sun in Colonia Agrippina, and a working class that reads the emperor's decisions within weeks and reads them criticized in a rival sheet the same month.

## IV. The Money

Revenue about four thousand million sesterces. Land tax is still the largest single line at roughly thirty-five percent — this remains, by revenue, a farming empire with factories on top of it. Industry is second at a fifth to a quarter and climbing, then trade and customs, mining, luxury duties, miscellaneous levies. Plunder has been a zero for two hundred and twenty-five years. Against that: military and frontier at sixty-three percent and rising, administration, infrastructure, grain dole, and one hundred twenty million on research that is honestly a military line.

**How much genuinely free money do we have?** Two hundred and eighty million a year on paper — seven percent. In truth, near zero, and that is the most important number here. Reserves are a few months' revenue at most and can be erased by one serious war. We have no bond market and cannot conceive of one; the state cannot borrow. When pressed we debase the coin, levy emergencies, confiscate, or take advances from tax-farmers.

And we have just committed an oceanic colonial program to that seven percent: hundreds of thousands of colonists moved four thousand miles, garrisons on two islands and a hostile mainland shore, prospecting parties up unmapped rivers, escorts, and the tax those emigrants no longer pay at home — all while the China arms race climbs, the gunpowder program approaches its expensive weaponization phase, and the iron-hull and screw programs run.

The honest projection is that the free margin goes negative within fifteen years and we reach for the mint. Debasement in a peasant economy is a slow theft nobody can name. Debasement in a monetized wage economy at seventy percent literacy with competing newspapers is a price rise every Rhine factory worker can read about, argue about, and organize against by pamphlet within a month. We have never done this to *this* population before.

One trajectory the court misreads: revenue has risen sevenfold since 1 AD but per head only from eighteen sesterces to thirty-six — *doubled in five hundred years*. We are not much richer per person than the Republic was; we are vastly larger and more mechanized, and almost every marginal sesterce of the industrial dividend went into the frontier. "Revenue-rich and cash-poor" is not a bad century. It is the permanent shape of this state.

## V. What We Can Build

Compound steam engines of one hundred fifty to four hundred horsepower, found by tinkering, with no theory of heat behind them. Locomotives at fifteen to forty miles an hour on hand-laid iron, speed-limited because we have no signalling. Twenty to thirty steam warships, four to six knots, six to seven hundred miles on a coal load, going from iron-reinforced wood to iron plate. Several hundred ocean sailing hulls and hundreds of river steamers treated as plumbing. Rolling mills, blast-furnace steel, coal gas lighting, observation balloons, pneumatic tubes, telescopes, longitude at sea, early petroleum, mechanized farming, distilled-spirit antisepsis, lightning rods, standardized critical parts.

Absent: gunpowder. Electricity. Screw propulsion in service. Chemistry. Any answer whatsoever to an infantryman who can kill our shield wall before it closes.

**Researchers.** Roughly four hundred thousand in the technical workforce, 0.37 percent of the population, of whom perhaps forty thousand design rather than maintain. Almost none are *researchers* in any organized sense. They are literate mechanics fixing real machines and printing their fixes, and that distributed swarm is our greatest asset — it took the engine from a leaking mine pump to four hundred horsepower in three hundred fifty years without a single theorem. Directed programs are small and military: gunpowder, the screw and iron hull, naval boiler metallurgy, frontier survey, and a thin patronage line for natural philosophy.

**What blocks us.** Not money, not men, not skill. *Theory.* Every wall we have hit in two hundred years is where empiricism stops. Gunpowder is chemistry and we have none — we spent a hundred and eighty years assuming the Han weapon must be a pressure vessel because pressure is what *we* had mastered. Electricity is invisible and cannot be stumbled into. Better engines wait on metallurgy, and metallurgy waits on knowing what steel *is*. Rubber we cannot source or explain. Medicine has four centuries of superb printed observation and no idea what causes anything.

The one crack in that wall opened this generation and the file records it as a footnote. The observatories confirmed the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter; the men who look through the glass hold heliocentrism whatever the schoolmen say; and the printed question "if Ptolemy was wrong about the sky, why believe him about the sea" is what licensed the African survey that reordered our map of the world. That is the first time in our history that an overturned *theory* produced a strategic result. If one thing deserves funding out of all proportion to its apparent worth, it is the men with the telescopes — they are the only Romans currently practising the habit that produces chemistry. We will not fund them properly. Natural philosophy has no constituency, no general, and no seat on the Industrial Council.

## VI. The Peers

**Egypt.** Six hundred and twenty years of wanting. Everything that made Egypt worth a war in 100 BC is still there and larger: the grain, the canal, the Bab-el-Mandeb toll on every eastern luxury a Roman ever bought, the wealth, the opacity. This generation our three ships came home from the African Atlantic and told us Egypt is not a river kingdom with a fleet but a two-ocean continental power: three thousand miles of planned stone cities in the Egyptian manner, coastal semaphore, foundries on the equatorial waterfront, subject populations beyond counting, and at the Cape — where Africa *ends*, and open sea runs east — the greatest city and largest fleet on the coast. One ship shadowed our survey its entire length in relay and let it go home. Two hundred-plus of their largest hulls passed us northbound, loaded, none returning.

That killed two of our oldest beliefs at once. The Indian Ocean is not enclosed — Ptolemy was wrong for four centuries and we held the counter-evidence for one of them, because the Han told us their ships had reached Indian waters and nobody in this building asked how they got in. And Gibraltar, reinforced last generation to bottle Egypt out of the Atlantic, closes a door Egypt walked around before we conceived of it.

We now rate Egypt probably wealthier than us, decisively greater at sea, holding communications and propulsion we cannot copy, unattackable in its own waters. Allies, no. Enemies, not declared — they have never attacked us. Liars, constitutionally, but by omission and misdirection: our own doctrine of managed disclosure, practised better than we practise it. Dangerous supremely, annoying unbearably.

**Han China.** The dominant military power in the world and the only one that could break a Roman army in the field. Individual firearms as standard infantry. Ninety Maurya warships sunk for no loss. Rail to the Syr Darya. Civilian towns hardening on the far Dnieper bank in a settlement pattern that is our own veteran-colony method in different clothes. Our file calls this our "most transparent and commercially productive" relationship — and it is also the one in which we handed over a complete military survey of our roads, cities, forts and supply lines in 300 AD for a scaleless dirt sketch, which they have now held two hundred and twenty-five years. They asked four times where we had sailed west, because they had measured the sphere, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned the Americas into existence before we blundered into them. Transparent is the wrong word for a power that good at asking questions. We mistook their courtesy for candour for two centuries.

**The Maurya.** Commercially valuable, strategically unreliable, and our own file says so: banditry they denied while it happened, cannon on the Syr Darya never disclosed while they pressed us four times about Chinese firearms, a unilateral doubling of the transit tariff. And we have just offered them the complete working method of a marine steam engine — boiler through transmission, seals, failure points, fuel figures, Roman engineers in their yards — against a *written account of a third party's weapon*. I return to this.

**West Africa.** Five ships lost across fifty years, two of them steam warships, one of which never closed the coast. We have now reassessed the killer as Egypt or Egyptian subjects, and concluded Egypt fed us a fiction of reefs, currents and canoe-peoples for a century.

**The Caribbean and the Maya.** Islanders with no metal at all, now ours. A walled stone city on the Yucatan with writing and a merchant class, which we could butcher at will and have not, only because we do not know how large the interior behind it is.

**How far ahead or behind are we?** Ahead of no one that matters and behind two. Militarily we are the finest pre-gunpowder army ever assembled, which is exactly the problem. At sea, a distant second to Egypt. Industrially we may lead — we do not truly know Egypt's or China's factory base — but industrial lead does not stop a bullet. Every year we out-build them in tonnage and lose ground in kind.

## VII. Philosophy, Fears, and Governing Style

**Our philosophy** is law, permanence, and *know before you strike*. The Delta and Carrhae are six hundred years old and still doctrine: intelligence precedes commitment, diplomacy precedes force, force is reserved for what we understand well enough to win. We build institutions to outlive the men in them, write everything down and print it, and treat an empire as a machine with a maintenance schedule. We do not believe in destiny. We believe in engineering.

**Governing style:** cunning rather than backstabbing, legalistic rather than forgiving, patient to a fault, survival-minded above all. We co-opt before we crush — the Industrial Council bought the new money into the structure before it could find a general to fund; Britain got fiscal autonomy after its usurpation, not a purge; labour got shift limits framed as productivity protection rather than as rights. We lie easily: classified programs framed as mining research, managed disclosure as formal doctrine, an aid fleet to Alexandria in 365 that was thirty spies with grain. We are comfortable with all of it, and less comfortable admitting how often we lie to ourselves.

**How logical are we, actually?** More than most states and much less than we advertise. Our failures are all one failure: we reason from what we ourselves have mastered. Steam, so the Han weapon must be pressure — one hundred eighty wasted years. Optics, so Egypt's fast messages must be mirrors. Mechanics, so when India said the copper carried a natural force we said no, it is compressed air in tubes, and built the tubes. Two centuries of confidently naming the wrong category, every time by projecting our own competence onto someone else's problem.

**Genuine worries:** the firearms gap; the free margin going negative; the Dnieper; Britain; the labour settlement of the 350s, built to last two generations and now two generations old; and the possibility that Egypt and the Han have an understanding.

**Irrational fears, stated honestly:**

- *That Egypt is unconquerable in the nature of things.* It was unconquerable in 100 BC because of the Sinai, the Delta marsh, summer fever, and a fleet we could not match. We have since acquired rail, iron hulls, steam transport and reliable navigation, and never re-examined the conclusion — because re-examining it means admitting our ancestors' defeat was contingent. It is now a superstition wearing a strategic argument.
- *That any peer war brings all three down on us.* Never tested, never probed. The same arithmetic protects us: none can move on Rome without baring itself to the other two. We treat the three-front problem only as a cage and almost never as a shield.
- *The southern sea as cursed ground.* Five ships in fifty years is a real loss and a poor sample. We reclassified an entire ocean as denied territory on it — then sent three ships with orders not to close the shore and surveyed three thousand miles of it without a shot fired.
- *That China is a monster.* We have never fought them. Every assessment rests on one frightened soldier's shot in 290, one sighting of dead raiders, and two secondhand accounts of a battle in seas no Roman has ever seen, given us by the victor and the loser.
- *Britain.* Real risk, wildly disproportionate dread. It has revolted once in five hundred years.

**Why have we not collapsed?** Because what killed our historical counterpart was *speed* — armies and orders at marching pace, so the centre could not answer a distant crisis before it became a breakaway empire. Rail solved it: an emperor who moves legions in days suppresses a usurpation before it consolidates, and the railroad is simultaneously our economy and our best anti-civil-war weapon. Add print for consistent law, veteran colonies embedding loyal populations, no barbarian migration because the barbarians are our farmers, and after 450 a single unifying faith. Not seamless — the succession crisis of 318–325, the British usurpation, the Rhine succession war, labour risings put down with troops. The correct model is a race between integrating technology and fragmenting pressure, not a victory.

## VIII. War

**Do we want to attack anyone?** Institutionally, always. We are a fully militarized state that has run out of wars it can win, which is not a peaceful one. We want Egypt and have wanted it for six centuries. We would take the Maya interior tomorrow if we knew its size, and we will take it.

**Will others attack us?** Not soon, by the same arithmetic that restrains us. The Han have every advantage and no evident appetite for Europe — steppe favours horses, our farmland lies beyond a river they agreed to, and silk pays better than war. Egypt has never in six hundred years attempted to take Roman ground and has no instrument for it; its power is naval, commercial and southern. The Maurya could not.

**Can we beat any single rival one-on-one?** The Maurya, probably, on land, at ruinous cost and to no purpose — and less certainly once they hold the steam navy we have just offered them. Egypt: not at sea, not in the Delta, not anywhere near its own water. China: no. Their infantry defeats the premise of ours.

**Could we survive two together?** Yes, and the court underrates this. Egypt cannot invade Europe; it can strangle our trade, take our islands and burn our coasts. China can hurt the Dnieper and would find a hundred and fifteen million people, rail, and the deepest garrison network in the world behind it. Two together would cost us the ocean, the American venture, the eastern trade, and probably a province. It would not end Rome. Rome ends from inside, as it always has.

**What we have actually fought:** everything in Europe, and won. Egypt around 100 BC and lost — won the battles, lost the campaign to desert, marsh, fever and a fleet. Carrhae, and lost. Britain 355–362, four to five years to suppress one island. The Rhine succession war. Two labour risings. Five ships off Africa, for nothing. Island chiefdoms this generation, for gold. Our army has never once met a firearm.

**The navy** is our weak limb and our growth area — but note the one thing it can do that no rival has been *seen* to do: move a colonial migration across an ocean.

## IX. Intelligence

We have had something like a permanent service for two centuries: the Alexandria network, the Dnieper watch, merchant reporting on the eastern routes. Our best operation ever was the 365 aid fleet — thirty observers into a wrecked Alexandria for three weeks, worth more than twenty years of espionage. This generation we kept the network standing after an agent went silent, quarantining that branch rather than walking a replacement into it. Doctrine is shifting from espionage toward *presence*: Roman engineers openly in Indian yards are better long-term eyes than any bribed clerk.

**Counter-intelligence: we do not have one.** There is not a line in our record about it. And a fact sits in this turn's file that should have set the building on fire: *Egypt learned of the Americas within months.* From where? Our own ports, dockyards, crews, gazettes. We assume we are the ones doing the watching, in a seventy-percent-literate empire with competing newspapers, share markets, and an open Egyptian legation in our capital. We are the most penetrable state in the world and have never once written the word down.

## X. Where This Document Is Wrong, or Lying

**1. The fevers.** The file states the crossing's fevers "are not, in fact, severe" and that our physicians "determined" the second voyage's sickness to be common flu, coincidental to the voyage. This is the most dangerous sentence we possess. Our physicians have no theory of disease; they cannot distinguish a coincidence from a cause because they have no concept of a cause. This is a bureaucracy's reassurance dressed as a finding, produced exactly when the treasury needed the migration to look cheap. Wet tropical country has eaten armies before — it ate ours in the Delta, in this same file. Plan on losing a large fraction of every colonist cohort and price the venture accordingly.

**2. And its mirror, which nobody has thought of at all.** We intend to work the islands with subjugated islanders beneath our veterans. Those people have never met anything we carry. If our diseases do to them what their air may do to us, the island economy loses its hands within a generation and we hold gold creeks with no labour, no coal, and a standing need to buy bodies — from Africa, whose Atlantic coast we have just concluded is Egyptian and which has killed five of our ships. That is not a colony. It is a leash, and Egypt holds the other end. This is the most probable concrete failure of the whole program, and the file does not contain the thought.

**3. The Egyptian reassessment is real evidence over-read.** Three captains, two miles off, through glass, along one coast. What they *saw* is reliable: cities, harbours, semaphore, foundries, a fleet, a shadow relay. What we *concluded* — that all of it is Egypt — rests on architecture. Our own cities contain Egyptian shrines and we are not Egyptian; Hellenistic kings built in the Greek manner without being Greece. A religious and cultural sphere, a confederation of client ports, a daughter civilization, or an independent Atlantic-African maritime power that trades with Egypt and shares its gods would each look exactly like what we saw. A relay ship is what a coast watch does; it is not proof of one command. If the coast is *not* Egypt, two things follow: Egypt told us the truth in 420 about the maritime peoples who killed our ships, and there exists an Atlantic power with reason to resent Egypt that we could talk to. We may have just converted a possible ally into an enemy on the strength of a temple façade.

**4. The northbound column and the shipbuilding surge may be feed.** Our file holds the reading open, and credit to it. But consider who benefits if Rome believes an Egyptian armada is racing it west: Rome moves its fleet, its colonists, its money and its attention four thousand miles from the Mediterranean and stakes its free margin on the outcome. That is the most valuable thing Egypt could buy from us, and the price would be a few dozen ships sailing north in daylight past three Roman hulls it had shadowed three thousand miles and deliberately let go home. An agent in our Alexandria network has gone silent. We quarantined that branch and kept trusting the rest. Assume the network is compromised until proven otherwise, and treat everything it has told us this generation — the tenfold surge above all — as possibly authored.

**5. The Maurya steam trade may be the worst bargain in our history, and the file presents it as shrewdness.** We give a working, proven, decisive industrial-military technology, complete, in one delivery, with our engineers building the first one in their yards. We receive a *written account* of a weapon its authors do not possess, from a partner our own file classifies as practising systematic impression management, describing a capability they concealed from us for a generation while asking us about it four times. They will pad it, and our only stated check is that "a padded account will show its seams" — no check at all across a gap where one message crosses per generation. Meanwhile we hand a neighbour on our Euphrates frontier, one holding undisclosed cannon and desperate to replace the navy China sank, the means to build it. The justification — that a steam Maurya pressures Egypt to our benefit — is the reasoning by which every state that ever armed the enemy on its own border justified it.

**6. "We cannot reach Egypt" has never been re-audited since 100 BC.** The Delta beat wooden ships, marching columns and pack trains. We now hold iron-plated steam hulls, rail toward the Levantine approaches, balloons that see over a treeline, mobile steam launchers, longitude, and gunpowder six years from a stable ratio. I do not propose the war. I propose the *reasoning* be redone from the present rather than inherited, because at present it is not analysis. It is scar tissue.

**7. The tables are our own tax rolls, and tax rolls lie in both directions.** Land is assessed by men whose careers depend on the assessment; heads are counted by men whose neighbours would rather not be counted; literacy is inferred from who buys printed sheets. Per-head revenue *doubled in five hundred years* while our capability multiplied beyond recognition — that is either an astonishing failure to tax the industrial economy, or the population is overstated, or the revenue is understated. Somebody in the fisc knows which and has no incentive to say.

**8. The file's forward claims are its least trustworthy part.** "Coal not far inland up the northern rivers" is a prospector's hope. "Full iron hulls in twenty-five to thirty years," "gunpowder in six," "the Maya interior taken on next generation's reconnaissance" — these are budget requests written in the past tense. Our record on such forecasts is poor: fifteen years to a steam warship took twenty-five; a hundred and eighty years of gunpowder research produced nothing; the Maurya route that was to break Egypt's grip captured only the luxury margin and was revised down within a decade.

**9. What the file omits entirely is where the next crisis is.** The 350s labour settlement was built to last two generations, and has. There is now a seventy-percent-literate industrial working class organizing by pamphlet, a state faith that argues human dignity, an Industrial Council whose proper power is publicly argued in print, and an imperial venture about to require debasement. Not one line of this turn's record addresses it. We are looking west across an ocean while the crisis assembles in the Rhine valley.

**10. And the structural one.** We have built an oceanic economy whose ports are Britain and Iberia — and rail, the technology that solved the problem that killed our predecessor, *cannot cross the Channel*. Every ship, every colonist, every ingot of American gold makes Britain richer, more maritime and more independently capable, on the far side of the one gap our unifying technology cannot bridge, in the province that has already declared an emperor once. We spent five hundred years engineering against fragmentation and this generation, without noticing, we financed it.

**Is the founding divergence realistic?** The divergence itself is fine — a forty-year-old king not dying is the cheapest counterfactual available, and Egypt's survival needed no genius, only the absence of a regency vacuum. The chain afterward strains hardest at one link: a leather-packed piston driving water out of a shaft in 150 AD asks more of iron-founding and boring than that century's metallurgy should give, and everything we are rests on it. Paper from papyrus scarcity is sound; print eighty years after paper is fast but defensible on a twenty-three-letter alphabet and a screw press already crushing olives. Never suffering a collapse for seven hundred years is the real luxury — and the one thing in this file no institution should ever assume continues.

---

*Filed. The gazettes get the islands, the gold, and the maize.*
