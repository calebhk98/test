# The Han Empire at 525 AD — A Private Audit for the Throne

*For the inner council. Not for the censors, not for the archives, not for any foreign eye.*

---

## I. What We Actually Hold

On paper: an arc of ten to thirteen million square kilometers, eight thousand wide, run from one capital — Korea's northern commanderies and tributary south, the Japanese waters, the Yellow and Yangtze valleys, Vietnam, the Shan Plateau, Manchuria, Mongolia, the Kazakh steppe, the Siberian river posts, the Minusinsk pocket, the Pontic grassland to the Dnieper. That is the claim. The possession is smaller, and the difference is the most important thing on this page.

We genuinely *hold* — tax, register, conscript, defend — the two river valleys, Korea's north, Vietnam's delta, Hexi, the settled Mongolian belt, and the rail-served Kazakh corridor with its three planned cities. Perhaps three million square kilometers of real state. We *occupy* — garrison chains, supply lines, no rival present — the rest of the steppe and the Pontic line, now rail-served to the Dnieper: another two to three million. We merely *claim* Siberia and the whole northern forest and interior, which our appendix calls "held without contest, since no rival power can reach across that space to dispute it," an "ownership no one else can challenge, whatever the sparseness of the flags on the ground."

That is the most self-flattering sentence in our records. It defines possession as the *absence of a competitor* rather than the presence of a state; by that standard we owned Siberia before we ever went there. We have a scatter of fur stations and one farmable basin at Minusinsk across territory the size of the rest of the empire, and we do not know its coasts, its peoples, or its interior. The Northern Road we funded this period is, whether the court admits it or not, an admission that we do not hold the north — if we held it we would not need twelve purpose-built hulls to go find out what is there.

**How we expanded, and how fast.** Twenty to twenty-five kilometers a season, for four hundred years, by outpost chain: fort, supply line, colony, next fort. Attrition against grassland rather than conquest. Its genius is that it never needed a decisive battle; its curse is that it never produced one, so we have almost no institutional experience of a real war against a real state.

**Tributaries.** Yes, extensively, and preferred wherever conquest is not cheap: southern Korea, Japan, the Shan highlanders, the Wusun before absorption, the submitted Xiongnu. It lets the throne be the center of the world at almost no cost. It is also why we built no navy until the Maurya forced one on us at the age of five hundred and seventy-five.

**Borders that matter.** Two. The Syr Darya, where the Maurya stand, and the Dnieper, where Rome stands. Everything else is sea, mountain, jungle, or empty. Two live borders in eight thousand kilometers is why we have spent six centuries at war footing without ever fighting a peer war on land.

**Are we nice?** No. We are *orderly*, and the court has spent six hundred years mistaking the two. Our own file records that the peoples of the western steppe, displaced by our advance in a chain running two centuries, were "ground down between the two advancing worlds" until by 500 they were "a dwindling and landless scatter rather than a people with a territory" — a five-century dissolution of whole nations, written in the passive voice, in our own hand, without comment. No one reading our records honestly would call this a gentle empire.

---

## II. The Population Number Is a Tax Roll, Not a Census

Seventy million at 500, which our documents call a demographic ceiling. Two objections.

First, it is a *registered* population. We fought land concentration for a hundred and sixty years and only closed the dependent-registration workaround this period — great families registering land, and the people on it, under dependents and clients so the state could not see them. If that worked at scale for land it worked at scale for households. The true number is very likely eighty to ninety million, and the missing millions are the tenants of the great estates. Our extraction rate is therefore below eleven percent, our land tax more eroded than the ledgers show, and the great families richer than the estate rolls admit.

Second, the ceiling. Our own appendix concedes the exception — the Pontic black earth, "a breadbasket the empire has claimed and not yet eaten" — and concedes that what stopped us was ox-work and distance, both of which the rail has removed. Add Manchuria, which our documents barely discuss as farmland, and six centuries of iron tools. The ceiling is a plateau we chose by shipping our surplus poor to dry steppe instead of wet soil. That is a policy, not a limit.

**Literacy, language, learning.** Five to seven million literate; urban and administrative literacy eight to fifteen percent. Latin held by a dedicated corps since the fourth century, our best intelligence investment ever, because it removed the Maurya from the middle of our Rome correspondence. Sogdians interpret for us westward. Chinese script, printing since the third century, movable type since the fourth, and an examination that now weights mathematics and natural philosophy over classical mastery.

Our appendix predicts literacy climbing toward "four in five and beyond." That is the boldest unsupported claim in the record. No agrarian society reaches four-fifths literacy without compulsory schooling and an economy that pays for reading in ordinary work; our script takes years, and a tenant's son who studies is a farmhand lost. Twenty-five to forty percent is the honest projection, and the forecast of "several times the research tier within a few generations" should be discounted accordingly.

**Religion.** Confucian officialdom, Taoist alchemy (which built our weapons program and is therefore untouchable), state ritual, ancestor practice. Christianity is small and foreign, in the western trade towns; Buddhism does not exist, there being nothing in India to send it. Consequence: no organized religion here can mobilize millions, a real stability asset.

**Slavery.** Small, domestic, convict and debt-bond — not an economic institution and not the source of our cheap labor. Our cheap labor is free peasants with nothing, a distinction that matters enormously for the mill question below.

## III. Money: Vast, and None of It Free

Revenue four thousand tonnes of silver-equivalent against an economy near thirty-six thousand; extraction eleven percent, high for any agrarian state and possible only because a third of our income comes from monopolies — salt, iron, saltpeter — that do not care who owns the farmland. That concentration-proof half of the revenue is the actual reason this dynasty has survived seven centuries. Not the gunpowder. The salt.

Composition at 500: land tax 34, monopolies 33, poll tax 13, trade tolls and silk 9, frontier metals and fur 11. Spending: military 62, civil 12, waterworks 10, granaries 4, rail 8, surplus 4.

**Genuinely free money: perhaps one hundred and sixty tonnes a year at 500, and less now.** The Dnieper rail ended early this period and should have freed three hundred tonnes; it freed nothing, going straight into the southern trunk survey, the twelve Northern Road hulls, the Pontic settlement grants, and the navy. The militarist constituency converts surplus into the next program before the surplus can be observed. That is not a failure of the treasury; it is the design of the state. An empire with four thousand tonnes of revenue has, in a good year, one to two hundred tonnes of maneuver.

**We are grain-rich, cash-poor, and structurally incapable of absorbing a shock** — a two-front emergency, a sudden closure of the Silk Road, a rebellion needing three armies paid in cash for two years. We would have to debase or seize from the great families; the second is civil war and the first is the classic death of empires. Our appendix sets inflation aside as a modeling convenience, and that convenience deletes the most common way states like ours die.

**Change over time.** Revenue tripled in six centuries while population grew by a third. The gains came from territory and deeper extraction, not a richer peasantry: the household on the ground is ten to twenty percent better off than under Emperor Wu, and no more. A state that grew rich while its people did not — that is why rebellion is permanent background weather, and it is the fact the court is least willing to state.

## IV. Technology, Researchers, and What Blocks Us

**In hand at 525:** rifled naval cannon to seven or nine hundred meters; field breech-loaders sealed by the paper cartridge; mature hand cannon, shell, mortar, rocket; coal-fired steel of the finest grade made without any theory of why; precision boring and gauges three hundred and fifty years old; copper-clad purpose-built warships with side ports; animal-drawn iron rail from the Yellow River to the Dnieper; movable type, paper, deposit notes; precision lenses, a reflecting telescope, pendulum clocks, balloon observation; a verified spherical earth at forty-five thousand kilometers; a rotary steam engine turning a mill shaft and one steam hull in home trials; a hand-cranked generator, a jar storing a single burst, and a taxonomy of what carries the electrical force and what arrests it. And a written proposition, still a minority view, that physical phenomena follow mathematical rules discoverable by measurement.

**Researchers.** Investigation tier around a thousand at 500, plausibly twelve to fifteen hundred now; technical workforce forty to fifty thousand at 500, plausibly sixty thousand now. Working on: the continuous electrical source; glass that corrects color; marine steam, boiler safety, and the paddle; breech-loading safety; rifling at scale; the mirror telescope; charting and longitude for the Northern Road; and the Egyptian disease knowledge.

**What blocks us.** *Materials* — the achromatic lens waits on a glass we have not made, and we have ordered the alchemists to melt every mineral on their shelves into the batch, which is exactly how we found gunpowder. *Theory* — we have no chemistry; we make the finest steel in the world and cannot say what makes it steel. *A continuous source of the electrical force* — Egypt lights a headland all night; we make a spark. And *demand*, the real blocker, which our file diagnosed at 500 with painful accuracy: labor is cheap because the estates make it cheap, the frontier drains the surplus poor outward instead of concentrating them in cities, the state monopolizes the heavy industry that would be the seedbed, and the coal sits a thousand kilometers from the people.

Here is what the court has not said aloud. **In this last period we began dismantling three of those four.** We licensed private capital to build works. We closed the dependent-registration workaround, pushing hidden tenants back into the visible labor market. And we surveyed a trunk rail from the Mongolian coal country south toward the Yangtze — the exact line our own analysis says has never existed, cheap energy meeting dense labor.

Our records concluded at 500 that "none of the conditions that would start it is yet in place." That is now out of date, and the throne authorized its obsolescence without noticing. This is the most consequential fact in this document, in both directions: the opportunity of the age, and a loaded weapon pointed at the arsenal monopoly on which the entire political order rests.

## V. The Other Powers

**Rome.** Europe from the western ocean to the Dnieper, plus the Baltic and thin holdings toward Finland — five to six million square kilometers, and now an ocean crossing, islands, and a coast beyond. Movable type, iron rail, precision compasses, glass, and no gunpowder, a thing it told us plainly and unprompted this period, adding that it is not a fool and does not need to be told.

Rome has dealt straighter with us than any power in six hundred years: disclosed the crossing before we could discover it, admitted it does not know how far that land runs, asked nothing, reaffirmed the river agreement. It is the only party never caught in a lie.

And here is the discipline the court must hold: *Rome's honesty is cheap.* It tells us true things about places we cannot reach and cannot contest, while keeping the currents and the reckoning to itself — openly, which is honest, and which is also the whole of what matters. An honest partner who withholds the one operative fact behaves exactly as a shrewd one would. Keep trusting Rome, on two centuries of evidence, and stop congratulating ourselves for it.

**The Maurya.** Everything south and west of the Syr Darya: Sogdia, Ferghana, Bactria, Persia, the subcontinent. Six to eight million square kilometers, and quite possibly a larger population than ours — a thing we have never seriously estimated and should. The richest farmland in Central Asia is theirs, on the far bank.

Track record: inflated their Strait authority, which our captains disproved; inflated Egypt's tolls to steer our trade through their infrastructure; claimed mastery of an electrical force they do not have. They lie as standing practice, and we have never once told them we know. They have held cannon on the Syr Darya since the 450s, twenty to forty pieces by 470, with commanders computing trajectories. They lost above ninety warships in 477 and gave way on money while refusing on the cannon — correctly, since the cannon were never part of the quarrel. They are now arming seven to fifteen hulls with unseasoned crews; their powder is cheap in civilian markets while their naval crews are short-rationed in drills. Read cold: they have the formula and not the volume, and manufacturing gaps close.

**Egypt.** Smallest in land, richest per head, master of the Red Sea and the canal, thirty percent in tolls applied without favor. A hundred and fifty years ahead of us in the electrical force, generations ahead in steam at sea. This period it widened the scholar exchange fourfold and opened its work on the smallest living things and the sicknesses they carry, freely and at once, asking only continued access to our optics and mathematics. The best trade offered this court in six hundred years. It may finally open the tropical country that has bounded us at the Shan Plateau for four centuries.

And: Egypt warned us that Rome, feeding on two continents, is transformed and dangerous — at the precise moment such a warning was most useful to Egypt. When we pressed whether it had *seen* Rome's settlement plan or merely heard of it, Egypt conceded without defense and withdrew an earlier discourtesy. That is either genuine honesty or the most sophisticated trust-purchase in the world, and a court with our record should hold both and act on neither.

**How far ahead or behind?** We lead in powder, artillery range, precision metalwork, printing volume, the size of our technical class, and the scale of what we administer. We are behind Egypt in the electrical force and steam at sea, and behind everyone in theory. We are ahead of the Maurya in everything but position. Rome is not comparable.

**Does the gap matter?** Yes, in one way that has nothing to do with battle. Egypt passes messages on copper wire *faster than any messenger* — that is the honest statement of what our people at Aden have established. They have seen the wires, seen the function, and never once seen the construction; what they can attest is that a query sent between quarters of Aden returned answered sooner than a rider could have carried it. Whether it is instantaneous or merely very fast we do not know, and the difference does not change the conclusion. Our defining problem is eight thousand kilometers run from one center. A power that answers across a city faster than a horse could, and lights a headland all night, holds the beginning of the solution to the fundamental constraint of our existence. That is why the continuous source is the most important unsolved problem in the empire — above steam, above glass, above powder.

**And it is a priority we cannot spend money against.** This must be said plainly rather than buried. Our own scholars judged that a continuous source, like the achromatic glass, comes from a lucky accident during unrelated work rather than from deliberate search, and the throne funded both as open-ended, many-hands, all-failures-recorded empiricism *specifically so the money would not be pulled when they failed to land on schedule*. That is an honest admission that the problem cannot be driven, and we should not pretend otherwise by calling the standing search a program.

There is one argument that directed effort now works where it did not before, and it is worth stating because it is the only one we have: what changed this period is that the scholars moved past folklore into taxonomy. They can now say which materials carry the force and which arrest it, and they can produce it reliably by crank and store it in a jar. A search that can be *bounded* — try every known carrier, every arrangement of dissimilar metals, every wetting, and record every failure — is no longer a blind hope. That is precisely how we found gunpowder: not by looking for it, but by systematic combination across a bounded shelf of materials by men funded to keep going. It is a real argument and it is also the argument every court makes to justify money it cannot account for, and we should hold it as the second of those until it produces something.

So, honestly: we fund the search at a level we can sustain indefinitely and never at a level that requires a result — a standing charge, small, protected from the censors, immune to being judged. Meanwhile we spend the real money on what *is* tractable: marine steam, which is engineering and answers to effort; the trunk rail; rifling and breech safety; the disease knowledge from Egypt, which is the one large gain available for certain. And the court does with an undrivable priority the only thing it can — it keeps twenty scholars standing at Aden where the working installations are, because if we cannot buy the discovery we can at least be positioned to recognize it, and it accepts that our most important problem may be solved by a man doing something else.

## VI. Philosophy, Fear, and the Shape of Our Mind

Our stated philosophy is Confucian order under a Mandate. Our actual operating philosophy, revealed by seven centuries of behavior, is three propositions: *deny the ground before anyone can grow on it; never let anyone see the end of your supply line; where you cannot be strong, be believed strong.*

We are a bluffing empire. The Syr Darya line could not have survived a long war in 225 and we held it two hundred years on appearance. We sketched a map in the dirt for Rome. We threatened Ternate in 477 with a fleet whose powder was nearly spent. We refused the Syr Darya war this period because the fleet's victory was won on wind, range, and bluff, and Ternate remains an untested stone fort — a truth the court states to itself and must never let an enemy learn.

Every one of those bluffs worked. **That is precisely the problem.** We have run the same trick for three centuries against opponents who each had a separate reason not to call it, and recorded each success as evidence of our cunning rather than their forbearance. The Maurya now have cannon and are arming ships. The reasons not to call us are eroding.

**Our worries.** The powder monopoly is gone and every peer will have powder within two generations. The land problem is held, not solved. The treasury has no slack. The Maurya are arming at sea and our range advantage has a clock on it. Rome may be growing on ground no one contests.

**Our irrational fears.** The great one is Baideng. In 200 BC a founding emperor was surrounded and had to negotiate his way out. Seven hundred and twenty-five years ago, and still cited in council. We crossed eight thousand kilometers of grassland at the largest sustained military cost in our history — a project we have *just now* admitted lost money for three centuries — because a second Xiongnu might form on ground we did not hold. No cavalry confederation has been able to threaten this empire since the first century. We spent three hundred years preventing something that had already become impossible.

And we are about to do it again. The Northern Road is Baideng transposed onto an ocean: a rival grows on uncontested ground, therefore be present on that ground. Our own memorandum concedes the survey needs fifty to seventy-five years, that capability trails the survey by another generation, and that we would arrive beside an established Rome at the thin end of the longest supply line ever attempted. We wrote that down and funded it anyway. That is doctrine wearing analysis as a coat.

Second: the empty north. We hold Siberia not because it is useful but because another power there is unthinkable — and there is no other power there, and none can get there. Third: being seen to be weak. We would rather lose an argument than reveal a supply line, an instinct usually correct that has calcified into an inability to probe our own assumptions aloud.

**How logical are we?** Extremely, within our premises, and almost never about them. We audit tactics ruthlessly — the naval reports name the wind dependency, the rigging fire, the spent ammunition, the depleted marines, all honestly — and we overturned two settled doctrines this period. But the premises beneath, that the frontier must be held and that presence denies ground and that the outpost chain answers every problem, have not been examined in seven centuries. We are the most rigorous empiricists alive about everything except why.

## VII. Why We Have Not Collapsed, and What We Got Wrong

**Why we survive.** The monopoly revenue, a third of income independent of who owns the farmland, so the agrarian death spiral never had a fiscal engine here. The centralized arsenal, which keeps succession fights at court and makes warlordism structurally impossible. The frontier as relief valve, six centuries of shipping the landless outward instead of letting them concentrate and detonate. And the examination ladder, the one path upward that is not revolt — revolt having a five-century record of catastrophic failure.

**Rebellions.** The Trung sisters, 40–43, weeks. The Yellow Turbans, 180–200, months. The saltpeter revolts of 355–360, four to six months and real casualties, because the rebels had crude powder. Continuous minor uprisings throughout. The trend goes one way: *suppression gets harder as powder spreads.* The whole political order sits on an asymmetry narrowing every decade.

**Our mistakes.** The westward expansion — three centuries, the largest sustained military cost in our history, returning nine percent of revenue, admitted three hundred years late. Building rail west to the frontier instead of south to the people. Declining Japan in the first century as not worth a sea crossing; it now sits astride the Northern Road we have committed to. Giving the Maurya, in the fourth century, free intelligence that Rome reached further north than they believed. Six centuries of craft without theory, so Egyptian geometry landed as a revelation in the fifth century instead of being ours in the second. Letting the land problem run four hundred years because we could always suppress the consequence. And building our external intelligence apparatus on Sogdian merchants, who are Maurya subjects: our eyes belong to our nearest rival.

## VIII. War, Spies, and Lies

**Do we want to attack anyone?** The institution does. The military faction wanted the Syr Darya this period; the throne refused at real cost and bought land reform with the capital. That refusal will be reopened in the next reign; refusals of this kind always are.

The genuinely dangerous idea now circulating is a preventive strike on the Maurya fleet before their hulls are cannon-armed. It has the shape of every argument this court has ever accepted — a closing window, a demonstrated victory, an enemy that lies. It is the same argument that took us to the Syr Darya in 200 AD expecting Zhang Qian's world of small kingdoms. Name it now, while naming is cheap.

**Will others attack us?** Rome at the Dnieper: no, on two centuries of evidence and no motive. Egypt cannot reach us and does not want to. The Maurya at sea: plausible once armed, and they would be answering a war we started in 474. On land at the Syr Darya: only if they learn what our garrison commanders know. The attack we should actually fear is internal, and it is not a peasant revolt — it is the day a great family or a mill house, with a boring machine, precision gauges, purchased iron, and skilled metalworkers under one private roof, makes barrels.

**Can we beat any single rival one-on-one?** We can *not lose* to any of them. We can conquer none of them. We cannot cross the Syr Darya and win a land war — true for three hundred years, and the rail improved it without solving it. We cannot reach Rome or Egypt with an army at all. At sea we beat the Maurya in 477 against a fleet with no cannon, in favorable wind, at a range we chose, and our own reports say plainly that in calm water or confined channels our advantage erodes. Our entire peer-war experience is one naval campaign against an unarmed opponent. We should stop calling ourselves a naval power on that basis.

Our fourth-century assessment that "China's army could handle any single one of these powers in a straight fight, supply lines set aside" is the most dangerous sentence in our archive. Setting supply lines aside is setting aside the only thing that has ever mattered at eight thousand kilometers.

**Two at once?** The only real two-front case is the Maurya on land and at sea, and we would hold — badly, expensively, and by conceding the sea. The two-front war that would break us is the Maurya plus a powder-armed rebellion in the heartland. Nobody at court is modeling it, and it is the likeliest catastrophe we face.

**Spies.** We have a permanent intelligence *practice* and no named intelligence *service* — a weakness disguised as a tradition. Its parts: the Sogdian merchant networks (Maurya subjects, worked through commercial districts and dockyards, deliberately kept off military facilities); the station at Aden, five scholars for fifty years and now twenty, watching Egypt's lights and steam hulls; the Latin corps that cut the Maurya out of our Rome correspondence; the frontier debriefing habit that turned one soldier's account of eight armored men into a strategic assessment; and rigorous control of what delegations may see. Counter-intelligence is absolute on silk and on weapons manufacture, and this period the steam hull was withheld from every foreign harbor. Good instincts. But we have never once considered that the Sogdians sell our movements to Pataliputra as readily as they sell Pataliputra's to us, or that a fourfold scholar exchange runs in both directions.

**Are we comfortable lying?** Completely, we are good at it, and we have never been caught. We lied at the Syr Darya for two centuries, at Tidore, about our maps. We concealed from the Maurya that we knew their Strait authority was hollow. We conceal from everyone that our garrison model rests on an asymmetry that is gone. What we do *not* do is lie to partners who have dealt straight over long horizons — Rome above all — and this period we chose honesty with Rome over spying on it. That is not virtue; it is a correct valuation.

**Founding divergence, honestly assessed.** Gunpowder at 100 BC is defensible: the fangshi had the materials, the patron, and the method, and the ninth-century date was an accident of chance, not necessity. The second divergence is weaker. That a permanent weapons constituency prevented Confucian moderation is plausible. That it also produced *seven hundred and twenty-five years of unbroken dynastic continuity* — no Wang Mang interregnum, no Three Kingdoms, no warlord era, three succession crises all resolved politely — is the least believable thing in our entire record. No dynasty in our own history came close. The mechanism offered (the center holds the arsenals, so no regional bid is possible) assumes the center itself never fractures, never falls to a regency or a eunuch faction, never loses the arsenal bureaucracy's confidence. It has held. There is no reason written down anywhere explaining why it should keep holding.

## IX. Where the Document Is Wrong or Lying

The hardest question, answered in order of how much it would cost us to be wrong.

1. **The garrison model.** "A fifty-man position with explosive weapons holds against many times its number" is the load-bearing assumption of the entire empire — every frontier, every colony, every suppression, the whole fiscal logic of holding eight thousand kilometers cheaply. It has been true for four hundred years against enemies with no powder. Powder is now general and our own rebels have held it since the 350s. **We have never re-tested the model against an enemy that has it.** If it fails, the cost of holding our frontier multiplies overnight and we cannot pay it. That is not a technology gap; it is a solvency question.

   *How would we test it?* Four ways, and the court should be honest about each. **Exercise against ourselves** — build a garrison post to standard on a testing ground and let arsenal troops assault it with crude powder of the grade a rebel could make. This is cheap, entirely within our control, tells no one anything, and is the obvious thing to do. It is also the weakest evidence, because an exercise cannot reproduce the one variable that matters, which is whether fifty men hold when the men attacking them do not stop. Every siege in history turns on that and no drill can supply it. **The Maurya cannon reports as proxy** — we already have observers computing what twenty to forty pieces and a trajectory doctrine would do to a fortified point, and this is free intelligence we are collecting anyway. But it measures a state army with siege guns against a frontier fort, which is not the case that frightens us; the case that frightens us is a provincial rising with crude powder against a garrison of fifty. **A deliberate provocation somewhere cheap** — pick a distant, expendable post and invite the test. We will not do this and should not: we have no province where a defeat can be made small, and a garrison that falls anywhere is read everywhere, by our own peasants first and by Pataliputra second. **Read our own suppression records honestly** — the saltpeter revolts of 355 to 360 took four to six months and real casualties where the routine was weeks, and that is not a proxy, it is the actual experiment, run once, a hundred and seventy years ago, at the crudest possible level of rebel powder. We recorded the duration and never re-read the file as a measurement.

   The honest answer is that the assumption cannot be tested at the scale that matters without inviting the thing it prevents. We can drill, we can watch the Maurya, and we can re-read 355 — and none of that will tell us whether the empire's internal peace still has a physical basis. So let it be written down without ornament: **this court knowingly rests seven hundred years of internal peace on an article of faith it has chosen not to examine, because the only examination available is the one that ends it.** That is not confidence. It is a wager the dynasty renews every year without pricing, and the correct posture toward it is not reassurance but contingency — a standing plan for what we do in the first month after a garrison somewhere does not hold.

2. **The mills.** We licensed them under the salt-and-iron framework, taxed them as an ordinary weaver, and required them to buy iron through the monopoly, which we called a hand on the throat. It is a hand on their *iron supply* — not on their machinery, their engineers, their capital, or their walls. For six hundred years the arsenal monopoly kept precision metalworking inside the state, and that monopoly is what prevented warlordism, contained every rebellion, and held every succession at court. We have just permitted, for modest revenue, the first private concentration of skilled metalworkers and precision machinery in our history, and approved it as a tax matter.

3. **Seven centuries without a dynastic break.** Presented as the natural consequence of arsenal centralization. It is the single largest unexplained thing in our history, and the mechanism offered is far thinner than its results.

4. **Siberia**, claim written as possession; and **the population**, seventy million registered rather than real, with a "ceiling" that is a policy about where we sent our poor rather than a limit of the soil.

5. **The bluffs and the naval war.** Recorded as triumphs of statecraft; better read as a run of luck against opponents who each had one contingent reason not to test us, all expiring. In 477 we beat a fleet carrying no cannon, in favorable wind, at a range we chose. Our commanders wrote the caveats down honestly; the court quotes the numbers and not the caveats.

6. **"Precision, metallurgy, and range are what a rival with crude powder cannot copy quickly."** The new doctrinal comfort that replaced the old one. Rome has movable type, precision compasses, and the rail, and could bore a rifled barrel within a generation of deciding to. We adopted the claim because we needed something to be superior at.

7. **Egypt's generosity**, explained as costing Egypt little. The unexamined alternative: Egypt is buying a permanent obligation from a seventy-million-person empire on the far side of Maurya and Rome, and disease knowledge — the one thing that could open the tropics to us — is the cheapest possible price. We should ask what Egypt expects us to do with a key to fever country.

8. **The frontier verdict.** We declared "the frontier pays for itself" false this period, three hundred years late, then funded the Northern Road — the same argument in a new coat — in the same session in which we retired its parent.

9. **The literacy projection**, four in five with no precedent anywhere, on which our entire forecast of future research capacity rests.

10. **"None of the conditions for industrial transformation is in place."** Written at 500 and already false by 525 through our own decisions. The most important thing happening in the empire is the thing the file says is not happening.

## X. What We Will Do That Is Not Wise

Written down so it can be recognized when it happens.

We will fund the Northern Road generously despite knowing it cannot matter for seventy-five years, because "deny the ground before a rival grows on it" is the closest thing this court has to scripture and no one can argue against Baideng. We will raise the Syr Darya cannon again and be refused again, because letting it lie is an admission and this court does not make admissions. We will withhold the steam hull, the telescope, and everything of powder from every partner including the one that gave us its disease knowledge for nothing, because concealment here is reflex rather than calculation. We will not tax the mills properly for a generation, because no faction wants that fight until the profits are indecent and the houses are strong enough to argue back. We will keep Siberia at a loss forever, because the alternative is unthinkable rather than because it is expensive.

And we will keep bluffing, because it has worked every single time, which is exactly how an institution learns the wrong lesson.
