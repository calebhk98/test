# THE HAN EMPIRE, 525 TO 550 AD
## The Reign of Emperor Su — Eras Zhengshi (527–539) and Guangding (540–550)

*Everything in this document is written from inside the Han court. Where it describes another power, it describes what Chinese officers saw, what Chinese agents reported, or what the court concluded from those reports. The court's conclusions have been wrong before. Acts by Rome, Egypt and the Maurya are not written here as facts; where the empire has acted toward them, the act is recorded and the response is left where it belongs.*

---

## I. The Succession, and the First Act

The old emperor died in the winter of 526 after thirty-one years. His second son took the throne in the third month of 527, aged thirty-one, and reigned the whole of this period; he is called here by his posthumous style, **Emperor Su**.

The Grand Commandant **Huo Sui** had spent the previous reign's last years assembling the argument that the Syr Darya cannon question be settled by force before the Maurya finished arming their hulls. Su received that memorial in his second month and did not refuse it. He referred it, with the whole of the naval and frontier correspondence, to the Censorate, instructing the Censor-in-Chief **Pei Wen** to report not on whether the empire should fight the Maurya but on **what the empire actually knew about whether its own garrisons could hold anything against an enemy with powder.** He had read, in the handover briefing, the passage observing that the revolts of 355 to 360 in Bing province were not a proxy for that question but the actual experiment — run once, a hundred and seventy years earlier, and never re-read as a measurement.

The order was possible for three ordinary reasons. The succession was uncontested, so the throne owed nothing to any faction. Su had not yet spent political capital on anything. And he framed the order as preparation for the war Huo Sui wanted, which left the Grand Commandant unable to oppose an inquiry into the readiness of his own frontier. Huo Sui assented, and by assenting lost control of the answer. The Directorate of the Shangfang, the central weapons administration, had held for six centuries that the sufficiency of the garrison was settled doctrine; the inquiry did not overrule that view, it audited the file behind it.

---

## II. The Pei Wen Commission (528–533)

The commission sat six years: eleven officials, four of them examination-trained mathematicians seconded from the Chang'an natural-philosophy circles. That composition was unusual — the Censorate's precedents for commission membership run to legal and archival training — and it was the reason the commission produced arithmetic rather than a précis. It held four archives: the Bing province suppression files of 355 to 361 at Jinyang; the Shangfang's powder-issue ledgers for those years at Chang'an; the frontier garrison returns for the Syr Darya and Pontic lines, 450 to 527; and the after-action reports of the naval war of 474 to 477. Its findings are the **Jinyang Report**, submitted the eleventh month of 533.

**First: the posts did not hold.** The received account is that provincial garrisons suppressed a saltpeter-armed revolt in four to six months rather than the customary weeks. The ledgers say otherwise. Of nineteen fortified posts in the affected districts, garrisons of fifty to eighty men, **two were taken by the rebels within thirty-one days and a third abandoned by its commander before assault.** They were recovered eleven weeks later by a field column of four thousand marched from Jinyang. The four-to-six month figure is the duration of that column's campaign, not of any successful defence, and the two lost posts were entered in the returns as "withdrawn for want of powder" — a phrase that passed through six generations of clerks without anyone asking what it meant.

**Second: what it meant was arithmetic.** A fifty-man post carries a standing powder allowance sized against a raid; the Shangfang's schedule, unchanged since 366, provides three to five days of continuous engagement. The doctrine that a small post defeats a large force rests on the attacker being obliged to close and being broken when he closes. An attacker who also has powder is not obliged to close. He stands off and throws charges for as long as he can make powder, which in Bing province, sitting on the northern saltpeter beds, was indefinitely. The post does not fall to assault. It runs dry and is walked into.

**Third: the interior held in 355 because Jinyang was four days away.** Every internal suppression in the empire's recorded history has occurred within a few days' march of a garrison town holding a field force and a magazine, so the model has only been tested in the one condition where its failure does not matter. On the Syr Darya the relief column is now four days by rail from **Zhenxi**, which is why that frontier is sound; likewise the Pontic line as far as the rail runs. **Beyond the rail — the Irtysh and Ob posts, the Minusinsk basin, the Amur stations, the whole northern forest — the nearest relief is six weeks to five months.** There the model has not been tested against anything, and the arithmetic says it cannot work.

**Fourth, offered unasked:** the naval files show the same shape — at Tidore the fleet expended over four fifths of its carried powder in one afternoon against a fortification with no cannon.

Pei Wen delivered the report in person and did not soften it. Huo Sui asked one question — whether the commission proposed the empire abandon the north — and was told it proposed nothing, having been instructed to measure and not to advise.

## III. What the Court Did With It

**The powder-allowance reform (534).** The Shangfang's issue schedule of 366 was replaced. Posts on rail-served lines rose from five days to fifteen; posts beyond rail from five days to sixty, with a hardened magazine to the specification mandatory inside city walls since the fire of the 420s. Carried by Su against the Da Sinong's objection on cost, it is the largest new recurring charge of the reign: roughly **135 tonnes of silver-equivalent annually**, rising to about 160 by 550.

**The doctrine amendment (535),** possible only because Huo Sui was bought. The frontier is reorganised from a mesh into **nodes and eyes**. Designated *holding* positions — the rail-served forts, the Kazakh cities of **Ningyuan**, **Fuyuan** and **Suiyuan**, **Zhenxi** and its four sister works on the Syr Darya north bank, **Anhe** at the Dnieper railhead, and the commandery seats — are garrisoned at strength, magazined to the new standard, and expected to hold. Everything else — the Irtysh posts, the Ob approaches, **Minusinsk**, the Amur stations, the northern-forest fur posts, and the thin scatter across the Pontic between **Anhe** and **Guangtian** — is reclassified **observation**.

On paper it is one category of post reclassified and one clause added to the standing frontier regulations: commanders of observation posts may withdraw on contact rather than stand, and a post lost by withdrawal is no fault in its officer. The Shangfang's clerks find no earlier instance of that discretion in the regulations, which is a statement about the filing and not about the heavens. But the thing underneath the clause is not administrative. Since the Mongolian garrison chain of the first century, the empire's whole method has been that a post does not leave, because a post that may leave is not a claim. The clause concedes that beyond the rail the empire's claim is a claim and not a possession, and the men who had to sign it knew exactly that.

**What it cost to carry.** The fight ran two years and the throne lost ground in it.

- The **Directorate of the Shangfang's garrison-establishment office**, which sets post strengths and has done since the third century, opposed outright. Its argument was that a written permission to withdraw is a written permission to withdraw everywhere, and that a frontier officer reads regulations for what they allow. It was overruled and it exacted its price in the wording: the clause entered the regulations as *preservation of force for counter-concentration*, a phrase that office drafted, and the Jinyang Report was sealed at its insistence rather than the emperor's. The office kept its authority over post strengths, which is what it actually cares about; what it lost was the presumption that its own schedule was sound, and it has been answering to the Bureau's arithmetic ever since.
- Two commandery governors on the northern line, **Yuan Kai** of Youbeiping and **Cui Shu** of Wuyuan, memorialised against, on the ground that a garrison told it may leave will be treated by the peoples around it as one that will. Neither was punished; neither was promoted again in the reign.
- **Wei Zhao**, general of the Pontic line, asked to be retired rather than read the clause to his commanders, and was retired with honours in 536 at fifty-four. The court let him go because refusing would have made a martyr of him and because the alternative was a general executing a regulation he had said in writing he did not believe.
- **Huo Sui's price was two things, not one.** He raised the Syr Darya cannon question formally in council in 536 and Su refused him — made easier by the 135 tonnes a year and a doctrine written by his own arsenals, harder by eighty years of the military faction being told the far-bank cannon were an open matter. That refusal cost Su perhaps half of what he had. But Huo Sui did not assent to the reclassification for nothing: he took the Bamo authorisation of 543 and the standing of his party in the argument that followed it, which is why section VII notes that the fever country is now his faction's project and not the throne's.

Su spent what remained on the note issue, and by 540 he had no capital left to spend on anything, which is visible in what he refused after that date and how briefly he refused it.

**The omission.** The Jinyang Report was not published. A digest circulated to the commanderies as *On the Sufficiency of Magazines*, carrying the reform and the new doctrine with the arithmetic that produced them removed. The full report — the two lost posts of 356 named, the finding that beyond rail the model cannot work — was sealed to the inner council, the Censor-in-Chief, and the Director of the Shangfang.

The reasoning stated in council is the reasoning this court has always used. If it becomes generally known that fifty men with powder cannot be held off by fifty men with powder, the arithmetic is available to every landless tenant in Ji and Yan provinces who can read, and the empire has spent a century making sure most of them can; if it becomes known outside, the Maurya learn what the Syr Darya has never told them. So the empire holds, in three copies, the proof that its internal peace has no physical basis beyond the reach of iron rail, and keeps the proof in a box. The clerk's minute records Su saying that a truth locked in a box is still a truth, and the box does not make it less so, only later.

**What the reform leaks, and how fast.** Pei Wen forced the court to face this in 537 and it did not enjoy the exercise. The document is sealed; the substance is not sealable. A twelvefold rise in beyond-rail powder issue is visible to every arsenal clerk who fills the requisition, every quartermaster who moves it, every carter who counts the wagons, and every post commander who now holds a standing order permitting withdrawal. The Sogdian carters on the western line are Maurya subjects, and the empire's own western intelligence is bought from the same trade.

The court's own estimate, entered by the Shangfang: the *fact* of a large increase is outside the empire within one to two seasons and cannot be concealed at all. The *shape* — that beyond-rail posts got sixty days and rail-served posts fifteen, which is the arithmetic itself in disguise — takes five to ten years to assemble from convoy sizes. The withdrawal clause is worst: it is read aloud to garrisons, and a regulation that permits a post to leave is discussed in every frontier market within a season. Inside the empire the conclusion is available to anyone in Bing or Ji who can read a requisition and count, which is a growing number.

Three things were done about it, all of them cover rather than concealment. The reform was published as *strengthening*, the digest's title chosen so that a larger magazine reads as a richer frontier rather than a frightened one, and the rail-served increase was announced first and loudest so that the beyond-rail figure arrives as an afterthought. The withdrawal clause was drafted into the standing regulations as *preservation of force for counter-concentration*, a phrase the Shangfang invented for the purpose, so that a commander leaving a post is executing a manoeuvre rather than admitting a limit. And the observation posts were given something to be for: the fur and survey returns from the Irtysh, Ob and Amur stations were made a formal annual report to the throne, so that a post which is not expected to fight has a visible reason to exist.

Su's own minute is the honest one. He wrote that the empire is not hiding the conclusion, it is buying the years between the conclusion becoming visible and the conclusion becoming actionable, and that those years are what the rail is for.

## IV. Money: The Iron Re-Rating and the Ever-Normal Note

Revenue at 525 stood near 4,050 tonnes with a surplus near 170; the new charges came to something above 400. Four instruments were proposed; three were refused.

The Da Sinong **Sang Ju** proposed reducing the weight of the bronze cash by a fifth, covering all of it in two years. Su refused, on the argument — recorded, and the best single evidence of the man — that the frontier armies, the rail contractors and now the mill houses all reckon in a coin the state issues, and that a state which quietly makes its own coin worth less has begun taking from everyone who trusts it, in a way it cannot stop once begun and cannot see the bottom of. Sang Ju served until 548.

Raising the saltpeter monopoly price was refused because the largest buyer of monopoly saltpeter is the Shangfang; the empire would have paid itself at a loss. A new levy on the mills, proposed by the Excellency over the Masses **Kong Zhan**, was refused not by the emperor but by arithmetic: a fiscal instrument for merchant manufacture would have required the court to define what a mill *is*, obliging the Confucian faction to concede it deserves its own name in law, and Kong Zhan's own party would not carry that. The merchants' framing of 517 — a works is one man with an unusual tool, taxed as any weaver — survived because nobody who disliked it would pay the price of killing it.

**The iron re-rating (535).** The mills must buy iron and machine parts through the state iron monopoly under their licences. The Iron Commissioner was directed to establish a **second schedule of prices for iron sold to licensed works**, at roughly twice the ordinary schedule, on the stated ground that works consume iron in forms — shafting, gearing, bored cylinders, plate — costlier to produce than plough iron. It is not a tax: it creates no instrument, requires no definition of a mill in law, cannot be appealed as an irregular exaction, and rises automatically as the mills grow. It returned about 30 tonnes in 537 and about 95 by 549. **Gu Zan** of Wu protested privately and could not protest publicly, since that would have required stating what the works earn.

**The ever-normal note (538),** on which Su spent his remaining capital. The empire's reserve has always been grain: a famine buffer of great depth and almost no other use, because grain rots, costs more to move than it is worth, and cannot pay a soldier at **Zhenxi**. The deposit notes in use since the third century are each tied to a particular deposit and a particular pair of offices. Su directed his ministers to make one that is not.

The **changping chao** was authorised in the eighth month of 538: issued only by the Da Sinong, in four denominations, against the certified stock of the ever-normal granaries; redeemable at any commandery granary in grain at the standing ever-normal price or at any commandery treasury in bronze cash; receivable for the land tax, which gave it value at once. Use is restricted by edict to frontier military pay, rail contracts, and magazine stores, and issue capped at **one third of certified granary stock**. Pei Wen made it the condition of the Censorate's endorsement that cap and certification sit in statute rather than regulation, that his own office certify the stock annually, and that a warning stand in the preamble: nothing prevents a later throne raising the cap; the note's worth rests entirely on belief that the grain behind it exists; and a throne in difficulty will find no easier thing to do than issue more.

**What it cost to carry.** This is the largest change to imperial finance since the monopolies, and it was resisted accordingly. It took twenty months from proposal to statute and the throne conceded on four points.

- **Kong Zhan and the Confucian faction** opposed on the ground that a paper the state issues against a store it counts itself is the instrument of Wang Mang, and that the dynasty's whole legitimacy rests on not being that. This was not an argument Su could answer, and he did not try to; he bought it off by conceding that the note may not be used for civil salaries, for tax refunds, or for any payment to a subject who has not contracted for it. That concession cut the instrument's scope by more than half and is the reason it carries 290 tonnes and not 700.
- The **great houses' deposit-note business** — the Wu and Shu trading families who have issued paired-office notes since the third century and take a charge on every one — lost standing outright, since a state note receivable for the land tax is better than any of theirs. **Gu Zan** of Wu, already sore from the iron re-rating, worked against it through the Excellency over the Masses and was beaten. The court noted the alignment and did not comment on it.
- The **commandery treasuries' remittance establishment**, whose entire function is moving coin under guard, lost work and men; roughly a fifth of the escort details on the western remittance road were stood down by 546. Their supervising office was compensated with the granary-certification duty, which is how four fifths of a count came to rest on the word of men who had just been given a reason to want the instrument to succeed. Nobody at the time saw that as the flaw it is.
- **Pei Wen's conditions** were the price of the Censorate's endorsement and they are real costs to the throne: statute rather than regulation, annual certification by an office the emperor does not control day to day, the countersignature, and a preamble that says in the empire's own law that a later emperor will be tempted to abuse this. No Chinese throne has previously written a warning against itself into the enacting clause of its own instrument.

**Who guards the cap.** The statute names three offices and no more. The **commandery granary intendants** count the stock, each in his own commandery, by physical measure once a year after the autumn levy. The **Censorate**, through Pei Wen's office, certifies the count, by inspectors who visit a rotating fifth of the granaries in person and accept the rest on the intendants' returns. The **Da Sinong** alone may issue, and only against a certificate carrying the Censorate's seal. Issue beyond the third requires an edict under the emperor's own hand countersigned by the Censor-in-Chief — and it was Pei Wen, not Su, who insisted on the countersignature, on the ground that an emperor who wants money will always find a Da Sinong who will give it to him and should have to find a Censor-in-Chief as well.

Every part of that is weaker than it reads. Four fifths of the count is the intendants' own word, and an intendant whose granary is short has every reason to write it full. A Censor-in-Chief is appointed by the throne. And the countersignature is a requirement of the statute, and statutes are amended by the same throne that finds them inconvenient.

**What happens when the silver is not there.** It has not happened in this reign, and that is the whole of the reason the cap has held. The nearest thing was 543, when the Kuhan relief and the second Northern Road squadron and the Shouchun rail all fell in the same two years; Sang Ju proposed a supplementary issue of ninety tonnes above the third, Pei Wen refused the certificate, and Su did not overrule him. He did not overrule him because he agreed, and because refusing cost him nothing he needed that year.

The court should be plain with itself about what that proves, which is nothing about the instrument and everything about the men. The cap holds because a Censor-in-Chief with standing said no and an emperor with judgement let the no stand. Neither of those is in the statute. When the shock comes that this reign has been spared — a two-front emergency, a closed trade road, a rising that needs three armies paid in cash for two years — the throne of that day will find a paper instrument that can be made to yield silver by the stroke of a brush, a granary count that is four fifths hearsay, and a cap that requires only its own amendment. The note gives the treasury a mobile claim on a reserve that was previously immobile, and in the same statute it makes a debasement available that requires no touching of the coin Su refused to touch. He knew it. The preamble is Pei Wen's warning and Su let it stand unedited, which is the most he could do and less than the thing requires.

By 550 the note carries roughly 290 tonnes of annual payments, mostly frontier pay, against a statutory ceiling near 615. Mobile resources roughly doubled in eleven years without a new tax, and the old fragility — grain-rich and cash-poor, badly placed against any shock demanding silver quickly — is reduced. It is not removed, and the arithmetic in the money declaration below shows how little of the empire's revenue is actually free to move.

**The registration artifact.** Closing the dependent-registration workaround brought hidden households onto the rolls through the 530s; registered population rises from about 70 million to about 76.5 million. Sang Ju stated in council that perhaps four fifths of that is administrative recovery — the empire has not grown by six and a half million, it has found five million it had lost. Huo Sui's memorials quote the seventy-six million without the qualification. Both men are in the record.

## V. The Mills, the Trunk Line, and the Weavers of Wu

By 550 there are **thirty-one licensed works** in Wu and Kuaiji and the lower Yangtze counties, the largest — the Gu house at Wu — employing somewhat over three thousand hands under one roof. The machinery did not stay with silk: **fourteen of the thirty-one are hemp and ramie cloth works**, the research having been paid for by the silk houses and the machine being faster than a hand at either. They still run on water, and the good river sites are largely taken by 550, which is the specific commercial pressure that makes the rotary steam engine worth buying rather than admiring.

**The southern trunk line.** **Datong** coal country in Bing province south through **Jinyang** to **Luoyang** on the Yellow River, completed 543. Its memorial justified it on fuel carriage first and arsenal supply second, which is the reverse of every western line the empire has built. **Luoyang** south to the Huai crossing at **Shouchun**, begun 544, roughly two thirds built at 550. The continuation across the Yangtze into the Wu mulberry country is surveyed and not begun; the Yangtze silk houses part-funded the surveyed portion and were named openly in the memorial, concealment inviting a censor's report. Datong coal now reaches the Luoyang and Chang'an arsenals at roughly a third of its former carriage cost, which is much of why the Shangfang absorbed the powder reform without the foundries starving.

**The riot at Wu, autumn 545.** The mills displaced household weavers, numerous in Wu and Kuaiji, who had no name for what had happened to them. In the ninth month a crowd of perhaps four thousand, largely women, moved on the Gu house works, burned two outbuildings and a dyeing shed, and were dispersed by the commandery garrison in a day and a half. Eleven died, nine in a crush at a gate. No firearms were used, on the administrator's own order, and none were held by the crowd.

The administrator was commended for not firing. The Gu house compensated the families within the month, which the court noted it could easily afford. The Ministry of Justice, asked what statute the rioters had offended, found the case did not fit — not tax rebels, not sectaries, not bandits, their grievance against a licensed private undertaking rather than the state — and disposed of it as affray. Kong Zhan's memorial, received and not acted upon, drew the right conclusion: the empire has permitted a thing that can take a livelihood from ten thousand households without any law being broken, and has no category for the resulting people. The court noted with relief that nobody at Wu had powder. Pei Wen minuted that the absence of powder at Wu in 545 tells the throne nothing about its presence at Wu in 575.

## VI. Research: What Answered to Effort, and What Did Not

Heavy money to what answers to effort, a small protected standing charge to what does not, and no pretence that the second is a programme.

**The Bureau of Measured Things (536).** The **Duwu Ju**, under the Ministry of Works, at Luoyang with a second hall at Chang'an: one hundred salaried posts, recruited by an examination in mathematics and measurement rather than the classical papers. First Director **Cheng Bi**. It holds the standards of length, weight and time, records and publishes observations, and keeps the failure ledgers of the standing searches. Twelve tonnes a year. The posts sit on the ordinary civil establishment, which makes investigation a graded appointment rather than an attachment to an arsenal or a mine.

The Ministry of Personnel fought the recruitment clause for a year and won most of it. Its objection was that a man graded on a paper of measurement holds office without having demonstrated the learning that office presumes, which if conceded generally unmakes the establishment. The settlement was that the Bureau's posts are capped at the eighth and ninth grades with no promotion path above the seventh, and that a Duwu Ju man wanting higher office must sit the classical papers like anyone. So the Bureau is a career and a ceiling at once, and Cheng Bi's best three students left it for the arsenals within a decade, where the pay is worse and the ceiling is not written down.

**Rifled naval cannon (achieved).** The Chang'an arsenal under the Director of the Shangfang **Ma Qian** carried rifling from trial to standard fitting, with refits at **Longbian** and **Panyu** through the 540s.

**Breech-loading: the seal, not the breech.** The record needs correcting here, because the reign has been claiming credit for the wrong thing. The wedge breech was not new in 525. Crude breech-loaders reached frontier issue between 505 and 508, sealed on the insight of the 490s that the paper cartridge swells under firing pressure and closes the gap itself. What the file did not say, because no arsenal writes down its own embarrassment, is that **the paper seal never worked properly and everyone at the sharp end knew it.** Paper obturation chars, blows past on the second or third round, and vents gas and flame sideways into the man working the breech. The Shangfang's own casualty returns for the frontier arsenals, read by the Pei Wen commission in 531 as a side-finding, show burn injuries to gun crews running at four to seven a hundred per year of issue on the breech pieces. Garrison commanders had quietly gone back to muzzle-loading for anything sustained, which is why a weapon fielded in 508 had not changed a single tactical practice by 525.

The fix came out of the ship stores and not out of the gun shops. The empire has been cladding hulls in Kazakh copper since the 480s and the arsenals hold it in sheet and ring. **The obturating ring adopted in 541 is soft copper, seated in a recess cut in the breech face, which the firing pressure expands outward against the chamber wall and which relaxes when the pressure drops** — the gas does the sealing work itself, and the paper cartridge goes back to being what it always should have been, a measured charge and nothing more. The ring is consumable, issued in tens, and replaced by the gun captain when it will no longer take a seat. Ma Qian's man **Shi Yuan** at Chang'an is credited with it in the arsenal record, having come to it from the copper sheathing yards at Panyu rather than from any gun shop.

That is the achievement of the reign, and it is a metallurgical one: not a new action but a seal that does not burn the man using it. Reliable from 541; general issue to holding-position garrisons complete by 549; about three times the sustained rate of fire of a muzzle piece and, more to the point, a rate a crew will actually use. The doctrine amendment depends on it entirely — a garrison told to hold sixty days needs to spend less powder per hour to do it, and it needs gunners who are willing to work the breech.

**Marine steam (achieved late, at cost).** To be worth anything on the northern ice coast the hull needed pressure, which is what the Commissioner of the Southern Yards **Lü Heng** was pushed to find. In the fourth month of **534** a boiler burst during a moored pressure trial in the Gulf at Longbian. **Nineteen men died**, among them the master shipwright **Zhou Yi**, the one man who understood the whole plant and who had kept most of it in his head rather than on paper. The programme did not merely lose a year; it lost the knowledge. This was the risk the last period's own file had named — boiler safety at sea, carried forward as an open marine problem — arriving in the ordinary way such risks arrive, at a pressure trial rather than at sea.

The Censorate opened an inquiry and Huo Sui's party demanded the works be closed, arguing that the empire had a proven sailing navy, no rival that steam would defeat, and no reason to keep killing shipwrights for a curiosity. Su held it, at three prices: every plate, seam, rivet spacing and test pressure written down and lodged with the new Bureau before any further trial; trials run with the vessel unmanned and the boiler worked from shore by line; and the Aden station tasked specifically to watch and record whatever of Egyptian yard practice is visible from the quays and the open harbour. The result is the **Zhenhai**, completed at Longbian in **546**, and the **Jinghai** in **549**, working the coast between Longbian and Panyu — not trusted for ice, not sent abroad, never shown to a foreign eye. Twenty-four years and 210 tonnes for two coastal hulls and a written specification; Su's assessment is that the specification is what was bought and the hulls are incidental.

**The two open searches (not achieved).** Both continue as standing charges of about three tonnes a year, protected by edict from the annual estimates, all failures recorded by the Bureau.

*Glass — and a funding line that has outlived its justification.* The standing order to vary the batch produced, at the Chang'an hall around 541, a dense heavy glass from a lead-bearing melt. Cheng Bi's student **Fu Yi** established by grinding that it spreads colour further than ordinary glass for the same bending of light, and wrote in 547 that two glasses spreading colour differently might, shaped against each other, bend the light while cancelling the colour. **No such lens exists at 550.**

But the reason it does not exist has changed, and Fu Yi said so in a minute to the Bureau in 548 that the court records because it is a criticism of the throne's own habits. The lens was funded as an open-ended search on the argument that it could not be driven — the same standing given to the electrical work, and correct while nobody knew what to look for. **A written, testable proposition ends that argument.** The wall now is not the idea. It is glass: the lead melt comes out of the pot streaked with cord and striae, seeded with bubbles, and different from batch to batch, so that a disc large and clear and uniform enough to grind into a working element cannot be got reliably. That is a furnace, stirring, annealing and batch-control problem, which is exactly the sort of problem that answers to money and repetition.

Fu Yi's minute states that the charge is being continued at three tonnes on reasoning nobody has revisited since 517, and that this is a budget habit and not a judgement. The Bureau re-rated it in 549: a **directed programme at nine tonnes a year** with a dedicated pot house at Chang'an, a written batch register, and a stated object — optical-quality lead glass in discs of usable size and repeatable quality. Cheng Bi's estimate to the throne is that the glass lands in the 550s and the lens within a year or two of the glass. That estimate is on the record and can be held against the Bureau.

*The continuous source — and why nobody has seen it.* The Chang'an electrical workshop under **Lou Zhen** worked the bounded search: every material known to carry the force, in every arrangement, wetted and dry, paired and stacked, all failures recorded. The ledger holds something over nine thousand entries. **There is no continuous source at 550.** The hand-cranked machine and the storing jar remain the whole of the capability, unchanged from 525.

The court must be exact about why, because the position is narrow and it will not last.

One entry from the eleventh month of 547, made by a junior against **a single pair** of copper and zinc plates in brine, records a persistent rather than momentary deflection of the indicator. It was recorded and set aside as an uninteresting anomaly, and the reason is the instrument. Every detector the workshop owns was built to measure the electrical force as the workshop has always known it — the sharp, powerful, momentary discharge of the crank machine and the stored jar. Pith balls, straw indicators and a leaf gauge all answer to a strong shove and to nothing else. A single pair of plates in brine gives a push so feeble by comparison that on those instruments it barely registers, and what it produced was a small steady lean where every interesting result the workshop has ever had was a violent kick. **It is not the chemistry that hid this. It is the detector.** A man holding an instrument that measures loudness cannot hear a note held quietly for an hour.

So the search that names "paired and stacked" in its own protocol has in fact tried pairs and stacks — of many things, in many arrangements — and has been measuring all of them with a gauge that cannot see what they do. The court claims nothing from the 547 entry. Su's instruction on the estimate for 549 reads that the charge is to be continued and is not to be reported on.

**And the file records that this cannot hold.** The single-pair reading is the whole of the empire's ignorance here, and it is one experiment wide. The moment anyone at Chang'an stacks twenty of those pairs in a column — which the protocol already licenses, which costs nothing, and which the ledger's own logic points at — the effect scales past any instrument's threshold and past a man's forearms, and the thing announces itself. The Bureau's own view, minuted by Lou Zhen in 549 and not acted upon, is that this happens within fifteen years whether the throne funds it or not. The empire should expect to have a continuous source before 565 and should not congratulate itself on the finding when it comes.

## VII. The Fever Country: Bamo

The largest genuinely new capability of the reign came out of the Egyptian disease knowledge given in the last period and was not a machine. What Egypt gave was method and a principle: how fever country is crossed and held, how a camp is sited and its water managed, how movement is timed against the season, how a port is closed against a plague coming up a trade road — and the proposition, new to Chinese medicine, that particular fevers answer to particular plants rather than to regimen alone.

**None of the plants transferred.** The species named in the Egyptian material do not grow in Jiaozhi or Yongchang and no substitute for them could be imported at any useful scale, which is why the work took ten years and not two. The physicians of the Imperial Academy at Luoyang and Guangzhou had to run the principle against their own pharmacopoeia, and what they arrived at by 542 is Chinese:

- **Qinghao**, the sweet wormwood of the southern hills, taken as the principal agent. Its preparation is the part nobody understands and the part that decides everything. The Academy's first trials, prepared as any physician would prepare a herb — decocted, boiled long, taken hot — did almost nothing, and the programme was nearly abandoned in 538 on that result. What saved it was an accident at Guangzhou: a batch prepared by wringing and steeping the fresh plant in cold water, because the physician **Yu Jian** was working in the field away from a fire, worked where the boiled preparations had not. It was replicated deliberately, and the finding is now standing doctrine — **the virtue of qinghao is destroyed by heat and the infusion must be cold.** No one at the Academy can say why. It is recorded exactly as gunpowder was recorded, as a thing that is true and unexplained, and it is written into the regimen as an absolute prohibition on boiling because the physicians are afraid a cook will improve it.
- **Changshan** root as the secondary agent, decocted in the ordinary way, effective against the fevers qinghao does not answer and violently emetic at any dose above the schedule, which cost the trial several men before the dose was bounded.
- The camp discipline from the Egyptian material entire: siting above the flood line and away from standing water, boiled drinking water, movement timed to the dry season, and the port quarantine practice extended from the Indian Ocean protocol of the 470s.

The empire has been stopped at the **Shan Plateau** since the second century. Below it lies the Irrawaddy lowland and beyond that the Bay of Bengal — a road to the western ocean that does not pass the Strait of Malacca.

The trial, authorised 543, is deliberately small. **Colonel Teng Yin** of the Yongchang command took 400 men down to **Bamo** on the upper Irrawaddy in the dry season of 544, built a stockaded post above the flood line, and stayed, resupplied twice yearly, through 550. Losses over the first two seasons were **eighty-nine dead of sickness, twenty-two per hundred**, against half or more in a single season in every previous Han attempt in that quarter recorded in the Yongchang returns; it fell to about nine per hundred by the fourth year.

The court has adopted Teng Yin's judgement of 549: the lowland is **possible and not yet worth it**. Four hundred men can be held there; forty thousand cannot be supplied there; the river is unmapped below Bamo; the middle-valley peoples have not been approached; the distance to the sea is unknown. What has been bought is the removal of an absolute barrier and the beginning of an argument — a Chinese port on the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal, the Malacca toll made irrelevant. The proposition is now arguable where the plateau limit had made it unarguable, and Huo Sui's party has taken it up with enthusiasm, which is the strongest reason to be careful with it.

## VIII. The Northern Road, and the Ice at Kuhan

The twelve hulls commissioned in the last period went to work in 529. The court had been told in writing, in 525, that the survey needs fifty to seventy-five years, that capability trails survey by a generation, and that the empire would arrive beside an established Rome at the thin end of the longest supply line ever attempted. It funded the programme anyway, then and again now, because the proposition that a rival must not be allowed to grow on ground nobody contests is the oldest article this court holds and it does not lose arguments.

**The southern squadron**, working north from Japanese waters, made the better progress — the coast is worse but the ice is less. It charted **Kuye**, rounded its northern cape by 534, surveyed the **Qiandao** chain through the 530s and 540s, and reached the peninsula the survey names **Kanchajia** by 543, posting **Yanhuo** on its eastern shore in 549. Restaging at northern Honshu ports was requested of the king of Wa through the ordinary tributary channel in 528; the squadron's standing orders provided that if the courtesy were not extended it would restage through the Korean commanderies and its own Kuye watering place instead, at the cost of about a season a year. The squadron's returns record it working from both.

**The eastern squadron** worked east from the Siberian river mouths under **Feng Ao**, reporting to the Commissioner of the Northern Road **Su Jian**, and is where the money and the men were lost. Posts were established at **Beihai** at the Lena mouth in 531 and **Yanhe** at the Yana in 536, both supplied down the rivers, both marginal. The coast east of the Yana is the worst in the survey's experience: shallow, foggy, ice-choked into the seventh month, no timber. Su Jian's instructions from Luoyang, renewed in 539 and 541, were to press the season — sail earlier, stay later — on the reasoning that every year saved off a seventy-five year survey is a year Rome does not get.

In the summer of **542** Feng Ao took six hulls east past the Indigirka toward the Kolyma. The freeze came early. Four were beset and crushed against the shore ice in the last week of the ninth month; two worked clear and ran west to Yanhe. The crews of the four came ashore near the Kolyma mouth with what they could carry and wintered there. **Two hundred and thirty-eight men died**, of cold, of scurvy, and at the last of starvation. Ninety-one were brought off by the relief in the summer of 543. The place is entered on the charts as **Kuhan**, bitter cold, and a stone was set there in 545 with the names.

Three consequences. The squadron was rebuilt to four hulls rather than six, the yards being unable to replace them faster. The instruction to press the season was withdrawn by edict in 544 and replaced by an order that no hull is to be east of the Yana after the first day of the ninth month, slowing the survey by roughly a third. And Su Jian's recalculation of 546, accepted by the throne, is that the survey reaches the far strait not in two periods but in **three or four** — a hundred years at the pace supply can carry.

By 550 the eastern squadron has charted the coast as far as the great bay east of the Kolyma and posted **Beihai**, **Yanhe**, and a relief station at **Kuhan**: roughly nine hundred kilometres of new charted coast in twenty-one years — about 43 km a year — at four hulls and two hundred and thirty-eight men. The posted line stands at the Yana with one marginal station beyond it; the charted line runs some six hundred kilometres further east than anything the empire can supply.

The southern squadron's arithmetic is the opposite and the territory declaration sets both out side by side, because a single combined figure flatters one squadron and libels the other. It has charted roughly **three thousand four hundred kilometres** in the same span — Kuye's western and northern shores, the Qiandao chain, the Kanchajia coast — against one garrison of forty men at Yanhuo and a watering place with nobody on it. Fast survey, almost no posting. Between them the two squadrons have charted about 4,300 km and put roughly 155 men on it, at 62 tonnes a year.

Su's edict of 544 states that the men at Kuhan died because Luoyang told them to sail in a month the ice does not permit, that the fault is the throne's, and that the throne will not repeat it. It was published in full, which the Jinyang Report was not. The court's rule is visible in the difference: a failure that can be admitted without arming anyone is admitted, and a failure that would arm someone is sealed.

## IX. The Eastern Crossing: What the Court Did With a Proven Route

The Northern Road is not the only eastern asset the empire holds, and the Board of Naval Affairs said so in 537 in terms the court found uncomfortable. The expedition of the last period did not merely produce evidence for a future coastal chain. It produced a **route**: out from the Korean ports at forty degrees north on the westerlies, landfall on the far coast in about five months; home by descending that coast south to about twenty degrees north, turning west, and riding the low-latitude wind, about three months. A round voyage, with refit and the wait for a season, is fourteen to twenty months. Two of three ships came back. It has been sailed.

The Board's memorial put three courses to the throne and the court took them in order.

**Settlement was refused, on arithmetic.** A colony on that shore can be reached twice in three years at the very best, which means it must be self-feeding from its first winter or it is a way of killing people slowly. The empire's entire frontier method for seven centuries is a chain — fort, supply line, colony, next fort — and there is no chain across five thousand li of open ocean, nor any prospect of one. **Sang Ju's figure**, entered against the proposal: a settlement of a thousand souls capable of holding itself would cost more each year than the whole Northern Road, and the Northern Road at least ends somewhere the empire can walk. Huo Sui's party argued for it and lost that argument on numbers rather than on nerve, which the record should note, because it is the first time in this reign the military faction was defeated by the Da Sinong's ledger alone.

**Doing nothing was also refused,** and the reason is the court's oldest article. A route nobody sails is a route somebody else sails.

**What was authorised, in 539, is small and repeatable.** The **Eastern Crossing establishment** under **Xue Rong**, three purpose-built ocean hulls from the Longbian and Panyu yards — the *Dongwang*, the *Chengfeng*, the *Anlan* — deep-built for the westerly storm track, copper below the waterline, neither the ice hulls of the Northern Road nor the warm-water merchantmen of the Egypt run, both of which were examined and rejected as wrong for the passage. About 50 tonnes to build and **about 40 tonnes a year to run**. The standing orders are four lines and the last two are limits:

- Sail the route again, and again after that, until it is a route and not a lucky season.
- Chart the far coast north and south of the landfall, and the return leg down it.
- Land and mark a cache above the tide — iron, salt, grain, cordage, sailcloth, powder, and a written statement of what it is and who left it.
- **Do not settle. Do not garrison. Do not treat with any people found there beyond what is needed for water, wood and food, and do not sell them iron or powder on any account.**

**What happened, and what it cost in men.** The first crossing sailed from the Korean ports in the fifth month of 542 with 128 aboard three hulls, made landfall in the tenth, wintered ashore in a stockade at a point the charts name **Fusang**, charted some seven hundred kilometres of coast north and south through 543, and came home by the southern road in the third month of 544. All three hulls returned. **Thirty-one men did not** — twenty-four per hundred, nearly all of them on the outbound leg and nearly all of the same thing: the swelling gums, the opening of old wounds, the black legs and the sudden death on exertion that Chinese ships have always taken on long passages and have always written down as the sea's ordinary charge.

Five months out of sight of land is longer than any Chinese crew had previously been asked to go, and the Board of Naval Affairs did what this court does with a number it dislikes: it read the file. Xue Rong's surgeon **Ji Wen** was set to the question in 544 with the Kuhan wintering reports beside him, where the same sickness had killed a large share of the two hundred and thirty-eight. What he found is empirical and the reason he believed it is a comparison, not a theory. Across both records the men who kept longest were the cooks and their boys, and what distinguished the cooks was that they ate green things the rest of the crew did not — sprouted beans grown in trays for the pot, the pickled cabbage kept for flavouring, and the ginger and citrus peel carried for the officers' table. Ji Wen's recommendation, adopted for the second crossing, was blunt and made no claim to know why: **bean sprouts grown continuously aboard in shallow trays and issued to every man daily, pickled greens issued weekly, and citrus peel and ginger carried as ship's stores rather than cabin comforts.** Men were flogged for skipping the ration, which tells the court how little the crews believed it.

The second crossing sailed 546 with 134 and returned in 548 having lost **twelve, nine per hundred**, extended the chart, and rebuilt the cache in stone. The Board's assessment is that the trays are the reason and that it cannot prove it from two voyages. The practice has since been ordered for the Northern Road squadrons and for the Egypt run, where it is resented as an imposition on hulls that make landfall every few weeks anyway.

The court records the honest form of this: a quarter of the first crew died to buy the route, that loss was the strongest argument the Da Sinong had against continuing, and the reason the establishment survived the 545 estimates is that Ji Wen's finding arrived first and offered a number that could be improved.

And then the court did the thing it does. On Xue Rong's recommendation, twelve volunteers under **Yu Ling** were left at Fusang in the ninth month of 548 to hold one cycle at the cache — to keep it, to learn the winter, and to be there. The relief sailed from Korea in the fourth month of **550**. **At the close of this period it has not returned and the court does not know whether those twelve men are alive.** That is the honest state of the empire's presence on the far continent: a stone cache, a chart, and twelve men whose condition is unknown.

**Why the Northern Road still gets sixty-two tonnes a year when a route already exists.** The court's stated reasoning, and it is sound as far as it goes: a sea route carries knowledge and cannot carry weight. Three hulls crossing twice in six years is a thread. A coastal chain, if it is ever built, is a rope, and everything the empire has ever done on a frontier requires a rope. Su's own minute is less flattering and is in the file: that the Northern Road is funded because no minister can argue against Baideng and win, and the Eastern Crossing is funded because it answers the same fear at a fortieth of the price and he could get it through in a single session. The private audit's charge — that the Northern Road is the old doctrine wearing analysis as a coat — is not answered by this reign and the court does not pretend it is.

**And this is what the letter to Rome is for.** The empire has looked squarely at the cost of denying that ground by presence and found it cannot pay it in this century. Where it cannot deny ground with garrisons it is trying to deny it with a written rule, which is cheaper than a fleet by three orders and is the only instrument available to a treasury with forty-five tonnes of free money. The letter of 548 discloses the crossings, the cache, and the twelve men, because a court that proposes a rule of restraint while quietly holding a stone depot on the far shore has proposed nothing.

## X. The Sea: The Escorts, and What They Reported

The Egypt run continued under armed escort throughout. The standing rules of engagement of 465 were revised in 539 on the Board of Naval Affairs' recommendation: escorts are to interpose between any approaching armed force and the merchants under their protection, to open at the outer limit of rifled range rather than at the old three to five hundred metres, and not to close. The change follows from the rifling and not from any incident.

What the escort captains filed through this period, and what the Dunhuang network's factors reported from the Strait ports and the Kalinga yards, agree on one thing and are silent on others. Chinese captains report sighting Maurya hulls carrying mounted pieces — short and smooth-bored by the look of the muzzles at distance — where a decade earlier they reported none. The factors report the same hulls being fitted and report crews described by hired dockyard labour as unpractised. Nobody has put a number on how many. Nothing in the reports establishes whether any piece is rifled, and the court records that as not known rather than as concealed; nobody has written it down because nobody has seen it.

The Board's reading, endorsed by the throne: the empire's advantage at sea is range, and range rests on rifling and barrel steel, which is to say on the Chang'an and Luoyang arsenals, on Kazakh copper and on Datong coal. The court's estimate is that this holds perhaps two generations and not three, on the reasoning that a manufacturing gap of this kind has always closed and this one is being worked at. The response is neither war nor complacency but to keep the gap open — which is why the rifling money was spent and why the Board's recommendation to build twenty further purpose-built hulls was **refused**: twenty hulls would consume the note issue and the powder reform together, and hulls outranged in sixty years are worth less than the arsenals that keep them from being outranged.

The preventive strike on the Maurya fleet, named in the private audit of 525 as the dangerous idea then circulating, was proposed in council in 540 by the Board's junior faction and refused by Su without referring it. His minute gives two reasons and no third: that the empire's entire experience of peer war is one naval campaign in favourable wind against an opponent carrying nothing that could answer, and that a war begun for a closing window has to be finished with powder the empire has just discovered it cannot spare from its own frontier.

Chinese observers at **Zhenxi** continue to report cannon in the emplacements on the south bank of the Syr Darya that observers have reported there since the 450s. The Silk Road toll is levied at twelve per cent, as it has been since 477, and no change has been recorded at any Chinese post on the road.

## XI. Diplomacy: What the Empire Did

Recorded here are the empire's own acts. What any other court does with them is not in this file.

**The Dnieper crossing.** Chinese rail from **Anhe** was completed to the crossing point in **541**, with a transfer yard, sidings, cranes and a gauge-matching bogie shop built on the Chinese bank, on the terms of the standing Agreement. The empire built to the water and built the apparatus a junction would need. Whether iron meets iron there is Rome's to determine, and the message of 548 carries the offer in plain terms. The Anhe customs returns record outbound silk tonnage from the Chinese bank rising by about half between 541 and 550, which is the empire's own number and reflects what the Chinese rail delivered to the crossing, not what happened west of it.

**Egypt.** The scholar exchange stands at twenty per decade both ways, as agreed in the last period, and the disease knowledge given then has been the most useful thing another court has put into Chinese hands in this reign — Bamo is what came of it. After 534 the standing tasking of the Aden and Alexandria scholars was sharpened toward boiler practice, plate, and the working of iron under pressure: what they may lawfully observe, what they may ask, and what they may record. The empire continues to withhold the electrical work, the steam hulls, and everything of powder. No request for powder knowledge appears anywhere in the court's Egyptian correspondence.

**What was disclosed to whom, and the court's reason.** The eastern crossings, the Fusang cache and the twelve men were disclosed to Rome in full and to no one else. The reason entered in the minute is that Rome is the only court with a stake on that continent and the only one that disclosed to us first; Egypt cannot reach it and has not asked; and the Maurya would learn from it only that Chinese deep-ocean hulls exist and where they are. The court is aware this is the same reflexive concealment the private audit of 525 named, and records that it made the choice deliberately this time rather than by habit.

**Messages.** Three letters were sent this period, one to each of the three courts: to Rome in the spring of 548 by the Dnieper road; to Egypt in the autumn of 546 by the Aden station; to the Maurya in the summer of 547 by the Zhenxi crossing under the ordinary frontier channel. All three are set out in the declarations below. None of their answers is written here.

Su's argument in council for the Rome letter was that where the empire cannot deny ground with garrisons it may deny it with a boundary, and that the cheapest boundary in history is one drawn before either party has arrived. He added the part Huo Sui could not answer: that Rome told China of the crossing unprompted, first, and asked nothing, and that a court which repays that with silence and a surveying fleet will find in eighty years that it has taught the one power that has never lied to it not to be honest with it.

His argument for the Maurya letter was narrower and colder. Both fleets now carry cannon at sea; the empire's captains have standing orders to open at eight hundred metres; a war neither court has decided upon can be begun by a lieutenant with a poor estimate of range. A convention for encounters costs nothing to offer and, if taken up, removes the one way this reign could stumble into the war Su has twice refused.

## XII. The Ledger at 550

Registered population about 76.5 million. Economy near 39,000 tonnes of silver-equivalent; revenue near **4,390 tonnes**, extraction around eleven per cent. Investigation tier about 1,600, of whom 100 hold salaried posts in the Bureau; broad technical workforce about 64,000; functionally literate 13 to 15 million. The full arithmetic behind all of these is in the declarations.

**Gained since 525:** rifled cannon as fleet standard; reliable breech-loaders issued to the holding garrisons; two seagoing steam hulls in coastal service and, more to the point, the written specification of one; a paper instrument that turns immobile grain into mobile silver; a rail from the Datong coal to the Yellow River; a regimen that lets men live and work in fever country; a post at Bamo; about four thousand three hundred kilometres of newly charted northern coast across two squadrons, and a post at Yanhuo on Kanchajia; two completed crossings of the eastern ocean, seven hundred kilometres of far-coast chart and a stone cache at Fusang; a bureau that holds the empire's standards and its failure ledgers; and a sealed report showing that the garrison sufficiency doctrine does not hold beyond the reach of rail.

**Lost:** two hundred and thirty-eight men at Kuhan, nineteen at Longbian, eleven at Wu, four hulls. Twenty-one years of the Northern Road's schedule to the revised estimate. A general of the Pontic line who would not read the new regulation aloud. And the assumption on which the north was garrisoned, which was not lost so much as measured and found wanting.

**Unresolved at the close:** twelve men at Fusang, relief sailed and not returned.

The examination share has risen from roughly a third at 500; the Bureau's hundred posts are the first appointments recruited by a mathematics-and-measurement paper rather than the classical papers.
- **Sub-official clerks, scribes and runners on commandery payrolls: about 430,000.** Not officials, mostly literate, and the layer through which any arithmetic in a requisition leaks.
- **Living men who studied to examination standard and did not take office: on the order of 900,000.** Basis: cycle pass rates against candidate registrations over forty years. A large share teach, which is the engine that keeps lowering the price of instruction.

**Literacy — re-derived, in three tiers.** The single-number estimate used previously was wrong in both directions, because it counted one thing where the empire actually has three, each with its own engine and its own brake.

*The engines, stated plainly.* Printing has broken the price of a book, which is the brake that stops most societies at a tenth. The examination is the only ladder to office and office is the only durable security a household can obtain, so every family that can spare a son will spend on one. Crucially, **a son who fails still reads**, and a reading man who does not govern is a teacher, a clerk, a factor, a granary tally-man, a mill foreman or a monopoly weighman — all of which pay several times a field hand and none of which existed in this number a century ago. That is a self-propagating machine: each cycle's failures are the next cycle's cheap instructors, and there are on the order of nine hundred thousand of them alive. Add the first literate generation now becoming parents, who teach the first two hundred characters at the hearth for nothing. Add, new this period, thirty-one licensed works whose foremen must read a specification and a gauge, and a rail whose gangers must read a schedule. Every brake that holds an agrarian society at a tenth is off at once, and the court underestimated this.

*The brake that remains* is the script, and it is not small. A man reaches useful reading in a year or two of part-time study; he reaches command of the literary language in ten. No hearth teaching crosses that second gap. So the tiers separate permanently rather than converging.

| Tier | What it means | At 550 | Share of registered population | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | Reads common characters — a notice, a contract, a tally, a printed manual, a gauge mark | **13 to 15 million** | ~17 to 19% | 5–7M at 500 compounding at ~1.7% a year on the base against a population growing at ~0.2%; the rate is the teacher surplus, the literate-parent effect, and the new paid uses of reading |
| **Literary** | Composes in the literary language; can hold a clerkship or draft a memorial | **~4.5 million** | ~6% | the clerical establishment of ~430,000 plus the private and commercial scribal trades and the whole examination-attempting layer |
| **Examination-standard** | Full classical and mathematical mastery; could sit and place | **~950,000 living** | ~1.2% | forty years of candidate registrations against cycle pass rates |

*Projection, and it is higher than the audit of 525 allowed.* That audit put the ceiling at twenty-five to forty per cent and rejected four in five. Both are now judged wrong at the tier they were arguing about. **Functional literacy has no brake left except the script's own cost and the reach of the last isolated valleys, and the honest projection is forty-five to sixty-five per cent within two centuries** — not four in five, because the script is a real and permanent tax on entry that alphabetic writing does not levy, but well above the fifth or the third at which societies stop when books are dear and reading pays nothing. Literary competence flattens far lower, at perhaps a tenth to a seventh, because no household can teach it. Examination mastery stays near one to two per cent forever, since it is defined by a competition and not by a skill.

*Consequence for the research figures above, and it is not what one would expect.* The investigation tier stands at 1,600 not because 1,600 is the supply. The supply is the 950,000, and it is rising fast. The tier is the number of **salaried posts the state funds**, and that is the binding constraint — which is exactly why the Bureau's hundred posts mattered out of all proportion to twelve tonnes a year, and why Cheng Bi's best students left for the arsenals when the Ministry of Personnel capped their grade. The empire's limit on investigation is fiscal and institutional, not human. It is producing far more capable minds than it employs, and the surplus is going into teaching, into private mills, and into the merchant houses.

## E. MESSAGES OUT

Three letters. Their answers are not written here.

### E1. To Rome

**To the Emperor of Rome, from the Emperor of the Han, by the Dnieper road, spring of the twenty-second year of the reign (548 AD). Under the standing Agreement, which continues.**

To the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

Twenty-three years ago your court told mine of a crossing of the western ocean: islands six weeks west of the Canaries on a one-way wind, a coast beyond them running more than a thousand miles without a found end, a walled white city of merchants who read and write. You told us before we could have learned it, and told us plainly that you would keep the currents and the reckoning to yourselves rather than let us discover it. You asked nothing.

We owe you a fact of the same kind and send it before you learn it elsewhere.

Before your letter reached us we sent three ships east into our own ocean to learn whether land lay on the far side. Two returned. From ten degrees south to twenty degrees north the wind blows steadily westward and an eastbound ship makes no way against it; that band is closed. At forty degrees north the wind blows the other way, and a ship crossing there reaches a far coast in about five months and returns by descending south and riding the low-latitude wind home. There is land east of us. It is reachable, and only by the high northern latitudes.

We tell you also what we are doing about it, in full, because a court that asks for a rule of restraint while quietly holding ground on the far shore has asked for nothing.

We are surveying the northern coast eastward from our Siberian rivers and northward from the islands beyond Wa. It is slow: we have lost four ships and two hundred and thirty-eight men on that coast, our furthest chart lies six hundred li beyond our furthest post, and our reckoning is that the survey will not reach the far strait for a hundred years, and that we could put no weight on any far shore for a generation after that.

We have also crossed the ocean twice more since, by the forty-degree road, in the years we count 542 to 544 and 546 to 548. Three ships were built for it and all three have come home each time. We have charted some seven hundred li of the far coast about our landfall, which we call Fusang. We have built there a cache of stores in stone, above the tide, with a written board saying what it is and who left it. And in 548 we left twelve men to keep it through the cycle. Our relief sailed this spring and has not yet returned, so at the hour of writing we do not know whether those twelve are living.

That is the whole of it. Twelve men, a store of iron and grain, and a chart. We have refused a settlement, because our treasurer showed the court that a thousand souls fed across that ocean would cost more each year than our entire northern survey. We have forbidden our captains to treat with the peoples of that coast, to settle, to garrison, or to sell iron or powder there on any account, and those orders are in writing and we will show them to your envoy at the Dnieper if you wish to read them.

So we propose nothing about the present beyond what we have just told you. We propose something about a time when neither of us will be alive.

Our two courts have kept a river between us for two hundred years, and it held not because either of us is virtuous but because it was written down early, when neither wanted the far bank badly enough to lie. We propose the same again, before either of us is there at all: that neither empire settle, garrison, or claim ground within some stated distance of the other's established settlements on that continent; that first arrival gives no claim beyond what is actually held and worked; that neither approach the other's positions in force without prior notice by the ordinary channel; and that neither arm the peoples found there against the other. We do not propose a line, since neither of us knows the shape of the land well enough to draw one honestly. We propose the rule by which a line will later be drawn.

We say frankly why. Our court has one old fear, older than anything else in it, of a power growing strong on ground nobody is watching. Seven hundred years ago our founder was surrounded on the northern grass and had to talk his way out; every emperor since has been raised on that story, and we crossed eight thousand li of grassland at ruinous cost so it could not happen again. We know what that fear costs and that it is sometimes foolish. We would rather spend a letter on it than an army, and rather do that with you than with anyone, because you have not once lied to us in two hundred years and we have not forgotten which of us disclosed first.

We have also completed our iron road to the eastern bank of the Dnieper at Anhe, with the yard, cranes and transfer works a junction would require, all of it on our own bank. If your rail is brought to the western bank, silk moves from our wagons to yours without touching the ground, at greater volume and lower cost, and your transit tariff takes its tenth of a larger flow. If you would rather not, the crossing serves our carts as it always has and we shall say no more about it.

If you will not agree to the far-continent rule, say so and we will not press; if you would rather something narrower, propose it. The river between us stands regardless.

We send with this letter, as gifts and not as trade: the pendulum-regulated clock and its method complete, with the caution, learned at our own cost, that it keeps excellent time ashore and none at all on a moving deck; our tables of the sun's angle from our northernmost and southernmost posts, with the circumference we derive from them, near forty-five thousand li in our measure; and our method for making paper from bast and rag, which will cost your presses less than what you use now.

We do not send the tube of two lenses that lets a man see a banner across a valley. We tell you we have one and are not sending it, because we would rather you knew we had kept something than believed we had kept nothing.

We want nothing west of the Dnieper. We have said this for two hundred years and say it again.

### E2. To Egypt

**To the Court of Egypt, from the Emperor of the Han, by the hand of the senior scholar at Aden, autumn of the twentieth year of the reign (546 AD).**

Greeting.

Four years ago our physicians finished the work your scholars began for us. What you gave us was method — the siting of camps, the managing of water, the timing of movement against the season, the closing of a port — and one proposition our physicians had not held before, that particular fevers answer to particular plants and not to regimen alone. What we had to do with it took ten years, because not one of the plants you named grows in our south and none could be carried in any useful quantity. We had to run your principle against our own herbs and find our own answers.

We tell you what we found, since you gave freely. Our agent is the sweet wormwood we call qinghao, and the thing that decided the whole matter is its preparation: boiled, as any physician would prepare a herb, it does nearly nothing, and we nearly abandoned the work on that result. A physician of ours working away from a fire steeped it cold instead, and cold it works. We cannot tell you why. We have forbidden our surgeons to heat it, in the same words we would use to forbid them to poison a man, because we are afraid a cook will improve it. Our second agent is changshan root, decocted in the ordinary way, and it will empty a man violently above the dose so the dose is written down.

It works. In the fourth year of the trial we lost nine men in a hundred where we had always lost fifty or more in a single season, and we now hold a post through the wet season in country that stopped our armies for four hundred years. We tell you this for two reasons. The first is that you gave the knowledge freely and at once, asking only continued access to our optics and our mathematics, and a court that takes a gift of that size and then reports nothing is not a partner but a pocket. The second is that you should know what your gift did, since you may not have intended to hand us a frontier.

We will not pretend to you that it was only a medical matter. It was, for us, the removal of a wall. What we do with the ground behind that wall is not yet decided and we do not undertake to tell you when it is.

We continue to send you what we said we would: our lens geometry and our grinding, our tables and our measured circumference, our clock and its escapement, and now our method for the dense glass we melted at Chang'an in 541, which spreads colour further than ordinary glass for the same bending of light. We have not made a lens of it that corrects colour. If your workshops have, we would rather be told than left to spend another twenty years.

We ask one thing and name it plainly rather than let our scholars circle it. We are building hulls that carry fire under pressure. In 534 a boiler burst at our southern yard and killed nineteen men, among them the only man who understood the whole of it. We have rebuilt the knowledge on paper and we have two vessels working our own coast, and we are certain we are doing several things badly that your yards stopped doing badly generations ago. We ask for what you judge you can give of plate and seam and rivet, of the pressures your yards hold, and of how you test a vessel before you put men in it. We ask for the testing above all. If the answer is no, we will take the no without complaint and our scholars will continue as they are.

And we tell you what we withhold, since you have not asked and will find it out eventually: we do not send you our work on the electrical force, which would show you only how far behind you we are; we do not send our steam hulls or their drawings; and we send nothing of powder, which you have never requested.

The standing question our scholars have carried since the year 425 is unanswered and we put it once more, without expectation: what is it that produces the force continuously, rather than in a single stored burst. Nine thousand recorded failures at Chang'an say we will not find it by looking.

### E3. To the Maurya

**To the Court of the Maurya, from the Emperor of the Han, by the Zhenxi crossing and the ordinary frontier channel, summer of the twenty-first year of the reign (547 AD).**

Greeting.

We write about one thing only, and we write about it because it is cheap now and will not be cheap later.

Both our fleets now carry cannon at sea. Ours are laid to open at long range and our captains have standing orders not to close. Yours, by what our own captains report seeing, are shorter pieces and newer aboard. We say nothing about your gunnery and make no claim about your intentions; we do not know them.

Here is the difficulty. Two fleets that both shoot, in weather, at distances neither crew can judge well, with merchants under escort between them, can begin a war that neither of our courts has decided to fight. In 474 something very like that happened and it cost both of us a great deal. Our own losses that year and the state of our magazines afterward are matters we have not previously discussed with you and do not propose to now.

We therefore propose a convention for encounters at sea, to bind both of us and to be renounceable by either on a year's notice:

- That an armed vessel of either empire approaching a convoy of the other shows its flag and holds beyond two thousand metres unless it intends interception, and that interception is signalled before it is begun.
- That neither empire's ships fire on a vessel that is showing its flag and is neither closing nor interposing.
- That each of us name a port — we propose Panyu and any port you choose — where a commander of the other may send a written account of an incident within ninety days, and that a written account so sent be answered.
- That an incident so reported and answered is not treated by either court as an act of the other's state until both accounts have been read.

We propose no change to the toll, which we pay at twelve parts in a hundred as we have since 477, and we ask no change in what you administer. This is not a treaty about the sea lanes. It is a rule about what our lieutenants may do on a bad morning.

We add one thing plainly, because you will hear a version of it from the carters if we do not. We have this reign increased the powder held at our frontier posts, substantially, and reorganised how those posts are classified. We did this after auditing our own records and finding our arrangements older than they should be. We tell you because you will learn the fact within a season and would otherwise learn it as a secret, and a secret discovered is read as a preparation.

Your cannon stand on the south bank of the Syr Darya where our observers have reported them since the 450s. Ours stand on the north bank. We raised the question of yours in 477 and we are not raising it now. Our position is that the river has held for three hundred years because both of us have found it cheaper than the alternative, and that nothing about the present makes it dearer.

If you will not have the convention, say so and we will not press it. Our escorts' orders stand either way.

## G. SPY TASKINGS

**1. Maurya — ship armament and powder production.**
- *Who:* Sogdian merchant factors and their hired local labour, run through the Office of Frontier Interpretation at Dunhuang under **Kang Mi**. No Chinese officer travels.
- *Cover:* silk and lacquer factors and their agents, and hired dockyard labour, at Bharuch, the Kalinga coast yards, and the Strait ports. No approach to military facilities, arsenals, or powder works.
- *Target:* how many hulls carry cannon and of what bore; whether any are rifled; whether saltpeter has risen in civilian markets, indicating production drawn into state hands; whether naval crews remain short-rationed in drills.
- *Known weakness, entered in the file:* the Sogdians are Maurya subjects and sell in both directions. The court has never priced what they carry out of the empire, and this reign did not price it either.
- *Age:* Sogdians have interpreted for the empire since first contact at the Syr Darya, ~325 years; as a tasked network run from Dunhuang, ~220 years.

**2. Maurya — eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal.** *(new, 546)*
- *Who:* the same Sogdian factors extended south, plus two Yongchang-recruited traders of the Shan highland peoples working the upper Irrawaddy.
- *Cover:* medicinal herb and resin trade out of Yongchang and the plateau; ordinary coastal factoring at the Bengal ports.
- *Target:* whether the Maurya court has noticed Bamo; what presence exists on the eastern shore of the Bay and how far up the Irrawaddy it reaches; whether the river is navigable below Bamo and at what draught.
- *Age:* Sogdian element as above; highland element raised 546, unproven.

**3. Egypt — eyes only, no clandestine work.**
- *Who:* the twenty scholars of the standing exchange, at Aden and Alexandria. *Cover:* none; they are what they are said to be.
- *Target:* boiler practice above all — plate thickness, seam and rivet work, the pressures held and how vessels are tested; and, standing since 425 and unanswered, what produces the electrical force continuously.
- *Limits:* observation of what is shown or publicly visible, and questions asked openly. No infiltration has ever been attempted and none is authorised; a failed attempt forfeits the window itself.
- *Age:* Chinese presence at Aden since ~400; the formal exchange since 425, widened fourfold in 522.

**4. Rome — no espionage.** Reaffirmed. No agent, no cover, no target. The court has just asked Rome for a far-continent rule and told it we are sailing that way; being found in Roman harbours the same decade is worth less than the asking.
- *Overt collection only, declared as such:* frontier trade officials at **Anhe** record rail gauge, sleeper spacing, wheel-flange profile and published freight rates as observed at the crossing — all visible to any merchant on the platform and necessary to working a junction.

**5. Internal — unlicensed powder, and the mills.** *(new, 535, from the Jinyang Report)*
- *Who:* the Censorate's provincial inspectors with officers of the Saltpeter Monopoly Office under the Da Sinong. *Cover:* routine monopoly inspection, which is mostly what it is.
- *Target:* unlicensed saltpeter workings in Bing, adjacent Ji, and the northern beds; and, more delicately, whether the boring machinery, gauges and skilled metalworkers now concentrated in the licensed works of Wu and Kuaiji — the Gu house above all — could produce a cannon or hand-cannon barrel, and whether any has been attempted.
- *Result to 550:* eleven prosecutions for unlicensed saltpeter; no evidence of barrel-making. The court records that as no evidence found, not as no attempt made.
- *Age:* the provincial inspectorate is as old as the dynasty; this tasking is fifteen years old.
