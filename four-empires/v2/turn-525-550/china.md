# THE HAN EMPIRE, 525 TO 550 AD
## The Reign of Emperor Su — Eras Zhengshi (527–539) and Guangding (540–550)

---

## I. The Succession, and the First Act

The old emperor died in the winter of 526 after thirty-one years. His second son took the throne in the third month of 527, aged thirty-one, and reigned the whole of this period; he is called here by his posthumous style, **Emperor Su**.

The Grand Commandant **Huo Sui** had spent the previous reign's last years assembling the argument that the Syr Darya cannon question be settled by force before the Maurya finished arming their hulls. Su received that memorial in his second month and did not refuse it. He referred it, with the whole of the naval and frontier correspondence, to the Censorate, instructing the Censor-in-Chief **Pei Wen** to report not on whether the empire should fight the Maurya but on **what the empire actually knew about whether its own garrisons could hold anything against an enemy with powder.** He had read, in the handover briefing, the passage observing that the revolts of 355 to 360 in Bing province were not a proxy for that question but the actual experiment — run once, a hundred and seventy years earlier, and never re-read as a measurement.

An ordinary emperor could not have opened that archive. The Directorate of the Shangfang, the central weapons administration and the most self-protecting institution in the empire, had held for six centuries that the sufficiency of the garrison was settled doctrine, and no minister who valued his career proposed to audit it. Su could order it because the succession was uncontested, because he had not yet spent anything, and because he framed the order as preparation for the war Huo Sui wanted. Huo Sui could not oppose an inquiry into the readiness of his own frontier. He assented, and by assenting lost control of the answer.

---

## II. The Pei Wen Commission (528–533)

The commission sat six years: eleven officials, four of them examination-trained mathematicians seconded from the Chang'an natural-philosophy circles — itself the innovation, the Censorate never before having put men in a room whose training was in measurement rather than precedent. It held four archives: the Bing province suppression files of 355 to 361 at Jinyang; the Shangfang's powder-issue ledgers for those years at Chang'an; the frontier garrison returns for the Syr Darya and Pontic lines, 450 to 527; and the after-action reports of the naval war of 474 to 477. Its findings are the **Jinyang Report**, submitted the eleventh month of 533.

**First: the posts did not hold.** The received account is that provincial garrisons suppressed a saltpeter-armed revolt in four to six months rather than the customary weeks. The ledgers say otherwise. Of nineteen fortified posts in the affected districts, garrisons of fifty to eighty men, **two were taken by the rebels within thirty-one days and a third abandoned by its commander before assault.** They were recovered eleven weeks later by a field column of four thousand marched from Jinyang. The four-to-six month figure is the duration of that column's campaign, never of a successful defence, and the two lost posts were entered in the returns as "withdrawn for want of powder" — a phrase that passed through six generations of clerks without anyone asking what it meant.

**Second: what it meant was arithmetic.** A fifty-man post carries a standing powder allowance sized against a raid; the Shangfang's schedule, unchanged since 366, provides three to five days of continuous engagement. The doctrine that a small post defeats a large force rests on the attacker being obliged to close and being broken when he closes. An attacker who also has powder is not obliged to close. He stands off and throws charges for as long as he can make powder, which in Bing province, sitting on the northern saltpeter beds, was indefinitely. The post does not fall to assault. It runs dry and is walked into.

**Third: the interior held in 355 because Jinyang was four days away.** Every internal suppression in the empire's history has occurred within a few days' march of a garrison town holding a field force and a magazine, so the model has only ever been tested in the one condition where its failure does not matter. On the Syr Darya the relief column is now four days by rail from **Zhenxi**, which is why that frontier is sound; likewise the Pontic line as far as the rail runs. **Beyond the rail — the Irtysh and Ob posts, the Minusinsk basin, the Amur stations, the whole northern forest — the nearest relief is six weeks to five months.** There the model has never been tested against anything, and the arithmetic says it cannot work.

**Fourth, offered unasked:** the naval files show the same shape — Tidore expended over four fifths of the fleet's carried powder in one afternoon against a fortification with no cannon.

Pei Wen delivered the report in person and did not soften it. Huo Sui asked one question — whether the commission proposed the empire abandon the north — and was told it proposed nothing, having been instructed to measure and not to advise.

---

## III. What the Court Did With It

**The powder-allowance reform (534).** The Shangfang's issue schedule of 366 was replaced. Posts on rail-served lines rose from five days to fifteen; posts beyond rail from five days to sixty, with a hardened magazine to the specification mandatory inside city walls since the fire of the 420s. Carried by Su against the Da Sinong's objection on cost, it is the largest new recurring charge of the reign: roughly **135 tonnes of silver-equivalent annually**, rising to about 160 by 550.

**The doctrine change (535),** possible only because Huo Sui was bought. The frontier is reorganised from a mesh into **nodes and eyes**. Designated *holding* positions — the rail-served forts, the Kazakh cities of **Ningyuan**, **Fuyuan** and **Suiyuan**, **Zhenxi** and its four sister works on the Syr Darya north bank, **Anhe** at the Dnieper railhead, and the commandery seats — are garrisoned at strength, magazined to the new standard, and expected to hold. Everything else — the Irtysh posts, the Ob approaches, **Minusinsk**, the Amur stations, the northern-forest fur posts, and the thin scatter across the Pontic between **Anhe** and **Guangtian** — is redesignated **observation**, with written orders that they are not expected to hold, that commanders are to withdraw on contact rather than stand, and that a post lost by withdrawal is no dishonour to its officer. In six centuries the empire has never issued an order permitting a garrison to leave.

Huo Sui's price was the Syr Darya. He raised the cannon question formally in council in 536 and Su refused him — made easier by the 135 tonnes a year and a doctrine written by his own arsenals, harder by eighty years of the military faction being told the far-bank cannon were an open matter. It cost Su perhaps half of what he had; he spent the rest on the note issue.

**The omission.** The Jinyang Report was not published. A digest circulated to the commanderies as *On the Sufficiency of Magazines*, carrying the reform and the new doctrine with the arithmetic that produced them removed. The full report — the two lost posts of 356 named, the finding that beyond rail the model cannot work — was sealed to the inner council, the Censor-in-Chief, and the Director of the Shangfang.

The reasoning stated in council is the reasoning this court has always used. If it becomes generally known that fifty men with powder cannot be held off by fifty men with powder, the arithmetic is available to every landless tenant in Ji and Yan provinces who can read, and the empire has spent a century making sure most of them can; if it becomes known outside, the Maurya learn what the Syr Darya has never told them. So the empire holds, in three copies, the proof that its internal peace has no physical basis beyond the reach of iron rail, and keeps the proof in a box. The clerk's minute records Su saying that a truth locked in a box is still a truth, and the box does not make it less so, only later.

## IV. Money: The Iron Re-Rating and the Ever-Normal Note

Revenue at 525 stood near 4,050 tonnes with a surplus near 170; the new charges came to something above 400. Four instruments were proposed; three were refused.

The Da Sinong **Sang Ju** proposed reducing the weight of the bronze cash by a fifth, covering all of it in two years. Su refused, on the argument — recorded, and the best single evidence of the man — that the frontier armies, the rail contractors and now the mill houses all reckon in a coin the state issues, and that a state which quietly makes its own coin worth less has begun taking from everyone who trusts it, in a way it cannot stop once begun and cannot see the bottom of. Sang Ju served until 548.

Raising the saltpeter monopoly price was refused because the largest buyer of monopoly saltpeter is the Shangfang; the empire would have paid itself at a loss. A new levy on the mills, proposed by the Excellency over the Masses **Kong Zhan**, was refused not by the emperor but by arithmetic: a fiscal instrument for merchant manufacture would have required the court to define what a mill *is*, obliging the Confucian faction to concede it deserves its own name in law, and Kong Zhan's own party would not carry that. The merchants' framing of 517 — a works is one man with an unusual tool, taxed as any weaver — survived because nobody who disliked it would pay the price of killing it.

**The iron re-rating (535).** The mills must buy iron and machine parts through the state iron monopoly under their licences. The Iron Commissioner was directed to establish a **second schedule of prices for iron sold to licensed works**, at roughly twice the ordinary schedule, on the stated ground that works consume iron in forms — shafting, gearing, bored cylinders, plate — costlier to produce than plough iron. It is not a tax: it creates no instrument, requires no definition of a mill in law, cannot be appealed as an irregular exaction, and rises automatically as the mills grow. It returned about 30 tonnes in 537 and about 95 by 549. **Gu Zan** of Wu protested privately and could not protest publicly, since that would have required stating what the works earn.

**The ever-normal note (538),** on which Su spent his remaining capital. The empire's true reserve has always been grain: a famine buffer of great depth and almost no other use, because grain rots, costs more to move than it is worth, and cannot pay a soldier at **Zhenxi**. The deposit notes in use since the third century are each tied to a particular deposit and a particular pair of offices. Su directed his ministers to make one that is not.

The **changping chao** was authorised in the eighth month of 538: issued only by the Da Sinong, in four denominations, against the certified stock of the ever-normal granaries; redeemable at any commandery granary in grain at the standing ever-normal price or at any commandery treasury in bronze cash; receivable for the land tax, which gave it value at once. Use is restricted by edict to frontier military pay, rail contracts, and magazine stores, and issue capped at **one third of certified granary stock**. Pei Wen made it the condition of the Censorate's endorsement that cap and certification sit in statute rather than regulation, that his own office certify the stock annually, and that a warning stand in the preamble: nothing prevents a later throne raising the cap; the note's worth rests entirely on belief that the grain behind it exists; and a throne in difficulty will find no easier thing to do than issue more.

By 550 the note carries roughly 300 tonnes of annual payments, mostly frontier pay. Mobile resources roughly doubled in eleven years without a new tax, and the two-century fragility — grain-rich and cash-poor, helpless against any shock demanding silver quickly — is materially reduced for the first time since Wu. It is also, as the preamble says, a door opened and not shuttable.

**The registration artifact.** Closing the dependent-registration workaround brought hidden households onto the rolls through the 530s; registered population rises from about 70 million to about 76.5 million. Sang Ju stated in council that perhaps four fifths of that is administrative recovery — the empire has not grown by six million, it has found five million it had lost. Huo Sui's memorials quote the seventy-six million without the qualification. Both men are in the record.

## V. The Mills, the Trunk Line, and the Weavers of Wu

By 550 there are **thirty-one licensed works** in Wu and Kuaiji and the lower Yangtze counties, the largest — the Gu house at Wu — employing somewhat over three thousand hands under one roof. The machinery did not stay with silk: **fourteen of the thirty-one are hemp and ramie cloth works**, the research having been paid for by the silk houses and the machine being faster than a hand at either. They still run on water, and the good river sites are largely taken by 550, which is the specific commercial pressure that makes the rotary steam engine worth buying rather than admiring.

**The southern trunk line.** **Datong** coal country in Bing province south through **Jinyang** to **Luoyang** on the Yellow River, completed 543 — the first rail in the empire built primarily to move fuel rather than powder. **Luoyang** south to the Huai crossing at **Shouchun**, begun 544, roughly two thirds built at 550. The continuation across the Yangtze into the Wu mulberry country is surveyed and not begun; the Yangtze silk houses part-funded the surveyed portion and were named openly in the memorial, concealment inviting a censor's report. Datong coal now reaches the Luoyang and Chang'an arsenals at roughly a third of its former carriage cost, which is much of why the Shangfang absorbed the powder reform without the foundries starving.

**The riot at Wu, autumn 545.** The mills displaced household weavers, numerous in Wu and Kuaiji, who had no name for what had happened to them. In the ninth month a crowd of perhaps four thousand, largely women, moved on the Gu house works, burned two outbuildings and a dyeing shed, and were dispersed by the commandery garrison in a day and a half. Eleven died, nine in a crush at a gate. No firearms were used, on the administrator's own order, and none were held by the crowd.

The administrator was commended for not firing. The Gu house compensated the families within the month, which the court noted it could easily afford. The Ministry of Justice, asked what statute the rioters had offended, found the case did not fit — not tax rebels, not sectaries, not bandits, their grievance against a licensed private undertaking rather than the state — and disposed of it as affray. Kong Zhan's memorial, received and not acted upon, drew the right conclusion: the empire has permitted a thing that can take a livelihood from ten thousand households without any law being broken, and has no category for the resulting people. The court noted with relief that nobody at Wu had powder. Pei Wen minuted that the absence of powder at Wu in 545 tells the throne nothing about its presence at Wu in 575.

## VI. Research: What Answered to Effort, and What Did Not

Heavy money to what answers to effort, a small protected standing charge to what does not, and no pretence that the second is a programme.

**The Bureau of Measured Things (536).** The **Duwu Ju**, under the Ministry of Works, at Luoyang with a second hall at Chang'an: one hundred salaried posts at the eighth and ninth grades, recruited by an examination in mathematics and measurement rather than the classical papers. First Director **Cheng Bi**. It holds the standards of length, weight and time, records and publishes observations, and keeps the failure ledgers of the standing searches. Twelve tonnes a year, and for the first time a man may make a career of investigation without being attached to an arsenal or a mine.

**Rifled naval cannon and breech-loading (both achieved; figures in the research declaration).** The Chang'an arsenal under the Director of the Shangfang **Ma Qian** carried rifling from trial to standard fitting, with refits at **Longbian** and **Panyu** through the 540s, and settled the breech's wedge closure and cartridge seal by 541. The breech-loader is what the doctrine change most depends on: a garrison told to hold sixty days needs to spend less powder per hour to do it.

**Marine steam (achieved late, and it cost lives).** To be worth anything on the northern ice coast the hull needed pressure, which is what the Commissioner of the Southern Yards **Lü Heng** was pushed to find. In the fourth month of **534** a boiler burst during a moored pressure trial in the Gulf at Longbian. **Nineteen men died**, among them the master shipwright **Zhou Yi**, the one man who understood the whole plant and who had kept most of it in his head rather than on paper. The programme did not merely lose a year; it lost the knowledge.

The Censorate opened an inquiry and Huo Sui's party demanded the works be closed, arguing that the empire had a proven sailing navy, no rival that steam would defeat, and no reason to keep killing shipwrights for a curiosity. Su held it, at three prices: every plate, seam, rivet spacing and test pressure written down and lodged with the new Bureau before any further trial; trials run with the vessel unmanned and the boiler worked from shore by line; and the Aden station tasked specifically against Egyptian boiler practice. The result is the **Zhenhai**, completed at Longbian in **546**, and the **Jinghai** in **549**, working the coast between Longbian and Panyu — not trusted for ice, not sent abroad, never shown to a foreign eye. Twenty-four years and 210 tonnes for two coastal hulls and a written specification; Su's assessment is that the specification is what was bought and the hulls are incidental.

**The two open searches (not achieved).** Both continue as standing charges of about three tonnes a year, protected by edict from the annual estimates, all failures recorded by the Bureau.

*Glass.* The standing order to vary the batch produced, at the Chang'an hall around 541, a dense heavy glass from a lead-bearing melt. Cheng Bi's student **Fu Yi** established by grinding that it spreads colour further than ordinary glass for the same bending of light, and wrote in 547 that two glasses spreading colour differently might, shaped against each other, bend the light while cancelling the colour. **No such lens exists at 550.** The material exists, the proposition is written, the work continues.

*The continuous source.* The Chang'an electrical workshop under **Lou Zhen** worked the bounded search: every material known to carry the force, in every arrangement, wetted and dry, paired and stacked, all failures recorded. The ledger holds something over nine thousand entries. **There is no continuous source.** The hand-cranked machine and the storing jar remain the whole of the capability, unchanged from 525. One entry from the eleventh month of 547, made by a junior against paired copper and zinc plates in brine, records a persistent rather than momentary deflection of the indicator; it was not replicated at strength, no senior man has looked at it, and it stands as one unexplained result among nine thousand. The court claims nothing from it. Su's instruction on the estimate for 549 reads that the charge is to be continued and is not to be reported on.

## VII. The Fever Country: Bamo

The largest genuinely new capability of the reign came from Egypt and was not a machine. Egypt's disease knowledge — which fevers answer to which barks, how fever country is crossed and held, how a port is closed against a plague — was worked at Luoyang and Guangzhou through the 530s by physicians of the Imperial Academy, matching the Egyptian plants to what grows in Jiaozhi and Yongchang. By 542 a regimen existed: a bark decoction taken before and throughout exposure, and a camp discipline governing water, siting above the low ground, and the timing of movement against the season.

The empire has been stopped at the **Shan Plateau** for four hundred years. Below it lies the Irrawaddy lowland and beyond that the Bay of Bengal — a road to the western ocean that does not pass the Strait of Malacca and does not pay the Maurya.

The trial, authorised 543, is deliberately small. **Colonel Teng Yin** of the Yongchang command took 400 men down to **Bamo** on the upper Irrawaddy in the dry season of 544, built a stockaded post above the flood line, and stayed, resupplied twice yearly, through 550. Losses over the first two seasons were **eighty-nine dead of sickness, twenty-two per hundred**, against half or more in a single season for every previous Han attempt in that quarter; it fell to about nine per hundred by the fourth year.

The court has adopted Teng Yin's judgement of 549: the lowland is **possible and not yet worth it**. Four hundred men can be held there; forty thousand cannot be supplied there; the river is unmapped below Bamo; the middle-valley peoples have not been approached; the distance to the sea is unknown. What has been bought is the removal of an absolute barrier and the beginning of an argument — a Chinese port on the eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal, the Malacca toll made irrelevant. It is on the table for the first time in six centuries, and Huo Sui's party has taken it up with enthusiasm, which is the strongest reason to be careful with it.

## VIII. The Northern Road, and the Ice at Kuhan

This is what the reign paid for its fear.

The twelve hulls commissioned in the last period went to work in 529. The court had been told in writing, in 525, that the survey needs fifty to seventy-five years, that capability trails survey by a generation, and that the empire would arrive beside an established Rome at the thin end of the longest supply line ever attempted. It funded the programme anyway, then and again now, because the proposition that a rival must not be allowed to grow on ground nobody contests is the oldest article this court holds and it does not lose arguments.

**The southern squadron**, working north from Japanese waters under restaging agreed with the king of Wa at his northern Honshu ports, made the better progress — the coast is worse but the ice is less. It charted **Kuye**, rounded its northern cape by 534, surveyed the **Qiandao** chain through the 530s and 540s, and reached the peninsula the survey names **Kanchajia** by 543, posting **Yanhuo** on its eastern shore in 549.

**The eastern squadron** worked east from the Siberian river mouths under **Feng Ao**, reporting to the Commissioner of the Northern Road **Su Jian**, and is where the money and the men were lost. Posts were established at **Beihai** at the Lena mouth in 531 and **Yanhe** at the Yana in 536, both supplied down the rivers, both marginal. The coast east of the Yana is the worst in the survey's experience: shallow, foggy, ice-choked into the seventh month, no timber. Su Jian's instructions from Luoyang, renewed in 539 and 541, were to press the season — sail earlier, stay later — on the reasoning that every year saved off a seventy-five year survey is a year Rome does not get.

In the summer of **542** Feng Ao took six hulls east past the Indigirka toward the Kolyma. The freeze came early. Four were beset and crushed against the shore ice in the last week of the ninth month; two worked clear and ran west to Yanhe. The crews of the four came ashore near the Kolyma mouth with what they could carry and wintered there. **Two hundred and thirty-eight men died**, of cold, of scurvy, and at the last of starvation. Ninety-one were brought off by the relief in the summer of 543. The place is entered on the charts as **Kuhan**, bitter cold, and a stone was set there in 545 with the names.

Three consequences. The squadron was rebuilt to four hulls rather than six, the yards being unable to replace them faster. The instruction to press the season was withdrawn by edict in 544 and replaced by an order that no hull is to be east of the Yana after the first day of the ninth month, slowing the survey by roughly a third. And Su Jian's recalculation of 546, accepted by the throne, is that the survey reaches the far strait not in two periods but in **three or four** — a hundred years at the pace supply can carry.

By 550 the eastern squadron has charted the coast as far as the great bay east of the Kolyma and posted **Beihai**, **Yanhe**, and a relief station at **Kuhan**: roughly nine hundred kilometres of new coast in twenty-one years, at about 62 tonnes a year, four hulls, and two hundred and thirty-eight men.

Su's edict of 544 states that the men at Kuhan died because Luoyang told them to sail in a month the ice does not permit, that the fault is the throne's, and that the throne will not repeat it. It was published in full, which the Jinyang Report was not, and the difference is the whole of the court's character: a failure that can be admitted without arming anyone is admitted; a failure that would arm someone is sealed.

## IX. The Sea: The Exchange off Malacca, 538

In the second month of **538** a Chinese convoy of nine merchantmen with three escorts, on the Egypt run southwest of the Strait, was approached by a Maurya squadron of eleven. One Maurya hull opened fire with a mounted cannon at roughly 250 metres and missed. The senior Chinese escort, the *Fuyuan*, answered with rifled shot above 800 metres and hulled the same vessel twice. Both disengaged. No Chinese casualties; Maurya losses unknown.

Neither side treated it as war. The Maurya learned that Chinese rifled naval cannon reach roughly three times as far as their own mounted pieces and hit at that range, which is the only fact that has kept the sea lanes quiet since. China learned directly, rather than through Sogdian dockyard talk, that **Maurya ships now carry cannon**, short and smooth-bored, and that their crews shoot badly — the profile of a capability recently acquired and not yet drilled.

The Board of Naval Affairs entered the reading and the throne endorsed it: the range advantage is the whole of the advantage and rests on rifling and barrel steel, which is to say on the Chang'an and Luoyang arsenals and on Kazakh copper and Datong coal, none of which the Maurya copies quickly. It holds perhaps two generations and not three. The response is neither war nor complacency but to keep the gap open — which is why the rifling money was spent and why the Board's recommendation to build twenty further purpose-built hulls was **refused**: twenty hulls would consume the note issue and the powder reform together, and hulls outranged in sixty years are worth less than the arsenals that keep them from being outranged.

The Silk Road toll stands at twelve per cent, unchanged. The Maurya cannon on the south bank of the Syr Darya stand where they have since the 450s; the empire raised the question once, in 536, was refused, and dropped it — as in 477, for the same reason.

## X. Diplomacy

**Egypt.** The scholar exchange runs at twenty per decade both ways, and the disease knowledge has been the most valuable thing any power has given this court. It was tasked more sharply after 534, toward boiler practice, plate, and the working of iron under pressure — knowledge Egypt has and did not refuse. China continues to withhold the electrical work, the steam hulls, and everything of powder, which has never been asked for.

**Rome.** The Dnieper rail junction proposed in the last period was completed in **541**: Chinese rail from **Anhe** now meets Roman rail at the crossing, on the existing agreement's terms and Rome's ten per cent transit tariff. Silk volume roughly doubled in five years — the most profitable single thing either court has done with the other in two hundred years.

Rome is also the recipient of this reign's one formal message. The empire faces on the far shore exactly the situation it has spent seven centuries refusing to face anywhere — a rival growing on ground it cannot deny by presence, because presence is a century away. Su's argument in council was that where the empire cannot deny ground with garrisons it may deny it with a boundary, and that the cheapest boundary in history is one drawn before either party has arrived. He added the part Huo Sui could not answer: that Rome told China of the crossing unprompted, first, and asked nothing, and that a court which repays that with silence and a surveying fleet will find in eighty years that it has taught the one honest power in the world not to be honest with it. The message went west along the Dnieper road in the spring of 548.

**The Maurya.** No message sent, none answered. The relationship is the ledger and the guns, and both are stable.

---

## XI. The Ledger at 550

Registered population about 76.5 million. Economy near 39,000 tonnes of silver-equivalent; revenue near **4,420 tonnes**, extraction around eleven per cent, the growth from the iron re-rating, doubled Dnieper silk volume, Datong coal on rail, and the black-earth counties beginning to pay. Spending: military about 60 per cent, including the powder reform's standing 160 tonnes; rail 9; civil administration 12; water works 10; granaries 4; net surplus about 5 per cent, roughly 220 tonnes, mostly committed. The ever-normal note carries about 300 tonnes of annual payment, at less than half its statutory cap. Investigation tier about 1,600, of whom 100 hold salaried posts in the Bureau; technical workforce above 60,000.

Gained since 525: a working seagoing steam hull and, more to the point, the written specification of one; reliable breech-loaders on the frontier; rifled cannon at 900 to 1,100 metres as fleet standard; a paper instrument that turns dead grain into mobile silver; a road from the coal to the Yellow River; a regimen that lets men live in fever country; a post at Bamo; nine hundred kilometres of new arctic coast and a post on Kanchajia; and a sealed report proving that what the empire has believed for six hundred years is false beyond the reach of rail.

Lost: two hundred and thirty-eight men at Kuhan, nineteen at Longbian, eleven at Wu, four hulls, twenty-one years off nothing, and the last of the innocence with which the north used to be garrisoned.

# DECLARATIONS

## MESSAGE OUT

**To the Emperor of Rome, from the Emperor of the Han, by the Dnieper road, spring of the twenty-second year of the reign (548 AD). Under the standing Agreement, which continues.**

To the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

Twenty-three years ago your court told mine of a crossing of the western ocean: islands six weeks west of the Canaries on a one-way wind, a coast beyond them running more than a thousand miles without a found end, a walled white city of merchants who read and write. You told us before we could have learned it, and told us plainly that you would keep the currents and the reckoning to yourselves rather than let us discover it. You asked nothing.

We owe you a fact of the same kind and send it before you learn it elsewhere.

Before your letter reached us we sent three ships east into our own ocean to learn whether land lay on the far side. Two returned. From ten degrees south to twenty degrees north the wind blows steadily westward and an eastbound ship makes no way against it; that band is closed. At forty degrees north the wind blows the other way, and a ship crossing there reaches a far coast in about five months and returns by descending south and riding the low-latitude wind home. There is land east of us. It is reachable, and only by the high northern latitudes.

We tell you also what we are doing about it, because you would hear it from merchants within ten years. We are surveying the northern coast eastward from our Siberian rivers and northward from the islands beyond Wa. It is slow: we have lost four ships and two hundred and thirty-eight men on that coast, and our reckoning is that the survey will not reach the far strait for a hundred years, and that we could put no weight on any far shore for a generation after that.

So we propose nothing about the present. We propose something about a time when neither of us will be alive.

Our two courts have kept a river between us for two hundred years, and it held not because either of us is virtuous but because it was written down early, when neither wanted the far bank badly enough to lie. We propose the same again, before either of us is there at all: that neither empire settle, garrison, or claim ground within some stated distance of the other's established settlements on that continent; that first arrival gives no claim beyond what is actually held and worked; that neither approach the other's positions in force without prior notice by the ordinary channel; and that neither arm the peoples found there against the other. We do not propose a line, since neither of us knows the shape of the land well enough to draw one honestly. We propose the rule by which a line will later be drawn.

We say frankly why. Our court has one old fear, older than anything else in it, of a power growing strong on ground nobody is watching. Seven hundred years ago our founder was surrounded on the northern grass and had to talk his way out; every emperor since has been raised on that story, and we crossed eight thousand li of grassland at ruinous cost so it could not happen again. We know what that fear costs and that it is sometimes foolish. We would rather spend a letter on it than an army, and rather do that with you than with anyone, because you have not once lied to us in two hundred years and we have not forgotten which of us disclosed first.

If you will not agree, say so and we will not press; if you would rather something narrower, propose it. The river between us stands regardless.

We send with this letter, as gifts and not as trade: the pendulum-regulated clock and its method complete, which your navigators will find of use; our tables of the sun's angle from our northernmost and southernmost posts, with the circumference we derive from them, near forty-five thousand li in our measure; and our method for making paper from bast and rag, which will cost your presses less than what you use now.

We do not send the tube of two lenses that lets a man see a banner across a valley. We tell you we have one and are not sending it, because we would rather you knew we had kept something than believed we had kept nothing.

We want nothing west of the Dnieper. We have said this for two hundred years and say it again.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Maurya — ship armament and powder production.**
- *Who:* Sogdian merchant factors and their hired local labour, run through the Office of Frontier Interpretation at Dunhuang under **Kang Mi**. No Chinese officer travels.
- *Cover:* silk and lacquer factors and their agents, and hired dockyard labour, at Bharuch, the Kalinga coast yards, and the Strait ports. No approach to military facilities, arsenals, or powder works.
- *Target:* how many hulls carry cannon and of what bore; whether any are rifled; whether saltpeter has risen in civilian markets, indicating production drawn into state hands; whether naval crews remain short-rationed in drills.
- *Age:* Sogdians have interpreted for the empire since first contact at the Syr Darya, ~325 years; as a tasked network run from Dunhuang, ~220 years.

**2. Maurya — eastern shore of the Bay of Bengal.** *(new, 546)*
- *Who:* the same Sogdian factors extended south, plus two Yongchang-recruited traders of the Shan highland peoples working the upper Irrawaddy.
- *Cover:* bark and resin trade out of Yongchang and the plateau; ordinary coastal factoring at the Bengal ports.
- *Target:* whether the Maurya court has noticed Bamo; what Maurya presence exists on the eastern shore of the Bay and how far up the Irrawaddy it reaches; whether the river is navigable below Bamo and at what draught.
- *Age:* Sogdian element as above; highland element raised 546, unproven.

**3. Egypt — eyes only, no clandestine work.**
- *Who:* the twenty scholars of the standing exchange, at Aden and Alexandria. *Cover:* none; they are what they are said to be.
- *Target:* boiler practice above all — plate thickness, seam and rivet work, the pressures Egyptian yards hold and how they test them; and, standing since 425 and unanswered, what produces the electrical force continuously.
- *Age:* Chinese presence at Aden since ~400; the formal exchange since 425, widened fourfold in 522. No infiltration has ever been attempted and none is authorised; a failed attempt forfeits the window itself.

**4. Rome — no espionage.** Reaffirmed. No agent, no cover, no target. The court has just asked Rome for a far-continent treaty and told it we are sailing that way; being found in Roman harbours the same decade is worth less than the asking.
- *Overt collection only, declared as such:* frontier trade officials at **Anhe** record Roman rail gauge, sleeper spacing, wheel-flange profile, and published freight rates at the junction — all visible to any merchant on the platform and necessary to working the junction.

**5. Internal — unlicensed powder, and the mills.** *(new, 535, from the Jinyang Report)*
- *Who:* the Censorate's provincial inspectors with officers of the Saltpeter Monopoly Office under the Da Sinong. *Cover:* routine monopoly inspection, which is mostly what it is.
- *Target:* unlicensed saltpeter workings in Bing, adjacent Ji, and the northern beds; and, more delicately, whether the boring machinery, gauges and skilled metalworkers now concentrated in the licensed works of Wu and Kuaiji — the Gu house above all — could produce a cannon or hand-cannon barrel, and whether any has been attempted.
- *Age:* the provincial inspectorate is as old as the dynasty; this tasking is fifteen years old and has produced eleven prosecutions for unlicensed saltpeter and no evidence of barrel-making.

## TERRITORY AT 550 — WHAT IS HELD THAT WAS NOT HELD AT 525

**Pontic black earth, east of the Dnieper (newly settled and registered):**
- **Heitu** county, on the Vorskla — seat established 533, first tax return 537.
- **Guangtian** county, on the Donets — established 536.
- Two further registered counties on the Anhe–Guangtian rail corridor, 541 and 546, not yet separately named in the returns.
- Registered settlers at 550: approximately 41,000, funded from the existing estate-tax frontier-settlement earmark redirected from the Kazakh programme.

**New rail (territory made holdable, not newly claimed):**
- **Datong – Jinyang – Luoyang** trunk, completed 543.
- **Luoyang – Shouchun** (Huai crossing), ~two thirds complete.
- **Anhe – Dnieper junction** with Roman rail, completed 541. *(For the ledger: Chinese and Roman iron physically meet here. Both flags stand at the same crossing, on the standing Agreement, by consent.)*

**Northern Road — eastern squadron (east from the Siberian river mouths):**
- **Beihai**, at the Lena mouth, established 531. Held.
- **Yanhe**, at the Yana mouth, established 536. Held.
- **Kuhan** relief station, near the Kolyma mouth, established 545 on the site of the 542–543 wintering and the memorial stone. Held, marginally.
- Coast charted east of the Yana as far as the great bay beyond the Kolyma, ~900 km. Not held; observation only under the doctrine of 535.

**Northern Road — southern squadron (north from Japanese waters):**
- Western and northern shores of **Kuye** charted, northern cape rounded 534. Watering station only, no garrison.
- The **Qiandao** chain northeast of Kuye charted through the 530s and 540s.
- **Yanhuo**, eastern shore of **Kanchajia**, established 549. Garrison 40. The empire's easternmost position in the world.
- *Note:* restaging through northern Honshu ports was **with the assent of the king of Wa**, a tributary courtesy. No Japanese territory is claimed, garrisoned or administered. Kuye and the Qiandao were found under no state's authority.

**Southern frontier — the fever country:**
- **Bamo**, stockaded post on the upper Irrawaddy below the Shan Plateau, established 544, held continuously through 550. Garrison 300, resupplied twice yearly from Yongchang. **The first Han position ever held beyond one season in tropical lowland country.** No claim below Bamo.

**Redesignated, not lost, no longer expected to hold** *(node-and-eye doctrine, 535)*: the Irtysh posts, the Ob approaches, the **Minusinsk** basin colony, the Amur river stations, the northern-forest fur posts, and the scattered Pontic posts between Anhe and Guangtian. All remain occupied and administered; all now carry written orders to withdraw on contact rather than stand.

**Nothing was taken from any power. No territory was lost.**
