# The Maurya Realm, 525 to 550 AD

## Part XIII: The Reign That Held the Reins and Did Not Drive

---

### The Ruler and the Inheritance

The exceptional ruler who had raced for the southern continent died in 525, and the throne passed to a man the realm's historians assess at forty-nine.

He was not a bad man. He purged nobody, took no favourites worth naming, and did not steal. He was also not a ruler who could see a board. He inherited a commitment larger than any in the realm's recorded history — three hundred and fifty founded places on a continent nobody in the council chamber had seen — and had neither the conviction that began it nor the hardness to end it. So he kept every line where his predecessor had put it, changed no standing order, promoted and destroyed no one, and let the realm run on a dead man's momentum until the momentum ran out.

What he inherited:

- A treasury reserve at or near nothing, the southern venture having consumed some 6,250 million paṇas by draining it to the floor.
- A granary reserve at roughly a third of establishment, because grain not bought is a saving no ledger records and pressed treasurers go there first.
- An infrastructure line cut from 228 million to about 150 million, the reduction falling on the newest and least-defended works: the flood embankments and canals of the Tigris and Euphrates valley, the road patrols of the Mesopotamian trade run, the roads of the Iranian plateau and Sogdia, and the tanks, wells and seed-distribution of the dry Deccan and the northwest.
- A famine relief line cut from 46 million to about 25 million.
- An armament line at 230 million, doctrine-locked with the advisory council behind it.
- A standing directive that the first marine steam engines go to the southern route before the navy.
- A formal court finding that Egypt's guidance on the galvanic force had been sabotage.
- And an assessment submitted to the council in 525 observing that the reign just ended was the first in seven hundred and eighty-five years to close without a reckoning of the realm's numbers. He did not read it. Nobody made him.

---

### The Southern Program Under a Man Who Did Not Believe In It

He did not stop the southern program, because stopping it meant declaring his predecessor's great work a folly and fighting the interest that had grown around it in twenty-five years: the Tamil and Bengal shipowners living on convoy charter, the shipwright families transplanted to the northern yards, the Malay and Sumatran pilot houses, the temple foundations endowed at the new ports, and the founding families, who had already disobeyed the throne once and been ratified after the fact.

He did not reinforce it either, because money could only come from the army, the research line, or the temples. So the 250-million line stayed where he found it, and less and less of it arrived at the coast.

**Where it went instead.** By 540 the assessors at the Strait could account for perhaps 175 to 185 million actually landing on the continent as ships, stores, iron, tools and wages. The difference went three ways: the swelling of the program's own establishment at Pataliputra, the Tamil ports and the Strait, which grew from a hundred and forty officials to over five hundred without any increase in what it moved; the ordinary peculation of a fund disbursed five thousand miles from any inspector, which nobody measured because nobody was sent; and, in 529 and 530, a deliberate diversion of two ocean hulls out of the season's convoy to a purpose that had nothing to do with the south at all.

**The convoy.** The standing seasonal convoy of forty ocean hulls out of Nagapattinam and the Sri Lankan ports fell to about thirty by 535 and twenty-two by 545. Hulls lost were not replaced. Ten were wrecked across the reign, six on the reef coast in the northeast, which is the plainest evidence that the pilotage of that shore was never learned before settlements were placed on it.

**The northwest chain, which is where the rival comes.** The arid western shore had been lined at intervals of roughly two hundred and fifty kilometres with fifty-man posts, kept alive not by the land but by water and grain delivered on a fixed schedule. When the convoy thinned, the schedule failed first at the ends of the chain, because that is how a schedule fails.

Of fourteen posts on that shore at 525:

- **Pūrvasthāna**, the original secret depot in the sheltered sound behind the islands of the far northwest, still stands at about seventy men — the only real work on that coast.
- **Aśvatīrtha**, at the seasonal river mouth two hundred and fifty kilometres south of it: thirty-one men in 550.
- **Śuṣkakūla**, on the iron-red coast further south: forty.
- **Vātamukha**, at the long cape jutting westward into the ocean, was found in 546 by a relief ship to be held by nine men and a Malay water-carrier, the rest dead or gone inland.
- **Antyaśilā**, the southernmost post, at the great double bay of the west coast, was abandoned by its own garrison in 544; they went north and were absorbed into Śuṣkakūla, and the post was struck from no list.
- Four further posts between these were abandoned across 543 to 547 the same way: the men left when the ship did not come, and the ledger went on carrying them at fifty each.
- Five more have had no visit from any Maurya hull in four years and are carried at founding strength. The court does not know whether they exist.

So the coast the realm spent three years' revenue to claim before Egypt could reach it is held at 550 by roughly a hundred and fifty men across two thousand kilometres, in five places, with the ledger showing seven hundred in fourteen — precisely the Spice Island error, counting the establishment and not the graves, repeated on a larger coast by an administration that had written the lesson down and filed it.

**The fertile east and the north.** The forty-odd major anchors did not all survive contact with ground the survey had never charted.

- **Suvarṇatīra**, the great sheltered harbour on the middle of the east coast, is the success: the southern seat, eleven thousand people at 550, a working yard, a stone fort at the harbour mouth, its own grain from the river flats, a hinterland pushed forty kilometres inland.
- **Kāntitīra**, on the great enclosed bay near the continent's southeastern corner, is the second: good soil, cold clean water, four thousand people, too far down the coast for the convoy to reach reliably. It feeds itself and builds its own small craft.
- **Nadīmukha** and **Vārigrāma**, river mouths north and south of Suvarṇatīra, hold at eighteen hundred and nine hundred. **Prabhāsa**, on the wide shallow bay north of Nadīmukha, silted its anchorage within a decade — placed by men reading a survey of a different coast — and is a village now, its stone carried to Nadīmukha.
- **Śilātaṭa**, on the reef coast in the far northeast, was the reign's outright loss: wet, fevered, behind an uncharted reef, three ships lost approaching it and more men buried than landed. Abandoned 541 by an order the ruler signed without discussion.
- **Uttaradvāra**, at the river mouth on the north coast west of the deep gulf, is the northern gate and the true yard of the continent, the monsoon forests behind it giving timber the arid west never could. It builds hulls, and buries men at a rate the capital's returns do not show. **Śaṅkhamukha**, on the eastern shore of the deep northern gulf, and **Trikūṭa**, on the eastern side of the long northern cape, are fever stations holding the claim and watching the passage to the Spice Islands.
- **Madhyatīra**, at the river mouth on the mid-west coast, and **Kṣāratīra**, on the long southern shore facing the empty ocean, are the overflow settlements sent to indifferent country: small, neither growing nor dying. **Himatīra**, on the southeastern harbour of **Śītadvīpa**, holds sixteen hundred and has extended along that coast.
- **Nīlaparvata**, on the south coast of the great mountainous island north of the continent, is the second loss. The fever is worse than at Uttaradvāra and the interior peoples numerous and unwilling. Its garrison of six hundred stood at ninety-one effective in 548. It has not been formally abandoned. It has been abandoned.

### The Fire-Setters

The previous reign's chronicle spoke of raising settlements "on ground no Maurya hand had worked before." That was true of Maurya hands. It was not true of the ground.

The mainland has people on it, in numbers the realm cannot count, living without kings, fields, towns, or anything the realm's statecraft is built to bargain with. Our people call them the **Agnikāra**, the fire-setters, from what struck the first settlers most: they burn the country deliberately, in patches and in season, and the burnt ground draws game and comes back green. Our officers read this first as war-signalling, then as waste, then not at all.

The realm's method for taking a reluctant people is seven centuries old and it is economic: make cooperation pay better than resistance, buy the leaders, tax the trade, leave the faith alone. At Suvarṇatīra and along the rivers behind Uttaradvāra that method failed for the first time in our history, for a reason the council never understood. There was no treasury to enrich, no hierarchy to buy, no trade to tax, no single man whose word bound a hundred others. We offered iron and cloth and got curiosity, then exchange, then nothing, because the offer assumed a want the Agnikāra did not have.

Having no instrument, the settlements used the only other one. The hinterland behind Suvarṇatīra was enclosed for grain and stock, the river flats behind Uttaradvāra likewise, and the water at Kāntitīra was taken. Enclosure of water in a dry country is war whether or not it is declared. There was skirmishing from about 533: never a battle, always a killing — a shepherd speared, a camp burnt in answer, a well fought over. The musters record ninety-odd settlers and soldiers killed across the reign. They do not record the other side and no one asked them to.

And the pox went through the Agnikāra. Our settlers carry the old Ayurvedic guard as a matter of course before sailing. The Agnikāra had never met the disease. It ran through the river peoples behind Suvarṇatīra in 536 and along the northern rivers in 539 and 545, and the physicians recorded the dying carefully and did not connect it to the ships, because the realm knows how to guard against the pox and does not know what the pox is. What the settlers reported was that the country was emptying, and what they concluded was that the land had been meant for them. The court entered it in the register as favourable news about the availability of ground.

This is set down because a later reign will want to know when it began.

### The Coffee Voyage, and the Closing of the African Shore

The realm has hunted the coffee plant for a hundred and eighty years. It is not strategic. It never was. The court cannot let it go.

In 529 the commandant at Oman, acting on a standing search order nobody had ever cancelled, authorized a voyage. Two ocean hulls were taken out of that season's southern convoy — the only deliberate theft from the south in the reign. They went under a Tamil master out of Nagapattinam long resident at Oman, **Ilaṅkōvan**, who had worked the African run for eighteen years and knew its coves, its currents, and the trick of passing a guarded harbour mouth by night. Cover was ordinary: a licensed trading voyage of the kind Maurya hulls had made along that shore by right for four centuries, carrying Omani copper, dates and Gujarati cloth. The target was the river mouths of the Horn country that climb inland toward the high ground where the bean was believed to grow, and the plan had been argued at Oman for a decade: land a small party at night below the guarded ports, walk inland with hired guides, buy living plants and unroasted seed, be gone before a patrol closed.

The party of eleven landed in spring 531 at a river mouth south of the last harbour any Maurya captain had entered. It got perhaps six days inland and was taken by men who were not the local people and who were waiting — which the survivors' account makes plain and the court has never wanted to discuss. Ilaṅkōvan waited nine days offshore, was closed on by two patrol craft, ran, lost his second hull, and reached Oman in 532 with forty-one men and no plants.

Egypt's answer came in 533 and it was not a protest but an administrative act. The permission standing since the first meeting at Aden five hundred years before — trade as you please, hold no base, claim no ground — was withdrawn along the whole East African shore. Every Maurya hull was turned from every Egyptian-held harbour from the strait to the far southern limit. Not fought; turned.

The cost, which the assessment of 525 did not price:

- **Revenue.** The African and eastern-approaches traffic out of Oman and the Gulf was worth 55 to 70 million paṇas a year to the treasury in port dues, transit charges and landed-goods duties, and several times that to the merchant houses of Gujarat, Oman and the Tamil coast. It went to nothing inside two years.
- **The eyes.** The agents who detected the Egyptian fleet's provisioning were factors, harbour clerks and chandlers' men resident in those ports. When the trade stopped, the residence stopped. The network that gave the previous reign its one great intelligence coup was dismantled in eighteen months, by us, for nothing, in pursuit of a shrub. The realm now has no eyes on the African coast, the Red Sea, or Egypt's yards, and cannot say whether the great fleet ever sailed.
- **The precedent.** Egypt has demonstrated that it can strip the realm of a five-hundred-year access by writing an order, and that the realm's answer will be a letter.

The court's reckoning, entered 534, was that the commandant at Oman had exceeded his warrant. He was removed and given a smaller post. Nobody asked who had left the search order standing for a hundred and eighty years, because the answer was everybody.

### The Egyptian Finding, Acted Upon

The previous reign had run a botanical programme on Egypt's counsel — that the galvanic force be sought in the realm's own growing things — and concluded, when twenty-five years produced no electrical theory from it, that the counsel had been sabotage. This reign acted on the finding, which is what findings are for.

In 535 the plant-path programme was closed as tainted, its botanists dispersed back to the medical houses of Pataliputra, Taxila and Ujjain, its apparatus broken up. A standing instruction went to every envoy and every factor holding a diplomatic warrant: no technical guidance offered by Egypt is to be accepted, acted upon or carried home, and any such offer is to be declined with courtesy and reported.

What that cost:

- The plant path had been feeding the wire path a second confirmation from an entirely different angle — tissue answering a live current, gas at the electrodes, metal laid down on metal. That cross-check ended, and the wire researchers slowed measurably in the following decade.
- The distillation-grade extract work, which produced the realm's antiseptics and its sedative-analgesic finds for surgery, sat inside the plant programme's establishment and went down with it. The materia medica has not advanced since 535, and two inoculation candidates under trial were abandoned unfinished.
- The realm has closed by written order the only channel through which any power ahead of it has ever handed it a working pointer.

The council debated two sittings. The argument against closure was made — that Egypt's anchor claim, that the torpedo fish's shock is the same force, is true and verifiable by any man with a net, and a saboteur has no reason to hand a rival a truth he can check; that the plant work produced real phenomena, which false theories do not usually do; and that the error is asymmetric, being fooled costing a share of one programme while refusing costs the channel forever. It was heard and it lost, for the reason such arguments lose at Pataliputra. The court has been walled out of the western sea for a hundred and eighty years, has just had its African trade struck out by an Egyptian clerk's order, and has eleven men in an Egyptian prison. It could not, in that room, in that year, find in favour of Egyptian good faith.

The finding was recorded as prudence. It was temper, and the realm's own assessment had predicted in writing, six months earlier, that it would be temper. Predicting a mistake is not the same as avoiding one.

---

### The Monsoons of 537 to 541

The monsoon was weak in 537 and weaker in 538, adequate in 539, and failed badly in 540 and 541 across the Deccan plateau and the central lands. No single season was of a kind the realm had not seen; it had ridden out exactly this in the fourth century on granaries and relief grain moving along the roads.

The granaries were at a third.

**What held.** The African grains — the sorghum, millet and cowpea gathered by accident from the failed coffee hunt a century before and bred by the farmers of that coast for drought and heat and thin soil — did what they were brought for, wherever they had actually been distributed. The northwestern provinces, the Punjab dry country and the western Deccan around the old iron districts had received seed and instruction in the 480s and 490s and came through with reduced harvests and no starvation. It is the best return the realm has ever had on a failure.

**What did not.** The seed-distribution to the eastern Deccan, the central plateau and the Godavari uplands sat in the agricultural works line, cut in 528 to help pay the southern convoy. Those districts entered 540 sowing what their grandfathers had sown. They failed.

**The reckoning.** Across four years the land tax fell by 130 to 210 million paṇas a year in the affected provinces — remitted, uncollectable, or uncollected because the collector could find no one. Relief actually moved was about 40 million a year against a need the provincial assessors put at three to four times that. People moved off the worst land toward the Godavari and Krishna valleys, toward the Tamil ports, and in a thin steady stream the southern program was glad of and did not advertise, onto the convoy ships. The registered population, which has risen in every reckoning since the founding, is entered at 550 at **ninety-six million** — the first fall in the realm's registers in seven hundred and eighty-five years, and not caused by war.

The realm takes a sixth to a quarter of every harvest, and the whole justification for that is that the state returns it as granaries, canals, wells and relief when the season turns. This reign kept the extraction and had already spent the return. The farmers of the central plateau paid the land tax of a state that had promised them grain and shipped the grain money to a continent they had never heard of.

### The Coin

By 539 the ruler faced arrears he could not carry: the southern line, the army at 930 million, the research line the council would not let him touch, and four provinces remitting tax. He would not cut the army, because that meant the generals; nor research, because that meant the council; nor the south, because that meant his predecessor's memory and every interest around it; and he would not raise the tax on provinces that were starving.

So the mints at Pataliputra and Ujjain reduced the silver in the paṇa by about one part in eight, quietly, without proclamation, in the ordinary course of recoining.

Merchant houses weigh coin, and detected it in under two years. By 543 the Rome-road traders at Ctesiphon and along the Euphrates run were pricing in Roman coin or demanding silver by weight; by 546 the Sogdian caravan factors were doing the same. The realm's credit instruments held, being drawn on named houses and not on the throne, but they now carry a discount against the coin. The reign traded a portion of the paṇa's eight-century reputation for perhaps 90 million a year of temporary relief, and entered it as a technical adjustment of the standard.

---

### Mesopotamia, Rome, and the Treaty Nobody Read

The road patrols along the Mesopotamian stretch of the Rome route sat in the infrastructure line cut in 528. Waystations went unrepaired, patrols went out at half strength, and the banditry the great reign of the previous century had thinned came back. By 542 Roman merchants were shying from the crossing and complaining to their own officials, and the complaint came to us from Rome, courteously, twice. The realm answered with words, appointed a commissioner, gave him no money, and the raids continued.

Mesopotamia did not revolt, and the reason is not to this ruler's credit: the previous century bound it by making it the richest transit ground on the most profitable road in the world, and the second capital on the Tigris makes the province feel governed rather than occupied. That binding is now strained from three directions — the road is less safe, the coin is worse, and the flood embankments are unrepaired. The Tigris has not risen high in this reign. It will.

**The treaty.** The overland agreement with Rome — twelve parts in the hundred through each empire's ground, fixed twenty-five years and renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gives notice — reached the end of a span at the close of this reign. The realm has never established, in two hundred years, when notice must be given, to whom, or through what channel. The frontier officials at the Euphrates report that none was received; the court has read that as renewal on the old terms and entered it so. This is precisely the reading habit that once read China's sea treaty as implying a toll and a port of call its words never contained, and the realm was wrong then.

The realm sent Rome no message this reign. It sent its message to Egypt instead, for reasons of feeling, and left the more important relationship to run on paper nobody had read.

---

### China Left Undisturbed, and What the Watchers Saw

The previous reign made silence toward China a deliberate act of state. This reign continued it by not thinking about it, which produces the same behaviour for none of the same reasons.

The Syr Darya was quiet for twenty-five years. The fifty to eighty fixed cannon stand where they were placed, in sight of China's watchers, at the cost of a garrison establishment of five hundred thousand men, six thousand elephants and a hundred thousand horse facing a river across which nothing has happened in three hundred and twenty-five years. Every loss the realm suffered this reign came at sea, in Africa, in the granaries, or in the mint.

The watchers reported three things. Chinese hulls continue to work the passage south of the Strait into the archipelago east of Sumatra, unseen by our facility and paying nothing, as they have since the 470s. The Spice Island garrisons are starving on the vine, supplied by the southern road and suffering every thinning of the convoy: Ternate at about seven hundred effective against an establishment of twelve hundred, Banda at three hundred against seven hundred, harbour powder for perhaps thirty shots. They remain the realm's because China has not chosen to take them.

And, unresolved: in spring 543 the watch at Trikūṭa, on the eastern side of the long northern cape, sighted two ships of unfamiliar build standing offshore for the better part of a day and then working away northeast. The watch had no spyglass — the brass-bound glasses went to the home and Gulf squadrons and never to the southern stations — and could not count masts or read the rig. The report reached Pataliputra in summer 544, fourteen months late, because the optical signal relays that carry word across the realm in a day stop at the Strait and everything beyond moves at the speed of a convoy. The ruler was asked whether a vessel should go and look, and answered that the season's hulls were all committed. That is the whole of what the realm knows.

---

### The Research Programmes, Which Are the One Thing That Went Right

The previous reign set the armament line at a full tenth of revenue and put the advisory council behind it, so that a later ruler minded to cut it would have to fight an institution rather than sign an order. This ruler was exactly the man that friction was built against. He looked at the line twice, in 531 and in 540 when the famine bit; both times the council refused and both times he let it go rather than have the argument.

The line held near 230 million through the whole reign and did not fall in the famine years when everything else did. It is the only bequest of the great reign to survive this one intact, and the strongest argument in the realm's history for building friction into institutions rather than trusting the man on the throne.

**The firearm and the powder.** The castings and locks were never the constraint; the powder was, as it has been for two hundred and seventy-five years. New works were built in the saltpeter country of the Gangetic plain at three sites and at Ujjain, slowly, because their capital competed with the southern line and lost. By 550 the arsenals hold about fifteen thousand finished one-man pieces and four thousand crew-served. Powder output sustains perhaps six to seven thousand men in drill and use; the rest stand in store beside magazines that are not full. The realm can arm a corps. It cannot arm an army, and no minister has said aloud that fifteen thousand firearms against an establishment of one million and eighty-four thousand is a rounding error with a doctrine attached.

**Steam.** The standing directive sent the first marine engines to the southern route ahead of the navy and was never revisited, so they went on going there. By 550: about nineteen engined hulls, fourteen on the Strait-to-Uttaradvāra and Strait-to-Suvarṇatīra runs where a hull that ignores the monsoon is worth three that wait on it, five with the home and Gulf squadrons. They burn charcoal, which nobody costed. At Uttaradvāra the monsoon forest is being cut for it faster than the yards cut for timber, and in the heartland the yard and mine engines now compete for charcoal with the iron smelters of the Deccan.

**The galvanic wire.** The wire path lost its cross-check in 535 and slowed, and still produced the reign's one genuine advance. Copper was drawn to fine wire by the Rajasthan zinc and brass workers, who had the nearest craft to it; insulation was worked out with wax, lac and gum, badly and then adequately. In 546 the researchers at Pataliputra ran a current down an insulated wire from one building of the school to another, some four hundred paces, and made a needle deflect at the far end reliably enough to pass agreed letters one at a time. In damp it fails entirely. It is not a network and is not within a generation of one. It is the first message the realm has ever sent without a rider, and the men who did it were not rewarded, because this ruler did not reward people.

**Paper and printing.** Paper plus the Brahmin compact spread the press further than any previous reign at little cost: administrative printing at twenty-eight or thirty provincial centres, cheap Brahmin-sealed texts of law, medicine and canon. The famine registers of 540 were the first printed identically across four provinces, which allowed the realm to establish with precision how much grain it did not have.

**Medicine.** Washing protocols spread with the printed texts and recoveries improved and nobody knows why. The materia medica stopped in 535.

**Optics.** No advance, no programme. The spyglass is unimproved since the 490s. The realm cannot grind well enough to see the small and has not asked anyone to try.

**Naval cannon.** Held rather than grown: about forty-eight cannon-armed vessels against thirty to forty at 525, crews better drilled, powder still short.

---

### What Was Not Examined

The elephant corps stands at six thousand, costs twenty-four million a year, and was not discussed. The mahout families are hereditary, the corps is the oldest pride in the realm, and no man assessed at forty-nine opens that question. That elephants panic under gunfire as horses do, that we put a firearm into our own infantry's hands this century, and that we handed the complete construction of that firearm to Rome — an empire on our Euphrates border — for an engine, was raised in one written assessment in 525 and in no council since.

The colonial ledgers were not audited. In 547 a naval writer of the Nagapattinam establishment, **Kannan**, went out with the convoy to regularize the accounts at Suvarṇatīra. He was given a house, a clerk, a share in the harbour dues and a wife's family, and stopped sending returns after his second season. No one was sent to find out why. That is the entire history of southern audit in this reign.

Which brings the record to the man who now governs the southern continent. **Vīrasena**, whose father was one of the founders who took ship for better ground in the last reign and reported it as accident afterward, has been senior officer at Suvarṇatīra since 534. He is able. He was neither promoted nor removed in sixteen years, which is what able men receive in a reign like this. When the convoys thinned he stopped waiting for Pataliputra: he opened Uttaradvāra and Suvarṇatīra directly to Malay, Sumatran and Bugis shipping without routing them through the realm's facility at the Strait, levied his own harbour dues, and kept the receipts on the continent to pay his yards and garrisons.

This was reported to the capital in 547 as a temporary expedient. In 548 the ruler ratified it after the fact, exactly as his predecessor had ratified the island-seizures, because refusing meant funding the south properly or losing it, and he would do neither.

At 550 the southern continent is substantially self-financing, keeps accounts that do not reconcile with Pataliputra's, builds its own hulls from its own timber, feeds itself from its own grain, reproduces the realm's caste order without reference to the realm, and pays the throne nothing. It is twenty-five years old. It has a competent man at its head who has been ignored for sixteen years, an interest that has twice disobeyed the throne and twice been ratified, and the route to the Spice Islands that Pataliputra depends on and does not control.

Nobody at court has said the word.

---

### The Reign at Its Close

The heartland is untouched. The subcontinent whole, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Bactria and Sogdia, Iran, the Caucasus highlands and Mesopotamia to the Euphrates are exactly as they were in 525, same administration, same tax, same undefeated army. Not one square kilometre of the continuous dominion was lost. The realm's own file predicted this — that the integrated heartland survives any reign and the newest ground goes — and it was right, and being right is no comfort.

What went was everything acquired since the fourth century that had not yet been made part of the realm: the African trade and the eyes that came with it, half the northwest claim, two settlements outright, the foothold on the great northern island, the credibility of the coin, the safety of the Rome road, the granary reserve, the materia medica, two million registered people, and the throne's actual authority over a continent that is still, on paper, entirely its own.

The ruler died in 550. His historians will assess him at forty-nine and note that he did no harm, using a definition of harm his farmers would not recognize.

# DECLARATIONS

## MESSAGE OUT

**To Egypt. Sent 534. Carried by sea from Oman to Aden under a herald's flag, delivered through the Egyptian officer commanding at the strait, translated by a Sogdian and a Yemeni interpreter in company.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Sovereign of Egypt, greeting.
>
> A vessel bearing our flag put men ashore upon your coast in the spring of the year before last, below the harbours where our ships have traded by your permission for five hundred years. Those men went inland without your leave. They were taken and they are in your hands.
>
> We say plainly what our own inquiry found. The master sailed upon an old standing order of our station at Oman which no living minister had reviewed, and he exceeded any warrant of ours. The officer who let that order stand has been removed. We did not send those men to take your ground and we do not claim it. Eleven men walked inland to buy a plant. That is the whole of the offence, and we dress it as neither more nor less.
>
> We ask two things.
>
> The first is our men — seamen, a physician's assistant, one interpreter. Return them and we count the matter closed, and we will set a heavier hand upon our own captains than you could set upon them.
>
> The second is the trade. For five centuries, since our ships and yours first met at your strait, the understanding has been the one you set: our merchants trade through your ports, hold no ground, claim no ground. We have kept it. We have built no base upon your coast and taken no harbour from you. Eleven men and a shrub have now closed to us every harbour from your strait to the southern limit, and that is a heavier answer than the fault. We ask that the old permission stand again upon your own old terms.
>
> In return we offer what is ours to offer, and it is not small. Egyptian shipping entering the Gulf at our station of Oman, and Egyptian shipping using our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait between the peninsula and the great island, shall pay no port due and no transit charge for twenty-five years, on the same footing as our own hulls, our harbour officers instructed accordingly. We have never made that offer to any power, and we do not make it to China.
>
> One further matter, set down so there is no misunderstanding later. In past years your people have offered our scholars guidance upon questions of natural philosophy, and ours have been grateful for it. Our court has determined that the realm will henceforth pursue such questions by its own scholars, in its own houses, upon its own reasoning, and our envoys are instructed to accept no such guidance in future. We say it to you directly rather than let your people learn it from a closed door. It touches nothing of the trade, and we would not have you read into it more than we mean.
>
> Send us your answer by the same hand.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. The Sogdian caravan channel — measuring the northern route.**
- **Who:** Nanaivandak, a Sogdian silk broker of Samarkand, third generation of a family that has carried our messages and goods since the second century; paid a retainer against a share of the transit toll he helps assess.
- **Cover:** none required; he is what he appears to be, a broker whose business is knowing where caravans go.
- **Target:** volume, route and cargo of the Chinese caravans on the northern steppe road that bypasses the realm — how much Silk Road revenue is actually lost and whether the loss still grows.
- **Network age:** 325 years, the oldest we hold. Its loyalty runs to the trade, not to us. The tasking asks the network to do only what it does anyway, which is why a mediocre reign approved it.
- **Result at 550:** steady and probably honest. Northern-route volume still rising slowly. Silk Road tolls confirmed at about 245 million against a peak of 320.

**2. Egypt — attempted reconstruction after the African expulsion.**
- **Who:** Dhanapāla of Bharukaccha, factor of a Gujarati gem and cloth house, resident at Alexandria; from 541 a second man, a Yemeni named Sa'īd, at the strait ports.
- **Cover:** ordinary resident commercial factors under the Mediterranean trading permission, which the African closure did not touch.
- **Target:** whether the great fleet detected provisioning before 525 ever sailed; when, with what, westward or southward. Secondarily the war harbour and the yards.
- **Network age:** the Alexandria merchant presence dates from the embassy of 365–367, so 185 years — but it has never been a service. It is merchants reporting what merchants see, has never produced anything of military value, and is all we now have, because the African network that produced the fleet report existed through residence in ports we may no longer enter.
- **Result at 550:** nothing. Dhanapāla reports harbour traffic, prices, and that he is watched. The yards remain closed. **The realm cannot say whether the Egyptian fleet sailed.**

**3. Rome — the treaty and the new continent.**
- **Who:** Mar Isḥaq, a deacon of the Christian community of Ctesiphon, and two men of his congregation.
- **Cover:** churchmen travelling to the Christian communities of Roman Syria, a traffic that crosses the Euphrates constantly and lawfully and which our rule of religious tolerance has always permitted without inquiry.
- **Target:** whether Rome has given or intends notice on the overland treaty; where its overseas colony lies and what it produces; whether Rome has fielded the one-man firearm whose construction we gave it in the last reign.
- **Network age:** new, founded 542, untested — our first use of a religious minority as a conduit.
- **Risk the council did not weigh:** if discovered, we have made spies of our own Christian subjects in the one province we spent three centuries persuading that its faiths are safe under Sanskrit administration, and handed Mesopotamia the grievance force never gave it.
- **Result at 550:** no notice detected. Rome's colony placed vaguely "beyond the western ocean," uncheckable. On the firearm, Mar Isḥaq reports Roman soldiers at the Syrian stations carrying arms he cannot describe. We do not know what we gave away.

**4. The southern continent — the audit that was not.**
- **Who:** Kannan, naval writer of the Nagapattinam establishment, sent with the convoy of 547.
- **Cover:** none; he travelled openly as an inspector of accounts.
- **Target:** the ledgers of Suvarṇatīra and the northern stations; the true establishment of the northwest posts.
- **Network age:** none. There has never been an intelligence establishment on the southern continent. The informer networks that watch every governor in the old dominion were never extended across the ocean, because the settlements were founded as a program and not as provinces.
- **Result at 550:** housed, married into the harbour interest, given a share of the dues, ceased reporting after his second season. Nobody was sent after him.

## RESEARCH

Line held at approximately 230 million paṇas a year for twenty-five years, about 5,750 million in total, defended twice against the throne by the advisory council. Establishment approximately 28,000 to 29,000 trained researchers and 45,000 to 60,000 craftsmen and bonded labourers, less the botanical staff dispersed in 535.

| Programme | Spend across the reign | What we believe we achieved |
|---|---|---|
| Gunpowder manufacture and firearm production | ~1,900M (incl. four new powder works) | ~15,000 one-man firearms and ~4,000 two-man crew-served pieces made. Powder output sustains only 6,000–7,000 men in drill. Production capacity, not design, remains the wall, as it has for 275 years. |
| Marine steam | ~1,150M | ~19 engined ocean hulls; 14 committed to the southern route by a standing directive nobody revisited, 5 to home and Gulf squadrons. Fuel unexamined; charcoal supply now competing with iron smelting. |
| Galvanic (wire path) | ~950M | 546: current carried down insulated drawn copper wire between two buildings at Pataliputra, ~400 paces, needle deflection at the far end, agreed letters passed one at a time, unreliable in damp. First message sent without a rider. Not a network. |
| Galvanic (plant path) | ~180M, then **closed 535** | Closed on the sabotage finding. Cost: the wire path's independent cross-check, the distillation-grade extract programme, and two unfinished inoculation candidates. |
| Naval cannon and carriages | ~700M | ~48 cannon-armed vessels, up from 30–40. Crews improved. Powder short. |
| Paper and printing | ~340M | Administrative and Brahmin-sealed sacred printing at 28–30 provincial centres. First identically printed relief registers, 540. |
| Medicine | ~330M | Washing protocols spread by printed text; recoveries improved; cause unknown. Materia medica halted 535. |
| Ballistics, metallurgy, mathematics, sundry | ~200M | Incremental. Mathematics continues toward the study of continuous change without state direction. |

## TERRITORY AT 550

**Unchanged and entire:** the subcontinent and Sri Lanka; Afghanistan; Bactria and Sogdia to the Syr Darya; the Iranian plateau; Armenia and Azerbaijan; Mesopotamia to the Euphrates; Oman; the tributary ports of the Gulf's Arabian shore; eastern Yemen; the Strait of Malacca and the realm's port at its eastern mouth. No part of the continuous land dominion was lost or gained.

**GAINED since 525 — all on the southern continent and its islands, all by enclosure and force, none by treaty:**

- **Suvarṇatīra** (great sheltered harbour, middle of the east coast): hinterland extended ~40 km inland along the river behind the harbour. ~11,000 people, stone fort, working yard, own grain.
- **Kāntitīra** (great enclosed bay near the continent's southeastern corner): ~4,000 people; farmland and water taken inland ~25 km.
- **Uttaradvāra** (river mouth, north coast, west of the deep northern gulf): principal yard of the continent; river flats enclosed; timber cut inland.
- **Himatīra** on **Śītadvīpa** (cold island across the short crossing past the south coast): extended along the southeastern coast; ~1,600 people.
- Minor extension at **Nadīmukha** and **Vārigrāma** (river mouths north and south of Suvarṇatīra).

**LOST or abandoned since 525:**

- **Śilātaṭa** (reef coast, far northeast): abandoned 541.
- **Prabhāsa** (wide shallow bay north of Nadīmukha): anchorage silted; reduced to a village, stone removed.
- **Nīlaparvata** (south coast of the great mountainous island north of the continent): 91 effective of 600 in 548. Not formally abandoned. Abandoned.
- **Antyaśilā** (southernmost post of the arid west coast, at the great double bay): abandoned by its garrison 544.
- **Four unnamed posts** of the northwest chain between Śuṣkakūla and Antyaśilā: abandoned 543–547; still carried in the establishment.
- **Five further posts** of the northwest chain: no hull has called in four years. Carried at founding strength. Existence unknown.
- **East African trade access** (not territory): the five-hundred-year permission to trade at every harbour from the Red Sea strait to the southern limit, withdrawn by Egypt 533.

**HELD BUT NOT CONTROLLED:**

- **Ternate** (~700 of 1,200) and **Banda** (~300 of 700): supplied by the southern road; powder for ~30 shots; ours only because China has not chosen otherwise.
- **The southern continent as a whole:** garrisoned, settled, self-financing, keeping accounts that do not reconcile with Pataliputra's, levying its own harbour dues on Malay and Sumatran shipping since 547 under an arrangement the throne ratified after the fact in 548, and remitting nothing to the treasury.

**SIGHTED AND NOT INVESTIGATED:** two ships of unfamiliar build, standing off **Trikūṭa** (eastern side of the long northern cape) for the better part of a day in spring 543, working away northeast. Reported to Pataliputra summer 544. No vessel was sent.
