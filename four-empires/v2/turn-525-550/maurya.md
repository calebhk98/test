# The Maurya Realm, 525 to 550 AD

## Part XIII: The Reign That Held the Reins and Did Not Drive

### The Ruler and the Inheritance

The exceptional ruler who raced for the southern continent died in 525, and the throne passed to a man our historians assess at forty-nine. He purged nobody, took no favourites worth naming, did not steal, and could not see a board. He inherited a commitment larger than any in our recorded history — three hundred and fifty founded places on a continent nobody in the council chamber had seen — with neither the conviction that began it nor the hardness to end it. So he kept every line where his predecessor had put it, changed no standing order, promoted and destroyed no one, and let the realm run on a dead man's momentum.

What must be said at the outset, because our own audit of 525 got it wrong and the error shaped everything that document concluded, is that the momentum did not run out. It was never going to. The realm's finances were sound when he inherited them and were sounder when he died.

**The audit of 525 was wrong about the money.** It reasoned from a single year's snapshot — a surplus of 112 million paṇas against a southern commitment of 250 — and concluded that the reign of 500–525 must have run 178 million a year of unfunded spending, emptied the reserve, and quietly gutted infrastructure and famine relief to pay for it. It then built a famine, a population collapse and a debasement on top of that conclusion. Every step of that chain fails at the first step. The 112-million surplus of 500 was not a ceiling on what the realm could spend; it was what the treasury happened to bank in a year when nobody had asked it for anything. The realm's revenue is near twenty-three hundred million paṇas and it commits under half of that to the army — the largest charge it has. The southern programme was funded out of a rising revenue, a real surplus, and trims to the household and temple lines, and the reserve was not touched. There were no concealed cuts. The granaries stood at establishment. The audit had assumed a fiction and then reasoned very carefully from it, which is the characteristic vice of this court and appears in our own record as often as in our enemies'.

What he actually inherited:

- A treasury reserve of about 1,450 million paṇas, six-tenths of a year's revenue — slightly *up* on 500, the southern programme having been carried on income.
- Granaries at establishment, and a famine-relief line of 46 million standing where it had stood for a century.
- An infrastructure line at 228 million, unimpaired.
- An armament line at 230 million, doctrine-locked with the advisory council standing behind it.
- A standing directive sending the first marine steam engines to the southern route before the navy.
- A formal court finding that Egypt's galvanic guidance had been sabotage.
- A written assessment submitted in 525 that told him most of what was wrong with the realm and was wrong itself about the money. He did not read it, and no one made him.

### The Money, Which Is the Correction This Record Exists to Make

The realm takes in more than it spends and always has. The pattern of eight centuries is a treasury whose army takes forty parts in a hundred, whose administration and works take another twenty-five, and whose remaining quarter is divided among religion, household, schooling, relief, coinage, diplomacy, and whatever the reign has decided to attempt. A quarter of twenty-five hundred million paṇas is a very large sum of money, and a reign that attempts nothing banks it.

**Revenue.** The old trades went on ebbing as they had since 450 and the base went on growing faster.

**The southern programme inside a healthy budget.** It stood at 250 million a year at 525 and fell to 105 by 550, not because anyone cut it but because founding costs more than keeping, and because the continent increasingly paid for itself. Fifty harbours and three hundred stations had been *built*; what remained on the treasury's account was the seasonal convoy, garrison pay, and the last directed foundations. Averaged across the reign it ran near 170 million a year, some 4,250 million in all. Set against an average revenue of about 2,590 million, that is one part in fifteen of the realm's income — a large commitment, the largest single discretionary line the realm carries, and not remotely a strain. The venture that our own audit called "nearly three years of the whole realm's revenue" was, across two reigns, about four years of the *army's* budget, spread over fifty years, on a continent.

**And the falling line is not a falling colony, which the treasury has confused and this record will not.** The appropriation fell because Pataliputra's share of the work fell. Everything the appropriation stopped paying for went on happening, paid for by the continent, by the settlers, and by the shipowners, and it happened faster in the second half of the reign than in the first. A treasury line is a measure of what a throne is doing. It is not a measure of what a colony is doing, and after about 540 the two figures move in opposite directions.

**What remained genuinely free.** After every standing commitment including the south, the reign banked between 20 and 130 million a year, rising as the southern line fell. Roughly 400 million of that went to one-off capital charged outside the standing lines — the machinery of six new powder mills, the eastern coal works and their drainage engines, forty new ocean hulls. The rest went to the reserve.

**The reserve.**

The realm reached the prudent target of one year's revenue in reserve for the first time since 400, and it did so under a mediocre ruler, by the simple mechanism of a rich state governed by men who attempted nothing new. **That is the true indictment of this reign and it is a fiscal one only in reverse.** A great reign would have found 2,750 million of banked silver an opportunity. This one found it a resting place. The realm ends the quarter-century richer than it has ever been and not one position better placed.

**And no, we did not debase the coin.** The paṇa left this reign at the weight and fineness it entered it. There were no arrears. The mint superintendents recoined at standard, as they have for eight centuries, and the merchant houses of Gujarat, Ctesiphon and Samarkand priced our silver at par throughout.

### The Southern Programme Under a Man Who Did Not Believe In It

He did not stop it, because stopping meant declaring his predecessor's great work a folly and fighting the interest grown around it in twenty-five years: the Tamil and Bengal shipowners living on convoy charter, the shipwright families transplanted to the northern yards, the Malay and Sumatran pilot houses, the temple foundations endowed at the new ports, and the founding families, who had already disobeyed the throne once and been ratified. Nor did he shape it. He signed the standing appropriation twenty-five times.

So the programme ran itself, and — funded — it mostly worked.

**The convoy** held at forty ocean hulls out of Nagapattinam and the Sri Lankan ports through 540, then rose to fifty-two as the engined vessels came in and the southern yards began launching their own. Ten hulls were lost across the reign, six of them on the northeastern reef coast, which is the plainest evidence that the pilotage of that shore was never learned before settlements were placed on it. From 544 two survey hulls were assigned to nothing but learning that reef, and by 549 there was a chart.

It also stopped being one convoy. An engined hull does not wait on the monsoon, and from about 542 the run south was made twice in a year instead of once and in either season, which halved the time a settler's passage tied up capital and doubled what a hull could earn on the route. That is why the Tamil and Bengal shipowners, who began the venture as requisitioned men grumbling at charter rates, ended it competing for berths. By 546 more than half the people carried south went as private passengers on private bottoms at their own or a relative's expense, and the treasury's convoy carried soldiers, iron, powder and officials. **The realm stopped paying to populate a continent somewhere around 545 and did not notice, because the line it watches is the appropriation and the appropriation is not the thing.**

**Some 130,000 people were landed on the southern continent across this reign**, against 150,000 to 200,000 across the whole of the last one — and the last one was the great directed effort of an exceptional ruler, while this one was a signature repeated twenty-five times and a shipping market left alone.

**The arid northwest, which is where a rival would come.** Fourteen fifty-man posts at intervals of roughly two hundred and fifty kilometres along the western shore, kept alive by water and grain on a fixed schedule at a running cost higher per post than anywhere in the programme, precisely because the land gives them nothing. The schedule was met. Twelve posts stand at or near establishment at 550; two — **Antyaśilā** at the great double bay and one unnamed post north of it — were consolidated into their neighbours in 543 on an assessor's recommendation that the intervals were closer than the coast required, and the men were moved, not lost.

The important thing done on that coast was not a post. In 538 the throne's assessors finally asked the question nobody had asked in a hundred and twenty-five years: the survey of 415 charted the *arid northwest*, and the realm had spent two reigns garrisoning a shore on the strength of it — but nobody had ever run the coast **south** of Vātamukha to see where the arid country ended. Two hulls out of Pūrvasthāna did so in 539 and 540. The arid country ends. Below it, at the continent's southwestern corner, lies a river mouth in a country of winter rain and dry summers, well grown and well watered, unlike any ground the realm had charted on that ocean.

**Paścimadvāra**, the western gate, was founded there in 542 on a hinterland of real farmland — the first major settlement the realm has planted on the shore that faces Africa, and the only thing on that whole coast that is a colony rather than a watch-post. It stood at about twenty-five hundred people at 550 with grain of its own, a stone work at the river mouth, and orders to grow.

**The fertile east and south, where the throne stopped mattering.** The forty-odd major anchors did not fare alike, and the pattern of which grew and which did not has nothing to do with the plan and everything to do with the ground, which is the whole lesson of this section.

**Suvarṇatīra**, the great sheltered harbour on the middle of the east coast, is the southern seat and the reign's great fact: about **sixty-two thousand people** against eleven thousand at 525. A stone fort at the harbour mouth, three yards launching ocean hulls from local timber, its own foundry from 544, grain and cattle from the river flats, and an enclosed hinterland pushed a hundred and forty kilometres inland. It has thrown off two daughters that no one in Pataliputra ordered, approved, or was told of until afterward:

- **Godhūmavāṭa**, on the tableland behind the coastal escarpment, found by graziers walking their stock over a pass in 536 in search of summer feed and settled without warrant. Wheat and cattle country, about **seven thousand** at 550, now feeding Suvarṇatīra rather than the reverse. The first Maurya settlement anywhere in the world founded by nobody, on the authority of nothing, and the fastest-growing place the realm holds.
- **Lavaṇatīra**, a salt-pan and fishing town south along the coast, about **three thousand five hundred**.

**Kāntitīra**, on the great enclosed bay near the southeastern corner, has the best farmland the continent has shown and has gone from four thousand to about **thirty-one thousand**, with wool becoming its trade — the western Deccan sheep took to that climate as nothing else has. It has thrown off **Śvetaśaila**, up the great river running inland from the bay, about **four thousand five hundred**, grain and grazing.

**Nadīmukha**, the river mouth north of Suvarṇatīra, was a village of two thousand and is a town of about **nine thousand**, for a reason nobody planned: coal was cut from the sea-cliff there in 543, of the same kind as the eastern seams at home, and it now bunkers every engined hull working the southern road and feeds the Suvarṇatīra foundry.

**Himatīra** on **Śītadvīpa**, the cold island, has gone from sixteen hundred to about **nineteen thousand** — the largest proportional growth on the continent — on wheat and barley that do not wait on a monsoon, timber, and the sealing trade of the southern water. It has thrown off **Uttarakūla** on the island's north coast facing the crossing, about **three thousand**.

**Paścimadvāra**, founded 542 on the fertile southwestern corner, stands at about **six thousand** in eight years, the fastest founding in the programme's history and the only one placed on ground that had actually been surveyed by the men who chose it.

**Dvigartā**, at the head of the twin gulfs, founded 546, about **two thousand two hundred** and rising.

**Uttaradvāra**, the northern gate and principal yard, has grown from four thousand to about **thirteen thousand** and is the one growth the record should not be proud of. It grows because the yard needs hands and the hands must be replaced; the monsoon fever kills at a rate the capital's returns do not show, because the returns count establishment and the graves are dug locally. Its true intake across the reign was near twice its increase.

**Stagnant:** **Vārigrāma**, south of Suvarṇatīra, about **two thousand six hundred**, a poor hinterland behind a fair harbour. **Prabhāsa**, whose anchorage silted and which was moved three kilometres in 544 at a cost that would have bought a proper survey twice over, about **fourteen hundred** and going nowhere; it is the monument to placing a harbour from a chart of a different coast. **Trikūṭa**, the northern watch station, about three hundred, which is what it is for.

**Reduced or abandoned:** **Śilātaṭa**, on the reef coast in the far northeast, abandoned 541 — wet, fevered, behind an uncharted reef, three ships lost approaching it and more men buried than landed; the order was signed without discussion. **Nīlaparvata**, on the great mountainous island north of the continent, cut in 547 from settlement to coastal trading post of about four hundred: fever worse than Uttaradvāra's, and interior peoples numerous, organised and unwilling. The realm did not admit that as a defeat and it was one.

**The rest is countryside, and it is most of it.** Behind and between the named towns lie the surviving lesser stations of the original three hundred, the pastoral runs pushed inland from every eastern harbour, the river farms, the timber camps, the salt works and the outstations, and this is where two-thirds of the realm's people on that continent now live. The enclosure of the interior was not decided anywhere. It happened at the rate at which stock walks.

**Total Maurya population of the southern continent and its islands at 550: on the order of four hundred thousand**, against perhaps a hundred and thirty-five thousand alive there at 525. About a hundred and sixty-seven thousand in the named towns; the balance rural. Some hundred and thirty thousand were landed across the reign; the remainder is births, on a young frontier population with land, meat and grain in quantities no cultivator of the Gangetic plain has ever seen, less the fevers of the north. **The realm's fastest-growing population by a very wide margin is one no minister has visited.**

**The continent's own money.** By 540 the settlements were victualling themselves, building their own hulls, and selling timber, grain, wool, hides, coal, sandalwood and iron into the Malay and Sumatran trade. **Vīrasena**, senior officer at Suvarṇatīra since 534 — whose father was one of the founders who took ship for better ground in the last reign and reported it as accident afterward — opened Uttaradvāra and Suvarṇatīra directly to Malay, Sumatran and Bugis shipping without routing them through our facility at the Strait, and levied his own harbour dues. He did it not from want, the convoys being funded, but because it paid. Reported as expedient in 547 and ratified after the fact in 548, exactly as the predecessor ratified the island-seizures. He also, without any authority whatever, began issuing land grants in the interior in his own name from about 541, which is the act that made Godhūmavāṭa and the runs behind it lawful in the only court that sits within four thousand kilometres of them — his own.

In 549 the continent remitted twenty-two million paṇas to Pataliputra, the first coin it has ever sent home, and kept the rest. At 550 the southern continent is self-financing, four hundred thousand strong and doubling in a generation, keeps accounts that do not reconcile with Pataliputra's, builds its own hulls, feeds itself, mines its own coal, grants its own land, reproduces our caste order without reference to us, and pays the throne a token. It is twenty-five years old, with an able man at its head neither promoted nor removed in sixteen years, an interest that has three times acted without warrant and three times been ratified, and it now holds the route by which the Spice Island garrisons are fed. Nobody at court has said the word.

### The Fire-Setters

The previous reign's chronicle spoke of raising settlements "on ground no Maurya hand had worked before." That was true of the hands, not of the ground.

The mainland has people on it, in numbers we cannot count, living without kings, fields, towns, or anything our statecraft is built to bargain with. We call them the **Agnikāra**, the fire-setters, from what struck the first settlers most: they burn the country deliberately, in patches and in season, and the burnt ground draws game and comes back green. Our officers read this first as war-signalling, then as waste, then not at all. The one settler who wrote that the burning is a form of husbandry and that the country we found was a made country, not a wild one, was a surveyor at Kāntitīra whose report went into the geographic archive and was not circulated.

Our method for taking a reluctant people is seven centuries old and economic: make cooperation pay better than resistance, buy the leaders, tax the trade, leave the faith alone. It failed for the first time in our history, for a reason the council never understood — no treasury to enrich, no hierarchy to buy, no trade to tax, no man whose word bound a hundred others. We offered iron and cloth and got curiosity, then exchange, then nothing, because the offer assumed a want they did not have.

Having no instrument, the settlements used the only other one. The hinterland behind Suvarṇatīra was enclosed for grain and stock and pushed to a hundred and forty kilometres; the tableland beyond the escarpment was taken outright by graziers who asked no one; the river flats behind Uttaradvāra likewise; the water at Kāntitīra and Dvigartā taken — and enclosure of water in a dry country is war whether or not it is declared. There was skirmishing from about 533 and it did not stop: never a battle, always a killing. The musters record two hundred and sixty settlers and soldiers killed across the reign. They do not record the other side and no one asked them to.

**And the growth is what did it.** A directed programme can be halted by an order from Pataliputra. Stock walking inland behind a frontier town of sixty thousand people cannot be halted by anything, because there is no one to order and nothing to order them to stop doing. The enclosure of the Agnikāra's country in this reign was not policy, was never debated, was never costed, and was three times the enclosure of the reign that was policy. This is the part of unsupervised colonial growth the record must not present as a triumph.

And the pox went through them. Our settlers carry the old Ayurvedic guard as a matter of course before sailing; the Agnikāra had never met the disease. It ran through the river peoples behind Suvarṇatīra in 536, along the northern rivers in 539 and 545, and through the southwest within three years of Paścimadvāra's founding. The physicians recorded the dying carefully and did not connect it to the ships, because we know how to guard against the pox and do not know what the pox is. What the settlers reported was that the country was emptying, and what they concluded was that the land had been meant for them. The court entered it as favourable news about the availability of ground.

### The Coffee Voyage, and What Happened on the African Coast

The realm has hunted the coffee plant for a hundred and eighty years. It is not strategic. The court cannot let it go.

In 529 the commandant at Oman, acting on a standing search order nobody had ever cancelled, authorized a voyage. Two ocean hulls under a Tamil master out of Nagapattinam long resident at Oman, **Ilaṅkōvan**, who had worked the African run eighteen years and knew its coves, its currents, and the trick of passing a guarded harbour mouth by night. Cover was ordinary: a licensed trading voyage of the kind our hulls had made there by right for four centuries, carrying Omani copper, dates and Gujarati cloth. The target was the river mouths of the Horn country climbing toward the high ground where the bean was believed to grow; the plan, argued at Oman for a decade, was to land a party at night below the guarded ports, walk inland with hired guides, buy living plants and unroasted seed, and be gone before a patrol closed.

The party of eleven landed in spring 531 south of the last harbour any Maurya captain had entered, got perhaps six days inland, and did not come back. What the surviving boat crew reported is that the party was taken by men who were not the local people and who appeared to be waiting — an account the court has never wanted to examine, because the obvious reading of it is that our preparations at Oman were known before the hulls sailed, and that reading points at our own harbour. Ilaṅkōvan waited nine days offshore, was closed on by two patrol craft, ran, lost his second hull, and reached Oman in 532 with forty-one men and no plants.

**What our own records then show, and nothing more than that.** From the summer of 533 the reports of our factors on that coast change. At Aden and the strait ports our hulls were told there was no berth and no water for them. Further south, at harbours where Maurya ships had watered for four hundred years, masters report being met at the harbour mouth and turned; some report being permitted to sell and not to land; some report nothing, because after 534 they stopped sending. Six of our resident factors between the strait and the far southern limit closed their houses and came home in 534 and 535; four more simply ceased to correspond and we do not know what became of them. By 536 the African run out of Oman had fallen from some ninety sailings a season to eleven, and by 540 to none that we can trace. Port dues, transit charges and landed-goods duties on that traffic, worth 55 to 70 million paṇas a year to the treasury and several times that to the merchant houses of Gujarat, Oman and the Tamil coast, are gone.

**The court's reading, marked as a reading.** The council concluded in 534 that Egypt had struck out the five-hundred-year permission in answer to the shore party, and it has proceeded on that conclusion ever since. It should be said plainly that we do not know this. No Egyptian order was ever shown to us, no Egyptian officer ever stated a policy to a Maurya master, and no message came to Pataliputra. What we hold is eleven men who did not return, a scatter of harbour refusals over three seasons, factors who left or fell silent, and a trade that ended. Those facts fit an Egyptian decision taken in Alexandria; they fit as well an order given by a district officer on the coast where the trespass happened; they fit port officers applying standing rules harder than before, as port officers do after an incident; they fit a general change in Egyptian port administration that has nothing to do with us; and they fit several of these at once. The court weighed none of these because it had spent a hundred and eighty years learning to read every closed harbour on that shore as a personal insult, and by 534 it did not require evidence to do so.

**The memorandum of 541, which is in the file and was never read to the council.** One man did the work. **Bhūtiśarman**, deputy factor of the Oman establishment, was set in 540 to closing out the African accounts, and in doing so collated the logs of every master who had made the run since 530 — sixty-one voyages — and wrote up what they actually said. His finding, submitted to Oman in 541 and forwarded to Pataliputra where it was filed under commercial correspondence:

> - The refusals were **not uniform and not simultaneous**. Of thirty-one attempted callings in 533 and 534, eleven were turned outright, nine were permitted to sell from the boat and not to land, seven were watered and served as before, and four were charged dues at rates the masters called new and did not call refusal. Two masters were turned at one harbour and received courteously at the next in the same week.
> - The refusals were **heaviest within four hundred kilometres of the place the eleven men went ashore** and thinned steadily with distance from it, north and south alike. At the far southern limit the change is barely detectable in the logs at all.
> - **Nothing was announced.** No master in sixty-one voyages was read an order, shown a document, or told that a permission had ended. Every one of them was told something by a harbour officer, and the somethings do not agree with each other.
> - **Our Alexandria and Mediterranean houses were never interrupted, not for a day**, and are not interrupted now. A sovereign that had struck out our trading permission in answer to an offence would be unlikely to strike it out on one coast and leave it standing on another, in the same year, in the same empire.
> - **Most of the collapse is ours.** The insurers of Bharukaccha and Nagapattinam raised the premium on the African run by half after Ilaṅkōvan lost his hull, raised it again in 534, and refused the risk entirely in 535. After that no master would offer and no house would lade. Of the fall from ninety sailings to none, Bhūtiśarman reckons that fewer than twenty a season were ever actually turned away, and the rest simply stopped being attempted. **The gate we describe as shut was, on this evidence, mostly a route our own merchants decided was not worth the trouble.**

He drew the conclusion the logs support, which is that what our ships met was local, piecemeal, and an officer's reaction to a trespass on a coast his garrison polices, hardening into habit and then into our own withdrawal — and that if this is right, the trade could very probably be recovered by a factor, a season, and a cargo, and does not require a sovereign at all.

Nothing was done with it. The finding of 534 was settled, the council had voted on it, and the standing instruction refusing all Egyptian dealings had been issued in 535 on the strength of it. A memorandum that unsettles a decided question must be carried to the throne by someone, and no one carries such a thing to a ruler who will not thank him for it. **The realm holds, in its own archive, at Pataliputra, the material for a different answer than the one it acted on, assembled by its own clerk, in its own hand, nine years before the reign ended. It is entered here so that the next reign can find it.**

What is not in doubt is the cost to us:

- **Revenue.** 55 to 70 million a year, absorbed inside a rising total and therefore never felt at court, which is exactly why nobody examined it.
- **The eyes.** The agents who detected the great provisioning of 500–505 — the intelligence coup that set the whole southern venture in motion — were factors, harbour clerks and chandlers' men resident in those ports. When the trade stopped the residence stopped, and the network went with it inside eighteen months. We now have no eyes on the African coast, the Red Sea, or any Egyptian yard. **We cannot say whether that fleet ever sailed, where it went, or whether it existed at the strength our rumours gave it.** We destroyed our own instrument for answering the question our whole strategy rests on, in pursuit of a shrub.
- **The precedent, correctly stated.** Not that a rival can strip us of a five-hundred-year access by writing an order — we do not know that any order was written. The precedent is that our access to that coast rested on a permission we never made binding, that it could end without anyone telling us why, and that we had no means of finding out.

The court's reckoning, entered 534, was that the commandant at Oman had exceeded his warrant; he was removed and given a smaller post. Nobody asked who had left the search order standing for a hundred and eighty years, because the answer was everybody.

### The Egyptian Finding, Acted Upon

The previous reign concluded, when twenty-five years of botanical work on Egypt's counsel produced no electrical theory, that the counsel had been sabotage. This reign acted on the finding, which is what findings are for. In 535 the plant path was closed as tainted, its apparatus broken up, and a standing instruction went to every envoy and factor holding a diplomatic warrant: no technical guidance offered by Egypt is to be accepted, acted upon or carried home.

What that cost: the plant path had been feeding the wire path a second confirmation from an entirely different angle — tissue answering a live current, gas at the electrodes, metal laid down on metal — and that cross-check ended. The wire researchers lost perhaps three years to its absence and said so. And we have closed by written order the only channel through which any power ahead of us has ever handed us a working pointer.

What it did not cost, because the money was there: the medicine. The distillation-grade extract work that produced our antiseptics and our sedative-analgesic finds sat inside the plant programme's establishment and would have gone down with it. The council re-founded it in 538 as a medical programme in its own right at 250 million across the reign, under the Pataliputra and Taxila houses, with no galvanic connection and no Egyptian thread.

Two inoculation candidates abandoned in 535 were resumed in 539, and here the record must set down a failure the physicians did not expect and cannot explain, because it marks the exact edge of what our medicine is.

**The seasonal fever of the Bengal delta and the wet Deccan** — the one that comes with the standing water after the rains, returns in a man every third or fourth day, and kills the young and the newly arrived — was taken up in 540. The reasoning was the oldest and best our tradition has: the guard against the pox works by taking a little of the matter from a light case and setting it into the skin of the well, and the well thereafter take a mild sickness and are spared the killing form ever after. It has worked for four centuries. Every physician in the realm knows it, and *no physician in the realm knows why it works*. So they did the obvious thing: matter from a light case of the fever, set into the skin of the well, in a proper trial across three seasons at Tāmralipti and Ujjain, some two thousand subjects.

**It failed entirely.** No mild sickness followed the setting, and no protection followed either — the treated took the fever in the next season at the same rate as the untreated, and the ones who had taken it before took it again, which they had also done before anyone treated them. Repetition at greater strength gave the fever itself and killed nine men.

The physicians are genuinely at a loss and their argument is recorded because it is the honest state of our knowledge. Some hold the matter was wrongly taken or the season wrong. Some hold, more usefully, that this fever must simply be a different *kind* of thing from the pox — that a man takes the pox once and the fever forty times, and that a guard which works by making the body remember cannot work against a sickness the body plainly never remembers. That is the best thought anyone had, it was written down in 547, and **nobody can take it further, because to ask why the body remembers one and not the other is to ask what either sickness is, and our medicine has no means of asking that.** We have a method that works, no account of why, and therefore no way of telling in advance which diseases it will work on. We find out by trying, at two thousand subjects and nine dead a time. That is the ceiling, it is not a funding ceiling, and it will not move until something beneath it moves.

The second candidate, against a cattle murrain of the Deccan, is in trial and looks real, which is worth exactly as much and no more.

The council debated closure across two sittings. The argument against it was made — that Egypt's anchor claim, that the torpedo fish's shock is the same force, is true and verifiable by any man with a net, and a saboteur has no reason to hand a rival a truth he can check; that the plant work produced real phenomena, which false theories do not usually do; and that the error is asymmetric, being fooled costing a share of one programme while refusing costs the channel forever. It was heard and it lost. The court had been walled out of the western sea for a hundred and eighty years, had just watched its African trade end without explanation, and had eleven men who did not come back. It could not, in that room, in that year, find for Egyptian good faith.

The finding was recorded as prudence. It was temper, and our own assessment had predicted in writing, six months earlier, that it would be temper. Predicting a mistake is not avoiding one.

### The Weak Monsoons of 537 to 539, and Why They Are a Short Section

The monsoon was weak in 537, weaker in 538, and adequate in 539 across the Deccan plateau and the central lands. Two thin years in succession is a thing this realm has met perhaps thirty times in eight centuries, and it is precisely what the granaries, the tanks, and the relief line exist for.

They were there. The granary reserve stood at establishment; the relief line at 46 million was raised to 62 for the two bad years and to 70 permanently thereafter; grain moved along the roads out of the Gangetic surplus into the Deccan and the Godavari uplands within one season of the first failure, as it had in the 330s. The African drought-grains, distributed continuously since the 480s and now reaching the eastern Deccan and the central plateau under an agricultural works line that was never cut, held yields where the realm's own seed would have failed outright. The land tax in the affected provinces fell by about 70 million in 538 and 45 million in 539 through remission, and recovered fully by 541.

There was hunger. There was no famine, no mortality event, and no flight worth the name. Some three hundred thousand people moved off the worst land in the Godavari uplands and most came back. This is what the extraction is for: the realm takes a sixth to a quarter of every harvest and returns it as granaries, canals, wells and relief when the season turns, and in this reign it kept the bargain — not through any decision of the ruler, who was not consulted, but because the lines stood where they had stood and nobody moved them.

The register at 550 stands at about **103 million**, against 98 at 525. The realm is growing again after a century of flat returns, and the reason is not mysterious: no plague reached the Gangetic plain in this quarter-century, the Tigris did not rise, no war was fought on our own ground, and dry country that fed nobody in 500 now feeds and is assessed.

### Mesopotamia, Rome, and the Treaty Somebody Finally Read

The road patrols and waystations along the Mesopotamian stretch of the Rome route stayed funded and were extended: eleven new waystations between 531 and 544, patrols at full strength, and a mounted escort service offered to merchant convoys at a small charge from 537. Banditry on the road fell to the lowest level recorded since the tariff was set. The traffic grew accordingly and so did the tariff receipts, from 155 million to 230.

Mesopotamia did not revolt, and the reason is only partly to this ruler's credit: the previous century made it the richest transit ground on the most profitable road in the world, and the second capital on the Tigris makes it feel governed rather than occupied. That binding held and was strengthened. The Tigris embankments and canals were kept and in 545 extended above the confluence, the work of the reign's best-spent thirty million.

**The treaty.** The overland agreement with Rome — twelve parts in the hundred through each empire's ground, fixed twenty-five years and renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gives notice — reached the end of a span in this reign. Our own audit of 525 observed that in two hundred years the realm had never established when notice must be given, to whom, or through what channel, and that Rome certainly had. This is the one thing in that document the court acted on, and it acted because the advisory council put it in front of the throne as a matter requiring only a signature. The realm sent to Rome to fix the mechanism in writing. That is set out in the declarations.

The habit the council was guarding against is our own and well documented: we once read China's sea treaty as *implying* a toll and a port of call that its words never contained, and were wrong. A frontier officer reporting that no notice has arrived is not the same as a treaty renewed, and this time we said so out loud.

### China Left Undisturbed, and What the Watchers Saw

The previous reign made silence toward China a deliberate act of state. This reign continued it by not thinking about it, which produces the same behaviour for none of the same reasons — until 548, when the treasury, not the throne, found a commercial reason to write. That is in the declarations.

The Syr Darya was quiet for twenty-five years. The frontier cannon stand where they were placed, in sight of China's watchers, at the cost of a garrison establishment of half a million men, six thousand elephants and a hundred thousand horse facing a river across which nothing has happened in three hundred and twenty-five years. Every loss we suffered this reign came at sea, in Africa, or in the ledger of things not attempted.

The watchers reported three things. Chinese hulls continue to work the passage south of the Strait into the archipelago east of Sumatra, unseen by our facility and paying nothing, as since the 470s; we have made no attempt to stop them and will not, having learned what that costs. The Spice Island garrisons were properly supplied by the southern road throughout — Ternate at about eleven hundred effective of twelve hundred, Banda at six hundred and twenty of seven hundred, harbour powder for a hundred and fifty shots against forty at 525. They are in far better condition than at any time since 470 and they remain exactly as indefensible, because their condition was never the question. They are ours because China has not chosen otherwise.

### The Strait and the Islands, Which Also Grew

Nobody ordered this either. An engined hull that does not wait on the wind changes what a chokepoint is worth, and the realm has held the eastern gate for two hundred and seventy-five years.

**Sindhudvāra**, our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait, was a customs house, a garrison and a berth in 525. It is a town of about **fourteen thousand** at 550, with ropewalks, a chandlery quarter, godowns that store rather than merely transship, a stone dry dock finished in 543, and — the thing that matters — **a coaling stage from 545**, bunkering Bengal coal brought south in the same hulls that carry it. An engined ship must fuel where the fuel is, and the realm has just made itself the only place between the subcontinent and China where an engine can be filled. The transit traffic rose accordingly and so did the tolls, and no minister has yet written down that a coaling stage is a stronger hold on a strait than a fort is.

**The dry dock is not an achievement and this record declines to present it as one.** A dry dock is a stone box with a gate and a means of emptying it. The realm has built in dressed stone since Ashoka, has run canal sluices and river gates for eight centuries, and has had steam pumps standing at the bottom of the Kolar shafts since about 465 lifting far more water than a dock holds. Every part of it was in hand for eighty years. What was missing was that nobody had put the three together, because ship repair was a beaching job done by the men who had always done it that way and no officer's advancement turned on changing it. It was built in 543 because a Tamil shipmaster at Sindhudvāra, tired of losing a season to careening engined hulls whose weight the old methods could not manage, asked why not, and no one had an answer. The next reign should take from this that the interval between holding every component of a thing and possessing the thing is, in this realm, measured in generations, and that the interval is administrative and not technical.

A second **coaling and watering stage** was established in 547 on the islands of the Bay on the northern approach, garrison of ninety, unremarkable and consequential for the same reason.

**The spice transplant.** The realm that has hunted the coffee plant for a hundred and eighty years because another power holds it did, in 538, finally think to ask why it holds cloves and nutmeg from two small islands it cannot defend. Seedlings and nuts went out from Ternate and Banda under the island administrators' noses — which is to say, under our own, since the islands are ours and the only people to hide it from were the islanders whose whole standing rests on the monopoly. Plantings were made in Sri Lanka, on the Malabar coast, in the wet country behind Uttaradvāra, and at Nīlaparvata. The Malabar and Sri Lankan nutmeg live and bear poorly; the clove has taken nowhere yet; the northern continental planting failed outright. It is not a substitute and in twenty years it may be. It is the single most far-sighted thing done in this reign and it was done by a superintendent of gardens.

**Ternate and Banda** themselves did not grow and were not meant to. Their Maurya population is a garrison and a factory, the islanders' resentment is fifty years old, structural, commercial, and entirely unaddressed by better supply, and the transplant programme — if the islanders learn of it, and they will — removes the last reason they had to prefer us to anyone.

And, resolved only in the sense that it was investigated: in spring 543 the watch at **Trikūṭa**, on the eastern side of the long northern cape, sighted two ships of unfamiliar build standing offshore the better part of a day, then working away northeast. The watch had a spyglass — the reign's one useful administrative act was to send the brass-bound glasses to the southern stations in 536 — and reported three masts, a rig none of them knew, and hulls broader in the beam than a Chinese trader. The report reached Pataliputra in fourteen months because the optical relays that carry word across the realm in a day stop at the Strait. This time a vessel was sent: two hulls out of Uttaradvāra in 545 worked eight hundred kilometres of that coast and the islands north of it and found nothing, no wreck, no beached party, no report from any island people of strangers ashore. We do not know whose ships they were. The court's guess is China; the court's guess has been wrong before; and the honest entry is that two ships of a build we could not name looked at our coast and went away.

### The Research Programmes, Which Are What a Rich Realm Buys

The previous reign set the armament line at a tenth of revenue and put the advisory council behind it, so a later ruler minded to cut it must fight an institution rather than sign an order. This ruler was exactly the man that friction was built against — not a purger but a drifter, who would have let the line erode by inattention. He looked at it twice, in 531 and 540; both times the council refused to release it and he let it go rather than have the argument. It rose with revenue from 230 million to 275, some 6,250 million across the reign, and it is the reason this quarter-century has anything to show for itself.

**Funded researchers at 550: about 33,000**, with some 60,000 to 80,000 craftsmen, bonded labourers and works hands behind them. The basis is the treasury's own long-standing reckoning, set out in our appendices: materials, fuel and equipment for this kind of work run about three times the wage cost, so roughly one part in four of the programme's funds pays researchers' wages, and a researcher's wage is about two thousand paṇas a year. At 265 million average that is 66 million in wages and some thirty-three thousand men. At 525 the same reckoning gave 28,000 to 29,000. The pool grew because the money grew; it did not grow faster, because it cannot — the ceiling is literacy and caste, not coin, and we could pay for four times as many and not recruit them.

**The powder wall moved, for the first time in two hundred and seventy-five years, and it was paid for in men.** Castings and locks were never the constraint; bulk manufacture of gunpowder of even quality was, and it is a question of works built, not of ideas had. This reign built six: four at Gangetic saltpeter sites, one at Ujjain, one in the western Deccan. It imposed a proofing standard on every barrel with gauged charges and a written return. And it put steam engines on the incorporating mills, which is the decision this section exists to record.

**Powder works burn. That is not misfortune, it is the trade.** Across the reign the six works and the two older ones lost, between them, some thirty mill-houses to fire or blast — an average of a little over one a year, most of them killing one man or two, a few killing a dozen, all of them normal and all of them budgeted for in the establishment as a running charge. The works are built the way our people learned to build them a century ago: mill-houses spaced and separated, three walls of masonry and the fourth of light timber facing away from the rest so that a blast goes out through the weak wall and not sideways into the next house, earth banks between, and the men on shift kept to the fewest the process needs.

That discipline held everywhere except where the engines were.

> **Bhadrapura, the eleventh of Māgha, 541.** The second Gangetic works, the largest and the first to be engined throughout. An incorporating mill took fire in the charge — the surviving mill-men, from two hundred paces, said it went with a flash and not a bang, which is what a charge does when it lights dry. The weak wall did its work and the blast went outward as designed, and the outward direction was the drive shafting, the belt gallery and the engine house, which had been built along the mill row because that is where a shaft has to run. Fire went down the gallery through eleven mill-houses in under a minute. **A hundred and eighty-four dead**, the works destroyed to the foundations, the saltpeter refinery beside it lost, and the whole Gangetic output halved for fourteen months while it was rebuilt.

**The argument that followed is the most serious technical debate of the reign and it was not settled by the throne.** The ordnance side held that the engine is a furnace with a firebox, a hot cylinder, hot shafting and iron running on iron, standing in a building full of the most sensitive mixture the realm knows, and that a water wheel is none of those things; that the realm had incorporated powder on water wheels for two centuries without losing a works entire; and that the throughput gained was not worth a hundred and eighty-four men and fourteen months. The works superintendents held that the engine had tripled what an incorporating mill could pass in a day, that engines run where water does not and run in the dry season when the wheels stand idle, that the whole gain of the reign came from exactly this, and that the loss rate was the price of the gain and should be stated and accepted rather than mourned.

Neither carried the council and the ruler did not choose. What happened instead was a compromise arrived at by the works themselves over four years, which is how things are decided in a reign like this: **the engines came off the incorporating mills** and back onto water wheels at the five works where water allowed, and were retained on pounding, granulating, glazing, pumping and haulage, where the material is wetter or coarser or not yet mixed. The engine house was thereafter built off the mill row and the drive taken by rope over a distance. Ujjain, which has no reliable water, kept its engines on the incorporating mills, and Ujjain has lost two mill-houses since.

**In consequence, at 550:**

- **Powder output about four times the rate of 525** — not the five it would have been. Bhadrapura cost roughly a year of the realm's total powder, and the return to water wheels cost perhaps a sixth of the throughput permanently.
- **One-man firearms: about 34,000**, against a few thousand at 525, with powder enough to keep two corps of fifteen thousand men in continuous drill and carry them through a campaign season. That is three parts in a hundred of a standing army of 1,119,000 — a real force and not an armed realm, and the record should not let anyone at court read it as the latter.
- **Two-man crew-served pieces: about 8,000.**
- **Frontier and fortress cannon: about 170**, up from 50–80.
- **Cannon-armed vessels: about 75**, up from 30–40.

**Coal, and the question nobody had asked.** Our own audit of 525 noted that no one had ever asked what our steam engines burn at scale, and that at Uttaradvāra the monsoon forest was being cut for charcoal faster than the yards cut timber while in the heartland the engines competed with the Deccan iron smelters. The council took the question up in 532. Surveyors sent into the eastern hills beyond the Gangetic plain — the broken country of the Damodar and the plateau south of it — found black rock that burns hotter and longer than charcoal, in seams at shallow depth, in quantity that has no bottom anyone has found. Shafts were sunk from 535 with the Kolar drainage engines set beside them from the first, and by 545 coal was moving by river barge to the Gangetic powder works and by road to the Deccan smelters. The charcoal competition is over. This is the largest single practical gain of the reign and it came out of a budget line for asking an obvious question.

Two things it did not do, and both are limits and not oversights.

**It will not smelt our iron.** Coal put to the furnace in place of charcoal gives metal that crumbles under the hammer when hot and takes no forging — short and brittle at red heat, sound enough when cold, which is a distinction our smiths know well and have no word for the cause of. They believe there is something in the rock that goes into the metal, on no better ground than that the charcoal has not got it and the coal has. They are right that it is in the rock. They cannot name it, isolate it, or drive it off, and they will not until this realm has a science of what things are made of, which it does not have and is not close to. So the smelters still take charcoal and the coal drives the engines and the mills, and the two never meet.

**And the deep seams kill.** The shallow workings, opened first, gave no trouble. From 543 the shafts followed the seams down, with the Kolar pumps beside them — and the pumps lift water, which was the Kolar problem, and are no answer at all to the one that was waiting. In the deep faces the air itself burns. It gathers where the roof is high and the air is still, gives no smell a man can trust, and takes fire from an open lamp with a soft blue flash that runs along the roof and is gone, and the men beneath it are dead of it or of what it leaves behind, which will not sustain a candle or a lung. **The workings at Rāṇīgarta and two others killed a hundred and thirty men between 544 and 549 in six separate firings**, the worst of them nineteen at once, and killed a further forty who went in after them and did not come out.

What the mines did about it is what men without a theory do: a boy sent ahead with a candle on a long pole and a wet cloth over him to burn the gas off deliberately before the shift goes down, which works and which sometimes kills the boy; shafts sunk in pairs so that air is drawn through rather than standing, which is the only real remedy anyone found and is dear; and the abandonment of the deepest faces at three of the eastern pits. **The consequence is that the realm's coal is abundant and shallow, and the realm's coal is capped.** Every seam within easy reach of the surface will be worked out in some number of decades, the deep coal is there and cannot be taken at a price in men the realm has been willing to pay, and no one at court knows that the fuel under the new machines has a floor.

**Fine optics, which our own audit named as the block beneath our medicine and which was funded at nil.** A programme was founded in 533. The realm has ground lenses since the 340s and built spyglasses since the 490s and had never once tried to grind *better*. The work went at glass first — lead glass from the Rajasthan zinc-and-lead workings, melted longer and stirred, giving fewer bubbles and a higher bending of light — and then at figuring and testing lenses to a standard rather than to a craftsman's eye.

The two-lens instrument in a tube was built first, in 541, because a tube of two lenses is what a spyglass is and our men had been making spyglasses for fifty years. **It was a disappointment and it is worth setting down why**, since the reason is the boundary of this whole art. Put a second lens behind the first to magnify what the first has made, and it magnifies the first lens's faults along with its image. Every fault the glass has is enlarged twice over: the coloured fringes that edge every bright thing seen through a single lens, which our men have known and cursed since the 340s and cannot remove because they do not know what light is or why a lens should sort it into colours at all; and the softness at the edge of the field. At the powers the medical men wanted, the instrument gave a large bright blur with a rainbow round it. It shows a beetle's leg admirably. It shows nothing small.

**What worked was the opposite of clever.** A gem-cutter of the Pataliputra shop, who had been polishing hard stone all his life and thought the tube business overwrought, drew a thread of the new lead glass, melted its end into a bead the size of a mustard seed, and set the bead in a pierced brass plate with a specimen pin and a screw behind it. One lens. No tube. The eye almost touching the glass, in the sun, in an uncomfortable posture no scholar would tolerate for long. **One lens has only its own faults, and a very small one made of very good glass has few.** By 544 the beads were resolving what the tube could not.

What they showed: in standing water, in scrapings from the teeth, in the matter of a running sore, and in the blood, there are small bodies that move of their own accord. They are everywhere. There are more of them in foul water than in clean, and more in a wound that goes bad than in one that heals. The physicians have written this down carefully and **cannot say what it means.** They do not know whether these things cause sickness, feed on it, or merely live where it is wet. Nothing in our medicine tells them how to find out. It is possible that the men who wash their hands and their instruments are removing these bodies and that this is why the recoveries improve; it is equally possible that the two facts are unrelated.

The limits are exactly stated. Perhaps twenty men in the realm can use a bead well, the posture and the light being what they are; the field is a pinhole; nothing can be drawn accurately from it by a second man looking over the first man's shoulder, because there is no room for a second man's eye. And the path back to a comfortable instrument runs through the coloured fringes, which nobody can remove and nobody can explain. **Until someone can say why a lens makes colours, the realm's medicine will be done by a handful of men squinting into the sun through a mustard seed.**

**The wire.** In 546 a current ran down an insulated drawn-copper wire from one building of the Pataliputra school to another, four hundred paces, deflecting a needle at the far end reliably enough to pass agreed letters one at a time. In 548 the line was extended along the river road to the station at Kāśī and beyond, some forty kilometres, on poles, with relay stations at eight-kilometre intervals where a man reads a deflection and re-sends it, the wire insulated with lac and oiled cloth. It carries perhaps forty letters an hour in dry weather. In the monsoon it fails entirely, and nobody yet knows how to insulate against it. The piles that drive it must be rebuilt every few days and their output falls as they age, so nothing sent on the wire can be trusted to arrive at the strength it left.

It is not a network. It is not within a generation of one. Egypt bound an empire with this and we have forty kilometres of it that stops when it rains. But it is the first message the realm has ever sent without a rider, and the route from here is now a list of named problems rather than an unknown. The men who did it were not rewarded, because this ruler did not reward people.

### What Was Not Examined

The elephant corps stands at six thousand, costs twenty-four million a year, and was not discussed. The mahouts are hereditary, the corps is the oldest pride in the realm, and no man assessed at forty-nine opens that question. That elephants panic under gunfire as horses do — and that this reign has just put forty thousand firearms into its own army and so proved the point on its own drill grounds, where the corps must now be exercised two kōs from any live firing — was raised in one written assessment in 525 and in no council since.

The colonial ledgers were not audited: the naval writer **Kannan**, sent in 547 to regularize the accounts at Suvarṇatīra, was given a house, a clerk, a share in the harbour dues and a wife's family, and stopped sending returns after his second season. Nobody was sent after him. The reserve stood at two and a half thousand million paṇas and would have paid for a hundred inspectors.

And the reserve itself was not examined. Two thousand seven hundred and fifty million paṇas, a full year's revenue, sitting in the vaults at 550 — enough to build a fleet that could contest the eastern sea, or to run the wire from Pataliputra to Ctesiphon, or to buy the whole of the powder capacity the army lacks, or to plant ten Paścimadvāras. The treasury reported the figure annually. The court read it as a comfort.

### The Reign at Its Close

The heartland is untouched and larger than it was — the subcontinent whole, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Bactria and Sogdia, Iran, the Caucasus and Mesopotamia to the Euphrates, same administration, same tax, same undefeated army, not one square kilometre lost, and five million more people on the roll. The reserve is at the prudent target for the first time in a hundred and fifty years. The army has a firearm in real numbers and a powder industry to feed it. The realm burns coal, sends messages down a wire, and has looked through a lens at things no one has ever seen.

And the southern continent tripled. That is the fact this record most wants the next reign to sit with. A hundred and thirty-five thousand people at 525 and four hundred thousand at 550, on ground that grows wheat, wool, cattle, timber and coal, doubling every twenty-odd years by births alone, with towns that were stockades now launching ocean hulls, with two settlements founded by graziers who asked nobody, with its own foundry, its own land grants, its own harbour dues, its own courts in all but name — and with a treasury line that *fell* through every year of it, because Pataliputra was paying for less and less of what was happening. A mediocre reign did not stop a colony. It only stopped steering one, and a colony on good ground does not need steering; it needs a throne that notices which way it has gone. The next ruler will inherit a province of four hundred thousand people that has never seen a minister, never been audited, never been assessed for tax, and never been told no.

What was lost was the African trade and the eyes that went with it; eleven men; two settlements and the ground beneath a third; and the only channel by which a more advanced power had ever handed us a thread, closed by written order in a bad temper.

What was not gained is the longer list, and it is the list that matters. No answer to China at sea. No use made of a full treasury. No audit of a continent. No question asked of the elephant corps. No embassy sent anywhere, to anyone, in twenty-five years except three letters written by councillors and clerks. A great reign takes a realm at 2,280 million and leaves it changed. This one took it at 2,280 and left it at 2,770, which is what happens to a well-built machine when nobody touches it.

The ruler died in 550. His historians will assess him at forty-nine and note that he did no harm. The assessment is accurate and it is not a compliment.

> **By the numbers, around 550 AD.** The realm's registered one hundred and three million people hold all they long have in the west, the Strait of Malacca, the spice islands but for Tidore, and a settled southern continent whose own Maurya population is near four hundred thousand and doubling in a generation. Yearly revenue is about twenty-seven hundred and seventy million paṇas, some eleven hundred and eighty million the land tax and near five hundred and fifty million from trade and transit, the rest from mines, monopolies and internal duties. The standing army stands near eleven hundred and twenty thousand: four hundred and forty thousand field infantry, five hundred and fifteen thousand in garrison and second line, a hundred thousand horse, six thousand elephants, and a navy of fifty-eight thousand crew with about seventy engined hulls and seventy-five cannon-armed vessels. About nine hundred and forty-five million goes to the army, two hundred and seventy-five million to weapons research and armament, two hundred and eighty-five million to roads and works, seventy million to relief and granary, and a hundred and five million to the southern continent. The technical programmes employ some thirty-three thousand trained researchers and sixty to eighty thousand craftsmen and labourers. The reserve stands near twenty-seven hundred and fifty million, a full year's revenue and the strongest position the treasury has ever held. Reading and writing, counted this reign for the first time by the enrolments of the provincial schools and the returns of the licensed printing houses rather than assumed, appear to reach about one person in eight.

# DECLARATIONS

## MESSAGES OUT

### 1. To Egypt

**Sent 534. Carried by sea from Oman to Aden under a herald's flag, offered to the Egyptian officer commanding at the strait, translated by a Sogdian and a Yemeni interpreter together.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Sovereign of Egypt, greeting.
>
> A vessel bearing our flag put men ashore upon your coast in the spring of the year before last, below the harbours where our ships have traded by your permission for five hundred years. Those men went inland without your leave, and they have not come back to us. If they are in your hands we ask for them.
>
> We say plainly what our own inquiry found. The master sailed upon an old standing order of our station at Oman which no living minister had reviewed, and exceeded any warrant of ours; the officer who let that order stand has been removed. We did not send those men to take your ground and we do not claim it. Eleven men walked inland to buy a plant. That is the whole of the offence, and we dress it neither more nor less.
>
> We ask two things.
>
> The first is our men — seamen, a physician's assistant, one interpreter. Return them and we count the matter closed, and we will set a heavier hand upon our own captains than you could set upon them.
>
> The second is the trade, and here we ask you first for information and only then for a favour, because we find we do not know what has happened. Since last summer our masters have been turned from harbours on that coast where they have watered for four hundred years, and our factors have closed their houses. We do not know whether this is done by your order, by the order of your officers on that shore, or for some cause that has nothing to do with us at all. We would rather be told than guess, and we have guessed enough already. If it is your answer to the eleven men, we say that it is a heavier answer than the fault, and we ask that the old permission stand again upon your own old terms — our merchants trade through your ports, hold no ground, claim no ground, which we have kept for five hundred years and kept even while we were angry.
>
> In return we offer what is ours to offer, and it is not small. Egyptian shipping entering the Gulf at our station of Oman, and Egyptian shipping using our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait between the peninsula and the great island, shall pay no port due and no transit charge for twenty-five years, on the same footing as our own hulls. We have never made that offer to any power, and we do not make it to China.
>
> One further matter, set down so there is no misunderstanding later. In past years your people have offered our scholars guidance upon questions of natural philosophy, and ours were grateful for it. Our court has determined that we will henceforth pursue such questions by our own scholars, in our own houses, upon our own reasoning, and our envoys are instructed to accept no such guidance in future. We say it directly rather than let your people learn it from a closed door. It touches nothing of the trade, and we would not have you read into it more than we mean.
>
> Send us your answer by the same hand.

*Our herald was received at the strait and the letter taken from him. Nothing had come back to Pataliputra by 550, by any hand, in any form. The eleven men have not returned. We do not know whether the letter travelled beyond the officer who took it.*

*A note on the third paragraph of that letter, which the council fought over for a month. The envoy's draft asserted that Egypt had closed the coast. It was struck out and replaced with a question, over the objection of most of the room, by the one councillor who pointed out that we did not know it and that a great power which accuses another of an act it did not commit gets an answer it will deserve. He was right and he was resented for it, and the letter went as it stands.*

### 2. To Rome

**Sent 539 by the overland road through Ctesiphon to the Roman frontier command in Syria, in Latin and Sanskrit, delivered to the Roman transit officials named in the treaty. Drafted by the advisory council; the throne signed it.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Emperor and Senate of Rome, greeting, and to the officers of the road.
>
> Our two empires have kept the overland agreement for two hundred years — twelve parts in the hundred through each empire's ground, fixed for twenty-five years and renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gives notice. It has been the surest revenue either of us draws from the other and the surest friendship. We write about the clause and not about the rate.
>
> We find, on searching our own registers, that we have never established when notice must be given, to whom it must be delivered, or by what channel, and we suspect we have been trusting to good luck and to your good faith. Both have held. Neither is a procedure. We propose, therefore, and would sign at once:
>
> That notice under the agreement be given not less than three years before the end of a span; that it be delivered in writing to the transit officers of the other empire at the Euphrates crossing and simultaneously to the other's court, and be effective only when both have acknowledged in writing; that each empire's transit officers exchange, at each midsummer, a signed statement that no notice has been given, so that silence is recorded as silence and not merely assumed; and that the span now running be confirmed as renewed for a further twenty-five years upon the existing terms.
>
> We ask this because our own habit has bitten us before. We once read a treaty of the eastern sea as implying a toll its words did not contain, and we were wrong, and we paid for the reading with a fleet. We would rather write down what we mean than discover in twenty years what we assumed.
>
> Three further matters, plainly.
>
> On the road itself: your merchants complained in the last generation of robbers on the Mesopotamian stretch. We have built eleven new waystations, kept the patrols at strength, and from two years ago we offer a mounted escort to any convoy that wants one at a charge we do not profit by. We believe the road is safer now than at any time since the tariff was set. If your merchants say otherwise we would hear it from you and not from rumour.
>
> On the firearm: we sent you, in the last reign, a complete and honest maker's account of the hand-weapon we had met in the eastern sea, and you gave us the marine engine, and both sides kept the bargain. We ask now whether you have made the weapon and put it in your soldiers' hands, and in what numbers, and what you have found. We ask because we have done so ourselves and have learned that the hard part is not the weapon. It is the powder — bulk manufacture of even quality, which is a question of works built and not of cleverness, and which has held this realm back for two hundred and seventy-five years. We will send you, if you want it, our proofing standard, our gauged charges, and the design of our incorporating mills, which are the whole of what we have learned in twenty-five years of building six of them. We ask nothing for it that you do not wish to give. We have concluded that on this one matter neither of us gains by the other's ignorance, since the power that has this weapon in numbers already is neither of us.
>
> On the western ocean: we know that you have found land beyond it and have settled it, and we learned that by accident from a merchant, which does neither of us credit. We have settled a great southern land of our own, reached from our eastern seas, and we tell you so directly rather than let you hear it the way we heard of yours. We do not propose to trade charts. We propose only that neither of us should again learn a thing of this size from a third man in a market.

*Receipt was acknowledged by the Roman transit officers at the crossing in 540 and the letter forwarded. No reply on the notice clause had reached Pataliputra by 550. Our frontier officials report that no notice has been given by Rome, and this record states that as what our officials report and not as a fact about Rome's intentions.*

### 3. To China

**Sent 548 by the Sogdian caravan channel to the frontier command across the Syr Darya, written in Chinese in our own hand. Proposed by the treasury, not the throne.**

> From the Throne at Pataliputra, to the Emperor of the Han, greeting.
>
> It is fifty years since our two realms settled the matter of the eastern sea, and the settlement has held on both sides without a word of complaint from either. We write on a matter of trade and on nothing else.
>
> Our transit charge upon the overland road stands at twelve parts in the hundred, where it has stood since we returned it to that figure under our agreement with you. We observe that a growing share of your western trade now goes by the northern grasslands and not by our road. We do not complain of it; a merchant takes the cheaper road and that is the whole of the science.
>
> We propose to lower our charge to ten parts in the hundred upon goods carried the whole length of our ground, and to hold it there for twenty-five years, and we tell you so in advance rather than let your merchants discover it. Our road is watered, walled, patrolled, and repaired; the northern grass is none of these. We would rather take ten parts of a great traffic than twelve of a shrinking one, and we say so plainly because there is no advantage to us in your not knowing it.
>
> If your merchants find our waystations wanting in any particular we will hear it and mend it. Our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait remains open to your hulls on the terms agreed, and the berth worked by your own harbour men stands as it was set.
>
> We ask nothing in return and we raise no other matter.

*Carried by Nanaivandak's house and receipted at the frontier. No reply had reached Pataliputra by 550. The toll reduction was proclaimed at the Sogdian cities and along the road in 549 whether or not any reply ever comes, the treasury holding that a rate set for a reason should not wait on a courtesy.*

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. The Sogdian caravan channel — measuring the northern route.**
- **Who:** Nanaivandak, Sogdian silk broker of Samarkand, third generation of a family carrying our messages and goods since the second century; retainer against a share of the transit toll he helps assess.
- **Cover:** none required; he is what he appears to be, a broker whose business is knowing where caravans go.
- **Target:** volume, route and cargo of Chinese caravans on the northern steppe road that bypasses us — how much Silk Road revenue is lost and whether the loss still grows.
- **Network age:** 325 years, the oldest we hold; its loyalty runs to the trade, not to us. The tasking asks it to do only what it does anyway, which is why a mediocre reign approved it.
- **Result at 550:** steady and probably honest. Northern-route volume still rising, but more slowly than in the 490s. Our tolls confirmed at ~245 million against a peak of 320. This reporting is what produced the toll reduction of 549, and it is the one piece of intelligence this reign both bought and used.

**2. Egypt — attempted reconstruction after the loss of the African coast.**
- **Who:** Dhanapāla of Bharukaccha, factor of a Gujarati gem and cloth house, resident at Alexandria; from 541 a Yemeni, Sa'īd, working the strait ports out of a dhow trade we do not own.
- **Cover:** ordinary resident commercial factors under the Mediterranean trading permission, which whatever happened on the African coast did not touch — our houses at Alexandria have traded without interruption throughout, a fact the council has never sat down and thought about, since it does not fit the reading it adopted in 534.
- **Target:** whether the fleet detected provisioning before 525 ever sailed; when, with what, westward or southward. Whether any order was ever issued about our access to the African coast, by whom, and in what terms. Secondarily the war harbour and the yards.
- **Network age:** the Alexandria merchant presence dates from the embassy of 365–367, so 185 years — but it has never been a service. It is merchants reporting what merchants see, has never produced anything of military value, and is now all we have, the African network having existed through residence in ports our hulls no longer enter.
- **Funding:** eleven million a year from 536, tripled from the previous reign, and it bought nothing, which is worth recording: this is not a problem money solves.
- **Result at 550:** nothing from the tasking. Dhanapāla reports harbour traffic, prices, and that he is watched and knows it. The yards remain closed to any foreigner. **We cannot say whether the Egyptian fleet ever sailed.** On the second question the tasking produced nothing and an unpaid clerk produced a great deal: Bhūtiśarman's memorandum of 541, collated from sixty-one of our own masters' logs at Oman, is the only serious piece of intelligence work done in this reign, cost the treasury a deputy factor's time, and was filed under commercial correspondence. The lesson the next reign should take is not about Egypt. It is that this realm buys collection at eleven million a year and does not read what it already owns.

**3. Rome — the treaty and the new continent.**
- **Who:** Mar Isḥaq, deacon of the Christian community of Ctesiphon, and two men of his congregation; from 546 also two Sogdian factors travelling the Syrian road on ordinary business.
- **Cover:** churchmen travelling to the Christian communities of Roman Syria — a traffic that crosses the Euphrates constantly and lawfully, which our rule of religious tolerance has always permitted without inquiry.
- **Target:** whether Rome has given or intends notice on the overland treaty; where its overseas colony lies and what it produces; whether Rome has fielded the one-man firearm whose construction we gave it in the last reign.
- **Network age:** new, founded 542, untested.
- **Risk the council did not weigh:** if discovered, we have made spies of our own Christian subjects in the one province we spent three centuries persuading that its faiths are safe under Sanskrit administration, and handed Mesopotamia the grievance force never gave it. This was raised once, by the same councillor who struck the accusation out of the Egyptian letter, and dismissed.
- **Result at 550:** no notice detected, which is consistent with none having been given and also with our not being able to detect one. Rome's colony placed vaguely beyond the western ocean, uncheckable. On the firearm, Mar Isḥaq reports Roman soldiers at the Syrian stations carrying arms he cannot describe, in what he judges to be small numbers. We do not know what we gave away.

**4. The southern continent — the audit that was not.**
- **Who:** Kannan, naval writer of the Nagapattinam establishment, sent with the convoy of 547; travelling openly as an inspector of accounts, no cover.
- **Target:** the ledgers of Suvarṇatīra and the northern stations; the true establishment of the western posts; the basis on which Vīrasena levies harbour dues and where the receipts go.
- **Network age:** none. There has never been an intelligence establishment on the southern continent — the informer networks that watch every governor in the old dominion, and that are the single reason this realm can govern a hundred million people, were never extended across the ocean, because the settlements were founded as a programme and not as provinces. Twenty-five years on, they are provinces, and nobody has changed the classification.
- **Result at 550:** housed, married into the harbour interest, given a share of the dues, ceased reporting after his second season. Nobody was sent after him. This is the cheapest failure in the record and the one most likely to cost the next reign.

### Not reached, and why

- **No theory of matter.** Combustion, distillation, electrochemistry, and no account of what any of it is made of. This is the ceiling under everything else.
- **No germ theory, and now the tantalising version of not having it.** We can see the small bodies. We cannot show that they cause anything. Our physicians wash because it works and now have a picture they cannot interpret.
- **Coke iron failed.** The coal will not smelt clean metal. We suspect an impurity in the rock and have no chemistry to find it.
- **No message network.** Forty kilometres that stops in the rain. Insulation, a pile of steady output, and a signalling scheme fit for volume are all unsolved, and are now at least *named*.
- **No answer to Chinese naval gunnery.** We have more cannon at sea and better crews and no idea how their line held its range against the wind or fired without pause. We did not seek one.
- **No interchangeable manufacture.** Gauges at the arsenals only. Egypt had a whole city of matched fittings in 367.
- **The army is not armed.** Forty thousand firearms against 1,119,000 men. Powder capacity, not design, remains the wall, as it has been for 275 years — but it is now a wall we are visibly climbing rather than one we merely describe.

## PEOPLE AT 550

- **Registered population, old dominion: ~103 million** (98M at 525). Basis: the provincial revenue registers. Growth resumed after a flat century because no plague reached the Gangetic plain in this quarter-century, the Tigris did not rise, no war was fought on our own ground, and dry country brought under the African drought-grains is now assessed where it fed nobody in 500. The figure remains a count of cultivators the revenue service can find, and understates.
- **Maurya population of the southern continent and its islands: ~400,000** (about 135,000 at 525). Basis: ~130,000 landed across the reign, of which more than half after 545 as private passengers on private bottoms; the balance is natural increase on a young population with land, meat and grain in quantities no Gangetic cultivator has seen, less the fevers of the north. About 167,000 in the named towns, the rest on runs, river farms, timber camps and outstations. Not on the old dominion's register, not counted by anyone the throne appointed, and the realm's fastest-growing population by a very wide margin.
- **Funded researchers: ~33,000** (28,000–29,000 at 525). Basis: the treasury's standing reckoning — one part in four of the armament line to wages, at 2,000 paṇas a researcher; 265M average × ¼ ÷ 2,000 = 33,125.
- **Programme craftsmen, works hands and bonded labourers: 60,000–80,000.**
- **Technical and scientific practitioners, all fields: 60,000–120,000.** Educated professional class 700,000–1,200,000.
- **Enslaved: one in ten to one in twenty**, five to ten million, now including a population on the southern continent whose labour founded it.
- **Literacy: about one in eight.** Counted this reign rather than assumed, from provincial school enrolments and the returns of the licensed printing houses. The old figure of one in ten had been copied forward without measurement since 250 BC; the change is mostly better counting and partly cheap paper. The ceiling remains caste, not coin.

## TERRITORY AT 550

### A. CONQUERED, SETTLED OR GARRISONED

**Unchanged and entire in the old dominion:** the subcontinent and Sri Lanka; Afghanistan; Bactria and Sogdia to the Syr Darya; the Iranian plateau; Armenia and Azerbaijan; Mesopotamia to the Euphrates; Oman; the tributary ports of the Gulf's Arabian shore; eastern Yemen; the Strait of Malacca and our port at its eastern mouth. Nothing gained, nothing lost, no frontier moved.

**Southern continent — GREW** *(population 525 → 550)*:
- **Suvarṇatīra**, the great sheltered harbour on the middle of the east coast. Southern seat. **11,000 → ~62,000.** Stone fort at the harbour mouth; three yards launching ocean hulls; own foundry from 544; hinterland enclosed and farmed to ~140 km inland.
- **Godhūmavāṭa**, on the tableland beyond the escarpment behind Suvarṇatīra. **Did not exist → ~7,000.** Founded 536 by graziers crossing a pass without warrant; wheat and cattle; made lawful in 541 by Vīrasena's own land grants, which he had no authority to issue.
- **Lavaṇatīra**, salt pans and fishery south of Suvarṇatīra. **Did not exist → ~3,500.** Daughter of Suvarṇatīra.
- **Kāntitīra**, the great enclosed bay near the southeastern corner. **4,000 → ~31,000.** Best farmland on the continent; wool from the western Deccan sheep; water and grazing taken ~90 km inland.
- **Śvetaśaila**, up the great river running inland from Kāntitīra's bay. **Did not exist → ~4,500.** Daughter of Kāntitīra.
- **Nadīmukha**, river mouth north of Suvarṇatīra. **2,000 → ~9,000**, on coal cut from the sea-cliff there from 543; now bunkers the engined hulls of the southern road.
- **Himatīra** on **Śītadvīpa**, the cold island, taken by force in the last reign. **1,600 → ~19,000**, the largest proportional growth on the continent: wheat and barley that do not wait on a monsoon, timber, sealing.
- **Uttarakūla**, north coast of Śītadvīpa facing the crossing. **Did not exist → ~3,000.** Daughter of Himatīra.
- **Paścimadvāra**, river mouth of the fertile southwestern corner. **Founded 542 → ~6,000.** The first real colony on the ocean a rival would cross, and the only settlement in the programme's history sited by men who had surveyed the ground themselves.
- **Dvigartā**, head of the twin gulfs on the southern shore. **Founded 546 → ~2,200.**
- **Uttaradvāra**, river mouth on the north coast west of the deep gulf. Principal yard. **4,000 → ~13,000**, and its true intake was near twice its increase; the monsoon fever kills at a rate the capital's returns do not show.
- **Sindhudvāra**, our port at the eastern mouth of the Strait of Malacca. **Garrison and customs house → a town of ~14,000**, with a stone dry dock (543) and a coaling stage (545) that is the strongest hold on that passage the realm has ever had and is recorded nowhere as a strategic asset.

**Southern continent — STAGNANT:**
- **Vārigrāma**, south of Suvarṇatīra. 2,000 → ~2,600; fair harbour, poor hinterland.
- **Prabhāsa**, on the wide shallow bay north of Nadīmukha. Anchorage silted; moved 3 km in 544; ~1,400 and going nowhere. The monument to siting a harbour from the chart of a different coast.
- **Trikūṭa**, watch station on the eastern side of the long northern cape. ~120 → ~300, which is what it is for.
- **The western chain**: **Pūrvasthāna** (the original secret depot in the sheltered sound behind the islands of the far northwest, ~70 → ~340 as the convoy's western victualling stop, with a well-field and a repair slip), **Aśvatīrtha**, **Śuṣkakūla**, **Vātamukha**, and eight further posts at roughly 250 km intervals, fifty men each, supplied on schedule throughout. Twelve of the fourteen posts of 525 stand at or near establishment. Deliberately stagnant: these are watchers, not colonies.
- **Marīcidvīpa**, five island trading stations north and east of the continent, taken by force in the last reign, garrisons of 60–150. Trade volume up; Maurya population ~1,200; resentment unaddressed.
- **Ternate** (~1,100 effective of 1,200) and **Banda** (~620 of 700), supplied by the southern road, harbour powder for ~150 shots against 40 at 525. Better supplied than at any time since 470 and exactly as indefensible, which was never a question of supply. Their monopoly is now being quietly undercut by our own transplant programme.
- **Bay coaling and watering stage**, established 547 on the islands of the northern approach to the Strait. Garrison 90.

**Southern continent — CONSOLIDATED, REDUCED OR ABANDONED:**
- **Antyaśilā** at the great double bay and one post north of it: consolidated into neighbours in 543 on an assessor's recommendation that the intervals were closer than the coast required; men moved, not lost.
- **Nīlaparvata**, south coast of the great mountainous island north of the continent: cut in 547 from settlement to coastal trading post of ~400 — fever, and interior peoples numerous, organised and unwilling. Recorded as a reduction. It was a defeat.
- **Śilātaṭa**, reef coast in the far northeast: abandoned 541.

**The countryside, which is two-thirds of it.** Behind and between every named place lie the surviving lesser stations of the original three hundred, the pastoral runs pushed inland from each eastern harbour, the river farms, timber camps, salt works and outstations. Roughly 233,000 of the continent's ~400,000 Maurya inhabitants live on them. No throne ordered a hectare of it and no register records it.

### B. SURVEYED, CHARTED OR CLAIMED ONLY

- **The northeastern reef coast** of the southern continent, from Trikūṭa south past the site of abandoned Śilātaṭa, some 1,600 km: charted 544–549 by two hulls assigned to nothing else, at the cost of one wrecked. A working pilotage of the reef now exists. No settlement, no garrison, no post; a stone marker at three river mouths.
- **The deep northern gulf** and the western side of the northern cape: run and charted 541–543 out of Uttaradvāra. Mangrove, no harbour worth a station, claimed and empty.
- **The southern shore west of Dvigartā to the head of the great bight**, some 1,400 km: charted 547–549. Cliff, no water, no anchorage. Claimed by marker. Nothing will ever be put there.
- **The west coast between Vātamukha and Paścimadvāra**: run in 539–540, the survey that found the fertile southwest. Charted throughout, garrisoned only at the existing post intervals.
- **The mountainous coast to the east-southeast of Śītadvīpa.** Two hulls out of Himatīra in 548, running east on the westerlies, raised a high mountainous land some two thousand kilometres out. They ran three hundred kilometres of its coast, landed once in a bay on its northwestern side, took on water, and met people — numerous, living in fortified hill villages, cultivating in terraces, organised under chiefs, and entirely unlike the Agnikāra in every particular. They were not hostile and they were not welcoming. The party left within a day, planted no marker beyond a cut stone at the waterline, made no claim, and came home in 549. The realm calls the place **Parvatatīra**, the mountain shore. It is charted for three hundred kilometres of a coast whose extent is unknown, it is not ours in any sense, and this record makes no claim to it. It is entered here because the next reign should know it is there and should know what was and was not done about it.
- **Two ships of unfamiliar build**, off Trikūṭa, spring 543. Three masts, a rig our watch could not name, broad in the beam. Investigated in 545 by two hulls out of Uttaradvāra along 800 km of coast and the islands north; nothing found. Whose they were, we do not know.

### C. LOST OR ENDED

- **The East African trade** — not territory, and the largest thing that went. Access to every harbour from the Red Sea strait to the far southern limit, held by permission for five hundred years, ended in practice across 533–536. Our factors gone, our sailings from ninety a season to none, 55–70 million a year of treasury revenue gone with them, and the intelligence network that gave the previous reign its one great coup gone in eighteen months. **Why it ended, we do not know**, and this record declines to state it as another power's decision when what we hold is our own masters' logbooks.
