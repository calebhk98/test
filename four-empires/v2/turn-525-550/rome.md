# ROME, 525–550 AD
## The Reign of Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus

---

## I. The Accession (526–528)

Iulianus Augustus died at Mediolanum in the winter of 526, of the lung complaint the physicians record for any man who has spent thirty years breathing Colonia Agrippina. He left no son and, for the first time in a century, no crisis: Consilium, Senate and Industrial Council had all been brought to one name before he died. The name was Gaius Antonius Severianus, thirty-nine, of an old senatorial house of Campania, married into the Rhine iron families of the Colonia district, and by profession a man of the fisc — eleven years *rationalis* of the western customs, four as *praefectus annonae*. He knew what was in the accounts because he had written much of it.

Acclaimed in the spring of 527, he did not campaign, build or distribute. He ordered the western programme's true costs consolidated into one ledger against the military line, the frontier line and the reserve, and had it read aloud to the Consilium in a single sitting that took most of a day. The conclusion: the free margin, two hundred and eighty millions against a revenue of four thousand, would be gone within twelve to eighteen years on the western commitment alone — before any war, usurpation, harvest failure or fresh Han demand.

His response settled the argument that would otherwise have consumed the reign, and the Consilium recorded it: *"We are the richest state in the world and we tax like a frightened one. Thirty-six sesterces a head. Alexandria would laugh. We do not have a money problem. We have a courage problem, and I intend to spend the courage rather than the coin."* Clipping the coin, he said, is what a state does when the ground will not yield, and Roman ground was not the problem. A debased denarius in an empire where every foreman reads a price sheet is not a quiet theft but a printed grievance with the emperor's face on it.

---

## II. The Fiscal War (528–535)

Four instruments, in deliberate order, easiest first.

**The *vectigal occidentale*, 528.** A duty of one part in eight on all goods of the western ocean at the moment of landing: gold, cacao, cotton, hides, dyes, the burning leaf, the new dyewoods. Levied at named ports of entry only — Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, Portus Namnetum, Londinium, Dubris — with landing anywhere else declared contraband. Tax the new thing; the new thing has no defenders yet. Projected at two hundred and twenty millions a year by 545.

**The *indictio maritima*, 529.** A five-year emergency levy on the coastal provinces profiting from the run — Baetica, Lusitania, Aquitania, Britannia, Hibernia — assessed on registered shipping tonnage rather than land. Extended twice.

**Advances against the western receipts, 530.** The tax-farming *societates* were sold the right to collect the *vectigal* for five-year terms, paid in full at the front, the great shipping houses buying in beside them: four hundred and sixty millions of ready money in three years against a nominal future value near six hundred. The state did not borrow, having no way to borrow. It sold tomorrow's customs at a loss to buy today's hulls, which is the same thing wearing a toga.

**Then the fight he had to win: the *collatio fabricarum*, 531–534.** A standing assessment on industrial output — factory, mine, foundry, rail, steam shipping — at a real rate, empire-wide, replacing the ragged patchwork of provincial levies the Council had spent a century keeping ragged. The only source large enough to carry the west, the arms race and the fleet together. Also a bill presented to the constituency that makes emperors: the succession of 318 was won by a Rhine general on industrial money, and everyone in the room knew it.

He did not decree it. He sold it, and paid in law rather than coin, which is the only currency Rome has ever had in surplus. What the Industrial Council received, enacted as the **Concordia Fabricarum** of 534:

- **Legal personality for the registered *societas*** — a chartered company distinct in law from its shareholders, able to hold property, sue, be sued and outlive its founders, with the shareholder's liability bounded at his subscription. Every great mine, factory, rail and shipping venture had been begging for this since the courts began improvising industrial law two centuries earlier.
- **The *privilegium fabricae*** — imperial protection of a registered method or machine for twenty years, enforced in every provincial court from one printed register at Rome, in exchange for deposit and publication at term's end.
- **Formal standing** — the Council's advisory remit converted into a right of prior consultation on any fiscal or commercial measure, its objections entered in the record and printed.

Rome received the *collatio*, yielding above three hundred millions a year by 545 and rising with output rather than with the assessor's mood. What made it survivable in the Senate: he raised the land assessment on the great estates at the same session, modestly, and said openly that he did it so the industrialists could not claim they alone were singled out. The senatorial houses were furious and stayed furious, and the old families never again put a man of theirs at his disposal. He knew the price when he paid it.

---

## III. The Rhine, and the One Thing He Saw

The labour settlement of the 350s was built to last two generations and had lasted four. Against a working class at eighty-five to ninety percent literacy in the Rhine cities, organizing by printed sheet, it was no longer a settlement. It was a lid.

Severianus saw this — and he saw it *because the money forced him into the room*. He could not have the *collatio* without negotiating with the industrial magnates, and he could not do that in the Rhine valley in the 530s without the Rhine valley's workers reading every word in the gazettes and drawing their own conclusion about who was being asked to pay. He did not go looking for the labour question. It was standing in the doorway of the room where the money was.

The second half of the Concordia, 534:

- **Collegia given standing to bargain, not merely to sue** — a registered collegium may treat with the owners on hours, wages and conditions, and the agreement is enforceable in the provincial courts as a contract.
- **The *curatores fabricarum*** — a standing inspectorate, salaried from the *collatio*, with right of entry to any registered works and power to close a shaft or a floor pending appeal. Two hundred and forty men at foundation, six hundred by 550, concentrated in Britannia, the Rhine, Silesia and the Dacian fields.
- **The accident fund** — levied on the industrialists at a fixed fraction of the *collatio*, paying on a printed schedule rather than at a magistrate's discretion.

The Patriarch of Rome blessed the Concordia in a published homily arguing the case in the language of human dignity rather than productivity — the first time in four centuries a Roman labour measure was defended on any ground but the state's convenience. The clergy of the industrial cities carried it into every congregation. It is not clear the emperor believed a word of the theology. It is entirely clear he understood what it was worth: the Church delivered him a settlement no number of *curatores* could have enforced.

The Rhine did not rise. That is the reign's quiet achievement and nobody will ever build a monument to it.

---

## IV. The Officium Custodiae, and the Quarrel with the Sheets

One line had troubled the Consilium since 523: Egypt knew of the western islands within months of the first fleet's return, and nobody had ever been made to account for how. Severianus founded the **officium custodiae** in 536, attached to the port administration rather than the army, on the reasoning that the leak was commercial. Its instruments are listed in the declarations; they were dull and correct except one — prior notification before any publication touching the western voyages, their positions, cargoes or losses.

The gazettes went to war. The *Acta Rhenana*, the *Diurna Londiniensia* and eleven other sheets refused registration and printed the refusal, arguing not liberty — not a Roman category — but property and precedent: no law had ever licensed a printer, and a magistrate who can suppress a shipwreck can suppress a peculating governor. The Council backed them, wanting the price and cargo news; the Patriarch's men backed them, because the western clergy were publishing things the *officium* wanted stopped. Severianus retreated in 540 with visible bad grace, keeping the rule only for positions, soundings, charts and coal, where it is unenforceable.

The *officium*'s unpublished first survey drew the conclusion that matters: an empire like this one cannot be sealed, because Rome's information does not leak through traitors but through the machinery that makes Rome work. Do not seal. Feed.

---

## V. The West: The Islands, and the Sickness

The islands were finished as a military matter early — Insula Aurea by 531, Insula Longa by 536, Insula Sancti Iohannis in 533 with four cohorts, Insula Fontium in 537 for its water and cattle ground — and the colonial architecture is named in the territory declaration below.

And then the thing nobody wrote into the plan.

Sickness follows our ships. It takes the islanders and largely spares our crews and colonists. It appears in a village after our people have been in it and not before, and it moves ahead of our people along the trade paths faster than our people move. The *procuratores* of Ozama and Portus Securus have tallied since 532, because Rome tallies everything, and the tallies are not ambiguous: the subjugated population of the two great islands, above four hundred thousand when the conquest began, is under seventy thousand in 550. Valleys of the Cibao that fed the gold works in 535 are empty ground in 548.

Rome cannot say why. Our physicians have no theory of any of this and were publicly wrong once already this generation, having called the crossing fevers common flu. But a line does not need a cause to be extended, and the extension is arithmetic a clerk can do: at the observed rate, the labour force the whole island economy was built on will not exist in a generation. The Cyprian quarantines applied from 538 did not work, and the *procuratores* said so in writing.

**A moral quarrel Rome did not expect.** The western clergy began publishing accounts of the dying villages in the Roman sheets from about 541, and the argument was theological and therefore unanswerable in an empire that had just made human dignity the language of its labour law. The bishop of Ozama's letter of 546 was read in three basilicas at Rome and printed in four cities. It changed no policy, and it is the first document in Roman history accusing the imperial economy, in print, of killing the people it employs. The men who made the Concordia understood what had been handed to the collegia of the Rhine.

**The leash.** From 543 Rome opened the purchase of African labour through the licensed factors at Alexandria, at Egyptian rates, under Egyptian licence, delivered to Gades and carried west in Roman hulls. The Consilium debated two sessions and the record is unusually honest: members stated plainly that Rome was placing the labour supply of its western possessions in the hand of the one power it cannot fight, that Egypt would know the size and destination of every consignment, and that Rome was paying Egypt to keep the western colonies alive. It passed because the gold works were stopping. Eleven thousand persons in seven years — a small volume establishing a dependency that is not small.

**And the emperor's real answer: he moved the centre of the western project off the gold islands entirely.**

---

## VI. Castra Cuzamil, and the Triumph He Refused

The Yucatan reconnaissance ran 528–542 out of **Castra Cuzamil**, the fort on the island of Cuzamil three miles off the mainland shore. Basing on an island was Severianus's own instruction, and his stated reason was the Delta: a garrison on a hostile shore must be relieved; a garrison on an island can be taken off.

The instruments were the balloon and the boat. Tethered balloons from the fort and from vessels standing offshore gave sightlines over the treeline along four hundred miles of coast; small parties went up the coastal lagoons and inland along the trade paths with interpreters bought over eight years, and the language was reduced to a printed grammar by 540. A trading post was licensed at **Statio Chetumalis** on the bay of Chactemal, whose real business was talking to merchants.

What Rome learned is the answer that was wanted. The interior is not one power. It is a landscape of walled cities, forty and more counted or credibly reported, none commanding the others, at war among themselves in shifting combinations, with a common writing, calendar and priesthood and no common king. That is the fractured case, and by the doctrine of the previous generation it is the case in which Rome takes them one at a time.

Rome did not take them, and this is the decision the reign should be judged on.

The reconnaissance also established the physical conditions, set down by the *magister militum* of the west in a memorandum printed for the Consilium: no navigable river into the interior; no draft animals of any kind and no road a wheeled column can use; surface water nearly absent across the northern plain, which drinks from shafts in the rock; forest closing the sightline at thirty paces, exactly where the balloon stops being useful; cities sixty to a hundred miles apart with nothing between that will feed a legion. Conclusion: Rome can destroy any army the country can raise and storm any single city, and cannot hold four at once for a year without a road system taking a generation and a garrison bill larger than Dacia's.

A party at Rome wanted it anyway — a war against people who work no metal, a triumph, forty cities, gold. Severianus refused it before the Senate in 542, on Pelusium and Carrhae, the argument this state has been making to itself for six hundred years and which this time was correct. He wrote to the *magister militum*: *"We may have the war whenever we like. That is exactly why we need not have it now."*

He gave the political audience maize instead. Stacked on the Ukrainian grain it is the slow dismantling of the oldest weapon Egypt holds over Rome, done by farmers who took it up because it pays them. Rome does not sell the seed.

## VII. Coal on the Flumen Novum

Rome's steam warships cannot cross an ocean. Everything in the western programme runs into that sentence, and the answer is not a better engine but a coal pile on the far side.

Prospecting parties went north from Portus Securus in 541 and 542 under orders naming fuel and not gold, entered the great bay of the northern seaboard — **Sinus Magnus Occidentalis**, a drowned estuary two hundred miles deep with four large rivers running into it from the west — and worked up each river with balloons and boats. The third river from the south, which Rome names **Flumen Novum**, gave it: some ninety miles up from tidewater the river breaks over a fall line, and above it, in the autumn of 543, the parties found bituminous coal in workable seams within eight miles of navigable water. That report is the most important document produced in the turn.

The commitment that followed is very large, very fast, and it is the emperor's bet: a coaling port at the river's mouth, a colony and pits at the fall line ninety miles inland, four miles of iron rail between pit and landing, a shipyard on the coastal oak and pine, veteran allotments along the river in the layered pattern that took Europe. All named and dated in the territory declaration.

It nearly failed. In the autumn of 546 the fall-line post — a stockade of some nine hundred, mostly colonists with two centuries of troops — was stormed by the confederated peoples of the river country, who had watched a year of survey parties and drawn an accurate conclusion. Four hundred and ten dead, the stockade burned, the survivors pulled back to tidewater. Rome refounded it in 547 behind a legion detachment of three thousand and a proper wall, and the campaign through 548 was short, one-sided, and not much given to the gazettes.

The pits raised first coal in 549 — eleven thousand tons in the first full year, a rounding error against the Rhine's millions — and in 550 a stationary engine of the second class began turning at Colonia Carbonaria, the first steam engine to run in the western world. Whether the bet pays is not known at 550. What is known is that a Roman steam warship can take coal at Portus Severianus, which was impossible in 543, and that everything Rome intends in the west for the next century runs through a landing ninety miles up the Flumen Novum.

---

## VIII. Iron, the Screw, and the *Fulmen*

The ocean decided two questions that had drifted for fifty years — iron framing and the screw, both in the research declaration. It also killed a ship.

In 543, before the coal was found and against the advice of the *magister classis*, Severianus ordered the first steam crossing attempted with depot ships transferring coal at sea. *Fulmen*, iron-plated, a hundred and twenty crew, lost her depots in weather west of the Insulae Fortunatae and was never seen again. It is the one impatience of an otherwise patient reign, it killed a hundred and twenty men, and it is what turned coal from an industrial ambition into an operational necessity in the emperor's own mind. He did not repeat it, and no steam vessel attempted the crossing again until the Portus Severianus depot was stocked in 549.

The road was built out of two waypoints: **Insulae Accipitrum**, found in 533 by a returning hull that ran too far north and sitting almost exactly midway on the return leg, garrisoned 535 with a depot and stone mole at **Portus Medius**; and the **Insulae Fortunatae**, constituted a province in 531, depot at **Portus Fortunatus**. Neither has coal of its own. Every ton is carried there, which is expensive, and is the price of an ocean.

## IX. Gunpowder

The programme reached a stable ratio in 531 and from 532 was a weapons programme. It went where two centuries of doctrine pointed: an emplaced vessel, because Rome has built fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers since 301 and its engineers reached for the shape they knew.

A hand weapon was attempted once. A *fabricator* of the Argentoratum works, Vibius Crispinus, argued in 543 that a small tube would do what a large one did, and made three. The second burst on its ninth firing and killed him. The programme's finding, entered 544: the metal cannot be made thin enough to carry and strong enough to hold; the hand weapon is a blind alley.

That finding is wrong. The Han carry the thing and Rome knows they carry it. What Rome does not have is any reason to believe the metallurgy is possible; the one man who might have persisted is dead; and the programme has a battery of successful bombards to point at instead. This is exactly how a hundred and eighty years went into pressure vessels, and nobody in the Consilium has noticed the shape repeating.

Rome performed its half of the Pataliputra exchange, and the terms of the performance are in the research declaration.

---

## X. Alexandria: The Turned Branch

The branch quarantined in the previous generation was tested in 537: the *officium* passed into it a specific, false, checkable item — that Rome intended to abandon Castra Cuzamil within two years. In 539 an Egyptian trade official referred to the Roman withdrawal from the mainland shore as a settled matter, in conversation with a factor of a clean branch.

The branch is turned, and Rome has not rolled it up. From 540 it is run as a deliberate channel. Whether Egypt believes any of it is unknown and probably not — a channel Rome knows is turned is a channel Egypt may know Rome knows is turned, and the *officium*'s own memorandum says so. It is nevertheless the first offensive use Rome has made of a compromised network rather than the first burial. The other taskings, and the one man Rome lost to them, are in the declarations below.

---

## XI. The Message to the Han, and Why

Rome's one message went east from Statio Borysthenia in the spring of 548, and the Consilium argued the destination across two sessions.

Alexandria lost the argument. To ask Egypt whose the African coast is would disclose that Rome sailed and looked — and Egypt, which shadowed the survey its whole length in relay and let it go home, already knows Rome sailed and looked and has chosen not to raise it. Asking would tell Egypt only what Rome concluded, which is the one thing Rome has that Egypt does not.

The Han won it on three counts and one ask. The junction reply was prepared a generation ago and never sent, and holding it another twenty-five years wastes a generation of silk revenue; the Dnieper is the only frontier facing the power that could break a Roman army, and a treaty that renews itself still requires that neither party go silent; and the Han asked four times where Rome sailed west, and Rome now has an answer.

The ask is what the emperor wanted and could not buy at home for any money: **the Han measurement of the sphere and the mathematics under it.** Rome's astronomers have telescopes, the phases of Venus, the lights at Jupiter and a heliocentric case settled among the men who look through the glass. What they lack is a method — a way from observation to a number that predicts. The Han measured the earth and reasoned a continent into existence from the answer before any Roman sailed to it. That is not a machine and cannot be copied by watching. It has to be asked for.

Severianus overruled the faction that wanted the west concealed, minuting his reason: the Han deduced the western land before Rome ever went, so they will not learn of its existence from us; and a partner who catches you hiding a thing he already knows has learned nothing about the thing and a great deal about you.

## XII. Britannia, the Vectigal, and the Constitutio Oceanica

This is what the reign cost, and the court has not understood that it cost anything.

The *vectigal occidentale* was laid on the western trade at its port of entry, on the excellent principle that the new thing has no defenders. The new thing turned out to have one.

Britannia and Hibernia build the Atlantic hulls, the yards being at Isca, Londinium and Gesoriacum where the timber and iron and coal are, and they crew them from the sailors of the Iberia and Iceland runs. By 540 over half the tonnage on the western run was Britannic-built and Britannic-crewed, and the shipping houses of Londinium had formed a combine that dealt with the *procurator* as an equal.

In 542 the rate was raised from one-eighth to one-fifth to fund Portus Severianus, the coal colony and the iron fleet. The Britannic houses did three things. They petitioned in print, on the argument that produced a usurpation in 355 and has never been answered since: British revenue funds imperial works while British infrastructure receives less than it generates. They under-declared, at a scale the *procurator* of Londinium put at a third of cargo. And they moved the landfall, running the western convoys into **Portus Magnus** in southern Hibernia and into the Clyde estuary — neither a named port of entry, both a short coastal hop from the named ones, the duty establishment at each consisting of one *procurator* and eleven men.

Rome refused the petition in 544. In the summer of 546 a customs squadron out of Isca seized three Britannic hulls at Portus Magnus. The crews were taken off by a crowd the port magistrates declined to disperse; the magistrates wrote to the governor of Britannia, who declined to send troops against the shipping carrying his own province's trade and wrote to Rome saying so.

There was no revolt. That is the point, and it is worse. What the incident established is that Rome cannot enforce a fiscal law against Britannia without a naval action against its own province, at a moment when every ocean-capable hull Rome owns is committed to the western run and the fleet cannot be in two oceans.

Rome settled in 547 with the **Constitutio Oceanica**: the western trade licensed through a chartered body, the *Societas Oceani Occidentalis*, seated at Londinium and holding the exclusive right of carriage on the run; registered hulls paying one part in eight where unregistered pay one in four, against open books, imperial inspection and carriage of imperial cargo at fixed rates; Portus Magnus and the Clyde constituted ports of entry; and the *Societas* granted legal personality and bounded liability under the Concordia Fabricarum — under the law Severianus himself had made three years before.

The fisc counted it a victory and it collected: the *vectigal* yielded almost nothing in 546 and ninety-two millions in 550, against a projection of two hundred and twenty, a shortfall the Consilium blames on under-declaration and expects to close.

What it actually did: by 550 roughly two-thirds of Atlantic tonnage is Britannic-registered, Britannic-crewed and Britannic-built, and the whole of Rome's ocean commerce is organized in one chartered corporation with legal personality, bounded liability, a monopoly of the route, its own capital, its own ships and its own men — seated on the far side of the one water Rome's rail cannot cross, in the province that has already made an emperor once. Not one paper in the Consilium's record connects the Constitutio Oceanica to the events of 355. The court spent its attention on the Rhine, won there, and paid for it here without noticing the transaction.

## XIII. The State of the Empire, 550 AD

**Population** about one hundred and twenty-seven millions in the old world; some four hundred and fifty thousand Romans and Romanized in the west, against a subjugated island population fallen from above four hundred thousand to fewer than seventy.

**Revenue** about 5,300 millions: land tax 1,520; industry with the *collatio fabricarum* 1,610; trade and customs 940; mining 470; luxury duties 300; other levies 320; *vectigal occidentale* 92; western gold and produce 110. **Expenditure**: military and frontier 3,300 (62 percent, including western garrisons and the ocean fleet); administration 640; infrastructure 470; social 280; research 165; **free margin about 445**.

The margin is restored, and it was restored by taxation and not by the mint. The coin has not been touched in the reign and Severianus has said publicly that it will not be.

**Technical workforce** about six hundred thousand; literacy about seventy-two percent. Fifty-two steam warships, twelve iron-hulled and nineteen screw-driven; the first steam crossing of the western ocean in 550; emplaced bombards at seven named positions; no hand firearm and a formal finding that none is possible; the western coal chain opened; maize ripening in Pannonia; eleven hundred methods on the *privilegium fabricae* register.

**Succession**: Severianus, sixty-two in 550, invested his sister's son Aulus Antonius Gratianus as Caesar in 548 and, more importantly, constituted the **Consilium Sacrum** — praetorian prefects, the Patriarch, the *magister militum* and four members of the Industrial Council, holding a fixed and printed role in investiture. Its purpose, he said, is to make an emperor unnecessary for a year at a time; he admitted taking the idea from the Han, who told Rome a century and a half ago that their succession is held by a bureaucracy enduring through weak emperors alike. Whether it works nobody will know until a bad emperor tests it.

# DECLARATIONS

## MESSAGE OUT

**To the Court of the Han, from Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus. Carried from Statio Borysthenia on the Dnieper, spring of 548.**

To the Emperor of the Han and to his ministers, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

*On the junction of the rails.* We accept the joining of our lines at the Dnieper, under the agreement standing between us, which renews itself and which we do not ask to change — as a yard of transfer, not a joining of roads. Our gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and we will not relay it; we do not ask you to relay yours. Your cars will come to the eastern bank and stop, ours will stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods will cross where wheels do not. The reason is honest and we state it rather than dress it: a rail running from one empire onto the other is a road for an army, and neither of us would lay the other's gauge inside his own ground. The yard will stand on the Roman bank under Roman garrison at Statio Borysthenia; your factors will work in it under Roman law exactly as our merchants are protected in your territory; and we will build the sheds, cranes and sidings at our own cost, because we want the silk carried rail to rail more than we want the argument over who pays.

*On the gifts offered.* We receive the mechanical clock and its escapement with thanks and will set it beside our own work, which has reached the accuracy of navigation and not yet beyond. We return the method of paper with respect and a plain statement: Rome has made paper from linen and flax for five hundred and fifty years and printed with cast type for four hundred and fifty. We say so not to diminish the gift but because a man who accepts a gift he does not need has told his friend a small lie for politeness, and small lies compound at this distance.

*On the question you have asked four times.* We answer in full, and you will not have to ask a fifth.

Rome has sailed west. Ships leave the islands we call the Fortunate Isles at twenty-eight degrees north, where the wind blows steadily west and a great current runs west beneath the hull, and together they make a road that carries a ship whether she wishes it or not. Six weeks on that road brings her to a chain of green mountainous islands, and west of them a coast, which our ships have followed more than a thousand miles without finding its end either way.

The lands hold peoples: island farmers of cassava and maize, skilled at sea in canoes cut from single trees; on a stone shore to the west, walled cities with their own writing, calendar, priesthood and merchants, forty and more, at war among themselves and under no single king; further north, scattered fishing and farming peoples of no great wealth. In all that country we have found no metal worked at all — not iron, not bronze, not copper, not as tool or weapon or ornament. Rome has taken the two largest islands and holds them, holds a fortified island off the stone coast, and has planted a station on a northern river for timber and provisioning. We tell you because you will hear it, and because a man who conceals what his friend already knows has concealed nothing and revealed himself.

We do not send a chart of our positions, and we tell you why rather than let you infer it. Two hundred and fifty years ago Rome gave your envoys at this same river a full survey of Roman roads, cities, forts and supply lines, and received a sketch without scale. Rome recorded that as its own error and has not repeated it with any power since. We hold to the rule with you not because we distrust you more than others but because we distrust ourselves in this particular way, having been foolish once in exactly this place.

*On the thing we ask.* Your envoys told ours that you had measured the sphere of the earth, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned the western land into existence before any ship of ours touched it. We ask for that — not the number, which is a curiosity, but the method and the mathematics beneath it: how the measurement is made, what is assumed, and how one reasons from a measured sphere to a place no man has seen. We ask further for the works of your astronomers and calculators, such as you are willing to send.

We offer what we have of the same kind: the construction of our telescopes, glass, figure and mounting, complete; the observations made with them, that Venus shows phases as the moon does and that small lights move about Jupiter and keep to it; and the argument these have raised among us, that the inherited model of a fixed earth cannot hold them and that the sun and not the earth may stand at the centre. Our schoolmen resist it. The men who look through the glass do not. We send you the dispute along with the observations, because a dispute is worth more than a conclusion.

*On residence.* We ask that not more than ten Roman men of learning reside at your capital, and offer the same number of yours the same at Rome. A man who lives among you twenty years will understand you and a man who visits twenty days will not, and every misunderstanding between us for two hundred and fifty years was born in that gap.

*On the boundary.* The Dnieper stands. We want nothing east of it, have moved no soldier across it, and will not without prior word to you. Your people on the eastern bank have grown into settled towns as ours have on the western, and we take that as two peoples farming and nothing else — and we say so plainly, so no clerk on either bank has to guess what we take it as.

Severianus Augustus.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Alexandria network — turned branch.** Founded c. 300 AD, 250 years old; branch compromised before 525, confirmed turned by controlled-item test 537–539. Personnel: the existing compromised agents, unreplaced, unwarned. Cover: commercial factors in grain and coffee, unchanged. Target: not collection — deliberate feed. Items passed 539–550: the intended abandonment of Castra Cuzamil (which surfaced in 539 and is how the branch was confirmed); island commitment overstated; northern-coast commitment understated; coal reports called disappointing; Portus Severianus called a timber station. Run by the *officium custodiae*, on the reasoning that a feed is a publication and not an operation.

**2. Alexandria network — clean branches.** Same founding, 250 years. Personnel: seven principal factors, Roman and Greek houses of Ostia, Puteoli and Antioch with legitimate business in the port. Cover: grain, coffee, dyestuffs, marine insurance. Targets in priority: size, tonnage and heading of the Egyptian shipbuilding surge reported from 520; any sign of westward provisioning out of Alexandria or through the canal; composition and destination of the northbound Atlantic column; identity of the southern delegations received at the administrative quarter.

**3. The African labour chain.** New, licensed 543, 7 years old. Personnel: the house of Vibulenus of Ostia, a genuine licensed buyer of African labour at Alexandria, and its factor Marcus Vibulenus Priscus. Cover: none required — the house is what it says and the purchases are real. Target: trace the chain backward from the Alexandria factors to its origin, and establish whether the coast of the southern survey and the labour source are the same authority. **Status: Priscus travelled south with a consignment escort in 546 and did not return. Nothing was said beyond that he had left the licensed route. No replacement sent.**

**4. The Dnieper watch.** Founded c. 300 AD with the frontier contact, 250 years old; redirected 525 from the Chinese firearm to logistics. Personnel: frontier *exploratores* and licensed caravan factors on the western bank, plus balloon observation from Borysthenia. Cover: customs and caravan administration, which is genuine work. Targets: tonnage from the Syr Darya rail terminus and its growth; depth and permanence of Han civilian settlement within two days of the eastern bank; whether any of it carries military stores. Findings 550: caravan volume from a dozen a year to over thirty; a settled Han strip of forty to sixty thousand within two days of the river.

**5. Presence at Barygaza and the Indian yards.** New, 528, 22 years old. Not an espionage network and not run as one. Personnel: fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus, sent openly under the steam exchange; three still resident in 550. Cover: none — they are exactly what they appear to be. Target: standing observation of Indian yard capacity, metallurgy, and any sighting of cannon founding or powder work. The *officium* rates them the best-placed Roman eyes in the world precisely because nobody is hiding anything from them.

**6. The officium custodiae — counter-intelligence, domestic.** Founded 536, 14 years old. Instruments: crew and passenger registers at the six named western ports; licensing of chart-makers and chart-sellers; sealed sailing orders opened at sea; classification of western latitudes, soundings and coal reports. Prior-notification of publication imposed 536, withdrawn for prices, cargoes and losses in 540 after the gazettes refused registration and the Council backed them; retained for positions, soundings, charts and coal, and unenforceable. **Western desk, from 547:** audit of the register and books of the *Societas Oceani Occidentalis* at Londinium under the open-books clause of the Constitutio Oceanica. Rome does not call this an intelligence tasking against a Roman province. It is one.

## RESEARCH

**Gunpowder and ordnance — 310 millions.** Stable ratio 531; weaponization from 532; first cast-bronze bombard at Argentoratum 538; emplaced batteries by 550 at Borysthenia, the Dacian corridor forts, Gades, Gibraltar, Cyrenaica and Portus Severianus. **Believed achieved:** siege and fortress artillery reducing stone in hours rather than weeks. **Formal finding entered 544 and believed by the programme:** a hand-carried tube is impossible, the metal being unable to be thin enough to carry and strong enough to hold — entered after the death of the *fabricator* Vibius Crispinus on a burst trial piece. Rome has no hand firearm and no expectation of one.

**Naval, iron hull and screw — 690 millions, the largest line of the turn.** Iron-framed construction from *Constantia*, Isca, 541; twelve in service by 550. Screw ordered for all ocean-service construction 544; nineteen screw hulls by 550, above half a paddle wheel's efficiency in flat water and superior in weather. Boiler and range folded in: from six or seven hundred nautical miles toward nine hundred to a thousand on a full load; first successful steam crossing of the western ocean by *Aquila Occidentalis*, spring 550, twenty-two days.

**Western fuel survey — 240 millions**, colonial establishment charged elsewhere. Parties to the Sinus Magnus Occidentalis 541–542 under orders naming fuel, not gold. Bituminous coal above the fall line of the Flumen Novum, autumn 543, within eight miles of navigable water. Pits opened 545–547; first coal raised 549, eleven thousand tons in the first full year; four-mile iron rail 548–549; first western stationary engine at Colonia Carbonaria 550.

**Natural philosophy patronage — 40 millions.** Observatories at Rome, Cyrene, Colonia Agrippina and Borysthenia; telescopes improved to greater aperture on the industrial sheet-glass furnaces; phases of Venus and the Jovian lights confirmed and printed repeatedly; heliocentrism settled among the observers and disputed by the schoolmen. **Believed achieved: nothing that predicts.** The 546 submission states that Rome can see what the inherited model cannot hold, cannot put a number to what should replace it, and that the deficiency is mathematical rather than optical — which is the reason for the ask carried to the Han. The emperor's margin note on that submission reads *"too small."*

**Agronomy, the western grains — 55 millions.** Trial stations under the *curatores agrorum* in Baetica, Campania, Pannonia, the Danube bend, the Dacian lowland and the black earth behind the Dnieper, with printed method sheets. **Believed achieved:** strains ripening reliably in Pannonia and on the Dacian lowland by 550 that would not have ripened north of Baetica in 530. Cassava and sweet potato in the western islands and southern Iberia only. Seed and method not traded to any peer; reaffirmed 534 and 546.

**Medicine and quarantine — 20 millions. Result: failed.** Cyprian protocols in the west from 538, forty-day hold at Portus Securus and licensed contact only. The *procuratores* report in writing that the sickness moves ahead of Roman parties along native trade paths and is not stopped by a forty-day wall. No theory of cause exists. Tallies maintained and printed.

**Communications — 45 millions.** Pneumatic tubes from the Rhine administrative centre to Rome, Londinium, Lugdunum and Borysthenia; optical telegraph with telescopes along the Dnieper line and the Atlantic coast. No progress on whatever Egypt actually has.

**The steam exchange with Pataliputra — charged to the naval line.** Fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus sailed Berenice to Barygaza in 528 with the complete method of the hull engine and built the first beside Indian engineers over four years; three remain resident in 550. Rome performed. What returns, and when, is not Rome's to enter in its own record.

Total directed research across the turn: about 1,400 millions, against a standing line of about 165 millions a year by 550.

## TERRITORY AT 550 — HELD, AND NOT HELD AT 525

**Held at 550, not held at 525:**

- **Insula Aurea** (Hispaniola), entire. Seat at **Colonia Ozamae**, mouth of the river Ozama, southern shore. Gold works at **Aurifodinae Cibaenses** in the Cibao interior. Veteran allotments on the Vega Real and the southern coastal plain.
- **Insula Longa** (Cuba), entire, reduced 536. **Portus Securus** on the enclosed northern bay; **Vallis Cauti**, settled valley of the river Cauto in the south-east.
- **Insula Sancti Iohannis** (Puerto Rico), taken 533, garrison harbour at **Portus Sancti Iohannis**.
- **Insula Fontium** (Jamaica), taken 537, garrison and water station at **Portus Fontium**.
- **Castra Cuzamil**, fortified island base off the Yucatan shore, held since 523, permanent stone works from 531.
- **Statio Chetumalis**, licensed trading post on the bay of Chactemal, mainland Yucatan shore. A walled post of two hundred — not a colony, and explicitly not a claim on the interior.
- **Portus Severianus**, founded 544 at the mouth of the **Flumen Novum**, southern bank, on the **Sinus Magnus Occidentalis**. Walled 546. Fleet coaling and repair station; shipyard from 547.
- **Colonia Carbonaria**, founded 545 at the fall line of the Flumen Novum, ninety miles inland. Burned 546, refounded 547 behind a legion detachment. Coal pits, four-mile iron rail, first western steam engine 550. Veteran allotments between Carbonaria and tidewater.
- **Insulae Fortunatae** (Canaries), constituted a province 531, depot at **Portus Fortunatus**.
- **Insulae Accipitrum** (mid-ocean, on the return road), found 533, largest island garrisoned 535, depot and mole at **Portus Medius**.

**Charted, not held:** the northern seaboard north and south of the Sinus, including the peninsula Rome calls **Ora Florida**; the three other great rivers of the Sinus; the Lesser Antilles; the Yucatan interior and its forty-odd walled cities; the Bahaman shoals.

**Not approached, formally closed:** the African Atlantic coast south of the Mauretanian provinces. The probe remains suspended; no Roman vessel went below that latitude this turn.

**Unchanged:** the old-world frontier in every direction, Ireland to the Dnieper and Scotland to the North African provinces. Not one mile taken, not one lost, no Roman soldier across the Dnieper or the Euphrates.
