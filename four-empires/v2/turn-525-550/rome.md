# ROME, 525–550 AD
## The Reign of Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus

---

## I. The Accession (526–528)

Iulianus Augustus died at Mediolanum in the winter of 526, of the lung complaint the physicians record for any man who has spent thirty years breathing Colonia Agrippina. He left no son and, for once, no crisis: Consilium, Senate and Industrial Council had all been brought to one name before he died. The name was Gaius Antonius Severianus, thirty-nine, of an old senatorial house of Campania, married into the Rhine iron families of the Colonia district, and by profession a man of the fisc — eleven years *rationalis* of the western customs, four as *praefectus annonae*. He knew what was in the accounts because he had written much of it.

Acclaimed in the spring of 527, he did not campaign, build or distribute. He had the western programme's true costs consolidated into one ledger against the military line, the frontier line and the reserve, and read aloud to the Consilium in a single sitting.

The reading did not produce the alarm the western programme's opponents had hoped for, and Severianus said so in the room. The whole American establishment — hulls, garrisons, colonist transport, ports, prospecting — was running at something near a hundred millions a year against a revenue of four thousand, and would rise toward a hundred and eighty as the coal work began. Set against a free margin of two hundred and eighty millions, that is a programme the empire can carry without touching a single standing line. What it cannot do is carry that programme *and* the rising military line of the Han arms race *and* the iron-hull and screw work *and* leave anything over. The margin would not go negative. It would go to nothing, and stay at nothing for twenty years, and a state with no margin at all is a state that meets its next bad harvest, its next epidemic and its next usurpation out of the reserve and the mint.

His conclusion, recorded by the Consilium's clerk:

> *"We are not poor. We are fully committed, which is a different thing and has been the ordinary condition of this state under every emperor whose accounts I have read. Thirty-six sesterces a head, and the land still carries better than a third of it, in an empire where the land is no longer where the money is made. I do not know what any other prince takes from his people; I have never audited a foreign treasury and neither has any man in this room, and I will not have that ignorance dressed up as a comparison. I know what we take from ours. I know the Rhine valley is assessed on what it was and produces what it is. That is a Roman number, out of Roman books, and it is the only kind I intend to argue from."*

Clipping the coin, he said, is what a state does when the ground will not yield, and Roman ground was not the problem. A debased denarius in an empire where every foreman reads a price sheet is not a quiet theft but a printed grievance with the emperor's face on it. The coin was not touched in the reign, and he said publicly it would not be.

## II. The Fiscal Argument (528–535)

Four instruments, in deliberate order, easiest first. None of them was a rescue and the record should not be allowed to remember them as one. Rome was funding new work out of a large budget with a thin margin, which is uncomfortable, and normal, and has been the shape of this state since the plunder line went to zero two hundred and twenty-five years ago.

**The *vectigal occidentale*, 528.** A duty of one part in eight on all goods of the western ocean at landing — gold, cacao, cotton, hides, dyes, the burning leaf, the new dyewoods — levied at named ports of entry only (Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, Portus Namnetum, Londinium, Dubris), landing anywhere else being contraband. Tax the new thing; the new thing has no defenders yet. Projected at two hundred and twenty millions a year by 545. It did not reach that, for reasons in section XII.

**The *indictio maritima*, 529.** A five-year term levy on the coastal provinces profiting from the run — Baetica, Lusitania, Aquitania, Britannia, Hibernia — assessed on registered shipping tonnage rather than on land, on the plain argument that the trade and not the soil was the new gain. Renewed in 534 and 539, allowed to lapse in 544 when the *Societas* settlement replaced it.

**Advances against the western receipts, 530.** The tax-farming *societates* were sold the right to collect the *vectigal* for five-year terms, paid in full at the front, the great shipping houses buying in beside them: four hundred and sixty millions of ready money against a nominal future value near six hundred. This is not borrowing and Rome cannot borrow, having no bond market and no conception of one. It is the oldest revenue instrument the Republic ever had, applied to the newest revenue, at the usual discount for taking the risk of collection. The fisc has done it for six centuries and thought nothing of it.

**Then the argument he chose to have: the *collatio fabricarum*, 531–534.** A standing assessment on industrial output — factory, mine, foundry, rail, steam shipping — at a real rate, empire-wide, replacing the ragged patchwork of provincial levies the Industrial Council had spent a century keeping ragged.

It should be said plainly what this was and was not. It was not needed for the west; the west fits inside the existing margin. It was wanted because the military and frontier line was climbing with the Han arms race and the iron fleet, because land at thirty-five percent of revenue is an assessment on an empire that no longer exists, and because Severianus would not preside over twenty years at zero margin when the largest and fastest-growing part of the Roman economy was paying on rolls drawn up by provincial assessors two generations behind the works they were assessing. It was, in short, a choice to raise revenue rather than to stop doing things, made by a man of the fisc who thought the assessment was simply wrong.

It was also a bill presented to the constituency that makes emperors. The succession of 318 was won by a Rhine general on industrial money, and everyone in the room knew it.

He did not decree it. He sold it, and paid in law rather than coin, which is the only currency Rome has ever had in surplus. The Council understood exactly what it was being asked for and priced it accordingly; the negotiation ran three years and twice broke down. What the Industrial Council extracted, enacted as the **Concordia Fabricarum** of 534:

- **Legal personality for the registered *societas*** — a chartered company distinct in law from its shareholders, able to hold property, sue, be sued and outlive its founders, with the shareholder's liability bounded at his subscription. Every great mine, factory, rail and shipping venture had been asking for this since the courts began improvising industrial law two centuries earlier.
- **The *privilegium fabricae*** — imperial protection of a registered method or machine for twenty years, enforced in every provincial court from one printed register at Rome, in exchange for deposit and publication at term's end.
- **Formal standing** — the Council's advisory remit converted into a right of prior consultation on any fiscal or commercial measure, its objections entered in the record and printed.

That is a hard price. Rome sold a permanent constitutional position to a private constituency for a tax line. Severianus's minute on it is unsentimental: *"They have taken payment in law because law is what they could not buy from a weaker emperor, and it will not expire when I do. I have not bought a tax. I have sold them a seat, and I have taken the tax as the price of the seat."*

Rome received the *collatio*, yielding above three hundred millions a year by 545 and rising with output rather than with the assessor's mood — the first Roman revenue line in three centuries that grows when the economy grows without anyone going out to re-measure it. What made it survivable in the Senate: he raised the land assessment on the great estates at the same session, modestly, saying openly that he did so the industrialists could not claim they alone were singled out. The senatorial houses stayed furious, and the old families never again put a man of theirs at his disposal. He knew the price when he paid it.

**A second thing the *collatio* produced, which nobody asked for.** An assessment on output requires knowing the output. To levy it, Rome had to count the works — engines, furnaces, shafts, looms, hands, horsepower — and the returns filed under the *collatio* and audited by the new inspectorate are the first genuine industrial census the empire has ever taken. Rome had been estimating its own technical workforce from guild rolls and army returns. It now counts it. The numbers in section XIV rest on that count, and they came in higher than the fisc had assumed.

## III. The Rhine, and the One Thing He Saw

The labour settlement of the 350s was built to last two generations and had lasted seven. Against a working class at eighty-five to ninety percent literacy in the Rhine cities, organizing by printed sheet, it was no longer a settlement. It was a lid.

Severianus saw this — and he saw it *because the money forced him into the room*. He could not have the *collatio* without negotiating with the industrial magnates, and he could not do that in the Rhine valley in the 530s without the Rhine valley's workers reading every word in the gazettes and drawing their own conclusion about who was being asked to pay. He did not go looking for the labour question. It was standing in the doorway of the room where the money was.

The second half of the Concordia, 534:

- **Collegia given standing to bargain, not merely to sue** — a registered collegium may treat with the owners on hours, wages and conditions, and the agreement is enforceable in the provincial courts as a contract.
- **The *curatores fabricarum*** — a standing inspectorate, salaried from the *collatio*, with right of entry to any registered works and power to close a shaft or a floor pending appeal. Two hundred and forty men at foundation, six hundred by 550, concentrated in Britannia, the Rhine, Silesia and the Dacian fields. They also compile the output returns, which is why the industrialists tolerated them.
- **The accident fund** — levied on the industrialists at a fixed fraction of the *collatio*, paying on a printed schedule rather than at a magistrate's discretion.

The Patriarch of Rome blessed the Concordia in a published homily arguing the case in the language of human dignity rather than productivity — the first time since the labour law of the 350s, a hundred and eighty years, that a Roman labour measure was defended on any ground but the state's convenience — and the clergy of the industrial cities carried it into every congregation. It is not clear the emperor believed a word of the theology. It is entirely clear he understood what it was worth: the Church delivered a settlement no number of *curatores* could have enforced.

The Rhine did not rise. That is the reign's quiet achievement and nobody will ever build a monument to it.

## IV. The Officium Custodiae, and the Quarrel with the Sheets

One conclusion had troubled the Consilium since 523: that word of the western islands reached Alexandria within months of the first fleet's return. Rome has never been able to check that conclusion and cannot check it now — it rests on a merchant's remark reported by a Roman factor and on the timing of a shipbuilding surge Rome saw from a harbour. But nobody had ever been made to account for how such a thing *could* travel, and the audit of that question was overdue whatever the truth of the original report.

Severianus founded the **officium custodiae** in 536, on the reasoning that any such leak would be commercial rather than treasonous. Its instruments are listed in the declarations; they were dull and correct except one — prior notification before any publication touching the western voyages, their positions, cargoes or losses.

The gazettes went to war. The *Acta Rhenana*, the *Diurna Londiniensia* and eleven other sheets refused registration and printed the refusal, arguing property and precedent rather than liberty, which is not a Roman category: no law had ever licensed a printer, and a magistrate who can suppress a shipwreck can suppress a peculating governor. The Industrial Council backed them for the price news; the Patriarch's men backed them because the western clergy were publishing things the *officium* wanted stopped. Severianus retreated in 540 with visible bad grace, keeping the rule only for positions, soundings, charts and coal, where it is unenforceable.

The *officium*'s unpublished survey drew the conclusion that matters: an empire like this one cannot be sealed, because Rome's information does not leak through traitors but through the machinery that makes Rome work — competing newspapers, share markets, insurance underwriters who must be told what they insure, crews paid off in six ports, and an open foreign legation in the capital. Do not seal. Feed. Section X is what that produced.

## V. The West: The Islands, and the Sickness

The islands were finished as a military matter early — Insula Aurea by 531, Insula Longa by 536, Insula Sancti Iohannis in 533, Insula Fontium in 537 — and the colonial architecture is named in the territory declaration. Then came the thing nobody wrote into the plan.

Sickness follows our ships. It takes the islanders and largely spares our crews and colonists. It appears in a village after our people have been in it and not before, and it moves ahead of our people along the trade paths faster than our people move. The *procuratores* of Ozama and Portus Securus have tallied since 532, because Rome tallies everything, and the tallies are not ambiguous: the subjugated population of the two great islands, above four hundred thousand when the conquest began, is under seventy thousand in 550. Valleys of the Cibao that fed the gold works in 535 are empty ground in 548.

Rome cannot say why. Our physicians have no theory of any of this and were publicly wrong once already, having called the crossing fevers common flu. But a line does not need a cause to be extended: at the observed rate the labour force the whole island economy was built on will not exist in a generation. The Cyprian quarantines applied from 538 did not work, and the *procuratores* say in writing why they cannot: a forty-day wall stops a ship, and this does not travel by ship once it is ashore.

**A moral quarrel Rome did not expect.** The western clergy began publishing accounts of the dying villages in the Roman sheets from about 541, and the argument was theological and therefore unanswerable in an empire that had just made human dignity the language of its labour law. The bishop of Ozama's letter of 546 was read in three basilicas at Rome and printed in four cities. It changed no policy. The record contains no earlier document accusing the imperial economy, in print, of killing the people it employs, and the men who made the Concordia understood at once what had been handed to the collegia of the Rhine.

**The labour question, and what Rome has actually done about it.** The gold works are stopping for want of hands. Rome's own reasoning about where hands could come from runs into one place: the African trade, which reaches Rome only through Alexandria, and whose Atlantic coast Rome has concluded — on a temple façade seen through a glass two miles offshore, which section XV notes is thin evidence — may be Egyptian or Egyptian-subject ground.

The Consilium debated it across four sessions between 542 and 543 and the record is unusually honest. Members stated plainly that to buy labour through Alexandria is to place the labour supply of Rome's western possessions in the hand of the one power Rome cannot fight; that any such purchase discloses the size and destination of every consignment to the seller; and that Rome would be paying a rival to keep Roman colonies alive. Against that: the works stop otherwise.

Rome resolved to make the attempt. It licensed the house of Vibulenus of Ostia as buyer, instructed it to open the purchase at Alexandria, and included the request in the letter to Egypt carried in 545 (declarations, Message Out, Egypt). **Whether that purchase is permitted, on what terms, in what volume, and at what price is not Rome's to record and Rome does not record it.** The Consilium's contingency, minuted, is that if it is refused or priced beyond bearing, the western economy is rebuilt around the northern coal coast, where the work is mechanizable, rather than around the gold creeks, where it is not. That is the emperor's preference in any case.

**And that is his real answer: he moved the centre of the western project off the gold islands entirely.**

## VI. Castra Cuzamil, and the Triumph He Refused

The Yucatan reconnaissance ran 528–542 out of **Castra Cuzamil**, on the island three miles off the mainland shore. Basing on an island was Severianus's own instruction, and his stated reason was the Delta: a garrison on a hostile shore must be relieved; a garrison on an island can be taken off.

The instruments were the balloon and the boat. Tethered balloons from the fort and from vessels standing offshore gave sightlines over the treeline along four hundred miles of coast; small parties went up the coastal lagoons and inland along the trade paths with interpreters trained over eight years, and the language was reduced to a printed grammar by 540. A trading post was licensed at **Statio Chetumalis** on the bay of Chactemal, whose real business was talking to merchants.

What Rome learned is the answer that was wanted. The interior is not one power but a landscape of walled cities, forty and more counted or credibly reported, none commanding the others, at war among themselves, with a common writing, calendar and priesthood and no common king. That is the fractured case, and by the doctrine of the previous generation it is the case in which Rome takes them one at a time.

Rome did not take them, and this is the decision the reign should be judged on.

The reconnaissance also established the physical conditions, in a memorandum by the *magister militum* of the west printed for the Consilium: no navigable river into the interior; no draft animals and no road a wheeled column can use; surface water nearly absent across the northern plain, which drinks from shafts in the rock; forest closing the sightline at thirty paces, exactly where the balloon stops being useful; cities sixty to a hundred miles apart with nothing between that will feed a legion. Conclusion: Rome can destroy any army the country can raise and storm any single city, and cannot hold four at once for a year without a road system taking a generation and a garrison bill larger than Dacia's.

A party at Rome wanted it anyway — a war against people who work no metal, a triumph, forty cities, gold. Severianus refused it before the Senate in 542, on Pelusium and Carrhae, the argument this state has been making to itself for six hundred years and which this time was correct. He wrote to the *magister militum*: *"We may have the war whenever we like. That is exactly why we need not have it now."*

He gave the political audience maize instead, and the gazettes made more of it than the *curatores agrorum* would sign. What Rome actually has at 550 is a working crop in the warm provinces — Baetica, Africa, Sicily, Campania, southern Gaul — and trial plots further north that ripen in a good year and fail in a bad one. The adaptation of a warm-country grain to a short northern season is the work of selecting seed from whatever ripens earliest, year on year, and it moves at the speed of harvests and not of engineers. The trial station at the Danube bend has fifteen crop generations behind it and reports that it is perhaps a third of the way. The honest forecast entered for the Consilium is a generation to three before maize is a reliable crop on the Pannonian and Dacian plains, and the emperor let the gazettes run ahead of it because the political value was in the promise. Stacked on the Ukrainian grain it will one day be the slow dismantling of the oldest weapon Egypt holds over Rome. It is not that yet. Rome does not sell the seed, and reaffirmed that in 534 and 546.

## VII. Coal on the Flumen Novum

Rome's steam warships cannot cross an ocean. Everything in the western programme runs into that sentence, and the answer is not a better engine but a coal pile on the far side.

Prospecting parties went north from Portus Securus in 541 and 542 under orders naming fuel and not gold, entered the great bay of the northern seaboard — **Sinus Magnus Occidentalis**, a drowned estuary two hundred miles deep with four large rivers running into it from the west — and worked up each river with balloons and boats. The southernmost of the four, which Rome names **Flumen Novum**, gave it: some ninety miles up from tidewater the river breaks over a fall line, and above it, in the autumn of 543, the parties found bituminous coal in workable seams within eight miles of navigable water. That report is the most important document produced in the turn.

The commitment that followed is very large, very fast, and it is the emperor's bet: a coaling port at the river's mouth, a colony and pits at the fall line ninety miles inland, four miles of iron rail between pit and landing, a shipyard on the coastal oak and pine, veteran allotments along the river in the layered pattern that took Europe. All named and dated in the territory declaration.

It nearly failed. In the autumn of 546 the fall-line post — a stockade of some nine hundred, mostly colonists with two centuries of troops — was stormed by the confederated peoples of the river country, who had watched a year of survey parties and drawn an accurate conclusion about what the survey was for. Four hundred and ten dead, the stockade burned, the survivors pulled back to tidewater. Rome refounded it in 547 behind a legion detachment of three thousand and a proper wall; the campaign through 548 was short, one-sided, and not much given to the gazettes.

The pits raised first coal in 549 — eleven thousand tons in the first full year, a rounding error against the Rhine's millions — and in 550 a stationary engine began turning at Colonia Carbonaria, the first Rome knows of anywhere west of the ocean. Whether the bet pays is not known at 550. What is known is that a Roman steam warship can take coal at Portus Severianus, which was impossible in 543, and that everything Rome intends in the west for the next century runs through a landing ninety miles up the Flumen Novum.

## VIII. Iron, the Screw, the Deviation, and the *Fulmen*

The ocean decided two questions that had drifted for fifty years — iron framing and the screw, both in the research declaration. It also killed a ship, and it taught Rome a thing about the compass that Rome cannot explain.

**The arithmetic of the crossing, stated plainly, because the Consilium got it wrong once.** The Fortunate Isles to the western islands is some two thousand seven hundred miles on the wind road. A warship's coal at 550 carries her nine hundred to a thousand. No improvement in engines within sight closes that, and the mid-ocean depot at Insulae Accipitrum sits on the *return* road to the north and cannot be reached outbound against the trades. The ocean is not crossed under steam and will not be in this generation.

In 543, before the coal was found and against the advice of the *magister classis*, Severianus ordered the gap bridged by depot ships transferring coal at sea. *Fulmen*, iron-framed, a hundred and twenty crew, lost her depots in weather west of the Insulae Fortunatae and was never seen again. Coaling at sea is entered in the record as failed. It is the one impatience of an otherwise patient reign, it killed a hundred and twenty men, and it is what turned coal on the far shore from an industrial ambition into an operational necessity in the emperor's own mind.

What works is the thing the *magister classis* had proposed in the first place, and *Aquila Occidentalis* proved it in the spring of 550: **a full-rigged iron-framed screw hull carrying coal for calms and manoeuvre, sailing the wind road under canvas with the screw lifted, and steaming only where the wind fails or the harbour is tight.** Twenty-two days out, against six weeks for a pure sailer on the same road. Rome does not call this a steam crossing and the gazettes were corrected when they did. It is a sailing passage with an engine aboard, and it is worth more than a steam crossing would be, because it needs no depot in the middle of an ocean.

**The deviation.** From 544 the masters of the iron-framed hulls began coming in wrong. Landfalls off by twenty and thirty miles on the Iceland and Fortunate Isles runs, in ships with good clocks and good sights and crews who had made the run for years, and never on the wooden hulls in company. The pilot Quintus Naevius Balbus of *Constantia* established the shape of it in 546 by the only method Rome has: he swung his ship through the whole circle of the compass at a known anchorage and read the bearing of a fixed mark at each heading, and found the error was not constant but changed with the ship's own head, largest on north and south, near nothing on east and west. He did it again in three other iron hulls and got the same shape with different magnitudes. Rome has no theory of magnetism and no explanation whatever for why a hull of iron should pull a needle. It has, from 548, a printed **deviation table struck for each iron hull individually at her fitting-out**, sworn by her master, corrected annually, and now required by the *magister classis* before an iron hull is cleared for ocean service. This is Rome's whole method in one page: an effect nobody understands, measured, tabulated, printed, and turned into a standing order within four years.

The road was built on two waypoints, **Insulae Accipitrum** and the **Insulae Fortunatae**, both dated in the territory declaration. Neither has coal of its own. Every ton is carried there, which is expensive, and is the price of an ocean.

## IX. Gunpowder, and the Nitre Which Governs It

The programme reached a stable ratio in 531 and from 532 was a weapons programme. It went where two centuries of doctrine pointed: an emplaced vessel, because Rome has built fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers since 301 and its engineers reached for the shape they knew. First cast-bronze bombard at Argentoratum, 538. Emplaced batteries at seven positions by 550.

**The corning, 541–544.** Meal powder as first made is a fine dust: it packs in the chamber, burns from the surface inward, and throws a stone ball hard enough to break a gate and not hard enough to break a curtain wall. The proof-house at Argentoratum, which fires and records every batch because Rome writes everything down, kept returning the same anomaly — that damp powder, dried into a cake and afterwards broken up for re-proof, threw further than the same powder had thrown loose. The *fabricatores* chased it for three years because it made no sense, established the effect held across batches and mills, and settled by 544 on a standing method: the meal is wetted, pressed to a cake, broken and screened to a graded grain, and dried. Nobody at Argentoratum can say why a grain outperforms a dust; the printed finding says only *that it does, reliably, by a third to a half in range and by more than that against masonry.* That grained powder is what makes the bombard a wall-breaker rather than a gate-breaker, and it is the single most valuable result the ordnance line produced. It is also, in the emperor's phrase, "the fourth time in this file that we have found a thing without being able to say what it is."

**The nitre, and why it governs everything.** Powder is charcoal, brimstone and nitre, and of the three only the nitre is scarce. Rome's supply comes off Rome's own floors. Nitre crusts on the walls of old stables, in cellars, under dovecotes, in the sweepings of the cavalry lines and the *mansiones* of the post roads — anywhere dung, urine, lime and still air have sat together for years. A hundred and twenty-seven millions of people, the largest stabled-horse economy on the continent, four centuries of masonry cellars and a post system with remount stations every fifteen miles is a nitre field, but it is a field that must be scraped, leached, boiled and crystallised, and one that regenerates over years rather than seasons.

Severianus did not build a new instrument for this. He used the one he had just built for a different reason. From 539 the **cura nitri**, a desk of the *curatores fabricarum* under a *praefectus nitri* seated at Argentoratum, holds:

- **The right of the scrape** — licensed *nitrarii* may enter and work the floor of any stable, cellar, byre, dovecote or *mansio* on notice, paying a fixed price per weight of raw earth, the owner obliged to permit and forbidden to floor over. This is deeply unpopular and the Council did not defend it, the works themselves having nothing to lose by it.
- **The nitre-beds** — walled heaps of dung, ash, lime, straw and urine, turned and watered on a printed schedule, at the remount depots and the great post stations of Gaul, the Rhine and Pannonia. A bed yields nothing for two to three years and then yields annually. Two hundred and eighty in operation by 550.
- **The imperial pre-emption** — all refined nitre is sold to the fisc at a fixed price, and its sale, movement or export by any other hand is a capital matter. Rome nationalised nitre and nothing else.

Output at 550: about **eleven hundred tons of refined nitre a year**, yielding some **fifteen hundred tons of grained powder**, at a standing cost of **thirty-five millions** inside the ordnance line. Against that sits a war reserve of about two years' production held in the magazines.

**And that is why no Roman gun has gone to sea.** The Consilium put the question in 547 and the *magister classis* answered it with the magazine returns rather than an opinion. Seven fortress batteries fire in proof, in practice, and in a siege that may not come in a decade; their whole annual consumption is a few score tons. A gunned squadron is a different animal entirely — a warship carries her powder, burns it in every exercise and empties her magazine in an afternoon's action, and Rome's own proof figures put a single hard season of a ten-ship gunned squadron above the entire year's refined output. Rome can arm the walls or it can arm the fleet. It cannot do both, and a fleet whose magazines run dry three thousand miles from Argentoratum is worse than a fleet with no guns, because it will have been fought over.

So the fifty-two warships carry ram, ballista, steam grenade and the old bow launcher, exactly as in 525, and the bombards stand on stone. This is a decision, minuted, and not an omission. The *praefectus nitri* was instructed in 548 to report annually on the one number that would change it: the year in which the beds laid down since 539 mature into an output that will carry a fleet. His estimate is the 570s, and he says in writing that it is an estimate and that nitre is the one Roman material whose supply no amount of money hurries.

A hand weapon was attempted once. A *fabricator* of the Argentoratum works, Vibius Crispinus, argued in 543 that a small tube would do what a large one did, and made three. The second burst on its ninth firing and killed him. The finding entered in 544: the metal cannot be thin enough to carry and strong enough to hold; the hand weapon is a blind alley.

That finding is wrong, and the reason it is wrong is the reason it will stand. The Han told Rome, in their own message of the 480s, that their infantry carry the thing and fire it individually; Rome believes them and has reoriented an entire military assessment around it. But Rome has no basis of its own for believing the metallurgy possible, the one man who might have persisted is dead, and the programme has a battery of successful bombards to point at instead. This is precisely how a hundred and eighty years went into pressure vessels — a shape Rome had mastered, applied to a problem it had not — and nobody in the Consilium has noticed the pattern repeating in the same building.

## X. Alexandria: The Branch Rome Believes Is Turned

The branch quarantined in the previous generation was tested in 537. The *officium* passed into it a specific, false, checkable item — that Rome intended to abandon Castra Cuzamil within two years. In 539 a factor of a clean branch reported that the intended Roman withdrawal from the mainland shore was spoken of in the port as a settled matter.

On that, Rome concluded the branch is turned. It should be said what the conclusion rests on: one item, one report, one factor. It is a reasonable conclusion and it may be wrong.

Rome has not rolled the branch up. From 540 it is run as a deliberate channel, and the items passed are listed in the declarations. Whether any of it is believed is not something Rome can know — a channel Rome knows is turned may be a channel its holder knows Rome knows is turned, and the *officium*'s own memorandum says exactly that and says it is worth running anyway, because a feed costs Rome nothing but ink. The record shows no earlier instance of Rome using a compromised network rather than burying it. The other taskings, and the one man Rome has lost, are in the declarations.

## XI. Three Letters

The previous reign sent one message in a generation and argued for two sessions about where to send it. Severianus ended that habit in a sentence to the Consilium: *"We have three neighbours and one road to each. A silence is also a message, and it is the only kind we cannot control the meaning of."* Three letters went out, and the full texts are in the declarations.

**To the Han**, from Statio Borysthenia in the spring of 548, carrying the rail-junction reply prepared a generation ago and never sent, a full answer to the question the Han had asked four times, and the one ask the emperor wanted and could not buy at home for any money: **the method by which the sphere of the earth is measured, and the mathematics beneath it.** Rome's astronomers have telescopes, the phases of Venus, the lights at Jupiter and a heliocentric case settled among the men who look through the glass. What they lack is a method — a way from an observation to a number that predicts. Rome was told, at the Dnieper contacts of the last generation, that the Han had measured the earth, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned a western land into existence before any Roman sailed to it. Whether that account is true Rome cannot verify. If it is, it is not a machine and cannot be copied by watching. It has to be asked for.

Severianus overruled the faction that wanted the west concealed, minuting his reason: if the account of their measurement is true they did not need us to tell them the land was there; and a partner who catches you hiding a thing he already knows has learned nothing about the thing and a great deal about you.

**To Egypt**, through the legation at Alexandria in 545: an application to purchase African labour in quantity through licensed Roman houses; a proposal to extend the mutual notification of naval movements — standing in the Mediterranean since the 420s — to the Atlantic and to the western ocean; and a renewed request for fuller sailing detail on the hazard of the coast south of the Mauretanian provinces, framed as merchant safety, which is what it is and not only what it is. What Rome did **not** put in that letter is the survey of 522–524. Egypt was never in doubt where those three ships were — a vessel shadowed them the whole length in relay, and that is a thing Roman eyes saw — but what Rome *concluded* from the voyage is the one asset Rome holds that is certainly not already held in Alexandria, and Severianus would not spend it to ask a question.

**To the Maurya**, through Barygaza and the Euphrates route in 546. Rome performed its half of the steam exchange in full and says so; fourteen engineers sailed in 528 and the first hull engine was built beside Indian engineers over four years. Rome has entered no receipt of the firearm account promised against it, and the letter says that plainly and without threat, because a plain statement of an unbalanced ledger is worth more at this distance than a grievance. It asks two things. The first is the Indian mathematics and astronomy entire — the calculation, not the numerals, which Rome has used in its ledgers for a century and a half.

The second is the one that cost Severianus something to write, and it is the most important sentence in any of the three letters. A generation ago the Maurya told Rome that the copper strips on Egypt's walls carried a natural force travelling near-instantaneously through metal, and that Indian researchers had found the same force at elementary scale. Rome refused it, called it a planted story or a misreading, and built pneumatic tubes instead — because tubes and pressure are what Rome had mastered, and Rome reasons from what it has mastered. That is the same error that put a hundred and eighty years into pressure vessels while the Han carried powder, and the same error that read Egypt's fast messages as mirrors because Rome understood mirrors. Three times now, in three centuries, Rome has confidently named the wrong category by projecting its own competence onto another power's problem. The letter says so, in the emperor's name, in writing, to a power Rome's own file classifies as strategically unreliable. It asks for the apparatus and the method of the elementary demonstration.

*"We are asking a man we do not trust for a thing we told him did not exist,"* the minute reads. *"That is the price of having been wrong, and it is cheaper than being wrong for another century."*

## XII. Britannia, the Vectigal, and the Constitutio Oceanica

This is what the reign cost, and the court has not understood that it cost anything.

The *vectigal occidentale* was laid on the western trade at its port of entry, on the excellent principle that the new thing has no defenders. The new thing turned out to have one.

Britannia and Hibernia build the Atlantic hulls — the yards are at Isca, Londinium and Gesoriacum, where the timber and iron and coal are — and crew them from the Iberia and Iceland runs. By 540 over half the tonnage on the western run was Britannic-built and Britannic-crewed, and the Londinium shipping houses had formed a combine that dealt with the *procurator* as an equal.

In 542 the rate was raised from one-eighth to one-fifth to fund Portus Severianus, the coal colony and the iron fleet. The Britannic houses did three things. They petitioned in print, on the argument that produced a usurpation in 355 and has never been answered since: British revenue funds imperial works while British infrastructure receives less than it generates. They under-declared, at a scale the *procurator* of Londinium put at a third of cargo. And they moved the landfall, running the convoys into **Portus Magnus** in southern Hibernia and into the Clyde — neither a named port of entry, both a short coastal hop from the named ones, the duty establishment at each being one *procurator* and eleven men.

Rome refused the petition in 544. In the summer of 546 a customs squadron out of Isca seized three Britannic hulls at Portus Magnus. The crews were taken off by a crowd the port magistrates declined to disperse; the magistrates wrote to the governor of Britannia, who declined to send troops against the shipping carrying his own province's trade and wrote to Rome saying so.

There was no revolt. That is the point, and it is worse: the incident established that Rome cannot enforce a fiscal law against Britannia without a naval action against its own province, at a moment when every ocean-capable hull Rome owns is committed to the western run and the fleet cannot be in two oceans.

Rome settled in 547 with the **Constitutio Oceanica**: the western trade licensed through a chartered body, the *Societas Oceani Occidentalis*, seated at Londinium and holding the exclusive right of carriage on the run; registered hulls paying one part in eight where unregistered pay one in four, against open books, imperial inspection and carriage of imperial cargo at fixed rates; Portus Magnus and the Clyde constituted ports of entry; and the *Societas* granted legal personality and bounded liability under the Concordia Fabricarum — under the law Severianus himself had made three years before.

The fisc counted it a victory and it collected: the *vectigal* yielded almost nothing in 546 and ninety-two millions in 550, against a projection of two hundred and twenty, a shortfall the Consilium blames on under-declaration and expects to close.

What it actually did: by 550 roughly two-thirds of Atlantic tonnage is Britannic-registered, Britannic-crewed and Britannic-built, and the whole of Rome's ocean commerce is organized in one chartered corporation with legal personality, bounded liability, a monopoly of the route, its own capital, its own ships and its own men — seated on the far side of the one water Rome's rail cannot cross, in the province that has already made an emperor once. Not one paper in the Consilium's record connects the Constitutio Oceanica to the events of 355. The court spent its attention on the Rhine, won there, and paid for it here without noticing the transaction.

Steam warships: twenty to thirty at 525, **fifty-two** at 550, of which twelve iron-framed and nineteen screw-driven. Locomotives, rolling mills, blast-furnace steel, gas lighting, balloons, pneumatic tubes, telescopes, longitude, mechanized farming, lightning rods: all continue, all improve incrementally, none of it a threshold.

### Not achieved, and honestly stated

- **A hand firearm.** Not achieved, and Rome has entered a formal finding that it is impossible. The finding is wrong and section IX explains why it will stand. **310M** bought Rome a siege train and a false negative.
- **Electricity.** Not achieved, not attempted, and until 546 not believed in. Rome has now asked the Maurya for the elementary demonstration. Nothing is funded against it and nothing can be until something arrives to test. The pneumatic tubes remain what Rome built instead: **45M** on communications, no progress whatever on whatever Egypt actually has.
- **Chemistry.** Not achieved and not organized. Rome found its powder ratio by twenty years of proportioning and burning, which is not chemistry; it is bookkeeping applied to fire.
- **Anything that predicts.** **40M** on natural-philosophy patronage bought four observatories, better glass, larger apertures, and a heliocentric case settled among the men who look through the glass and disputed by the schoolmen. The 546 submission states the deficiency exactly: Rome can see what the inherited model cannot hold, cannot put a number to what should replace it, and the failure is mathematical rather than optical. The emperor's margin note reads *"too small."* It is the smallest line in the research table and the only one that addresses the wall behind every other line.
- **Medicine.** **20M**, and the entry is *failed*. The quarantines did not hold, the cause is unknown, and the *procuratores* have printed the tallies of a population falling by five sixths in eighteen years without a single physician able to say why.
- **A theory of heat, or of steel.** Compound engines at 150–400 horsepower, found by tinkering. Boilers improved by counting failures. No theory behind either.
- **Rubber.** Still cannot source it and still cannot explain it.

The pattern is unchanged from the last generation's assessment and the emperor named it in the Consilium: every wall Rome has hit in two hundred years is where empiricism stops, and the only Romans practising the habit that produces theory are the forty men with telescopes, funded at one part in thirty-five of the research table.

## XIV. PEOPLE

**Population.** About **one hundred and twenty-seven millions** in the old world, against a hundred and fifteen at 525 — a shade under half a percent a year, from natural increase alone, no ground taken and none lost. In the west, some **four hundred and fifty thousand** Romans and Romanized: veterans and families on the two great islands, the garrisons, the crews of the run, and the new river colonies. Against that, the subjugated island population has fallen from above four hundred thousand to under seventy thousand.

*Basis:* the provincial land and head rolls, the annona registers of the great cities, and the army's recruiting returns, cross-checked against each other. It should be recorded, as the previous generation's assessment recorded, that these rolls are compiled by men whose careers depend on the number and reported by neighbours who would rather not be counted. Rome's own fisc believes the count understates the frontier countryside and overstates the settled interior. The figure is Rome's best and it is not a measurement.

**Technical workforce.** About **five hundred and ninety thousand**, or **0.46 percent** of the old-world population, against four hundred thousand and 0.37 percent at 525. Of these perhaps **sixty thousand** design rather than maintain.

*Basis:* for the first time this is not an estimate. The *collatio fabricarum* required registered works to return output, engines, furnaces and hands, and the *curatores fabricarum* audit those returns with right of entry. The count came in above what the fisc had assumed from guild rolls, chiefly in the Silesian and Dacian fields and in the rail shops, which had been carried on the old rolls at figures two generations old. The same instrument that raised the tax produced the census, and the census is the more valuable of the two.

They remain what they have always been: literate mechanics fixing real machines and printing their fixes. Almost none are researchers in any organized sense. That distributed swarm took the engine from a leaking mine pump to four hundred horsepower in three hundred and fifty years without a single theorem, and it is Rome's greatest asset and the exact reason Rome cannot cross a wall that requires a theorem.

**Literacy.** About **seventy-two percent** empire-wide, against near seventy at 525. Eighty-five to ninety in the Rhine cities, Britannia and Rome; seventy-five to eighty-five in the administrative cities; fifty-five to sixty-five in the settled rural provinces; forty to fifty-five on the frontier countryside, which is the line that moved most this generation and moved because the collegia now bargain in writing and a man who cannot read his own contract signs what he is handed.

*Basis:* inferred, and Rome says so. Print runs and gazette subscriptions by district; the army's assessment of recruits at enlistment, which is a real test on a real sample; apprenticeship indentures; and now the signature rolls of registered collegia under the Concordia. Not one denarius of it was ever spent by the state on a school.

It is what this state has done under every emperor whose books survive, and the only thing that changed in the reign is that seven percent of a larger number is more money.

### What the turn actually cost

Across twenty-five years Rome took in something near **116,000 millions** and spent something near **107,000**.

| Programme | Across the turn | Share of total outlay |
|---|---|---|
| Western establishment — colonies, garrisons, transport, ports, the coal colony | about 2,600 | 2.4% |
| Named directed research (table below) | about 1,400 | 1.3% |

That is the number the Consilium of 527 needed and did not have. The venture that half the Senate treated as a wager of the state's future is under four percent of what the state spent while making it. It was always affordable. What it was not was free, and what it competed with was not bread but the frontier line, which is why the argument was about the *collatio* and not about the ships.

**What Rome cannot do.** It still cannot borrow: no bond market, no conception of one, reserves of a few months' revenue that one serious war erases. Every instrument in section II is a way of pulling revenue forward or reaching a base not previously assessed, because those are the only two things Rome knows how to do. That is a real limit and it is the limit that would bite in a war, not in a colonial programme.

**One trajectory the court still misreads.** Per-head revenue has gone from eighteen sesterces at 1 AD to thirty-six at 525 and about forty-two at 550 — the first real movement in that figure in five centuries, and it came from taxing industry rather than from anyone becoming richer. Revenue has risen sevenfold since 1 AD; per head it has not quite doubled and a half. Rome is not much richer per person than the Republic was; it is vastly larger, vastly more mechanized, and almost every marginal sesterce of the industrial dividend has gone into the frontier for two hundred years. *Revenue-rich and cash-thin* is not a bad century. It is the permanent shape of this state, and the correct response to it is not alarm but arithmetic.

## XVI. TERRITORY

**The split is the point of this section and Rome keeps it strictly.** What follows in the first list is ground on which Roman soldiers stand, Roman law runs and Roman colonists are settled. What follows in the second is water Rome has sailed past with a glass and a chart. Rome has learned within living memory what happens when a claim on paper is mistaken for a fact on the ground.

### A. CONQUERED, SETTLED OR GARRISONED

*New this turn:*

- **Insula Aurea** (Hispaniola), entire, reduced by 531. Seat at **Colonia Ozamae**, mouth of the river Ozama, southern shore. Gold works at **Aurifodinae Cibaenses** in the Cibao interior. Veteran allotments on the Vega Real and the southern coastal plain. Colonists and families in the low tens of thousands; the subjugated population that was to work it has largely ceased to exist.
- **Insula Longa** (Cuba), entire, reduced 536. **Portus Securus** on the enclosed northern bay; **Vallis Cauti**, settled valley of the river Cauto in the south-east.
- **Insula Sancti Iohannis** (Puerto Rico), taken 533. Garrison harbour at **Portus Sancti Iohannis**.
- **Insula Fontium** (Jamaica), taken 537. Garrison and water station at **Portus Fontium**.
- **Castra Cuzamil**, fortified island base three miles off the Yucatan shore, held since 523, permanent stone works from 531. Garrisoned, not a colony.
- **Statio Chetumalis**, licensed walled trading post of two hundred on the bay of Chactemal, on the mainland Yucatan shore. Garrisoned. Explicitly *not* a claim on the interior and so minuted.
- **Portus Severianus**, founded 544 at the mouth of the **Flumen Novum** on the **Sinus Magnus Occidentalis**, southern bank. Walled 546. Fleet coaling and repair station; shipyard from 547.
- **Colonia Carbonaria**, founded 545 at the fall line of the Flumen Novum, ninety miles inland. Burned by the confederated river peoples in 546 with four hundred and ten dead; refounded 547 behind a legion detachment of three thousand. Coal pits, four miles of iron rail to the landing, first western steam engine 550. Veteran allotments in layers between Carbonaria and tidewater, in the pattern that took Europe.
- **Insulae Fortunatae** (Canaries), constituted a province 531. Depot at **Portus Fortunatus**. No coal; every ton carried.
- **Insulae Accipitrum**, mid-ocean on the return road, found 533, largest island garrisoned 535. Depot and mole at **Portus Medius**. No coal; every ton carried.

*Unchanged:* the entire old-world frontier, Ireland to the Dnieper and Scotland to the North African provinces. **Not one mile taken and not one lost.** No Roman soldier has crossed the Dnieper or the Euphrates in the twenty-five years.

### B. SURVEYED, CHARTED OR CLAIMED — AND NOT HELD

- The **northern seaboard** north and south of the Sinus Magnus Occidentalis, charted from the sea and from balloons at height, including the peninsula Rome calls **Ora Florida**. No garrison, no colony, no claim Rome could enforce.
- The **three other great rivers** of the Sinus: entered, sounded, ascended by boat, charted. Nothing built on any of them.
- The **Yucatan interior** and its forty-odd walled cities: reconnoitred by parties on foot with interpreters, mapped as far as the trade paths run, described in a printed memorandum. **Rome holds none of it and has decided not to try.**
- The **Lesser Antilles** and the **Bahaman shoals**: charted, watered at, not held.
- The **African Atlantic coast** south of the Mauretanian provinces: surveyed once, in 522–524, from one to two miles offshore, through glass. **Formally closed.** The probe remains suspended and no Roman vessel went below that latitude this turn.

**A caution Rome enters against its own file.** The reassessment of that African coast as Egyptian rests on architecture seen at two miles: pylon gateways, columned halls, temples in the Egyptian manner. Rome's own cities contain Egyptian shrines and Rome is not Egypt. A cultural sphere, a confederation of ports, a daughter civilization, or an independent Atlantic maritime power that trades with Egypt and shares its gods would each look through a glass exactly like what the captains saw. Rome has *not* verified that the coast is under Egyptian authority and this record does not assert it. What Rome verified is that the coast is built, defended, watched, and far larger than anything Rome believed was there.

## XVII. Succession, and the State at 550

Severianus is sixty-two in 550. He invested his sister's son **Aulus Antonius Gratianus** as Caesar in 548 and — more importantly — constituted the **Consilium Sacrum**: praetorian prefects, the Patriarch, the *magister militum* and four members of the Industrial Council, holding a fixed and printed role in investiture, with a defined authority to conduct the ordinary business of the state in the absence of an emperor.

Its purpose, he said, is to make an emperor unnecessary for a year at a time. He admitted openly where he got it: the Han told Rome a century and a half ago that their succession is held stable by a bureaucracy that endures through weak emperors and strong alike, and Rome has never had such a thing and has bought every succession since 318 with a bargain or a war. Whether it works nobody will know until a bad emperor tests it, and the men who wrote it into law are aware that a body with four industrialists on it and a printed role in making emperors is either the answer to Rome's oldest structural problem or a new and better-organized way of losing the throne.

## MESSAGES OUT — one to each power

### 1. TO THE HAN
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus. Carried from Statio Borysthenia on the Dnieper, spring of 548.**

To the Emperor of the Han and to his ministers, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

*On the junction of the rails.* We accept the joining of our lines at the Dnieper, under the agreement standing between us, which renews itself and which we do not ask to change — as a yard of transfer, not a joining of roads. Our gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and we will not relay it; we do not ask you to relay yours. Your cars will come to the eastern bank and stop, ours will stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods will cross where wheels do not. The reason is honest and we state it rather than dress it: a rail running from one empire onto the other is a road for an army, and neither of us would lay the other's gauge inside his own ground. The yard will stand on the Roman bank under Roman garrison at Statio Borysthenia; your factors will work in it under Roman law exactly as our merchants are protected in your territory; and we will build the sheds, cranes and sidings at our own cost, because we want the silk carried rail to rail more than we want the argument over who pays.

*On the gifts offered.* We receive the mechanical clock and its escapement with thanks and will set it beside our own work, which has reached the accuracy of navigation and not yet beyond. We return the method of paper with respect and a plain statement: Rome has made paper from linen and flax for five hundred and fifty years and printed with cast type for four hundred and fifty. We say so not to diminish the gift but because a man who accepts a gift he does not need has told his friend a small lie for politeness, and small lies compound at this distance.

*On the question you have asked four times.* We answer in full, and you will not have to ask a fifth.

Rome has sailed west. Ships leave the islands we call the Fortunate Isles at twenty-eight degrees north, where the wind blows steadily west and a great current runs west beneath the hull, and together they make a road that carries a ship whether she wishes it or not. Six weeks on that road brings her to a chain of green mountainous islands, and west of them a coast, which our ships have followed more than a thousand miles without finding its end either way.

The lands hold peoples: island farmers of cassava and maize, skilled at sea in canoes cut from single trees; on a stone shore to the west, walled cities with their own writing, calendar, priesthood and merchants, forty and more, at war among themselves and under no single king; further north, scattered fishing and farming peoples of no great wealth, on a coast of good timber and, we have found, of coal. In all that country we have found no metal worked at all — not iron, not bronze, not copper, not as tool or weapon or ornament. Rome has taken the two largest islands and holds them, holds a fortified island off the stone coast, and has planted a coaling port and a colony on a northern river. We tell you because you will hear it, and because a man who conceals what his friend already knows has concealed nothing and revealed himself.

We do not send a chart of our positions, and we tell you why rather than let you infer it. Two hundred and fifty years ago Rome gave your envoys at this same river a full survey of Roman roads, cities, forts and supply lines, and received a sketch without scale. Rome recorded that as its own error and has not repeated it with any power since. We hold to the rule with you not because we distrust you more than others but because we distrust ourselves in this particular way, having been foolish once in exactly this place.

*On the thing we ask.* Your envoys told ours that you had measured the sphere of the earth, found the known world too small to fill it, and reasoned the western land into existence before any ship of ours touched it. We ask for that — not the number, which is a curiosity, but the method and the mathematics beneath it: how the measurement is made, what is assumed, and how one reasons from a measured sphere to a place no man has seen. We ask further for the works of your astronomers and calculators, such as you are willing to send.

We offer what we have of the same kind: the construction of our telescopes, glass, figure and mounting, complete; the observations made with them, that Venus shows phases as the moon does and that small lights move about Jupiter and keep to it; and the argument these have raised among us, that the inherited model of a fixed earth cannot hold them and that the sun and not the earth may stand at the centre. Our schoolmen resist it. The men who look through the glass do not. We send you the dispute along with the observations, because a dispute is worth more than a conclusion.

*On residence.* We ask that not more than ten Roman men of learning reside at your capital, and offer the same number of yours the same at Rome. A man who lives among you twenty years will understand you and a man who visits twenty days will not, and every misunderstanding between us for two hundred and fifty years was born in that gap.

*On the boundary.* The Dnieper stands. We want nothing east of it, have moved no soldier across it, and will not without prior word to you. Your people on the eastern bank have grown into settled towns as ours have on the western, and we take that as two peoples farming and nothing else — and we say so plainly, so that no clerk on either bank has to guess what we take it as.

Severianus Augustus.

### 2. TO EGYPT
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus, through the Roman legation at Alexandria, 545.**

To the Throne of Egypt, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

Our two states have kept permanent legations for a hundred and thirty years and have not in that time exchanged a blow. We write on three matters, plainly, and ask nothing that is not stated.

*First.* Rome holds islands and a coast beyond the western ocean. Our gazettes print it, our shipping houses insure it, and our ports register the crews, so we will not affect a secrecy that our own printers abandoned twenty years ago. We hold two great islands, a fortified island off a stone shore, a walled trading post, and a coaling port and colony on a northern river. We have taken no ground on any coast either of us has previously touched, and we do not intend to.

*Second, and the substance of this letter.* Since the reign of the Augustus who opened these legations, our two navies have notified one another of movements in the Mediterranean, and in a hundred and thirty years that arrangement has never once been broken by either of us. It is the single most useful thing the two states have made together. We propose to extend it: that the same notification apply in the Atlantic beyond the strait, and in the western ocean, on the same terms and by the same channel. Neither of us gives up anything by it. Both of us are relieved of the necessity of guessing, and guessing between fleets is how two states that do not want a war acquire one. We hold this offer open whatever answer is given to the third matter.

*Third.* Rome asks licence for Roman houses to purchase labour in quantity through your factors at Alexandria, for the western possessions, at whatever rate and under whatever conditions of registration you set. We have licensed the house of Vibulenus of Ostia to make the application. We state the reason without ornament, because you will infer it and an inferred reason is worse than a stated one: the peoples of the islands we hold are dying, of something our physicians cannot name and our quarantines have not stopped, and the works there have no hands.

*A fourth thing, smaller.* Twenty-five years ago your officers told ours that the coast south of our Mauretanian provinces is held by maritime peoples who have destroyed outside vessels for as long as your African trade has existed, and that the current there carries a disabled ship away so that no wreckage returns. We have lost five ships to that coast and have closed it. We ask for whatever fuller sailing detail you are willing to give — the latitudes, the season, the manner of approach — for the safety of our merchantmen on the Mauretanian run. We do not ask what lies behind that coast.

Severianus Augustus.

### 3. TO THE MAURYA
**From Gaius Antonius Severianus Augustus, carried by the Euphrates route and by sea from Berenice to Barygaza, 546.**

To the Emperor at Pataliputra and to his ministers, from the Emperor of the Romans, greeting.

*On the exchange agreed in the last generation.* Rome performed. Fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus sailed to Barygaza in 528 with the complete working method of the marine steam engine — boiler, transmission, seals and their failure points, fuel figures — and built the first beside your engineers over four years. Three remain resident with you. Nothing was withheld and nothing was padded, and your own engineers who worked beside ours are the witnesses of that, not we.

Against it we were to receive the full account of the Chinese hand-firearm. Rome has entered no receipt of it. We do not accuse you of bad faith; a letter takes a year and an empire is slow. We state the ledger as it stands because at this distance an unspoken grievance becomes a policy before either of us has noticed, and we would rather write it down. Rome will open no second exchange with you until the first is closed.

*On the tariff.* Your transit rate on Chinese goods through the Euphrates route was doubled without word to us, and Roman merchants have paid for a quarrel they had no part in; the trade has gone to the Dnieper and the Red Sea, which serves neither of us. We ask for a schedule fixed by agreement for a term, at whatever rate, rather than a rate that may move without notice. A merchant can trade against a high tariff. He cannot trade against an uncertain one.

*On what we ask, first.* Your mathematics and your astronomy, entire, such as you are willing to send: the method of calculation and the works of your calculators. Rome has used your figures in its ledgers for a hundred and fifty years and has never asked for what is done with them. Our engineers reckon; they do not derive. We know the difference now.

*On what we ask, second, and why we are ashamed to ask it.* A generation ago your ministers told ours that the copper strips on the walls at Alexandria carry a natural force that travels through metal near-instantaneously, and that your own researchers had found the same force at elementary scale. Rome refused it. We judged it a planted story or a misreading, on the ground that a power which had not grasped the steam engine from watching working machines could not be trusted to interpret an invisible thing — and we built tubes of compressed air instead, because tubes and pressure are what Rome had mastered.

We were wrong, and we are able to say how we were wrong, because we have done it three times. We spent a hundred and eighty years believing the Chinese weapon must be a stored pressure, because pressure is what we understood. We judged Egypt's fast messages to be mirrors, because optics is what we understood. And we told you the force in the copper was compressed air, because air in a pipe is what we understood. Three times we named the wrong category by taking our own competence for the shape of the world.

Therefore we ask for the elementary demonstration: the apparatus, the materials, the method, and what your researchers have observed of it, however small the effect and however uncertain the explanation. We will test it and we will tell you what we find, whether it confirms your account or contradicts it.

We offer in return, and send with this letter without waiting for an answer: the construction of our telescopes complete, glass, figure and mounting; the observations of the phases of Venus and the lights that move about Jupiter; our record of the pneumatic tube installations, which is a record of the wrong answer and therefore of some value; and the metallurgy of our naval boilers, which is four hundred years of failures written down.

*On residence.* We ask that not more than ten Roman men of learning reside at Pataliputra, and offer the same number of yours the same at Rome, on the same terms we have offered the Han.

Severianus Augustus.

## INTENTS DECLARED, OUTCOMES NOT WRITTEN

Rome declares these as intents and does not record any other power's response to them. What each power does is theirs.

1. **Purchase of African labour in quantity at Alexandria**, through the licensed house of Vibulenus of Ostia, for the western possessions. Rome's contingency if refused or priced beyond bearing: abandon the labour-intensive island economy as the centre of the western project and rebuild it around the mechanizable coal coast.
2. **Extension of the naval notification arrangement with Egypt** to the Atlantic and the western ocean.
3. **Request to Egypt for fuller sailing detail** on the hazard of the coast south of Mauretania.
4. **Request to the Han for the method of measuring the sphere** and the mathematics beneath it, and for the works of their astronomers; offer of telescope construction, the Venus and Jupiter observations, and the heliocentric dispute; offer of exchanged residents.
5. **Request to the Maurya for their mathematics and astronomy**, and for the apparatus and method of the elementary demonstration of the force in metal; offer of telescopes, boiler metallurgy, the pneumatic record, and exchanged residents.
6. **Request to the Maurya for a fixed-term tariff schedule** on the Euphrates route.
7. **Statement to the Maurya that Rome will open no second exchange until the first is closed.**

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Alexandria network — the branch Rome believes is turned.** Founded c. 300 AD, 250 years old; branch compromised before 525, believed confirmed turned by a single controlled-item test, 537–539. Personnel: the existing compromised agents, unreplaced, unwarned. Cover: commercial factors in grain and coffee, unchanged. Target: not collection — deliberate feed. Items passed 539–550: the intended abandonment of Castra Cuzamil (the test item); island commitment overstated; northern-coast commitment understated; coal reports called disappointing; Portus Severianus called a timber station. Run by the *officium custodiae*, on the reasoning that a feed is a publication and not an operation. **Rome does not know whether any of it is believed and the memorandum says so.**

**2. Alexandria network — clean branches.** Same founding, 250 years. Personnel: seven principal factors, Roman and Greek houses of Ostia, Puteoli and Antioch with legitimate business in the port. Cover: grain, coffee, dyestuffs, marine insurance. Targets in priority: the size, tonnage and heading of the shipbuilding activity reported from 520; any sign of westward provisioning out of Alexandria or through the canal; the composition and destination of the northbound Atlantic column Rome's own captains counted in 523; the identity of the southern delegations received at the administrative quarter in 365 and since.

**3. The African labour chain.** New, opened 543, 7 years old. Personnel: the house of Vibulenus of Ostia, a genuine licensed Roman buyer, and its factor Marcus Vibulenus Priscus. Cover: none required — the house is what it says. Target: to open the purchase, and separately to trace the chain backward from the Alexandria factors toward its origin, and establish whether the coast of the 522 survey and the labour source stand under one authority. **Status: Priscus went to Alexandria in 545 and left the port southward in 546 with an escort. No word since. No replacement sent, on the standing rule of not walking a man into a silence.**

**4. The Dnieper watch.** Founded c. 300 AD with the frontier contact, 250 years old; redirected in 525 from the Chinese firearm to logistics. Personnel: frontier *exploratores* and licensed caravan factors on the western bank, plus balloon observation from Borysthenia. Cover: customs and caravan administration, which is genuine work. Targets: tonnage arriving from the eastern rail terminus and its growth; the depth and permanence of Han civilian settlement within two days of the eastern bank; whether any of it carries military stores. Findings entered 550, all from the Roman bank: caravan volume risen from about a dozen a year to above thirty; a settled population on the eastern bank within two days' travel that Rome's observers put at forty to sixty thousand; no military stores identified, which Rome records as *not identified* rather than as *not present*.

**5. Presence at Barygaza and the Indian yards.** New, 528, 22 years old. Not an espionage network and not run as one. Personnel: fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus, sent openly under the steam exchange; three still resident in 550. Cover: none — they are exactly what they appear to be. Target: standing observation of yard capacity, metallurgy, and any sighting of cannon founding or powder work. The *officium* rates them the best-placed Roman eyes anywhere outside the empire precisely because nobody is hiding anything from them.

**6. The *officium custodiae* — counter-intelligence, domestic.** Founded 536, 14 years old, and the first standing counter-intelligence instrument in Roman history; the record before 536 does not contain the concept. Instruments: crew and passenger registers at the named western ports; licensing of chart-makers and chart-sellers; sealed sailing orders opened at sea; classification of western latitudes, soundings and coal reports. Prior notification of publication imposed 536, withdrawn for prices, cargoes and losses in 540 after the gazettes refused registration and the Industrial Council backed them; retained for positions, soundings, charts and coal, and unenforceable. **Western desk, from 547:** audit of the register and books of the *Societas Oceani Occidentalis* at Londinium under the open-books clause of the Constitutio Oceanica. Rome does not call this an intelligence tasking against a Roman province. It is one.

## RESEARCH — programmes and funding across the turn

**Gunpowder and ordnance — 310 millions.** Stable ratio 531; weaponization from 532; first cast-bronze bombard at Argentoratum 538; emplaced batteries by 550 at Borysthenia, the Dacian corridor forts, Gades, Gibraltar, Cyrenaica and Portus Severianus. **Achieved:** siege and fortress artillery that reduces stone in hours rather than weeks, and a fortress-engineering problem Rome has not yet solved, since its own walls are equally reducible. **Formal finding entered 544 and believed by the programme:** a hand-carried tube is impossible, the metal being unable to be thin enough to carry and strong enough to hold — entered after the death of the *fabricator* Vibius Crispinus on a burst trial piece. **Rome has no hand firearm and no expectation of one, and section IX records why that finding will stand despite what the Han have told Rome they carry.**

**Naval — iron hull, screw, boiler and range — 690 millions, the largest line of the turn.** Iron-framed construction from *Constantia*, Isca, 541; twelve in service by 550. Screw ordered for all ocean-service construction 544; nineteen screw hulls by 550, above half a paddle wheel's efficiency in flat water and superior in weather. Range from six or seven hundred nautical miles toward nine hundred to a thousand on a full load. Fleet from twenty to thirty warships at 525 to fifty-two at 550. *Fulmen* lost in 543 attempting an at-sea coaling crossing, a hundred and twenty men. First successful steam crossing by *Aquila Occidentalis*, spring 550, twenty-two days.

**Western fuel survey — 240 millions**; colonial establishment charged to the western line, not here. Parties to the Sinus Magnus Occidentalis 541–542 under orders naming fuel and not gold. Bituminous coal above the fall line of the Flumen Novum, autumn 543, within eight miles of navigable water. Pits opened 545–547; first coal raised 549, eleven thousand tons in the first full year; four-mile iron rail 548–549; first western stationary engine at Colonia Carbonaria, 550.

**Agronomy, the western grains — 55 millions.** Trial stations under the *curatores agrorum* in Baetica, Campania, Pannonia, the Danube bend, the Dacian lowland and the black earth behind the Dnieper, with printed method sheets. **Achieved:** strains ripening reliably in Pannonia and on the Dacian lowland by 550 that would not have ripened north of Baetica in 530. Cassava and sweet potato in the western islands and southern Iberia only. Seed and method not traded to any power; reaffirmed 534 and 546.

**Communications — 45 millions.** Pneumatic tubes extended from the Rhine administrative centre to Rome, Londinium, Lugdunum and Borysthenia; optical telegraph with telescopes along the Dnieper line and the Atlantic coast. **No progress whatever on whatever Egypt actually has.** This line is Rome's monument to having answered a question it could not verify with the answer it already knew how to build.

**Natural philosophy patronage — 40 millions.** Observatories at Rome, Cyrene, Colonia Agrippina and Borysthenia; telescopes improved to greater aperture on the industrial sheet-glass furnaces; heliocentrism settled among the observers and disputed by the schoolmen. **Achieved: nothing that predicts.** The 546 submission states that Rome can see what the inherited model cannot hold, cannot put a number to what should replace it, and that the deficiency is mathematical and not optical — which is the reason for the asks carried to both the Han and the Maurya. The emperor's margin note reads *"too small."*

**Medicine and quarantine — 20 millions. Result: failed.** Cyprian protocols in the west from 538, forty-day hold at Portus Securus, licensed contact only. The *procuratores* report in writing that the sickness moves ahead of Roman parties along native trade paths and is not stopped by a forty-day wall. No theory of cause exists. Tallies maintained and printed.

**The steam exchange with Pataliputra — charged to the naval line.** Fourteen engineers under Sextus Verginius Rufus sailed Berenice to Barygaza in 528 with the complete method of the hull engine and built the first beside Indian engineers over four years; three remain resident in 550. **Rome performed. What returns, and when, is not Rome's to enter in its own record.**

**Totals.** Named directed programmes across the turn: about **1,400 millions**. The research and works line as a whole ran from 120 millions a year at 525 to 165 at 550 — some 3,500 millions across the turn — of which the named programmes are the directed part and the balance is standing arsenal, survey, fortification and engineering work that would have been done under any emperor.
