## 500 to 525 AD: The Nitre Sheds and the Second Crossing

The coal pollution legislation of 500 AD takes effect on schedule. Chimney-height minimums and housing setbacks are enforced first in Colonia Agrippina, where the inversion crisis originated, and the relocation fund resettles the worst-affected districts over the following decade. The weekly mortality counts the physicians now publish do not return to their pre-crisis baseline, because the underlying cause is untouched, but the acute spikes stop, and Rome reads this as vindication of a policy it does not understand the mechanism of. A second, milder inversion over Londinium in 511 AD produces a smaller mortality rise and a matching regulation extended to Britain's coal towns within the year, the precedent from Colonia Agrippina now a template applied without fresh argument.

The labor question the fourth-century law left unresolved comes to a head in 507 AD. A wave of printed pamphlets out of the Rhine valley, circulated faster than any provincial governor can answer them, argues plainly that collegium recognition without the power to negotiate wages is recognition in name only, and cites the Christian charity networks that carried the epidemic-era poor as proof that organized common cause need not mean riot. The Industrial Council, still dominated by owners but no longer able to ignore a literate workforce that reads its own arguments back to it in competing sheets within the week, proposes a compromise rather than risk a repeat of the Rhine violence of the previous century: a new Industrial Charter, passed in 509 AD, that gives registered collegia the standing to negotiate wages and hours directly with individual enterprises, backed by a slow arbitration process rather than a strike right, while leaving the owners' control of hiring and dismissal untouched. Neither side calls it a victory. The strikes that follow over the next several years are smaller and shorter than the ones the fourth century recorded, and the arbitration boards are overwhelmed from their first sitting, but the total absence of any comparable law is no longer true, and that alone changes the character of the disputes that follow.

The gunpowder program, reoriented toward combustion since the firearms revelation of the 480s, makes its first real progress and pays for it in blood. Chemists working from the mining and metallurgical base Rome has spent two centuries accumulating turn their attention to a substance long known but never studied: a white crust that forms on the floors of old cattle byres, dovecotes, and latrine pits, already gathered by farmers for its power to cure meat and known informally as sal petrae, stone-salt. Burned experimentally with charcoal in 512 AD, it produces a fire that catches faster and burns fiercer than charcoal alone, and by 514 AD Rome's investigators have isolated it as the active ingredient rather than a curiosity, the first correct identification in two centuries of guessing. What follows is not swift. The researchers do not yet grasp that the substance is an oxidizer supplying its own combustion air, only that it makes fire behave strangely, and they proceed by mixing it in increasing concentration with charcoal and, following a metalworker's suggestion, refined sulfur from the mining districts, in ratios chosen by trial rather than by understanding. On the ninth of March, 516 AD, a sealed clay vessel of the mixture under test in the classified laboratory outside Ravenna ignites during grinding, killing four researchers including the senior chemist who first isolated the substance, and burning the workshop to the ground with most of its written notes. The program does not stop, but the years immediately after are marked by new safety protocols, smaller batches, open-air testing at a distance, and a caution that slows the work considerably. By 525 AD Rome has a correct three-part mixture, sal petrae, sulfur, and charcoal, that reliably deflagrates rather than merely burns fiercely, and can be made to do so inside a sealed tube. It does not yet have anything resembling a weapon. The Ravenna disaster is filed alongside Carrhae and the Han map in Rome's private catalogue of costly lessons, and the classified program's annual report for 517 AD is the first in its history to open with a section on containment rather than results.

Egypt's formal relations continue on the pattern set in 400 to 425 AD: correct, unrevealing, and useful chiefly for the things Rome can independently verify against them. The mutual notification arrangement is tested for the first time in 505 AD, when an Egyptian steam vessel and a Roman one cross paths off Crete at night; both sides report the encounter through the agreed channel within the week, and nothing further comes of it, which Rome takes as evidence the framework functions under real conditions rather than only on paper. Rome's Alexandria observers count more of the quiet, wakeless vessels in the harbor with each passing year, and by 520 AD estimate that a majority of new Egyptian construction uses the submerged method rather than the paddle wheel, a ratio precisely inverted from Rome's own yards. Egypt volunteers nothing about the mechanism, and Rome's fifty-year underwater-propulsion program, still running, edges its own crude screw closer to serviceable without closing the gap.

The China relationship deepens in trade and stays narrow in substance. Rail traffic from the Syr Darya terminus grows steadily, and in 513 AD Rome formally requests, through the standing diplomatic channel, a sample firearm and instruction in its manufacture, offering in exchange anything short of its own classified weapons research. China declines the manufacturing knowledge but permits, at a reception in 515 AD, a demonstration firing witnessed by Rome's ranking envoy at a distance of some forty paces: a single loud report, a cloud of white smoke, and a lead ball driven through two fingers' thickness of oak. The envoy's report describes the flash and the smoke in close detail and can say nothing at all about what happens inside the weapon, since China's soldiers strip and clean it out of Roman sight. Rome comes away with its urgency confirmed and its ignorance unchanged. India, for its part, offers in 511 AD to lower its Euphrates transit tariff back toward the old rate in exchange for Roman diplomatic support against continued Chinese pressure over the Syr Darya frontier; Rome declines to be drawn into a dispute between its two eastern partners, and the tariff stays at its raised level, trade continuing to drift toward the Dnieper and Red Sea routes as it has since the increase.

The Caribbean question, filed at the end of the previous century for want of money and ships, returns to the Senate's attention once the immediate crises of 500 AD have settled. A second expedition, larger and better prepared than the first, departs the Canary Islands in the spring of 508 AD: three conventional sailing vessels, not steam, carrying interpreters trained on the word-lists the first crew brought home, seed stock and trade goods chosen this time on the strength of what was actually wanted rather than what Rome assumed would impress, iron tools and glass beads foremost among them, and orders to establish, if the reception allows it, a small permanent trading post rather than simply observe and return. The outbound crossing follows the charted route without incident, the current and the trade winds carrying the ships west in five weeks as before. Contact with the islands goes better than the first voyage, the interpreters' word-lists proving adequate for simple trade, and a modest post is raised on a coastal site offered by the local settlement in exchange for iron tools, the first Roman structure in the western ocean. But the fevers that afflicted the first crew return worse this time, moving through the anchored ships before the post is a month old, and by the time the expedition determines the sickness travels with standing water and biting insects rather than with the air, as the ship's physician eventually and correctly guesses, eleven men are dead and the commander orders the smaller of the three vessels, undermanned by the losses, broken up for parts rather than risk her on the return crossing. The other two ships come home in the winter of 509 to 510 AD by the Gulf Stream route already known, carrying the first Roman account of a coastline that runs unbroken to the south and west farther than any local guide could describe an end to, which Rome reads, cautiously, as evidence the islands are the edge of something considerably larger. The trading post survives, resupplied at long intervals, its small garrison rotated as fever losses require, and Rome now treats the Atlantic crossing as a known if costly route rather than a one-time curiosity, without yet having the ships, the money, or the freedom from the eastern arms race to do more with it than keep the door open.

The military budget continues its climb against the China relationship rather than against any declared threat, funding a third steam-fleet expansion program and the first trials of a fully iron-hulled hull section at the British yards in 521 AD, successful enough that a complete iron-hulled warship is laid down the following year and commissioned in 524 AD, roughly six years ahead of the twenty-five to thirty year estimate made at the close of the previous century. The saving comes directly from the rolling mills reaching full capacity sooner than projected, and the ship is judged by its builders to be the most survivable vessel Rome has yet put to sea, though against an Egyptian navy of unknown composition and a Chinese army carrying weapons Rome still cannot reproduce, what survivability is worth in a real engagement remains exactly as unknowable as it was in 500 AD.

**State of the empire, c. 525 AD.** Population ~118M; revenue ~4,450M HS (military approaching two-thirds and still climbing with the China relationship, land tax near a third, industry above a quarter); technical workforce ~460,000 (~0.39%); literacy ~70%. Labor: wage labor dominant, slavery residual and domestic. Technology: sal petrae identified as the active gunpowder ingredient and a working three-part mixture achieved after the Ravenna laboratory disaster of 516 AD, though still without a weapon to deliver it; the first fully iron-hulled warship commissioned in 524 AD, ahead of schedule; the crude screw propeller improved but still short of paddle-wheel performance; a permanent trading post established in the Caribbean at the cost of eleven dead to fever; an Industrial Charter granting collegia formal wage-negotiation standing, passed 509 AD. Territory: unchanged in Europe; a single trading post held in the western ocean. Timeline placement: about our 1850s to 1870s by population and capability alike, though still lacking a firearm, electricity, and systematic chemistry, a lead of roughly 1,350 years and, for the first time in the imperial record, no longer widening.
