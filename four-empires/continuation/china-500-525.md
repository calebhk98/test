## XXX. The Iron and the Grassland: Finishing the Pontic Line (500 to 515 AD)

The Pontic extension, sixty to seventy percent complete at the turn of the century, does not finish on the schedule the court set for it. The engineers' reports from 500 to 505 identify the cause plainly: the line and the arsenals compete for the same coal and iron, and in a year when the Syr Darya foundries need every ingot for hand cannon and naval fittings, the rail crews wait. The court reviews the project in 505 and revises the completion estimate outward by a decade, to a new target near 515 to 520, a revision the engineers present without apology, since the iron cannot be in two places at once.

The delay compounds when the construction gangs meet resistance. The peoples ground down between the advancing rail and Rome's line at the Dnieper, the dwindling remnant the frontier reports have tracked for two centuries, have nowhere left to retreat, and around 508 to 511 AD a band of several hundred falls on an exposed construction camp and supply depot in the western Pontic sector, killing perhaps sixty laborers and soldiers, burning stockpiled sleepers, and making off with tools and a small quantity of powder before the nearest garrison can respond in force. It is the costliest single loss of life the rail project has suffered anywhere along its length. The court debates a punitive campaign into the forest fringe and rejects it, on the reasoning that has governed this frontier for two hundred years: the raiders hold no territory worth the campaign, and chasing them off the garrison chain surrenders every advantage gunpowder gives a defended position. The response instead is the familiar one, doubled rather than reinvented: outpost spacing along the vulnerable stretch is tightened, mounted patrols added, and the powder at each depot dispersed into smaller, separately guarded caches. Construction resumes within the year, slower and better guarded, and no attack of comparable size recurs before the line's completion.

The rail reaches the Dnieper in 523, eight years later than the hopeful estimate given at 500, at a cost the treasury officials afterward describe as having run high in every year of construction. It is, even so, finished, and its completion does for the Pontic garrisons what the Syr Darya line did seventy years earlier: a frontier held by appearance more than substance for two centuries now has genuine supply behind it. The black-earth country the rail now serves begins, tentatively, to be broken to the plow; the first substantial harvests from newly opened black-earth fields are recorded around 521 to 524, still small against the empire's total grain output but the first return, after two centuries of holding the ground without farming it, on the richest soil the frontier has ever claimed.

## XXXI. The Examination Scandal (509 to 517 AD)

The education system's compounding growth produces in this period the first crisis to its legitimacy since printing began cheapening the classics. Around 509, an investigation opens into an unusually strong showing by several candidates from a single prominent clan in the lower Yangtze, after an examiner's clerk is found to possess printed sheets matching that year's questions before the sealed papers are distributed. The inquiry uncovers a wider arrangement: a printing house contracted to reproduce approved study texts has been selling advance copies of draft questions to wealthy families through intermediaries for at least three examination cycles, the questions drawn from an examiner's working notes rather than stolen outright, which is how the scheme evaded the safeguards built around the sealed papers themselves.

The scandal lands hard because it strikes the precise claim the previous century's expansion of education had rested on, that the examination tested ability rather than money. Frontier and merchant families, the constituency that had done best out of the system's widening, are the loudest in demanding correction, since it is exactly their advantage, hard-won numeracy against inherited wealth, that a leaked question undoes. The court's response, worked out over 512 to 517, is procedural: the examiners responsible and the printing house's principals are removed and their positions confiscated, but the standing reform is the copying of every submitted paper by a corps of clerks before it reaches a grader, so that no examiner can match a paper to a family through its handwriting, a practice extended within the decade to the drafting of the questions themselves, now the work of a rotating panel sequestered before each sitting rather than any single examiner whose notes might be sold again. The reform does not end favoritism, since a determined and wealthy family always finds some channel, but it closes the mechanism the scandal exposed, and the court is careful to be seen doing so, aware that the legitimacy the examination lends the whole administrative class cannot survive many more scandals of this kind without cost to the throne itself.

## XXXII. The Failure at the Naval Yard (513 to 519 AD)

The steam pump's long service in the Mongolian mines had by 500 raised, without resolving, the question of whether the same force could drive a hull, and the pressure to answer it grows through this period as reports from Aden continue to describe Egyptian vessels moving without sail or oar. Around 513 the naval command, with the court's backing and against the caution of some of the arsenal's senior engineers, authorizes a trial: a large boiler built to the naval yard's own specifications, at greater size and higher pressure than any mine pump has run, connected through a rocking mechanism to a paddle wheel fitted to a decommissioned hull, on the reasoning that if greater force moves more water from a mine shaft, greater force still should turn a wheel against the resistance of the sea.

The trial ends badly. In 518, during a test run intended to demonstrate sustained operation rather than a single stroke, the boiler ruptures at the naval yard outside the southern port where the vessel has been fitted, killing eleven of the engineers and crew present and injuring perhaps twice that number, several severely. The inquiry that follows, conducted with the same thoroughness the court brings to every failure of consequence, finds the fault not in the casting or the welding, both of which hold to the standard the arsenals have long since mastered, but in the reasoning behind the design itself: the engineers, understanding only that the mine pump's power grows with the heat and pressure applied to it, had pushed both well beyond anything the mine installations run, without any way to calculate in advance what pressure the vessel could safely hold, because no one in the empire yet possesses the principle that would let such a calculation be made rather than guessed at. The report's own language is unusually direct for an arsenal document, stating that the failure came from applying more force in pursuit of a result the engineers did not understand rather than from any fault of craft, and that craft alone, however skilled, cannot substitute for the missing theory.

The naval command does not abandon the project outright, but it is set back by years rather than months: further trials are suspended pending a review the court does not complete within this period, funding is redirected toward the rifled and breech-loading cannon programs already showing steady progress, and the yard where the accident occurred is given over to conventional shipbuilding for the remainder of the decade. China's engineers remain confident that the principle behind Egypt's ships can eventually be reached, but the confidence purchased at eleven lives is a costlier kind than the one the arsenals held in 500, and the court's private assessment, recorded in the aftermath, is that the gap with Egypt may be wider than the earlier reports from Aden had suggested, since Egypt's vessels evidently rest on an understanding China does not yet have any working substitute for.

## XXXIII. A Small Discovery at Court (505 to 522 AD)

Against this setback, the natural-philosophy circles that the examination reforms and the Egypt contact have been feeding produce a modest advance entirely of their own making. Scholars working with silk, amber ornaments, and the fine glass the Aden exchange has taught them to grind record, sometime around 505 to 515, that a rod of amber vigorously rubbed with silk cloth will afterward draw light scraps of paper and chaff to itself across a small gap, and that the same effect can be produced, more faintly, with glass rubbed against wool. The observation is written down, compared against the fragmentary secondhand accounts of the Maurya court's own claims to have observed something of the same force, and filed among the growing body of proto-scientific notes without any framework to explain it; the scholars who record it are careful to note that they do not know whether this is the same force Egypt calls the electrical arc, only that it resembles, at the small scale available to them, what little has been reported of it. No practical use follows, and none is expected in the near term, but the notice taken at court is real: it is the first time China has produced, by its own observation and without instruction from Egypt or report from the Mauryas, a piece of evidence bearing on a phenomenon it had until now known only at second hand, and the scholars responsible are given standing patronage to continue the work, on the same modest model that funded the fangshi six centuries before them, though the court is careful this time to record the funding as natural philosophy rather than the search for an elixir.

## XXXIV. The Frontier Ledger (515 to 525 AD)

Intelligence gathered through Silk Road merchants across this period places the Maurya land-cannon inventory at the Syr Darya and its approaches at somewhere between sixty and ninety pieces by 520, roughly double the estimate of fifty years before, with the same merchant reports describing Maurya foundries now casting barrels in-house rather than relying on captured or purchased pieces. China's assessment does not change in kind, only in degree: the gunpowder monopoly China once held alone continues its slow spread beyond its own borders, the age and quality advantage narrows further without closing, and the court's standing judgment, that China retains probable rather than decisive superiority at the frontier, is renewed rather than revised. The Han-Rome Agreement's transit terms are quietly renewed without renegotiation across this period, the trade in silk and western horses continuing to grow in volume year on year, and Rome's correspondence, consistent with the transparency it committed to two centuries earlier, reports a change of emperor in 511 that passes, as before, without disturbance to the Dnieper frontier.

### State of the Realm, c. 525 AD

The population stands near seventy-two million, recovering from the epidemic of the 460s and beginning, slowly, to draw on the newly broken black-earth grain of the Pontic frontier. The economy is near thirty-seven and a half thousand tonnes of silver-equivalent, state revenue near forty-two hundred, the extraction rate holding near eleven percent. The rail's completion to the Dnieper has removed one major drain on the treasury even as the naval yard disaster and the continuing cost of the breech-loading and rifled-cannon programs keep the surplus modest; the treasury's cash position is somewhat improved over 500 but remains thin against any sudden demand. Human capital continues its climb, the literate population approaching six and a half million, the investigation tier near nine hundred, the broad technical workforce above forty-five thousand. Military technology stands at mature rifled cannon in growing naval service, breech-loading land cannon entering cautious, limited field issue for the first time around 520 to 522 after decades in development, and steam propulsion set back by the failure of 518 to a footing no more advanced than the pump alone. The examination system, chastened by scandal but structurally strengthened by the reforms that followed it, continues to widen its base. Territorially the empire's western frontier stands, for the first time in its history, fully connected by rail from the heartland to both the Syr Darya and the Dnieper, the two most exposed positions the empire has ever held now among its most secure. The gunpowder monopoly that founded the whole history continues to narrow in the hands of others, slowly and without any single dramatic loss, and the empire enters the second quarter of the sixth century still the most heavily armed power it knows of, though by a margin it can no longer take entirely for granted.
