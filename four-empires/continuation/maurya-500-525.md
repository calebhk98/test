## Part XII: The Long Mending (500 to 525 AD)

### The Reign of the Rebuilt Fleet (500 to 525 AD)

The ruler who took the throne in 500 inherited a realm richer on paper than in fact: the largest dominion in the world, a treasury still turning a healthy surplus, and a navy that existed mostly in the shipwrights' yards. He was a capable man, raised as a provincial governor in Sindh before the succession found him, and he understood from the first day of his reign what the eastern defeat had actually cost. It was not the islands, which the realm still held. It was standing. A power that could not defend its own waters was a power other states would test again, and he set himself to closing that gap before anything else.

> **The reasoning.** He judged that the realm had answered the eastern crisis twice already, once with makeshift tactics that had failed at the Eastern Approaches, and once with a cannon program too young to matter at the Fall of the Eastern Fleet. A third answer built the same way, in haste and under pressure, would fail the same way. What the navy needed was not urgency but years, a proper corps of trained gun crews, hulls built from the start to carry the weight rather than fitted for it afterward, and a doctrine worked out in calm water before it was tested in anger. He gave the naval cannon program the resources it needed and the one thing it had never had before: time enough to fail in private.

The building went forward on the southern coast, at the yards that had grown up around the Tamil ports generations before, where the timber and the seamen and now the cannon foundries stood within reach of one another. By 508 the yards had put twenty new hulls into the water built specifically to carry guns, broader in the beam than the old warships and reinforced along the gun deck, a design worked out from the hard lesson of the mountings that had taken the previous reign years to solve.

### The Karwar Disaster

The pace of the building outran the caution behind it. In 511, eager to show the court a heavier vessel than any yet built, the master shipwright at the Karwar yards laid down a ship meant to carry six cannon where the standard hull carried two, reasoning that a heavier line of guns fired in concert would answer the Chinese fleet's volume of fire with volume of the realm's own. The keel was widened to bear the weight, but the recoil of six guns fired together was never tested short of the open sea, and the calculations of stress along the gun deck were the shipwright's own, checked by no one senior to him, for no one senior to him understood the mathematics as well as he believed he did.

> **What went wrong.** On its trial voyage out of Karwar harbor in 512, the new ship fired a full broadside for the first time, all six guns at once, to show the assembled court officials what such a vessel could do. The gun deck opened along a seam the shipwright's stress reckoning had not accounted for, and the ship took water fast enough that the crew could not save her. She went down within sight of the harbor mouth with the loss of most of her gun crews and her master shipwright, who did not leave the deck. Two of the six cannon were recovered from the shallows in the weeks after; the rest, and the ship, were not.

The disaster cost the naval program more than the one hull and the men aboard it. The court's confidence in rapid heavy construction broke with the ship, and for three years afterward every new keel was built conservatively, to the older two-gun and four-gun patterns already proven, while a commission of mathematicians and working shipwrights together worked out, this time on paper before it was tried at sea, what a heavier hull could actually bear. The commission's method, cross-checking a builder's craft sense against a scholar's arithmetic rather than trusting either alone, became the standing rule for every large vessel built after, and by 525 the realm again had ships carrying four guns apiece built to a design tested first in calculation and then in calm water rather than in front of the court. But the three lost years, and the loss of a master shipwright whose skill was not easily replaced, were never recovered, and the fleet at the reign's end was smaller than the reign's early ambitions had promised.

### The Southern Road and the Eastern Peace

The secret depot on the northwest coast of the southern continent, planted in the last years of the crisis, was quietly built out through this reign into a working station: a stockade, a well, storehouses roofed in local timber, and a small yard where hulls could be patched with wood cut on the spot. Supply ships ran the route from Ternate and Banda south and around rather than through the watched waters near the Strait, and by the reign's later years the islands' garrisons drew a meaningful share of their grain and powder from that hidden road without China, so far as the realm's agents could tell, ever remarking on it. The secret held. What the realm could not judge, having no eyes on the matter, was whether Egypt's own reach along the African coast, which had already surprised the realm once by its true extent, might in time come close enough to the southern continent's western shore to find by its own effort what the realm meant to keep for itself. The court noted the risk and did nothing further about it beyond keeping the depot small and quiet, judging that a larger, more defensible station would only draw the notice a small one escaped.

With China, the peace held on the terms the crisis had set. Chinese ships came and went through the Strait paying nothing, the old toll settled at its restored twelve parts in the hundred on the overland road, and the cannon stayed on the Syr Darya. Two Chinese trading missions reached Pataliputra in this reign, the second in 517 bringing a formal letter that asked, again and without heat, after the frontier cannon, and received, again, the same answer. The realm read the asking itself as proof that China had not forgotten the point and might yet press it when some future advantage made pressing worthwhile, and it kept the northern garrisons at full strength rather than let the long quiet lull them down, a caution the treasury bore without complaint. The realm's engineers, still working toward a gun a man could carry, brought a crew-served two-man piece to working order by about 513, lighter than any fixed cannon and moved by hand-cart rather than draught oxen, though still too heavy and too slow to load for one soldier alone. A true single-soldier firearm remained beyond them at the reign's end, the first crude hand-built trials producing pieces that scorched their users as often as they fired true, and the engineers put the realm's own start at such a weapon another decade or more distant, no nearer the fifteen-to-twenty-five-year span reckoned a generation before than the passage of years alone had made it.

### Kolar, Pataliputra, and the Scribes

The Kolar mines, worked now for the better part of eight centuries, gave the reign a harder problem than any battle. The steam pumps sent south in the previous reign had cleared the shafts that flooding had closed, and for some years the deeper ore came up again in real quantity. But the ore at that depth ran thinner than the shallow seams the mines had first made their name on, more rock moved for less gold recovered, and by the early 520s the engineers reported that a second ring of pumps, larger and more numerous, would be needed merely to hold the workings at their current output, let alone increase it. The ruler funded the second ring, judging the gold too old and too central a part of the realm's name to let go without a fight, but the court's own auditors noted plainly that the mine was being kept level by ever-greater engineering rather than growing, and that a third ring, when the ore ran thinner still, might cost more than the gold it raised. No one at court could yet say when that day would come, only that it was now visibly closer than it had been.

In Pataliputra the old quarrel over printing flared once more. A guild of copying scribes, whose livelihood the spread of administrative printing had already thinned across two reigns, petitioned the throne in 519 to have the printed law codes withdrawn from the provincial courts and the work returned to hand copying, arguing that a court's justice should rest on human judgment carried by a human hand and not on identical sheets struck without any judgment in them at all. The Brahmin establishment, whose deeper objection had always been to sacred and not administrative texts, did not join the petition, and without their weight behind it the throne refused the scribes' plea and let the presses run on as before, offering the guild's members places training as typesetters and correctors instead of copyists. Some took the offer. Others did not, and a small, bitter faction of scribes remained in Pataliputra through the rest of the reign, their trade shrinking year by year, a minor and unresolved sore that cost the throne nothing but their goodwill.

### Rome, Egypt, and a New Creature in the Water

The tie with Rome ran smoothly through the reign, the overland road busier than ever and its bandit trouble thinned further by a chain of new waystations built at the merchants' own cost, shared between the two thrones. Rome's letters, though, carried a changed tone in their closing years that the court set down and puzzled over: more of them now invoked the Christian god by name, in phrasing that read to the realm's Sanskrit-trained scribes as more than mere habit of speech, and one letter from the Syrian frontier governor pressed, gently but plainly, for assurance that the Mesopotamian Christians under Maurya rule were not made to suffer for their faith. The realm answered as its law had always answered, that belief was untaxed and conduct alone accountable, and the matter passed without friction. The court noted only that a question of that kind had never been asked of it before.

Egypt, in these years, gave nothing new of its great secrets, but the realm's own naturalists, set searching after Egypt's old hint that the galvanic force lived in living things as well as in metal and salt water, found in the rivers of the eastern Gangetic delta a fish that struck a hand held too near it with a shock unmistakably of the same family as the wire in salt water, sluggish and short-ranged beside what Egypt's torpedo fish was said to manage, but the realm's first creature of its own kind to study rather than a foreign report to take on faith. The scholars of the medical schools took to keeping the fish in tanks and testing it against every apparatus the galvanic program had built, and by the reign's end had confirmed the family relation beyond doubt, though no one yet had turned the finding toward carrying a message a single mile.

> **By the numbers, around 525 AD.** The realm's people number some hundred and one million, the population recovering its full pace of growth now that the eastern war and its levies lie behind it. Yearly revenue stands near twenty-four hundred million paṇas, a slow climb restored, some ten hundred and ten million the land tax and about seven hundred and sixty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls, though the mine share leans harder now on cost than on yield. The standing army holds near eleven hundred and thirty thousand: four hundred and forty thousand field infantry, five hundred and ten thousand in garrison and second line, a hundred thousand horse, and a rebuilding navy of near sixty thousand crew. About nine hundred and thirty million goes to the army, a hundred and sixty million to weapons research and armament, and two hundred and forty million to roads and works. The reserve, spent hard by the Karwar loss and the second ring of pumps at Kolar, has climbed back to something over seven-tenths of a year's revenue, and about one person in ten still reads.

The ruler died in his bed in 525, the succession already settled on a son raised, as the custom of the strongest reigns had always raised heirs, in the provinces before the capital. The realm he left behind held every mile of ground his predecessor had held, a navy smaller than his ambitions but built on surer footing than before, a peace with China that neither side trusted and both kept, and a gold mine growing more expensive to hold level with every passing decade. Nothing had been lost that the realm could not bear, and nothing had been gained that changed the shape of the board. It was, by the plain reckoning of the court's own historians, a capable reign and no more, the kind the realm had learned across seven and a half centuries to be grateful for.
