## Part X: The Uneasy Quarter-Century (500–525 AD)

Egypt enters the sixth century holding more territory, more revenue, and more accumulated knowledge than any state the council can find record of, and holding it, for the first time in four hundred years, without confidence in the one number that matters most: how much time is left before Rome decides the arithmetic favors war. The twenty-five years that follow do not answer that question. They narrow it, and they cost the empire something in the process.

### The Congo Works and the Nitrogen Program, 505–520 AD

The proposal that had waited on the aircraft now moves. Surveys at the Livingstone Falls, already bridged by the portage railway, confirm what the engineers expected: a fall of water sufficient, properly harnessed, to run furnaces no thermal plant could support at the same cost. Construction on the first generating works begins around 507 AD, funded jointly from the military budget and the research allotment, and by 520 the falls are driving arc furnaces and electrochemical baths on a modest scale, enough to matter to the instrument shops and the assay houses but not yet enough to free aluminum in bulk. The smelter proper, the installation that would turn the metal from a curiosity into an airframe material, is still under construction at the close of the period, its foundations poured but its full capacity years from completion. The engineers report no reason to doubt the design; the delay is the ordinary delay of a project this size in country this remote, moving men, iron, and cement along a rail line that ends at a waterfall in the middle of a continent.

The other program set in motion by the century's turn is quieter and less visible even inside the administration. The chemists who tamed acid and nitrated cellulose have known for a generation what gates a true weapon: nitrogen, in quantity, reliably supplied. From about 508 AD the crown funds niter beds on a deliberate, industrial scale, laid out near the Delta's old tanning and mummification-adjacent works where the raw material and the trained hands already exist, a multi-year commitment the council accepts because a security-conscious treasury can afford to wait for a fuse to mature. By 525 the beds are productive but young, yielding enough refined saltpeter for the arsenal's blasting charges and a handful of experimental cased rounds fired under guard at the Sinai proving ground, not yet enough to arm a field piece in numbers that would matter in a war. The council's own engineers write plainly that a serviceable field gun is a problem of barrel metallurgy as much as propellant, and that the metallurgy, drawing on the arc-furnace steel the Congo works are only beginning to supply at scale, is behind the powder rather than ahead of it. Firearms remain what they were at the century's turn: known to be reachable, not yet reached.

### The Warming Between Rome and India, 502–516 AD

Egypt's agents in the Roman east report, with the usual months of delay that commercial cover imposes, a change in the traffic between Rome and India that the council has feared since China broke India's eastern fleet. Indian embassies to Rome, once occasional and cautious, become regular after about 503 AD, and by 508 the Aden office confirms a standing Indian trade mission resident in a Roman provincial capital, the first such arrangement either power has maintained with the other. Egypt cannot see the substance of what is discussed; its intelligence is commercial and sees ports and caravans, not the rooms where Rome and India actually talk. What it can see is the shape of it, more embassies, more overland traffic between the two, less need for either to route goods or messages through Egyptian water, and the shape alone confirms the thing the council has spent four centuries trying to prevent from happening at all.

The council's response draws on the oldest instrument it has for a problem it cannot solve openly. War with either power over a diplomatic courtesy is unthinkable, so in 510 AD the intelligence service is instructed to do to the India–Rome channel what it has long done to the overland silk route: bleed it without a visible hand. Funds move through the same cut-out banking arrangements that have quietly funded Syrian banditry for over a century, this time toward disrupting the caravan traffic and courier routes that now carry the new embassies' correspondence and gifts between the two courts, the calculation being that repeated loss and delay will sour each power's confidence in the other's ability to secure the relationship.

It does not work, and it does badly. A raid on an Indian courier train in 512 AD kills two Roman escorts traveling with it, an outcome the Egyptian planners had not intended and had not adequately guarded against, since the courier route had not previously carried joint escorts. Roman investigators, working the incident harder than any earlier caravan loss had warranted, trace payment through two of the cut-outs to a banking house at Aden that the service had used for over a decade and had judged, wrongly, to be clean. Rome does not accuse Egypt publicly, and does not need to. What it does, over the following three years, is share what it has found with India through the very channel Egypt was trying to strain, quietly and without fanfare, the kind of gift that costs a great power nothing and buys a great deal. Egypt watches its own operation become the evidence that draws India and Rome closer rather than further apart, and by 516 the council accepts, in its own closed records, that the intervention achieved the precise opposite of its purpose. The banking house is dissolved, its principals resettled inland with new names and no further intelligence role, and the service is ordered to stand entirely clear of the India–Rome channel going forward, whatever it does. What Rome now believes about Egypt's hand in the wider pattern of accidents and banditry across the eastern trade of the last century, the council does not know, and has no way to find out.

### The Cape Verde Loss and the Tightening of the Atlantic Watch, 517–521 AD

Whether the Aden exposure is connected, the council cannot say with certainty, though it assumes the worst as a matter of discipline. What is certain is that Roman naval activity off the northwest African coast increases sharply after 517 AD, patrols run more frequently and further south than in living memory, and in 519 a Roman squadron operating off the coast near the Cape Verde peninsula intercepts and destroys a light Egyptian scout vessel running dispatches between the coal-depot chain and the heliograph relay. It is the first Egyptian hull lost in that theater in over a century of Roman probes, every earlier encounter having gone the other way, and it costs the empire eleven crew, a set of relay codebooks that the council must now assume compromised, and the certainty, for the first time, that Rome can reach and hold that stretch of water on something other than Egypt's terms. No survivors are recovered by either side, so what Rome actually learned from the wreck, whether the codebooks were read before they went down with the ship or salvaged intact, is exactly the kind of thing Egypt's own intelligence, built for ports and merchants, cannot answer. The council orders the relay chain's southern stations moved further from the coast and the codes replaced empire-wide, a costly and disruptive measure taken against a threat whose actual size nobody in Alexandria can currently state.

No war follows. The confrontation the council estimated at ten to twenty-five years out from its shock discovery of Roman industry in 408–412 AD has now been wrong for the better part of a century, and the council's current estimate, revised again in 521, is offered with less confidence than the last one carried, an acknowledgment on the page that the model has failed to predict twice and may fail a third time. What holds, through all of this, is the older logic that has held since the Sinai settlement of 90 BC: neither power finds the arithmetic of open war favorable yet, and both keep testing the edges of what the other will tolerate without crossing into where testing becomes fighting.

### Elsewhere, 500–525 AD

Against this quieter friction, the ordinary machinery of the empire runs largely as it has. The China partnership renews its terms without incident in 512 AD, paper output continues climbing as flax-and-hemp cultivation expands to meet it, and the telephone and radio networks extend into their third tier of provincial cities. Aksum and Himyar, five and six generations past conquest, send governors to the capital who are, by every measure the council tracks, indistinguishable from Alexandrian-born administrators in language, loyalty, and ambition. Proto-germ medicine and the steady spread of Old World crops through the African provinces continue to push the population past its old ceiling, and the reserve, still targeted at two years of revenue, absorbs both the Congo works and the Aden cleanup without visible strain.

### By the numbers, around 525 AD

- **Population:** approaching twenty-nine million, the growth still accelerating on the strength of crop transfer and disease control, no plague of consequence in the period.
- **Treasury revenue:** on the order of a hundred and thirty-eight thousand talents a year, the growth rate slowing as the trade-toll and gold streams mature and the agrarian base absorbs most of the new increase.
- **Reserve:** roughly two hundred seventy-five thousand talents, holding at the two-year target despite the Congo works and the Aden and Cape Verde costs, both absorbed without drawing the reserve below its floor.
- **Research establishment:** between twenty-five and forty thousand fully funded scholars and engineers, the growth incremental rather than accelerating, consistent with a program meeting its own industrial ceiling rather than a new wave of foundational discovery.
- **The Congo hydro works:** generating at partial capacity, the bulk-aluminum smelter under construction and not yet operational.
- **The nitrogen program:** productive but immature; explosives remain a mining and special-munitions capability, not yet a field-weapon one.
- **The confrontation estimate:** revised in 521 AD and held with declared low confidence, the council's working assumption being only that the window has not closed and cannot be assumed distant.
