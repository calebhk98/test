# The Maurya Realm: A History from the Kalinga Decision

### An Imperial Record, Compiled at Pataliputra

---

> **On this record.** This is the history of the Maurya realm as its own institutions understand it, drawn from the court registers, the treasury's reckonings, the military archives, and the reports of merchants, envoys, and the intelligence networks. Where the record describes foreign powers, it describes them only as the realm's agents have seen or heard them. Where the court has drawn conclusions about a foreign power's intentions or inner workings, those conclusions are marked plainly as the court's interpretation. The realm does not claim to know what it has not seen. Some of what follows about distant powers may in time prove mistaken. It is set down as it was understood.
>
> Throughout, the Maurya realm means the empire itself, and not any single family. The throne has passed through several ruling houses across these centuries, some by inheritance and some through a contested succession, but the record counts them as one continuous empire so long as the state held together unbroken, its army and its administration serving the throne rather than any bloodline. A change of dynasty is not a change of realm.
>
> A reader new to these matters will find a glossary of names, places, and terms in the final appendix. Sums of money are given in *paṇas*, the realm's standard silver coin.

---

## Part I: The Kalinga Decision (c. 261 to 260 BC)

The realm as it now stands begins with a single choice made by one man on a battlefield.

In the year that would be reckoned 261 BC, the emperor Ashoka completed the conquest of Kalinga, a stubborn coastal kingdom on the eastern seaboard of the subcontinent that had refused Maurya authority for generations. The campaign was won, and it was terrible. By the emperor's own later account, the killing, the deportations, and the ruin of the countryside reached a scale that stayed with him for the rest of his life.

A conquest of that cost can turn a ruler in one of two directions. It can break him inward, toward renunciation, grief, and the abandonment of the sword for the pursuit of peace of the soul. Or it can settle in him as knowledge, the plain understanding that rule is paid for in blood and that a throne unwilling to pay that price will not keep its lands. Ashoka turned the second way.

> **The reasoning.** Ashoka did not read the suffering of Kalinga as a summons to lay down power. He read it as the permanent cost of governing, a cost that does not vanish if a ruler looks away from it. Having paid that cost, he judged that the only waste would be to win Kalinga and then fail to build it into something worth the paying. He resolved to hold Kalinga, settle it, integrate it, and use it.

Ashoka had been trained for rule the hard way, as a provincial governor at Taxila in the northwest and later at Ujjain in the center, before he ever took the throne. He understood both armies and account books. Freed of any impulse to withdraw from the world, he remained a fully engaged ruler to the end of his life, and he spent that life preparing the ground for the conquests his heirs would make.

### The Consolidation

Ashoka did not press outward again in his own reign, though the desire for more land never left him. The statecraft he governed by held that a realm must keep expanding to stay secure, and he wanted the country beyond his borders. What he could not find was a way to take and hold it without stretching his supply past what it could bear, and a lunge past that limit would lose more than it won. So he turned the resources of the state inward and forward, to build the platform from which the next generation could reach what he could not.

He built roads across the realm, lined with rest houses a day's travel apart, wells, and relay stations where a rider could change horses and carry a message onward. He planted farming settlements in newly won Kalinga, turning occupied ground into productive ground that fed itself and paid taxes. Most deliberately, he pushed granaries, armories, and garrison towns progressively closer to the frontiers, so that the supplies an army needs would already be waiting near the edge of the realm rather than having to be dragged from the capital.

> **The reasoning.** Ashoka stated the principle to his court plainly. An army that must carry its supply from the capital can reach only so far before it starves. An army that draws its supply from ground the realm already holds can reach much farther. By moving the supply base outward in his own reign, he made the next generation's expansion possible without ordering a single new conquest himself.

### The Rolling Frontier Doctrine

Ashoka noticed a rhythm in his own family. His grandfather Chandragupta had built the realm by conquest. His father Bindusara had widened and steadied it. He himself had taken Kalinga. Each ruler had worked from the platform the last one left. Ashoka wrote this rhythm down as guidance for his heirs, and it became the settled doctrine of the dynasty: conquer only what you can hold and supply, then spend your reign turning that new ground into the platform from which your successor pushes again. A ruler who lunges past his supply destroys what he has. A ruler who digs in and builds hands his son a longer spear.

Underneath the doctrine sat an older body of statecraft, the tradition associated with the minister Kautilya and known as the *Arthashastra*, the science of governance. It is a cold and practical philosophy. It holds that a king who stops growing invites attack, that the neighbor on your border is your natural rival, and that the strength of a state rests on several pillars at once, its territory, its treasury, its army, its fortified cities, its allies, its wise counsel, and the king himself. A ruler must tend all of these together. Ashoka tended all of them.

When he died in about 232 BC, he left his heir a realm more tightly governed than the one he had inherited, with granaries and garrisons pointing plainly toward the northwest, toward the mountain passes and the young Greek kingdom of Bactria beyond them.

> **By the numbers, around 250 BC.** The realm holds some thirty-two million people and most of the Indian subcontinent, the lands of modern India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Bhutan, reaching into modern Afghanistan, short of the Tamil far south. Its treasury takes in about three hundred and seventy-five million paṇas a year, some two hundred and twenty-five million of it the land tax on the harvest and only a small part from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and internal tolls. The standing army runs near two hundred thousand, well under one man in a hundred: about ninety thousand field infantry, as many again in garrison and second line, ten thousand horse, and a small river-and-coast navy, all built around a corps of some five thousand war elephants. That elephant corps holds near five or six thousand for the whole of the realm's history, for elephants are caught and tamed rather than bred, and no coin makes them multiply faster. About a hundred and sixty million of the yearly spending goes to the army and fifty million to roads and works, the reserve is thin, and letters belong to perhaps one person in ten.

## Part II: The Foundation Reigns (232 to 200 BC)

Ashoka's son had been raised for rule in the same hard school, governing provinces before inheriting the throne. His achievement was the northwestern campaign that his father's roads and forward supplies had been built to enable.

He aimed northwest for two reasons. First, a Greek kingdom in Bactria, the region of modern northern Afghanistan and the lands just north of it, had broken free around 250 BC from the Seleucid realm that governed the eastern lands after Alexander, and it was growing into a real military power. It sat astride the historic invasion road into the subcontinent, the same passes through which every conqueror from the west had come. A strong hostile power left to harden on that road was a permanent knife at the realm's throat. Second, those same lands sat along the overland trade route that carried goods between the far east and the western markets, the route later generations called the Silk Road. Whoever held Bactria taxed that trade.

The campaign crossed the mountains through the established passes and struck the Greek kingdom during one of its bouts of internal quarreling over the succession. Here the realm's signature weapon proved itself again.

> **On war elephants and horses.** The Maurya army was built around a corps of war elephants, and its dominance for centuries rested on a simple fact of animal nature. A horse that has never been trained to bear the presence of elephants panics near them. The smell, the noise, and the sheer size of the beasts trigger blind flight that no rider can fully master. Enemy armies built on cavalry, and most of the realm's enemies were, could not hold their formation when the elephants came on. The Greek horsemen of Bactria, however skilled, broke like the rest.

The frontier advanced. In the latter part of his reign the son did what his father had done, settling the new ground with farms, garrisons, and roads, and extending the same patient development southward and eastward through the Deccan plateau so that supply capacity grew everywhere at once.

His reach ran south as well as north. During his reign the island of Sri Lanka, off the subcontinent's southern tip, was absorbed by force and brought under Maurya administration. Past the empire's southern frontier lay the Tamil kingdoms, the Chola, the Pandya, and the Chera, peoples too distant and too separate to govern directly, and these the son bound to the realm as tributaries, their payments secured by the presence of Maurya arms rather than by settled rule. A tribute of that kind was cheap to demand and dear to keep, resting wholly on a firm hand at the center.

By his death around 200 BC the realm held the whole subcontinent, the island of Sri Lanka, the Afghan regions, and a reach of Central Asia, with the Tamil kingdoms of the far south paying tribute at its edge. The Kolar gold fields of the southern Deccan, among the richest gold deposits in the known world, had come firmly into its hands and had begun steady production. Together, the gold and the growing Silk Road tolls made the treasury the richest the dynasty had yet held.

## Part III: Contraction and Recovery (200 to 75 BC)

Here the dynasty met the hard rule of its own governance: the realm rose and fell with the quality of the man on the throne, and the newest conquests were always the first to slip when that quality failed. The heartland held through any reign. The edges answered to the ruler.

### The Two Mediocre Reigns (200 to 150 BC)

The grandson of Ashoka, who came to the throne around 200 BC, was a competent administrator with neither ambition nor vision. He made no grave error and advanced no purpose. Under him the Greek kingdom of Bactria, recovered from its succession troubles and led again by able kings, pressed back against the northernmost Maurya gains beyond the mountains. The fortified Hindu Kush passes, old and deeply garrisoned, held without trouble, but the further Bactrian ground the son had taken fell back to more defensible lines. In the far south, the tribute of the Tamil kingdoms, which had always needed the visible threat of Maurya arms to collect, grew unreliable once that threat went unrenewed.

The ruler who followed, from about 175 to 150 BC, was weaker still, and his reign fell in the worst possible years. It overlapped the height of Menander, the ablest king the Greco-Bactrians ever produced. Menander pressed east against a realm whose military attention had lapsed through two slack reigns, and he stripped away what was left of the son's Bactrian conquests. The passes held, as they always held. Beyond them the frontier gave way. Tamil tribute in these years became a fiction, claimed in the records and collected nowhere.

Through both reigns, the things that did not depend on the ruler carried on. The integrated heartland governed itself. The gold mines produced. The Silk Road trade crossed Maurya ground and paid Maurya tolls. A weak king could lose an edge. He could not reach the core.

### The Near-Great Recovery (150 to 125 BC)

An abler ruler inherited the contracted realm around 150 BC, and his position improved from a cause outside his own doing. He did not throw his rebuilt army against Menander's strength. He spent his first fifteen years restoring the army's readiness, repairing an administration two mediocre reigns had let decay, and pressing the Tamil south back into firm tribute through a renewed show of force.

Then the opening came. Menander died around 130 BC, and the Greco-Bactrian kingdom broke apart among rival successors who could not combine. The realm moved fast. The northwestern campaign resumed against a divided enemy, and the frontier rolled forward once more.

### The First Great Reign of the Era (125 to 100 BC)

The next ruler reached the throne at the most favorable moment the realm had yet known. The Bactrian Greeks were fragmenting from within, and from the northern steppe a nomadic people, the Yuezhi, were pressing down on them from the far side. The Yuezhi at this time were still a loose scatter of clans, not yet unified into a single state, and each clan was manageable alone. Against enemies who could not act together, a strong Maurya army with its elephant corps applied the oldest method in the *Arthashastra*: single out the most isolated enemy, defeat him or win him over, and turn him against the next.

Clan by clan and city by city, the frontier advanced. The realm absorbed the Greek remnants and the nearer Yuezhi, took the Sogdian cities around Samarkand in the region of modern Uzbekistan, and with them the power to tax the Silk Road far closer to its source, along a much longer stretch of every caravan's road. It reached the gold in the Bactrian river valleys north of the mountains, so that a single advance yielded both the toll on the trade above ground and the metal beneath it.

### The Second Great Reign of the Era (100 to 75 BC)

The ruler who closed the era pushed in two directions at once. In the north he drove deeper into the Sogdian heartland, securing the great oasis cities of the central Silk Road. In the west he turned against Parthia.

Parthia was the power holding the wide country between the realm and the distant Mediterranean, a state built, like most of the realm's enemies, on the horse, though on light riders who shot from the saddle and wheeled away, feared across the western world for it. Against elephants these did not break as heavy cavalry broke. They would not stand for the charge at all, loosing their arrows and giving ground, so that a Parthian army was seldom shattered in a single afternoon; it slipped away to fight again. Where a pitched battle could be forced on them, the elephants won the field, yet an enemy who declines the decisive fight is slow to finish, and the western war became a long grind of taking ground Parthia would not die to hold and keeping it against those who fought on once the armies had ridden off. The realm saw that Parthia kept its armies and its attention fixed to the west, leaving its eastern province of Khorasan, in northeastern Iran, thinly held. That province was taken.

In these same years the sea began to speak. Merchants returning from the western waters carried word of the wealth moving along the Arabian coast: the incense of Yemen, the aromatics and East African goods of the Red Sea trade, and the shipping of the Persian Gulf. The Oman shore, commanding the mouth of the gulf, was marked as a maritime and commercial prize of the first rank.

> **The court's puzzle over Parthia.** The realm's analysts found Parthia's behavior strange and worth noting. Parthia's oldest and deadliest rival lay to its west. Yet Parthia kept turning east against the realm while neglecting that western frontier. The court concluded that Parthia must have reached some settled peace with its western rival, freeing it to look east. *This was the court's inference from Parthia's conduct, not a fact the realm ever confirmed. It is recorded as an interpretation.*

By 75 BC the realm held the subcontinent, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Central Asia with its trade cities, and northeastern Iran, with the Tamil kingdoms paying tribute at its southern edge and the wealth of the Arabian sea trade newly within its sight.

## Part IV: The Bad Reign and Its Repair (75 BC to 25 AD)

### The Mediocre Reign (75 to 50 BC)

A mediocre ruler inherited the realm at its greatest extent, and the newest conquests slipped, as newest conquests do. Parthia found the strength to press east again, took back part of Khorasan, and pushed the outermost Bactrian ground back to steadier lines. The mountain passes, the Sogdian cities, the subcontinent core, and the gold mines held, and the revenue came in as before.

### The Great Reign and Oman (50 to 25 BC)

A strong successor from about 50 to 25 BC saw two advantages his predecessor had wasted. Parthia's horse archers, feared across the western world, could not hold the field against elephants where a pitched battle was forced on them, and Parthia's eastern frontier lay exposed because its armies faced the other way. He drove west, retook Khorasan, and pressed on into Iran, and Khorasan, which had known Maurya administration once already, settled more easily the second time. He established a permanent military and administrative presence at Oman, the coast commanding the entrance to the Persian Gulf, so that ships passing in and out of the gulf came under the realm's eye and, in time, its tolls. The wealth of the Arabian coast, the incense of Yemen and the goods moving north through the Red Sea and the gulf, was by now well understood at court, and it was marked as the next great commercial prize.

In the south, the same reign made an end of the old tributary arrangement with the Tamil kingdoms. The Chola, the Pandya, and the Chera, whose payments had lapsed and been forced again a dozen times across the centuries, were conquered outright and their lands taken into the empire as governed provinces, so that the subcontinent was now held whole to its southern tip and the Tamil south was as much a part of the realm as the Gangetic plain, not a tributary at its edge. The prize there was as much sea as soil. The Tamil coasts, with Sri Lanka off their shore, were the realm's true seafaring country, their harbors and their sailors the ground the realm's navy had always grown from, and with the whole southern shore now the realm's own, that navy had the length of two coasts and their seamen to draw upon.

### The Bad Reign (25 BC to the Turn of the Era)

Then came the first genuinely destructive ruler the dynasty produced. He did active harm through the whole of his reign. He replaced capable officials with court favorites, seemingly out of suspicion of anyone competent. He let the loosely held eastern Parthian ground drift. He taxed the merchant networks arbitrarily through his corrupt appointees, and he let the army decay. The opening for a decisive western push stood open before him, a full treasury, a strong army, and a Parthia still fixed on its eastern adventure, and he let it pass. Twenty-five years were squandered, and Parthia, pressing east against his diminished response, clawed back the outermost gains.

What survived was what always survived: the deeply integrated heartland, the physical roads and the trade that ran on them, the gold mines, and the institutional culture of the elephant corps, all too old and too rooted for one bad reign to break.

> **By the numbers, around the turn of the era.** The realm's fifty million people now hold, beyond the subcontinent and Afghanistan, the island of Sri Lanka, the Silk Road lands of Central Asia (modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Turkmenistan), a foothold in northeastern Iran, and a naval station at Oman. Yearly revenue is about eight hundred and seventy million paṇas, some four hundred million the land tax and near two hundred million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls. The standing army stands near three hundred and sixty thousand: about a hundred and fifty thousand field infantry, a hundred and sixty thousand in garrison and second line, twenty-five thousand horse, and a navy grown to some fifteen thousand crew. About three hundred and thirty million goes to the army and a hundred million to roads and works, and the reserve holds something like half a year's revenue. Letters remain with about one person in ten.

### The Repair (0 to 25 AD)

His successor, a modest but honest ruler, spent his reign repairing the damage, replacing the corrupt, restoring tax collection, and rebuilding merchant confidence. On the western frontier Parthia still pressed, though with less urgency, and the restored military attention steadied the Khorasan line. The repair was adequate.

## Part V: Great Reigns and the First Great Encounter (25 to 100 AD)

### The Kushan Threat and the Meeting with Egypt (25 to 50 AD)

A capable ruler took the throne in about 25 AD at a moment of danger and opportunity. The Yuezhi clans that an earlier ruler had been absorbing were drifting toward unity under a single leader named Kujula Kadphises. A united steppe state on the northern frontier would be a formidable new enemy.

> **The reasoning.** The ruler judged that scattered clans were manageable one by one, and a unified state was not. He struck before the unification could finish. The elephant corps broke the clan cavalry in the usual way, the clans were driven apart and pushed north, and the coalescing state was shattered before it could form. The Yuezhi survived as a scatter of broken clans, and the great northern empire they might have built never rose.

With the north secured, the ruler renewed the western war. Parthia, still inexplicably facing the wrong way, lost its eastern lands again, and the frontier pushed deep into Iran toward Media, the region around modern Tehran and Hamadan. It was in this era that the realm's westward maritime probing met, for the first time, a power that had reached those waters before it.

Maritime forces working west along the southern Arabian coast from the Oman base reached Aden and the narrow strait at the mouth of the Red Sea. There they found garrisons, administrators, and a settled presence belonging to Egypt.

> **What the realm learned of Egypt, and how.** Egypt held the strait. Its soldiers were at Aden, its officials along the Yemeni coast and, it appeared, across the far shore of East Africa as well. Egypt delivered a plain message: the realm's merchants were welcome to trade through the Red Sea commercial system, but they would receive no base, no garrison right, and no territory inside Egypt's sphere. The realm weighed a naval war against a power holding the strait, judged the cost too high, and accepted the terms for the sake of the trade, keeping the hope that further down the western coasts, beyond Egypt's reach, some open shore might yet be found for a base of its own. *Of Egypt's inner workings and full extent the realm learned almost nothing in this era. What it knew was the edge of Egypt's control, and that edge was firm.*

So the Arabian coast fell into two spheres. Egypt held its Red Sea shore, Aden, and the strait, while the realm held the eastern shore, from the gulf mouth at Oman around to the tributary ports of eastern Yemen, and in time, as its grip closed on the whole gulf, the pearl and trading ports of the gulf's Arabian shore as well. Between the two lay the great Arabian desert, worth nothing to either power and claimed by neither.

With the western boundary settled at sea and the Parthian war grinding forward on land, the shape of the realm's commerce came clear. The realm taxed the overland Silk Road along its whole length, from the Sogdian cities through Iran and down toward Mesopotamia. Egypt held the Red Sea approach to the Mediterranean. Goods flowing west toward the Roman markets paid one power or the other, and often both, and the arrangement suited everyone who drew a profit from the trade.

### The Upper-Mediocre Reign (50 to 75 AD)

A capable but unremarkable ruler held what his predecessor had built. In the conquered western marches a new difficulty took shape. The Maurya army won every open battle it was offered, yet the people of the Iranian and Mesopotamian country it had overrun did not stay beaten. They fought on as scattered bands who knew their own ground, beyond the reach of any victory won in the open field, and the war in the west began to bleed men and coin without a clean end in sight. On the southern sea the Oman position was deepened from a military post into a proper garrison with its own administration, and the Roman trade ran strong and grew stronger, the western markets calling for more than the realm's merchants could carry.

### Mesopotamia Approached (75 to 100 AD)

A highly capable ruler took up the western war and pressed it hard. Parthia by now was reduced to western Iran, Mesopotamia, and perhaps a reach of eastern Syria, and it still threw its remaining strength eastward against the realm. The elephant corps went on winning every open engagement, while the resistance in the occupied country went on draining the treasury, the two truths running side by side through the whole reign. The momentum, for all the bleeding, ran west. By 100 AD Maurya forces stood near Ctesiphon, the Parthian capital by the Tigris in modern Iraq. Parthia as a great power was effectively broken, surviving but unable to project force.

> **By the numbers, around 100 AD.** Some sixty-three million people live under the throne, which now holds most of Iran as well, its armies standing at the edge of Mesopotamia. Yearly revenue is about twelve hundred million paṇas, some five hundred and thirty-five million the land tax and about three hundred million from trade, with mines, monopolies, and tolls making up the rest. The standing army has grown to roughly half a million: two hundred thousand field infantry, two hundred and twenty thousand in garrison and second line, forty thousand horse, and twenty thousand navy crew. About four hundred and forty million goes to the army and a hundred and thirty million to roads and works, and the reserve has risen to about nine hundred million, three-quarters of a year's revenue. About one person in ten can read.

## Part VI: The Western Completion and the Eastern Door (100 to 175 AD)

### The Mediocre Reign (100 to 125 AD)

The problem of the west now changed shape. Beating Parthia's armies had never been the difficulty. The difficulty was holding the ground afterward, because the people of Mesopotamia and Iran fought on as scattered irregulars long after their armies were gone, and irregular fighters who know their own country are not defeated by winning battles.

The ruler who faced this first, a man of middling ability, reached for force where the problem asked for something subtler. The court already knew, from how the realm had bound reluctant peoples before, that resistance of this kind dissolves when cooperation is made to pay better than fighting, and yet he leaned on the army and made slow, dear progress. The one steadiness of the reign was the Silk Road, the caravans arriving in their usual numbers and the trade revenue holding firm.

### The Great Completion (125 to 150 AD)

A strong successor from about 125 to 150 AD solved the western problem by other means, and settled three things at once.

> **The reasoning.** He understood that the Mesopotamians resisted occupation itself and cared little for the Parthian dynasty they had fought under. So he changed what occupation meant. He offered economic advantage for cooperation, let local governance continue under Maurya oversight, and respected local religion instead of suppressing it. When submitting became more profitable than resisting, the resistance dissolved community by community. Parthia as a political entity ceased to exist, its leaders either absorbed into Maurya service or gone west, and every toll that Parthia had taken from the trade route now flowed to the realm alone.

With that the west was complete, and Parthia's northern marches fell to him along with its heartland: the mountain country of the Caucasus, Armenia and the Azerbaijan highlands, which Parthia had held as thinly-garrisoned buffer ground and used chiefly as a threat to wave at Rome. He took them as he took the rest of the Parthian inheritance, and because Parthia had held them the succession looked like the natural order rather than a fresh conquest, so that Rome, far off and slow to hear, did not contest it until it was long settled.

Now the realm stood against Rome itself, across the Euphrates and the Caucasus. The Arthashastra held that a neighbor is an enemy and a state must keep growing, and by that teaching Rome too was fair game. But Rome lay far to the west beyond the barely-held Mesopotamian ground and carried a hard name as a fighting power, a name the realm knew only by old report and had never itself put to the test, so that to hurl an overstretched army against it, for country the realm did not need, was the very lunge the Rolling Frontier Doctrine warned against. He halted at the river and the mountains, where the ground made a line the realm could hold, and left Rome its own side. Rome was spared by that distance, by its hard name, and by the chance that trade across the line soon proved worth more than any ground beyond it. Armenia and Azerbaijan had none of those shields, weak and Parthian and near at hand, and were swallowed without a second thought; Georgia, on the Black Sea and in Rome's shadow, he left alone. He formalized the long quiet commerce with Rome into an acknowledged bond between states, with a framework for settling the disputes that trade throws up.

And he turned the realm's gaze east, for every western sea lane was closed to him.

That hope died in this reign. The realm's captains kept working west and south along the African shore, past Aden and down the coasts of modern Somalia and Kenya, looking for the open ground beyond Egypt's reach that they had told themselves must be there. It was not. Everywhere they landed they found Egypt before them, its garrisons and its administrators in place, giving the same flat answer it had given at the strait: trade as you please, hold no base, claim no ground. Down the whole length of that coast the answer never changed, until the realm's men were told plainly to trade and go home. Egypt had reached every one of these shores first, and held the Red Sea, the Levantine coast, the East African shore, and the strait at the Red Sea's mouth, so that wherever the realm turned toward the west it met the same wall and the same refusal. The trade still paid, for the realm's goods reached the Mediterranean through Egypt's hands and Egypt took its cut for passing them on, and it was exactly this that galled the court, a power that would take the realm's silver at its ports and grant it not one foot of ground beyond them. Wherever the realm looked to the west, the sea was Egypt's, and the court had grown heartily sick of it.

> **The eastern door.** The realm's merchants had traded with the ports of Malaya and Sumatra for generations, and no power in those waters had ever told them to stop. The strait between the Malay Peninsula and the island of Sumatra, the Strait of Malacca, was recognized as the eastern twin of what Egypt held at the Red Sea, a narrow gate through which all sea trade between the Indian Ocean and the seas of the far east must pass. Egypt had shut the realm out of every western door, and so it turned east, where Egypt's wall did not reach. The eastern door stood open, contested by no peer, and it was the realm's alone to take.

### The Plague Years (150 to 175 AD)

A mediocre reign followed and merely held the position. Across the western trade a shadow fell: a great sickness, reported only in vague and secondhand terms, struck the Roman markets and cut their demand. The goods that still came from the west fetched higher prices in thinner volume, and the realm's merchants read the change as some disruption of the Roman population. The realm knew of Rome's troubles only what its merchants carried home. The eastern door, marked and waiting, went another quarter-century unopened.

## Part VII: The China Century (175 to 300 AD)

### The Poor Reign and the Drift (175 to 200 AD)

The worst ruler in a very long time held the throne at the century's turn, replacing capable men with favorites, taxing the merchants arbitrarily, and neglecting the newer western provinces. Mesopotamia, the most recently won and most culturally distinct of the realm's lands, drifted back from feeling incorporated toward feeling occupied. The careful work of a generation partly came undone, and the treasury absorbed twenty-five years of corruption from its historic peak.

> **By the numbers, around 200 AD.** The realm holds some seventy-four million people and its full western extent, the subcontinent and Sri Lanka, Afghanistan and Central Asia, Iran with the Caucasus highlands of Armenia and Azerbaijan, and Mesopotamia to the Euphrates (modern Iraq), with Oman, the tributary ports of the Arabian gulf shore (modern Bahrain, Qatar, and the Emirates), and eastern Yemen. Yearly revenue is about fifteen hundred million paṇas, some six hundred and sixty-five million the land tax and near four hundred and thirty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls. The standing army runs near six hundred and fifty thousand, close to one man in a hundred: two hundred and sixty thousand field infantry, three hundred thousand in garrison and second line, fifty-five thousand horse, and near thirty thousand navy crew. About five hundred and fifty million goes to the army and a hundred and sixty-five million to roads and works, and the reserve, drawn down under the poor reign of these years, holds about seven hundred million. Reading and writing stay with about one in ten.

### The Recovery and the Eastern Groundwork (200 to 225 AD)

A capable ruler steadied the realm after the poor reign, halting the administrative rot and drawing back the Mesopotamian strongmen who had gathered authority while the center was weak. The work was sound rather than brilliant, and it held.

He also turned the eastern intention into preparation. With every western sea lane closed by Egypt, the east was the only direction left for the realm's own maritime reach, and the merchants working the Malay and Sumatran ports still met no power telling them to stop. He set about making that commercial presence a matter of state, sending official ships along with the merchant convoys and ordering a first survey of the harbors down the Malay Peninsula.

### The Encounter at the River (from about 225 AD)

In the last years of that recovery, the defining event of the realm's later history arrived from a direction no one had watched.

In about 225 AD a military force appeared on the far bank of the Syr Darya, the river that formed the realm's northern frontier in Central Asia. Its soldiers spoke a language unlike any tongue within the realm, and no interpreter could be found. The gap was bridged by the Sogdians, the Central Asian merchant people who had run the Silk Road for centuries and spoke the languages at both its ends. They had long suspected that some great civilization lay at the eastern end of the road, since the goods that came west along it had to begin somewhere. Through them, over months, the realm assembled a picture of who had come.

The power across the river was vast. By the Sogdians' account it had expanded a great distance westward to arrive at the river, and it had come looking for Parthia, the western empire it had heard of, unaware that the realm had swallowed Parthia years before. Its name, rendered through Sogdian mouths, was China.

> **The cannon.** Within a few years of first contact, an incident occurred. A Chinese weapon fired upon a fort on the realm's bank of the river. The wall took visible damage. The weapon threw fire, made a sound no soldier present had ever heard, and struck stone at a distance. The garrison's dispatches went south at speed, and the realm answered in the old instinctive way, bringing war elephants to the bank to show its strength. The Chinese cavalry horses, like all horses new to elephants, panicked. Both sides drew back to think. Each capital received the same lesson on the same day: across the river stood a power holding a weapon the other had never faced. The cannon shocked the realm. The elephants shocked China. Neither side pressed on. China stated, through the Sogdians, that it was satisfied with the river as the boundary for the present, and that it would keep the workings of its weapon entirely to itself.

### The Revolt and Its Lesson (225 to 250 AD)

A capable ruler carried the new contact into a working arrangement, opening the first steady diplomatic channel to China through the Sogdians and settling the frontier into an uneasy calm. His reign is remembered above all for a rising in the west.

The Mesopotamian strongmen who had gathered power under the poor reign, only partly checked since, made their bid for independence. The revolt failed, and failed easily, for nothing the western territories could put in the field could stand against the Maurya army. What it taught outlasted it. A hundred years of Maurya rule in Mesopotamia had produced a population that obeyed without belonging. The deep loyalty of the subcontinent had been built across centuries of roads, shared trade, common administration, and mingled custom, the work of time and money rather than of garrisons, and the west had been given the garrisons alone. The court drew the plain conclusion, that the western lands needed the same patient making the heartland had once received, done this time on purpose.

### The Second Great Reign of the Era (250 to 275 AD)

One of the ablest rulers the dynasty ever produced held the throne in the third quarter of the century, and he faced three tasks: the unintegrated west, the new power in the north, and the open door in the east. He addressed all three.

For the west, he acted on the lesson of the failed revolt of the previous reign, and gave the western lands the deliberate making the court had concluded they needed. He built a second imperial capital near the Tigris, a permanent statement that the realm invested in its western lands rather than merely occupying them. He trained local men in Maurya governance while letting them keep their own identity, formally embraced the Zoroastrian faith of Iran rather than merely tolerating it, and drew Mesopotamian and Iranian scholars into the realm's funded institutions. The aim was an identity that could be Maurya and Iranian at once, the way the heartland had long been many things at once.

For China, he built a formal relationship on the one thing both powers shared, the trade route both had been profiting from without knowing the other existed. Envoys were exchanged through the Sogdians. Both powers had reason to keep the trade flowing, and that mutual interest became the floor of a wary peace.

The cannon was the harder matter. The weapon China had fired at the Syr Darya was one the realm could not answer, and the court understood what that meant: the northern peace held only because China had chosen not to press it, and the realm did not know that it could stop a Chinese army that did. Its survival rested, for the moment, on another power's restraint, which was a thing no great state could be content to live by. Closing the gap became a matter of the realm's life.

He tried first to steal the secret. Agents went east through the Sogdian traders to learn how China shaped and charged its weapon, and the reports that came back were thin and useless. So he turned on his own scholars. The realm had funded great houses of learning for centuries, its libraries and schools and salaried men of letters the envy of every neighbor, and he put a hard question to them: what had all that patronage bought, if it could not deliver the one thing the realm now needed to survive? Prestige was not the return the crown had been paying for. He founded a serious, state-funded program of research to work out what kind of weapon could throw fire and force and break stone at a distance, and he threw the realm's scholarship and treasury behind it at a scale no mere point of curiosity could ever have justified.

For the east, he acted on the door his predecessors had identified. A naval expedition planted a permanent fortified presence at the Strait of Malacca. Local Malay and Sumatran kingdoms were offered favorable trade terms in exchange for acknowledging the realm's authority over the passage, and cooperation proved more profitable to them than resistance. The strait gave the realm a maritime gate that Egypt could not touch, and beyond it lay the Spice Islands, the sole source of cloves and nutmeg, identified as the next prize.

The eastern push brought the realm a small thing of great use. Its sailors, working the new strait routes and dealing constantly now with China's people through the Sogdian go-betweens, took up from them the use of the lodestone: a sliver of magnetized iron, floated on water or hung on a thread, that turned always to lie north and south. The realm's navigators had long known that a lodestone drew iron and would orient itself; to set a needle by it and read a heading from it was a short step, and it made the open-water passages the realm was now sailing far surer than the stars and the coastline alone had left them.

### The Very Poor Reign (275 to 300 AD)

The century closed in ruin at the hands of the worst ruler the dynasty had yet seen. Through paranoia, corruption, and the purging of able men, he did lasting harm. The gunpowder program, which had been making real progress, lost its senior researchers to his purges and collapsed, and years of work were lost. The intelligence operations aimed at China fell apart. The careful western integration frayed, and Mesopotamia slid back toward feeling occupied.

What survived him was what always survived. The river frontier held, for China had no reason to move. The trade route ran on, for roads and profit do not vanish with a bad king. The distant Malacca garrison, thousands of miles from the capital and funded by local trade, governed itself. And the ancient heartland, integrated across five centuries, was simply beyond the power of one bad reign to break.

### The Broken Succession (about 300 AD)

The very poor reign did the dynasty a harm beyond its wasted treasury and frayed provinces: it broke the ruling line's claim to the throne. When the ruler died about 300, leaving no heir the court could stomach and a name blackened by twenty-five years of misrule, the succession fell open, and for the better part of two or three years no one man held it securely. Factions formed about rival claimants, some of the old blood and some not, and the struggle ran through the court in intrigue and bought loyalty, with a handful of frontier commanders sounded out by one side or another.

That the empire did not tear itself apart in those years is the plainest proof of what the realm had built. The provinces were run by an administration that drew its pay and its orders from the state and cared little whose name sat above it. The army's loyalty ran to the throne itself rather than to the man or the house that held it, and its officers had been raised for centuries to see a march for one claimant against another as the ruin of the very thing they served. So the great machine of governance kept turning while the court fought over who would sit at its head. Taxes came in, the frontiers held, the roads ran, and the quarrel stayed a quarrel among great men rather than a war among provinces.

When it settled, the crown had passed out of the discredited line and into a new house, raised up by the officials and commanders who had held the state together through the interregnum and who wanted a ruler beholden to them. The realm did not count this a break in itself. What men called the dynasty was the empire, a thing that had already outlived several ruling houses, each giving way to the next through just such a struggle, weathered because the state ran older and deeper than the families that wore its crown. So the empire passed whole into new hands, its provinces held and its frontiers kept through all the years the court had spent quarrelling over its head.

## Part VIII: The Realm at 300 AD

Five centuries after the founding, with the western lands taken to the Euphrates and the eastern strait held, the realm stood as the largest, wealthiest, and most populous dominion in the world. Much of what gave it that standing held steady across the centuries on either side of this year, the shape of its society, the sources of its wealth, and the state of its crafts and learning, and it is gathered here, at the midpoint, where it can be seen whole.

> **By the numbers, around 300 AD.** The realm's registered eighty-eight million people hold the western dominion reached long since and, new in the east, the Strait of Malacca. Yearly revenue is near nineteen hundred million paṇas, some eight hundred and thirty-five million the land tax and about five hundred and thirty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls. The standing army runs near eight hundred thousand: three hundred and twenty thousand field infantry, three hundred and sixty thousand in garrison and second line, seventy thousand horse, and near thirty thousand navy crew. About six hundred and forty million goes to the army, seventy million to weapons research and armament, and two hundred million to roads and works, with a research core of some eight or nine thousand trained men and tens of thousands of craftsmen behind them. The reserve, drained by the crisis just past, holds under half a year's revenue, and about one person in ten reads.

### Society and Labor

The great mass of the realm's people were free farmers, herders, artisans, and traders, ordered by the caste system that fixed birth, occupation, and station, and that held much of the land's farm labor to its work without any need for ownership of the worker. Alongside the free stood a large enslaved population, an old institution of the realm set within the bounds of the Arthashastra. The enslaved numbered perhaps one in ten to one in twenty of all the realm's people, drawn from captives taken across five centuries of war, from men and women fallen into debt bondage, from the children of the enslaved, and from the East African trade. They were set to the heaviest and most dangerous work, the gold and mineral mines above all, and to great construction, skilled crafts, and the households of the rich. The law granted them some protection, limits on what labor could be demanded, some hold on property, and paths by which freedom might be reached, and those protections were kept unevenly, least of all for the enslaved of foreign birth.

### Faith

In religion the realm remained what the Kalinga decision had made it. Ashoka, choosing the throne over the monk's road, never gave Buddhism the imperial embrace that might in another age have carried it across the land and beyond, and so the old Vedic and Brahmanical order kept its place at the center of the realm's life, its priests and rites and its ranking of castes undisturbed at the summit. Buddhism and the Jain tradition endured as they always had, kept to their regions and their monasteries, tolerated and unpersecuted under the Arthashastra's rule that taxed conduct and not belief, but minor beside the Brahmanical mainstream and without the weight of the state behind them. In the conquered west and the Mesopotamian provinces the realm let the local faiths stand as it let the local tongues, so that the fire-temples of the Iranian lands and the synagogues of Mesopotamia kept their place under a Sanskrit administration that asked obedience and not conversion. At the summit of it all stood the Brahmins, their standing resting on their sole keeping of the sacred word and the learning bound up with it.

### The Wealth Beneath the Ground
The realm's wealth rested in part on a spread of minerals few powers could match. Its gold came chiefly from the Kolar fields of the southern Deccan and from the river valleys of Bactria north of the mountains. Its iron, the finest anywhere, came from the Deccan and the central lands and fed the steel that armed its soldiers. Copper came from the western deserts of the subcontinent and from Central Asia. Gems of great value came from many quarters, sapphires and other stones from Sri Lanka, diamonds from the Deccan, and rubies carried in from beyond the Bay of Bengal. Salt came from the great range of the Punjab and from the long coasts. Saltpeter, the white salt at the heart of gunpowder, lay abundant on the soil of the Gangetic plain, long gathered and known, and the realm sat on more of it than any rival possessed. Sulfur was drawn from within the realm and named in its old medical texts. With the strait now held, the tin of the southeastern islands came within reach as well.

### Coin and Credit

The realm ran on a settled coinage, the silver paṇa and its relations, of standard weight across the whole territory, backed by standard measures of length, volume, and mass that let a bargain struck in one province be honored in another. Above the coin worked instruments of credit kept by the merchant houses, letters and bills that carried the value of a caravan from one end of the realm to the other without the metal itself moving along roads where it might be lost or seized. This credit was what let the long-distance trade the treasury taxed run at the scale it reached.

### The State of the Arts

The realm's craft and knowledge led the world in several fields at once. Its steelworkers made a high-carbon steel of an edge no rival could match. Its roads ran the length of the empire, with rest houses, relay stations, and bridges kept up across five centuries. Its shipwrights built ocean-going vessels that worked the Indian Ocean, the Bay of Bengal, and the eastern seas, sailed by navigators who read the stars, kept charts of the coasts, and knew the turning of the monsoon and the run of the ocean currents. Its farmers were fed by deep works of wells, reservoirs, and canals that watered the largest population under any single throne. Its builders raised temples, forts, and public works in dressed stone.

Above the crafts stood the realm's learning, gathered in great funded houses of study, the most famed of them at Taxila in the northwest and in the capital at Pataliputra, with libraries whose gathered manuscripts had no equal in the known world and lesser schools in every provincial seat feeding students up to them. The state had paid for all of it since the founding, holding learning a pillar of the realm no less than the army or the granary. Its mathematics was the most advanced anywhere, so far beyond what any neighbor possessed that its scholars had no rival to measure themselves against. They wrote in a full decimal notation and handled the zero as a working number with rules of its own, worked a systematic algebra and a developed theory of numbers, turned trigonometry to the sky and the sea, carried geometry well past its old classical bounds by joining the Indian and Greek traditions the Bactrian conquests had brought together, and had begun to reach toward the study of continuous change, working with infinite series and the methods of approximation that lie at its edge. Its astronomers cataloged the stars, modeled the motions of the planets closely enough to serve both the calendar and the ship, and among them some had begun to argue a bolder frame, one that set the sun rather than the earth at the center of the wandering lights. Its physicians, in the long Ayurvedic tradition, practiced advanced surgery, held a deep pharmacology, and had learned much of how sickness spreads from long and direct experience, enough that generations before they had hit on a guard against the pox, taking a little of the matter from a light case and setting it into the skin of the well, so that most so treated took only a mild sickness and were spared the killing form ever after. Its chemists, in medicine and in metalworking, held the knowledge that the realm's weapons research had already begun to turn toward the study of burning and force.

## Part IX: Recovery and the Widening World (300 to 400 AD)

### The First Recovery and a Costly Sale (300 to 325 AD)

An adequate ruler, capable but unremarkable, arrested the worst of the damage without fully undoing it. He removed the most blatant of the favorites, whose corruption was plain and whose fall cost him nothing, and left a larger body of merely mediocre appointees in place, judging that to sweep out the whole apparatus at once would bring a chaos worse than the corruption. He recalled the officials his predecessor had purged, offering them the face-saving story that they had been falsely accused. Some returned. Several would not. A few had died in the interval. The networks of informers the Arthashastra relied on to watch the provinces had been gutted, and they came back only in part.

Two disasters fell in his reign. About 310 a sickness entered along the northern trade roads and ran through the crowded Gangetic plain, killing in numbers, breaking two harvests in a row, and thinning the northern caravan trade for some three years before it faded. It fell on a governing machine only half repaired, and it sorted the competent administrators from those who had merely coasted through easy years. Then about 320 an earthquake struck the Hindu Kush frontier, wrecking garrison works and breaking a pass on the road to the Bactrian lands, so that the supply line to the Syr Darya ran thin for months while men worked the repairs. That the officers overseeing the work owed their posts to court favor rather than skill made the repairs slower than they should have been, and the northern frontier stood less well supplied than at any time since its founding.

He restarted the gunpowder program his predecessor's purges had destroyed, in about 302 to 305, understanding as any competent ruler would that China's cannon was an asymmetry the realm could not carry forever. His choice of director was the reign's costliest administrative error. He gave the program to a distinguished mathematician of the Pataliputra schools, a man of pharmaceutical learning and high standing, whose gifts were theoretical when the work needed the hands and eye of a metalworker who knew combustion in the forge. The foundations were laid, and the wrong man laid them, so that progress was real and slower than it should have been.

The reign opened the first true dealings with China and Rome at once, and mishandled the currency of both. To China, through the Sogdians, the realm sent formal word, and China answered with more than was expected. Chinese scouts in the northern steppe had met Roman soldiers moving east along a river, far to the north of where the realm had ever placed Rome, the first sign that a road ran between China and Rome across the steppe, beyond the realm's reach. China shared this as one watchful power to another. Then China asked for a Latin interpreter and offered to pay any price the realm named.

> **The reasoning, and the cost.** The ruler took the payment and lent China an interpreter, and it was a mistake, though the court saw it only too late. After the Chinese party had gone the realm grasped what it had done, that it had let China speak to Rome around it, and its first feeling was alarm. Then it talked itself down. It was not so bad, the court reasoned, groping for the good in it: to sit as the interpreter between China and Rome was to sit inside every message they traded, hearing all of it, a service China had bought and would have to keep buying, a listening post the realm would be paid to keep. What it had never once considered was that China would not use the man to carry messages at all. It set him to teaching Latin to its own scholars, and once they had the tongue China had no more need of the realm, for messages or anything else, and its envoys sent warm thanks for so generous a gift, a gift the realm had never meant to give. The listening post never came to be and the fee was paid only once. What remained was a clean and total loss, and the court was left annoyed at its own cleverness. *This is set down as the court's retrospective assessment.*

Rome, in the same years, sent a mission to the Euphrates frontier and brought a puzzle with it, a sample of a written script met on Rome's northeastern edge, which Rome asked the realm to name. The realm's Sogdians knew it within days for the writing of China, the same power the realm faced at the Syr Darya, and the realm confirmed it to Rome. That confirmation made the realm the one power in direct dealing with both China and Rome, a place of real worth that this reign was too plain to use to the full. Rome also proposed a trade road that would run through the realm's own Mesopotamia to Rome's Syrian frontier and leave Egypt's tolls behind, and the realm answered with strong interest and agreed to talk. In its eagerness the realm told Rome too much, letting slip that it faced a weapon in China's hands it could not yet match, and so gave Rome a measure of its own weakness for nothing in return.

Answering the interpreter loss, the realm set its own scholars to learn to read Chinese, finding Sogdian merchants willing to teach them. The program was underfunded and slow, and by the reign's close it had its teachers and not yet a single scholar able to use the tongue.

### The Second Recovery and the Right Hands (325 to 350 AD)

A more capable ruler saw that the gunpowder program was led by the wrong kind of mind and replaced the mathematician with a director drawn from the crafts of metalworking and pharmacy, a man who had spent his life with heat and material and chemical reaction. Under him the work turned to systematic experiment, and here the sheer scale of the realm told: the state could set a small army to the problem at once, hundreds of trained investigators directing many thousands of craftsmen and bonded laborers, running trial against trial in parallel, testing mixture after mixture and vessel after vessel far faster than any smaller power could have managed. By about the middle of the century the combustion chemistry of the powder was understood. What remained was the harder engineering problem, how to build a vessel that directed the force outward usefully instead of bursting and destroying itself.

A run of weak monsoons from about 330 brought regional famine to parts of the Deccan and central India across some seven years. No one year failed catastrophically, yet the lean seasons compounded until grain reserves ran down and people moved off the worst land. The granaries and irrigation works built over centuries blunted the worst of it, and relief grain moved along the roads. The crisis showed which provincial governors could manage and exposed the thinner administrative reach of the western territories, where the works were newer than in the heartland. The ruler's response was competent rather than brilliant, which was enough.

This reign brought the trade route with Rome to formal terms, an overland road through the realm's Mesopotamia to Rome's Syrian frontier that carried goods clear of Egypt's tolls. Each empire set a transit charge of twelve parts in the hundred through its own ground, fixed for twenty-five years and renewing of itself for a further twenty-five unless one side gave notice to change it, with named officials in the trading cities to hear disputes and waystations kept along the way. Trade shifted onto the road quickly, and the realm's merchants in Alexandria reported Egypt's officials marking the traffic that no longer came their way. China, in the same years, signed a formal agreement on the Silk Road more readily than the slow talks had suggested, which the court read as genuine hunger for a quiet frontier. The realm was as glad of it, for it wanted no reckoning with China while China held a weapon it could not yet match, and a settled frontier bought time to close that gap while the trade ran on and paid. Throughout, China kept asking after Egypt and the sea lanes, and the realm answered, telling what it truly knew but painting Egypt in the darkest honest colors it could find, having no love for the power that had walled it out of the west. Its contacts on the coast beyond the Bay, where the far southeastern peoples traded, brought word that China had begun to build ships fit for the open ocean.

That word turned the realm's mind to the eastern sea before China's hulls were in it. The realm began to press China, in its letters, to acknowledge its authority over the Strait of Malacca, judging that such a thing would come easier before China's fleet had sailed those waters than after. China would not bind itself to any words about the Strait until its own ships had gone there and returned twice and seen the passage for themselves, and the realm, seeing the fairness of it, agreed to draft terms then and settle them after. The window stood open, and the realm knew it was closing.

The realm had let the eastern expedition sit half-funded for years, a thing of no great urgency while the islands' goods still came, if dearly, through other hands. Now, with China's hulls bound for those same waters, it wanted its own trade with the clove and nutmeg islands fixed in place before China arrived, and it poured into the neglected expedition the effort it had long withheld. Beyond the Strait lay the islands that alone in the known world grew cloves, nutmeg, and mace, goods that had reached the realm for generations only through the hands of go-between traders. Working out from the Malacca garrison and through the Malay and Sumatran seafarers who knew the routes and were taken as guides, the realm's ships felt their way east and made their first contact with the island peoples in these years. No steady trade was yet in place. The route was being learned.

Three quieter advances of craft and learning belong to this reign as well. In Rajasthan the zinc of the Zawar workings began to be won as a metal with growing reliability, and a few specialist centers in the northwest started making brass from it and copper. In the textile towns of Gujarat and the Coromandel coast, where Rome's hunger for fine cloth had pressed for generations, early spinning aids appeared to turn out thread faster. And in the Pataliputra schools a program was set to the mathematics of things in flight, the paths of thrown and shot objects, for the aiming of catapult and ballista. By the reign's close the Chinese-reading program bore its first fruit, a handful of scholars able to read a Chinese document without a Sogdian at their elbow, enough to loosen the realm's dependence on go-betweens whose first loyalty ran to the trade rather than to the realm.

The weapons work threw off gains beyond itself as well. The patient purifying and concentrating of saltpeter for the powder taught the realm's chemists to boil a liquid and catch its rising vapor cleanly, and that art of distillation they turned to other uses, to stronger spirits, to the pressed oils of plants for medicine, and to sharper acids for the metalworkers. And the same surge of skilled hands the program drew in set some of the realm's gem-cutters, long masters of grinding and polishing hard stone, to grinding curved discs of clear glass that magnified whatever was held beneath them, the realm's first working of lenses.

### The Upper-Mediocre Reign and the Window into Egypt (350 to 375 AD)

The next ruler was the ablest since the great reigns of earlier centuries, though not himself a great ruler. He solved the solvable problems set before him and managed the rest without worsening them.

He made the Rome route work as a standing thing, building waystations with water, shelter, and guard along the Mesopotamian stretch and appointing officials with real power to settle disputes, until the road carried a traffic that merchants could plan around. Through it, in these years, the realm sent west a new luxury, the refined sugar of its southern lands, unknown to Rome as a food. The realm's traders, taken with the stuff themselves, went to some trouble to explain to Rome's merchants and envoys what sugar was and what could be done with it, and so it passed first through physicians and wealthy houses, and Roman appetite for it began to grow. The tie with Rome deepened, and the two powers fell into a habit of trading word of Egypt's doings between them.

With China the reign moved from letters carried by others to letters written in the realm's own hand. The Chinese-reading scholars had grown able enough to carry the correspondence themselves, and the realm let China see that it wrote in Chinese now, a deliberate sign that it was investing in the tie and leaning less on the Sogdians whose loyalty ran to the trade. China answered in kind and sent a party of its own scholars and envoys to Pataliputra, who spent long weeks in the capital's institutions and libraries with the working life of the court open to them.

The larger business with China was the sea. Its ships had by now passed through the Strait of Malacca and returned, and the realm moved to fix its own hold on the passage. It raised a trading port at the eastern mouth, built and kept by the realm, and posted a toll on all ships alike that used the Strait, setting aside within its port a berth for China's ships, worked by China's own harbor men under the realm's single authority over the whole. China had asked at first for a separate anchorage of its own at the same place, and the realm refused, unwilling to set two authorities at one chokepoint, and mindful too of how Egypt had used its own sole grip on the western gates to shut the realm out, a lesson it had no wish to hand to China. The guarded berth within the realm's port was the compromise that gave China its convenience without dividing the command. That China let the question of authority rest when it might have pressed, the court's analysts marked as a sign that China wanted commercial access rather than a quarrel over the ground.

Then, in 365, a great earthquake struck Egypt and devastated the harbor of its chief city, Alexandria. The realm's merchant contacts carried word of its scale within months. Rome, the realm learned, sent Egypt a great relief fleet, grain from its northern lands, oil, and timber for the rebuilding, at prices that spoke of subsidy rather than profit, and Rome said nothing of it to the realm, which learned of it through its own merchants' eyes. The lesson was not lost on the court. Rome kept a warm hand with Egypt at the very time it shared with the realm a common front against Egypt's tolls, and Rome's web of dealings was wider and more tangled than the realm had understood.

Two years after the earthquake the realm sent its first formal embassy to Egypt, and the timing was chosen to serve the spying rather than the aid. Come at once, while the wreckage was fresh, and the realm would be of more use to Egypt but would see little through the chaos and be held at arm's length besides. Wait two years, the court reasoned, and the worst of the rubble would be cleared aside while the city, by any ordinary reckoning still largely in ruins, lay open enough to walk through and study. The realm expected to find Egypt perhaps seven parts in ten still wrecked, damage enough to make its offer of help genuine and to lay bare how Egypt built and repaired. The aid was the cover; the errand was to get inside and see what the most advanced power the realm knew was truly made of. It approached by sea through Egypt's own waters as a deliberate sign of respect for Egypt's authority, and carried physicians, fine steel tools, engineers seasoned on the realm's mountain frontier, and grain offered in aid.

> **What the embassy saw in Alexandria.** Egypt received the embassy politely, let it give a little of the aid it had brought, kept the envoys always accompanied, let them see a great deal that the court judged it meant them to see, and after no long while sent them home. The realm's agents returned with the most significant intelligence gathered in generations:
> - **A city all but healed.** Only two years after the earthquake that was said to have wrecked the harbor, the envoys could find almost no sign of it. Here and there an older foundation sat oddly beneath newer walls, betraying that damage had come and gone, but the city and its port stood whole and working, rebuilt faster and more fully than the realm would have thought possible, so that there was little left for the realm's own engineers to mend.
> - **Identical documents** in the hands of officials, vendors, and children alike. No hand could copy so exactly. Some method of printing exact copies from a master existed. The knowledge that it was possible was itself the prize, for the realm already had the paper crafts, inks, and carvers to attempt it.
> - **Ships under their own power**, moving in and out of the war harbor with neither sail nor oar, driven by some means the envoys could not see.
> - **A steady, unflickering white light** in the great lighthouse, unlike any flame.
> - **Lamps** burning a clean fuel with an unfamiliar smell, judged to be some oil of the earth from deposits Egypt controlled.
> - **Medical order**: physicians washing hands and instruments in prepared solutions before touching the sick, and better recoveries following. The realm's physicians could not say why the washing should help, but they were slow to write it off as mere ritual, reasoning that a people so far ahead in every other art would not keep an empty rite in its hospitals; and having marked a faint but real drop in deaths where it was done, they resolved to copy the practice and leave the reason for later.
> - **A hot dark drink**, coffee, served plainly to common people. The realm's merchants knew the drink already, for a little had trickled into its markets for years at high price through a handful of traders, and after this embassy Egypt sent more of it east than before. Asked for living seeds, the envoys were told the seeds die in transport. The court judged this a deliberate device: Egypt roasted all its exported beans so that none could be planted elsewhere, keeping the plant to itself. The plant's home, somewhere south of Egypt, became a standing object of search.
> - **Water gates** on the canals that lifted vessels between different water levels without hauling them overland.
> - **Interchangeable fittings** across the whole city, every hinge and bracket matching, a standardization the realm had never seen.
> - **Ornamental metal lines** run in bright bands along the walls of buildings throughout the city, worked in what looked to be gold and silver with some bronze and little copper among them, in patterns too regular for idle decoration. The envoys could not say what use they served, if any, and the deeper puzzle was why a power would lavish such precious metal on mere ornament.
> - **A mixed people in office**, men of many nations serving side by side at every level of the city's administration, the very knitting of peoples the realm had struggled toward in its own west for a hundred years without success.
>
> *The court's conclusion: Egypt is the most technically advanced power the realm has met, ahead in several unrelated fields at once, which suggests a deep and deliberate investment in such work. Egypt's power is commercial and technical, built on holding the sea gates and on making itself impossible to do without, and it threatens the realm's lands little by force of arms.*

This window set three efforts going. The realm put its craftsmen to working out how to print, for it had seen only the results in Egypt, the identical documents, and nothing of the method, and it had to reason its own way from its paper, inks, and carvers toward a press that could do the like. It began adopting the washing protocols in its hospitals, though it did not understand why they helped, and its physicians argued over what the Egyptians even washed with, some holding it was plain water, others sure that plain water could not matter and that it must be wine or vinegar or some sharper spirit in the basins, none able to say for certain. And it founded a program to study what had driven Egypt's sail-less ships, where it was groping in the dark from the start. It did not know the answer, and Rome, asked, could only guess at some engine of steam, furnace-heated vapor turning machinery. So the steam program began as pure inquiry into an unknown, its researchers setting out to discover the thing rather than to copy one already understood.

The coffee, though, the court could not let go. The envoys had been told the plant could not be moved, its seed dying within days, and so every bean sold roasted, and the court had seen through that at once, for a roasted seed cannot sprout. Egypt was killing its own seed on purpose, roasting every bean before it left, so that no one else could ever grow a plant of their own and its cheap street drink could be sold to the world forever at the price of a luxury. In the realm's markets, as in Rome's, a pound of the roasted bean fetched more than a pound of pepper, the dearest spice the realm knew, while in Alexandria the same drink was poured for laborers by the cup. The margin between the two was a fortune, and whoever found the living plant would take that fortune out of Egypt's hand. So finding it became a settled aim of the realm. The beans came down the Nile to Alexandria from the south, which put the plant in the high country of Africa above Egypt's river, ground the realm could not touch through the Red Sea, for that sea was Egypt's. The one way left was the long coast of Africa, worked south from the realm's Oman ports, east of Arabia and clear of the Red Sea, which the realm had lately begun to feel its way down in hopes of shores that Egypt did not already hold.

### The Third Great Reign (375 to 400 AD)

The finest ruler the realm had seen in living memory closed the century. He saw the whole board at once and made investments meant to outlast him.

He ordered the gunpowder engineers and the new steam researchers to share their work, recognizing that both were wrestling with the same problem from opposite sides: how to contain and direct great force inside a vessel without the vessel tearing itself apart. Rome, as a partner, added a crucial hint, that such vessels fail first at the joints between their parts, where metals expand and contract unevenly under repeated heat and force, rather than in the middle of a solid wall.

> **A note on Rome's hint.** At first the realm was simply grateful, and it applied the hint. Only some months later did someone at court think to ask how Rome had come to know where such vessels fail, and once the question was asked the answer was plain: Rome could know it only by having built such vessels itself and pushed past that point, quietly, before ever offering the hint. Rome was further along, and more careful, than it had let the realm believe. The realm took the lesson twice over, applying the hint and beginning to weigh its own gifts of knowledge as carefully as Rome weighed them, giving true knowledge but measuring first what each gift would grow into.

With the joint insight, the realm's metalworkers built the first functional gunpowder weapon in about 390 to 395 AD, two or three prototype pieces, each the work of great skill, that threw their force reliably without destroying themselves. The realm's mathematics of projectile flight, developed in the previous reign, was turned at once to the problem of aiming them. It was roughly a hundred and sixty-five years since the Chinese cannon had first fired on a Syr Darya fort, and the realm had at last a gunpowder weapon of its own, though only a handful of them, and the long road from a few master-made prototypes to an armed and drilled corps still lay ahead.

The steam program moved from theory toward demonstration in the same years. By the century's close its engineers could show the court a device in which heated vapor drove a wheel in steady rotation, proof that the realm held the mechanism and could reproduce it, though nothing yet able to move a boat or a cart. They set their sights on a first practical use, a vessel that could work upstream against a river's current at a steady pace without oar or wind, which would ease the long supply haul to Mesopotamia and the western lands.

This ruler also brought the realm's printing into practical use, confined to administrative work, tax rolls, law codes, military orders, and medical texts, because the Brahmin scholarly establishment, whose authority rested on their control of sacred learning, blocked its use for religious or educational texts.

> **On why printing stayed narrow.** The scholars who guarded sacred knowledge understood at once that cheap identical copies would loosen their grip on it, and the scribes who copied by hand saw their living threatened. Deeper than either, the matter touched caste itself, for the Brahmins' place at the head of the order rested on their sole keeping of the sacred word, and to let that word loose in cheap copies in any hand threatened their influence and, with it, the rank the whole order granted them. All of this had weight at court. The ruler chose the achievable path, printing only the documents no one held sacred, which standardized the realm's administration without provoking a fight he might lose.

Among the first documents struck in numbers were the medical texts carrying the Alexandrian washing protocols, so that the new practice reached the realm's hospitals in one identical form rather than drifting from copy to copy, and the tax codes of the frontier provinces now matched the capital's to the letter.

The eastern trade came to its fruit in this reign. The expeditions begun a generation before reached steady commercial dealings with the clove and nutmeg islands, with regular voyages sailing on the season's turn and the goods flowing back into the realm's markets and on westward along the Rome road. China's own ships, built on the far southeastern coast, were working the southern waters by now and could reach those islands from the eastern side without passing the Strait, yet the realm had come first and held the standing that any later comer would have to break. At the Strait itself the realm's trading port at the eastern mouth was finished and working by about 385 to 390, China's ships berthing at the realm's facility and paying its toll, their traffic recorded as the old loose arrangement had never allowed.

The scholarly tie with China was returned in kind. The ruler sent to China's capital the reciprocal embassy long promised, scholars of real learning rather than mere watchers, who were given the run of China's institutions and libraries as China's men had been given Pataliputra's, and who brought back the realm's closest look yet inside that civilization.

China's ships, now working far south and west, at last reached toward the waters Egypt held, and it fell to the realm, which sat astride the sea road between them and took its cut of the Chinese traffic, to make the introduction. The realm arranged it and asked in return only that China tell it afterward what had passed. China sent a delegation in person to Aden, bearing silk and lacquerware and worked steel, and framed its approach with great care. It let Egypt know that it was the fountainhead of all the silk reaching Egyptian and Roman markets, that it could now carry its own goods by sea, and that it came because the sea could move volumes the land road never could, not from any want or weakness. And it made its standing overland tie with Rome plain to Egypt, so that Egypt should understand China already held another road west and stood before it as an equal and not a supplicant.

He bound the western provinces to the realm by making Mesopotamia the richest transit ground on the most profitable trade route in the world, a firmer tie than force or paper concessions, so that a province tempted to revolt would be throwing away its own prosperity. And, with the very poor reign that had wrecked the close of the previous century still standing as the plainest warning, he built institutions meant to blunt the harm of future weak rulers, an advisory council with the standing to bring hard truths to the throne directly, and senior officials paid from the treasury rather than from patronage, harder to purge on a whim. These did not solve the deeper problem that a determined bad ruler could still do damage, but they added friction to the worst behavior and preserved skilled men across the turning of reigns.

The realm entered the fifth century stronger, richer, and more worldly than at any point in the recovery, holding prototype gunpowder, working steam demonstrations, administrative printing, a clove and nutmeg trade its rivals could not reach, and direct dealings with both Rome and China. Its lasting problems remained, managed rather than cured: a thinly held northern river frontier, a western population governed more than embraced, and an Egypt whose commercial and technical lead kept growing.

> **By the numbers, around 400 AD.** The realm's ninety-five million people hold their long-settled lands, with a clove and nutmeg trade now running through the Strait of Malacca to the spice islands of the Maluku in the far eastern sea (modern Indonesia). Yearly revenue is about twenty-three hundred million paṇas, the strongest the treasury has ever stood, some nine hundred and fifty million the land tax and near eight hundred million from trade, and the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls. The standing army has reached roughly a million: four hundred thousand field infantry, four hundred and fifty thousand in garrison and second line, ninety thousand horse, and forty thousand navy crew. About eight hundred million goes to the army, a hundred and forty million to weapons research and armament, and two hundred and fifty million to roads and works, the technical programs now employing some seventeen or eighteen thousand trained researchers and tens of thousands more craftsmen. A century of recovery has built the reserve up to nearly a full year's revenue, and reading and writing remain with about one in ten.

## Part X: Overreach and the Eastern Crisis (400 to 500 AD)

### The Mediocre Reign and the Reach East (400 to 425 AD)

An adequate ruler, able to carry forward well-formed plans, made the decision the previous two reigns had been building toward: to place a permanent military garrison on the Spice Islands, going beyond the trade relations of past reigns.

> **The reasoning.** Cloves, nutmeg, and mace grew nowhere else in the known world. China's ships were improving on a path that would one day let them reach the islands directly. The window to secure the islands before China's reach extended to them was closing. So the realm sent a large fleet, over forty vessels, with authority to plant a permanent garrison rather than merely to trade.

Between about 415 and 425 AD the realm established fortified garrisons on the three, Ternate taking the main work with a stone fort commanding the harbor mouth and roughly twelve hundred soldiers, Tidore a smaller watchtower and signal post of some six hundred, and the Banda group, where the nutmeg grew, about seven hundred more, roughly twenty-five hundred soldiers in all. The garrison was sized to control a cooperative local population and to guard against the trade rivals of the region, and for nothing heavier.

Two further facts of this garrison mattered. The island peoples had long sold their goods freely to whatever trader came, and now sold through one facility at prices the realm set, for the realm held the strait and the surrounding seas and could keep every rival buyer away, making itself the only market. That left the islanders three choices: trade with the realm, trade with no one, or fight. They chose to trade, for the realm had dealt fairly with them before, having paid them well in earlier years to learn which islands grew the clove and the nutmeg, and it paid a fair price still. They tolerated the garrison, and did not welcome it, and the resentment was commercial and structural, not to be soothed by good manners. Something drove the realm to bind this trade so tightly to itself, besides: Egypt's own grip on coffee, a monopoly whose sting the realm knew well and meant to answer with one of its own. And the soldiers, drawn from the plains and the plateau, sickened and died in the equatorial climate at a rate the realm never properly counted, so that the garrison had to be continually refilled with fresh drafts from home to keep its numbers, a steady drain of men that the capital's records, counting the establishment and not the graves, never showed.

In the same reign a survey expedition sent south and east reached the coast of a large dry landmass beyond the islands, mapped a stretch of it, confirmed it was a continent and not an island, and returned. The northwest shore it charted was arid and thinly grown, poor ground for settling, though the survey found fresh water at several points and marked that the growth thickened further north along the coast. The findings were filed in the geographic archive.

The gunpowder prototypes of the previous reign were brought toward deployable manufacture. Progress was steady and slow, and by the reign's end only fifteen to twenty finished weapons stood on the northern frontier and a few other points, all heavy fixed pieces, because the making of powder of consistent quality was the bottleneck on how fast the arsenal could grow. The frontier pieces were set on the realm's own bank of the Syr Darya, in plain sight of China's watchers across the water, a deliberate show that the realm now held the weapon too and meant to hold its line with it.

Away from the frontier, the reign carried the works of the great reign before it forward without enlarging them. The research budget held near a tenth of the realm's revenue. Administrative printing spread to eight or ten provincial centers, though the Brahmin objection still kept it from sacred and school texts. The steam engineers went on showing the court their turning wheels without yet moving a hull. The realm's mathematics pressed on toward the study of continuous change.

With China the great sea treaty was brought to its terms in this reign, and it came harder than the realm had hoped. China would finalize nothing until its own ships had made two return voyages through the Strait and seen the passage for themselves, and when the terms were at last settled China had conceded none of the plain language the realm wanted, no acknowledgment of Maurya authority over the Strait, no toll named, no fixed port of call set down. What it accepted instead was thinner and vaguer, framed around equal terms for all who used the route. The realm read into it that a toll and a port of call were implied and would in time be honored, and chose to be content with that reading, though nothing in the words bound China to either. With Rome the standing was easier and plainer, the overland treaty coming up for renewal and being extended for a second span of twenty-five years, the traffic on the road grown so heavy that both thrones counted it among their surest revenues. That very traffic had drawn robbers to the road, though, as a rich road always draws them. Bandits worked the long stretches between the waystations and grew bold enough that Rome's merchants began to shy from the crossing, and word of the complaint came to the realm from Rome itself, with a plain request that the realm keep its own reach of the road safe. The realm answered with more waystations and heavier patrols along the Mesopotamian run, which thinned the raids without ending them, for the road carried exactly the goods that tempt a robber, small in bulk and great in worth, the light costly wares that went overland precisely because they could bear the longer and dearer passage that kept them clear of Egypt's sea tolls.

### The Damaging Reign and an Accident in the Dark (425 to 450 AD)

The worst ruler since the crisis of the previous century did his harm quietly, through neglect rather than catastrophe. No single disaster marked his reign. Instead every part of the state that needed tending was let slip. He cut the research budget from a tenth of revenue to six or seven parts in the hundred and turned the difference to his court and his favorites. The making of new cannon nearly halted, so that after fifteen years the arsenal stood at only twenty-five or thirty pieces. The steam program lost its key people and stalled. The canals and embankments of Mesopotamia, which held the Tigris and Euphrates to their courses through each flood season, went unkept under a slackened provincial hand. On the Spice Islands he installed a governor chosen for court connection rather than competence, whose extractive misrule sharpened the islanders' resentment and opened the door to Chinese goods slipping in through the small traders the garrison could not watch. He was warned of the leak and did not act.

The one bright thread of the reign appeared by accident, in a research program he had cut but not killed.

> **The galvanic force.** A metalworker, joining a piece of zinc and a piece of copper by a copper wire while both sat in salt water, found a strange steady force in the wire. The wire warmed. A third piece of metal held near it was pushed or pulled in ways that did not follow the known behavior of lodestones. Break the connection and the force vanished; restore it and the force returned. He recorded it carefully. The court, in its degraded state, filed the report as a curiosity to be studied when resources allowed.

The realm's merchants also began reporting, in these years, that Chinese caravans were appearing further north than the established Silk Road, hinting at a new route through the steppe that would bypass the realm's territory entirely. The court noted this and did nothing, in keeping with the reign.

By these years the coffee had spread west into Rome as well, Egypt selling it there to a growing taste among working people who took it with the realm's sugar, so that the realm furnished one half of that fashion and Egypt the richer half and kept it whole to itself. When the realm's agents in Rome asked quietly after the bean's source, they found themselves marked by watchers they could not name, Egypt's own men, set to guard the secret even in a market not its own. That a power would spend spies abroad to protect a plant told the realm plainly how great the prize was.

The realm's hunt for that plant ran across the better part of two reigns, cheap enough to outlast even the lean years of this one, and it ran into a wall. The one road to the plant, barring the Red Sea, was the long African coast south of Egypt's sphere, and the realm's ships worked it, season upon season, out from the Oman ports and down past the last harbors any Maurya captain had known. What they found there remade the realm's whole picture of Egypt. The coast did not run free below the known ports. Egypt held it, harbor after harbor, stone marker after stone marker, patrol craft the whole way, mile upon mile without a gap, far beyond anything the realm had thought Egypt's reach to be. The realm's ships were closed on by Egyptian patrols and put to questions, turned from one harbor mouth and another, told, always, to trade if they must and then go home. Every river mouth that climbed inland toward the high country where the plant was said to grow was held and watched. The captains learned the coast as the years passed, the winds, the hidden coves where water could be cached against the long haul home, and the trick of slipping past the guarded ports by night, and so each ship reached a little further than the last, down past the old known harbors of modern Somalia and Kenya, on down the whole length of what is now Mozambique, to the borders of modern South Africa. Egypt held every mile of it. At the far end the land turned colder and greener than any country the crews knew, and there the sea itself stopped them, a fierce current setting south along the shore and, where it met the wind, seas worse than any the realm's sailors had ever ridden. Two ships were lost in that water and the rest came about, and the coast still ran on south below them, further than any Maurya hull would follow.

So the coffee could not be reached, and that was past argument: every river mouth to the high country was Egypt's, and the southern way was barred by the sea. But the reckoning ran wider than coffee. The realm had taken Egypt for a great trading power that held the sea gates, the Red Sea and the strait and a string of ports. It saw now that Egypt held the whole eastern coast of Africa unbroken, thousands of miles of it, every harbor and every river, a continental dominion whose African holdings alone dwarfed what the realm had reckoned the whole of Egypt to be. Egypt was no keeper of gateways. It was an empire on the order of the realm's own, and its coffee sat safe behind that wall.

The ships did not come home empty, though not with what they were sent for. Year on year they had taken aboard the cultivated seed and plant the coastal peoples prized and carried north to Egypt's markets, on the chance the coffee was among it, for the realm's botanists could not have known the unroasted plant by sight. The realm grew all of it out. The coffee was not there. But much of what came up was familiar, African strains of the sorghum and millet and cowpea the realm had farmed for a thousand years, bred by the farmers of that coast for drought and heat and thin soil, and these throve in the dry hard country of the Deccan and the northwest where the realm's own seed struggled. The coffee hunt had failed, and left behind, almost as an afterthought, a quiet and lasting gift: African grains bred for exactly the drought and heat that pressed the realm's own north and west.

### The Recovery Reign and the Gathering Storm (450 to 475 AD)

A capable ruler restored the research budget as his first act and set the programs working again. The gunpowder makers pushed the arsenal to forty or fifty pieces. The steam engineers solved the containment problem with a valve that released pressure before the vessel could burst, and their devices at last ran steadily for long stretches, though driving a boat or a cart with them remained a further problem unsolved.

If those engines could not yet move a boat, they could be made to sit still and work, and the realm found a use for them at once. The Kolar gold, worked for centuries, had reached the depths where the shafts filled with water faster than men with buckets could clear them, and it was that flooding, more than the digging, that now held the yield down. A steam engine too heavy and too fixed to carry anywhere was exactly the thing to set beside a shaft and let labor at a pump all day, and the first of them were sent south to lift the water from the deep Kolar workings, the realm's earliest steam engines put to real work at the bottom of a mine.

This reign also inherited a broken river. In the last years of the damaging reign the Tigris had risen higher than its keepers could hold, several winters of heavy snow in the northern mountains sending down more water than the neglected canals and embankments could carry, and the flood tore through the farmland of the central valley. The repair fell to this ruler, and his better provincial appointments told: the canals were rebuilt and the silt cleared, and by about 463 the valley was substantially restored. The loss across the flood years, in ruined harvests and interrupted trade along the Rome road, ran to several hundred million paṇas before the ground came back.

On the Spice Islands the ruler pulled out the patronage governor his predecessor had set there and put in an experienced administrator, who rebuilt the standing of the island leaders, eased the facility's terms enough to draw some trade back from the Chinese pedlars, and stopped the drift. The resentment bred over fifty years did not lift, and the active decay was halted. The garrison itself was another matter. Disease went on killing the garrison and the realm went on replacing it, drafting fresh men out to the islands to fill the places the climate emptied, so that it held near its full strength only by that constant refilling. Its gaps were filled unevenly, by local levies and by drafts that arrived when a supply ship got through, and it stood always a bad season away from falling short, while the capital's ledgers went on reckoning it at its establishment. In these years the first cannon of the realm's own making reached the islands, a handful of pieces for the harbor forts.

Egypt had become the realm's chief object of intelligence. A delegation sent to Alexandria about 440 had accomplished little beyond a first step: after much giving of gifts it won from Egypt an agreement in principle to an exchange of information between the two, nothing so binding as an alliance. The realm wanted no alliance in any case. The Arthashastra named Egypt an enemy, and the realm still meant to move against it if the day ever came when it could, remembering that Egypt held every western gate against it and would grant it no land. An exchange of knowledge, each side giving to get, was as far as either cared to go.

About 462 a second mission came to make that exchange real, and to learn what it could of the one wonder the realm judged Egypt might actually part with. The court had gone back over its old reports of Alexandria and settled on the ornamental metal that ran along the city's walls. It seemed no weapon and no war secret, the sort of thing a power might explain without much cost, and yet a power that hung such wealth so openly surely did so for a reason. So the envoys raised the question of the metal lines. The Egyptian they dealt with grew guarded at it and was slow to answer, and from that reluctance the realm's diplomat drew the opposite of comfort: the thing must be worth far more than it looked. He offered, then, all he could that gave nothing vital away, the realm's charts and harbor notes and the accounts of its eastern trade routes, and at these the Egyptian brightened past all expectation, so plainly eager for them that the realm knew it had offered too much. The bargain was struck all the same. The realm handed over its full account of the eastern seas, the charts, the harbor assessments, the route descriptions, and with them the location of the southern continent, knowledge Egypt had no other way to come by. In return Egypt told it what the metal was.

The lines were decoration, as they had seemed. But the copper woven through them carried a force that made distance almost nothing and bore word between points faster than any rider could carry it, and by that force Egypt bound its administration across the whole of its reach. That was the sum of what Egypt would give, the function alone, with not a word of the making. It would not say which places the network joined or how far it ran, only that it served the administration. Asked once more of the lighthouse, Egypt answered that mirrors threw its light, and said no more. Throughout the visit the envoys were held at the distance Egypt chose. They could see the war harbor, busier than on the first embassy twenty years before, and ships passing in and out of it under their own power with neither oar nor sail, but the yards where such ships were built stayed always beyond their reach, and the vessels could not be closely counted or examined. Egypt gave the realm exactly what it had promised and nothing it had not, holding its courtesy and its secrets in the same hand.

The copper-network account the embassy carried home did not lie idle. A scholar of the court set it beside the old galvanic report the damaging reign had filed away and forgotten, and argued that the strange force in the metalworker's wire and the force Egypt described running through its copper lines were one and the same. The court agreed, and turned the galvanic research toward the question of whether the realm's own force could be made to carry messages as Egypt's did. It was the most important scientific turn of the reign, and it grew straight out of the embassy's bargain, the one gain from Egypt the realm could set its hands to and build upon.

The realm's merchants confirmed, in these same years, that China had reached the Spice Islands' waters directly, by a sea route that ran south of the Strait of Malacca, through the archipelago east of Sumatra and into the open ocean below the passage, so that the realm's garrison at the Strait never saw those ships at all. Chinese goods appeared in the island markets outside the realm's facility, the toll on the passage plainly bypassed. The realm's Silk Road revenue had slipped in these years too, by something between a tenth and an eighth from its peak as the northern steppe route drew off a share of Chinese trade, and the realm answered by trimming its tolls a little and mending its worn stretches, which slowed the loss without turning it. The Strait was the sharper insult. At the first reports of Chinese hulls slipping past the passage without paying, the realm sent word to China that its ships owed the toll like any other, and China did not answer. So the realm resolved to collect by force, to put enough warships in those waters to stop the Chinese vessels, board them, and make them pay the toll or give up their cargo. Its first patrols watched the Strait itself and turned back the ships that used it, while the vessels on the southern bypass passed on unmet, the patrols set at the wrong gate. The realm moved its ships south to the eastern approaches of the islands, and there, for the first time, it met gunpowder at sea.

> **The Battle of the Eastern Approaches (about 474 to 476 AD).** A Maurya interception squadron, out under the standing order to turn back or board any foreign ship that would not put in to the realm's facility, sighted a Chinese convoy running west with the monsoon at the edge of the patrol line, several merchantmen under three armed escorts. The squadron closed to redirect it, as it had redirected many before. But the escorts did not run or dodge as merchant guards did. They swung across the convoy's line and held station, waiting. Then, at some four hundred paces, a range beyond any bow or ballista the realm carried, they opened fire, iron shot and bursting shells that struck and holed the Maurya ships while these were still closing and could not answer a blow. The squadron commander drove in rather than turn. He reasoned that his ships were already inside the enemy's reach against only three hulls, that turning to run would only hold them under the guns longer for nothing, and that at close quarters his rams, his fire arrows, and his greater numbers would tell. So the squadron bore in through the fire, taking loss the whole way. As it came close the Chinese swept its oar banks with shorter guns worked from the deck and with thrown fire, killing and scattering the rowers of ship after ship. But the realm's fire arrows found the rigging of one escort and set it ablaze, and as the squadron swept past close aboard, that escort lost way and was overwhelmed and destroyed. The other two escorts and the whole convoy, running before the wind, outran the pursuit and reached open water with their cargo whole. The realm lost eleven ships and had destroyed one, and let the rest slip. From it the realm drew what it could. The Chinese sea gun outranged everything the realm had and could not be answered at a distance, yet it was slow between shots, and the ships that bore it still went under sail and rigging that fire arrows could burn. Closing was murderously costly and it could be done, and once done, the larger force won. The whole difficulty was living through the approach.

The ruler died as the crisis broke, and the succession was contested for two to three years by rival court factions, a dispute that briefly drew in frontier commanders and froze the realm's strategic response at the moment it was most needed.

### The Eastern Crisis Reign (475 to 500 AD)

A competent ruler consolidated power after the succession quarrel, having lost two years to it, and inherited both the crisis and the delay. He faced a China that had grown far beyond the power that fired a single cannon at a fort two and a half centuries before.

The first battle had lit a fury at court that the succession quarrel had left with nowhere to go. To lose eleven ships to gunfire while enforcing the realm's own toll in the realm's own waters was read at court as an act of war. China might name it a commercial disagreement; the throne and its soldiers did not. As the ruler took full power he struck back in the one place China could be reached without a fleet, doubling the Silk Road toll on China's overland trade from twelve parts in the hundred to twenty-four, and warning that this was the least the road could do, for the realm would close it outright before it would be dictated to over the eastern sea. That was the one blow the realm could still land with its fleet outmatched: China's western trade crossed thousands of miles of ground the realm had held for five centuries, and the realm's larger economy could outlast the loss of it where China's could not. He demanded, too, that China account for the attack before any talk of island terms. And he set the eastern theater to answering the cannon by other means, since the realm had no gun of its own to match it. Its ships were to come on dispersed rather than in a line, spread wide and closing from many angles, so that the enemy's guns could strike only one group while the rest reached boarding range. Fire ships were to be built, cheap hulls packed with the realm's petroleum compounds and run at the enemy by skeleton crews who leapt clear before the blow, since a burning vessel that reached its mark did its work whether it was sinking or not. These were the makeshift answers of a power with no naval gun; for the lasting answer the ruler put the naval cannon program, which had languished since the last reign, on a proper footing for the first time. Then he ordered the approaches held, and the second battle came.

> **The Fall of the Eastern Fleet (about 476 to 478 AD).** After the first battle the court had ordered the approaches held and given the eastern fleet new tactics for the range problem: to come on in several groups from separate bearings rather than in one line, so that a single line of the enemy's guns could not bear on more than one group at once, and to send fire ships ahead, burning hulls meant to reach the enemy whatever his rate of fire. Under those orders the commander met the Chinese fleet, fourteen ships drawn in a single curving line, with about a hundred and ten warships and the eight fire ships the realm had built. His own orders pulled against themselves, to hold and not withdraw yet not to spend the whole fleet at a stroke, and pulling harder than either was the memory that the commander of the first battle had driven straight in and been rewarded for it. So he pressed the attack.
>
> The new tactics broke against an enemy that would not be closed with. His groups bore in from many quarters at once, and it changed nothing. The Chinese line held its distance with a precision he could not account for, three of his ships together unable to close on one of theirs, the range holding constant from whatever bearing his groups came, as though it was the enemy who chose where the fleets would meet. Each group was shattered by fire before it reached striking distance. The fire ships were holed or turned aside before a single one struck home. Having lost all order, the commander flung in the rest of his force together, fire ships and all, and bought only a heavier ruin for the same end. The realm lost ninety of its hundred and ten warships and all eight fire ships, and some twenty battered hulls came away. Not one Chinese ship was sunk, or crippled, or so much as seen to take harm.

The commander's report noted three things he could not account for. The Chinese ships held their exact distance regardless of the wind, as though they set their own speed. Their fire fell without the pause that reloading should force, as though each hull carried many guns speaking together. And fire arrows that struck their sails did not catch, as though the sails would not burn.

> **The court's reading.** Taken together, these pointed to Chinese warships no longer dependent on the wind for movement, carrying many guns each, with sails treated against fire or not their chief means of moving at all. Whether China had drawn this from Egypt's steam or reached it by its own path, the realm could not say.

Shortly after, the same Chinese fleet turned on the island of Tidore, and the commander of Ternate watched the whole of it from his own walls across the strait and could do nothing. The fleet anchored off Tidore at a distance the island's archers could not reach and threw fire and iron into the walls and the town. Tidore had no cannon to answer. Its signal tower, its one way to warn Ternate at distance, came down in the first hour; by midday the harbor wall was breached in two places and the settlement was burning. Then boats put men ashore down the coast, away from the heaviest resistance. The survivors who reached Ternate said the landing force was only a few hundred men, no army at all, and that each of them carried a gun he could hold and fire in his own hands, a weapon the realm had never met on land, and that they threw fire-pots that burst among the defenders in the breach. The garrison fought through the day, but nothing it carried could answer guns, bursting fire, and a wall already down behind it, and the line broke before dusk. A walled position that would have held against any conventional storm was carried by a few hundred men.

> **On the individual firearm.** China had now shown three distinct gunpowder weapons in the eastern sea: heavy cannon that struck at four hundred paces, shorter guns worked from a ship's deck, and now the firearm carried by a single foot soldier. The last the realm had no equal to and no quick path toward. Its engineers judged that shrinking a cannon to a weight one man could carry, while holding the same force, was a harder problem than the fixed cannon they had already solved, not an easier one. A piece carried by two men they set at five to ten years of work. A true single-soldier firearm they set at fifteen to twenty-five years at the least.

With Tidore taken and the eastern sea emptied of Maurya sail, China sent its terms, and sent them as a victor to a beaten foe, with word that the offer would not be made a second time. It required free passage for its merchant ships through the island waters and on to Egypt, with no toll and no use of the realm's facility, Chinese access to the island goods at market rates, and the realm keeping its garrisons on Ternate and Banda. It required too that the realm roll the Silk Road toll back down to the old twelve parts in the hundred it had doubled. And to these it added one demand more: that the realm remove its cannon from the Syr Darya frontier in the north, calling their removal the sign that the matter was closed.

> **The reasoning, and the refusal.** The commercial terms were painful but survivable. The realm kept the producing islands, which China had chosen to leave in its hands rather than pay again the price it had paid for Tidore. But the northern demand had nothing to do with spices or sea lanes. It asked the realm to give up the single weapon it had spent a hundred and seventy-five years building as its one answer to China's military edge, in exchange for settling a quarrel about island tolls. The ruler, in no mood to be dictated to by the power that had just sunk his fleet, refused that demand outright, kept the cannon, and took the rest of the terms. China pressed the cannon a while longer, urging its removal as a gesture of peace between great nations, and the realm would not be moved.

Ternate and Banda still stood. China could take them as it had taken Tidore, by bombardment and a landing, and had chosen instead to offer terms and spare itself the cost, so that for now the two islands remained the realm's. But they stood on borrowed time. Disease had left Ternate about twelve hundred strong and Banda about seven hundred. Their harbor cannon, between them, had powder for forty to sixty shots before the stores ran dry, and no ship could bring more through waters China's fleet now watched. The guns could make an enemy pay to enter the harbor, and they could not turn back a fleet that stood off beyond their range and battered the positions down at leisure before a landing. The garrison's task shrank to holding as long as it could and keeping the islands nominally the realm's.

In the end China let the cannon go. Unable to force the point and well content with what it had won at sea, it allowed that the matter could be left aside, in the manner of a great state that can afford to overlook a demand it cannot carry. So the settlement took its shape as the century closed. The realm rolled its Silk Road toll back to the old twelve parts in the hundred, granted China the free passage and the market access it had asked, and kept its garrisons on the islands, while the cannon stayed on the Syr Darya, the one demand the realm had refused and the one point left formally open, a thing both courts agreed to let lie.

The defeat forced the realm to a course it had long avoided. It saw at last the value of the southern continent it had known of for sixty years and told to no one. A supply depot was planted secretly on its northwest coast, with shipwrights sent to build vessels from local timber, so that the island garrison might be supplied by a southern route China had no reason to watch. The secret was kept absolutely, for the realm's sole knowledge of the continent was its entire worth, and the court reckoned that Egypt, to whom the realm had given the survey in the Alexandria trade, might reach the continent's western coast within fifty to eighty years and apply there its unbending rule of trade access without foreign bases.

The naval cannon program the ruler had set going after the first battle began, slowly, to bear its fruit. The cannon itself was never the hard part; the mounting was, for a gun that recoils on a stone wall pushes against the earth, while a gun that recoils on a wooden deck pushes against a floating thing that can crack or capsize. After years of failed carriages the realm produced a working one, and by the century's end it had between six and ten vessels each bearing a cannon or two, crewed by men with a few years' training and poor aim, their powder always short. It was a beginning and no more.

The crisis drove more than the naval and cannon programs; it set the realm's craftsmen to a scatter of smaller inventions, each answering a hard-learned need. A commander who first knew of the enemy's guns when their shot struck home needed to see further than his own eye reached, so the lens-grinders, who had magnified small things at a bench for generations, fitted curved glasses into a tube to carry the eye out to a distant sail, and by the century's close the realm's ships and frontier posts carried these brass-bound glasses that brought a far vessel near enough to count its masts. The weeks a rider took to carry word of the eastern war from the coast to the capital had cost the throne dearly, and since the realm could not send word through copper as Egypt did, it raised instead long lines of signal towers within sight of one another, each with great jointed arms or mirrors that a watcher could set to spell out a message the next tower would read and pass along, so that word might cross the realm in a day where it had taken a month, save in fog and rain when the lines went blind. And for the secret southern road, where a ship must find its reckoning east and west across trackless ocean, the realm's brass-workers set to cutting fine geared wheels and a ticking escapement to govern them, the makings of a mechanical clock exact enough to hold true time through a long voyage, the first of them coming into use at sea as the century turned.

Rome, hearing of the eastern war, pressed the realm with sharp and particular questions about the firearm the single soldier carried, its look, its reach, the time a man needed between shots, the wounds it made. The realm put those questions to the survivors of Tidore and sent Rome an honest and thin answer, for men fleeing a broken wall under fire had counted nothing, and could say only that the weapons were small enough for one man to bear and fire, that each carried his own, and that it was their fire that broke the line in the breach. To this the realm added what its engineers reasoned from the weight of the thing, a reach of perhaps fifty to a hundred paces before the aim failed, and a loading slower than a gun crew's but quicker in one trained man's hands with his charges made ready. Rome also challenged an old assumption of the realm's, that China's weapons must have come from Egypt. The realm conceded the point. China had worked gunpowder for three hundred years and could well have reached the hand-firearm on its own, with no help from Egypt at all, and so the realm stopped treating the Egyptian origin as a settled fact and held it instead as only one possibility among several, worth watching but no longer assumed. Rome said it had seen gunpowder used on its own northern river frontier and had met no such single-man firearm there.

In its last exchanges of the century the realm addressed the galvanic question to Egypt, and Egypt gave its most generous guidance yet: that its own study of the force had begun with the torpedo fish, whose shock its scholars had recognized as the same force, and that much of the path had run through the study of certain plants that carried the force before the link to metals in solution became clear. Egypt named no plant and revealed no construction. It said only that the realm should look for the force in its own growing things, in the trees and grasses and grain of its fields, for that was where Egypt's own long search had gone. The realm, grateful for any thread at all from so guarded a keeper of secrets, set every botanist of its medical tradition to testing plant matter of every kind against the galvanic apparatus, and counted the guidance a great gift.

China, in the same season of exchanges, shared without any evident guard what its watchers had seen of the northern weather, that the growing season across the steppe had shortened by two or three weeks over fifty years, that the frosts came earlier, and that the western grasslands were drying and the far northern fields turning marginal. The realm weighed what this meant. A steppe harder to farm was a steppe whose peoples would grow hungry and restless, and hungry peoples on the move press on the settled lands at their edges. The word matched the realm's own reading of a drying northwest, and it hurried the spreading of the drought-hardy grains the realm had gathered from its East African trade into the driest of its northern provinces.

The realm's printing, meanwhile, pressed against the limit of its own materials. Palm leaf and bark cloth served the tax rolls and the law codes, and neither took ink as cleanly nor bore as much handling as the paper the realm had seen for a century among China's trade goods. China guarded the making of it, no craftsman of the art had ever come over, and the finished sheets gave up nothing of how they were formed beyond that they were pressed from plant fiber worked in water. Toward the century's close the realm set its own scholars to work the problem from first principles, as it had worked others, and reckoned useful paper a matter of decades yet.

The realm also noted, without alarm, that the faith of Rome had changed. Christianity had spread to dominate Roman worship, and the Christian communities of Roman Syria bordered the realm's own Mesopotamian Christians. The realm's ancient rule of religious tolerance, which taxed conduct and left belief untouched, accommodated them without trouble, but the court marked that a Rome shaped by a new faith might in time hold different priorities than the Rome the partnership had known.

## Part XI: The Realm at the Close of the Fifth Century

> **By the numbers, around 500 AD.** The realm's ninety-eight million people hold all it long has in the west, the spice islands but for Tidore, lost to China, and the secret southern depot. Yearly revenue holds near twenty-three hundred million paṇas, tilting very slightly down as the old trades ebb, some nine hundred and eighty million the land tax and near seven hundred and fifty million from trade, the rest from mines, monopolies, and tolls. The standing army stands near eleven hundred thousand: four hundred and thirty thousand field infantry, five hundred thousand in garrison and second line, a hundred thousand horse, and a navy of near fifty thousand crew whose eastern fleet is destroyed. About nine hundred million goes to the army, a hundred and fifty million to weapons research and armament, and two hundred and thirty million to roads and works, the technical programs employing some eighteen or nineteen thousand researchers and tens of thousands of craftsmen. The reserve, spent hard in the eastern war, holds something over half a year's revenue, and reading and writing stay with about one in ten.

### Territory

The realm holds the largest continuous dominion of any throne in the known world: the whole subcontinent and Sri Lanka; the Afghan lands held for over eight hundred years; Bactria and Sogdia with the Silk Road cities of Central Asia; the Iranian plateau with the Caucasus highlands of Armenia and Azerbaijan; and Mesopotamia to the Euphrates, governed from a second capital by the Tigris. Its sea positions are Oman at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, the tributary trading ports of the gulf's Arabian shore, a tributary hold on eastern Yemen, the Strait of Malacca, the island garrisons of Ternate and Banda, and a secret depot on the northwest coast of the southern continent. Its frontiers are the Syr Darya in the north, where China holds the far bank; the Euphrates and the Greater Caucasus in the west and northwest, where the spheres of Rome and Egypt begin; the open sea to the east; and the ocean to the south. Along these lines the realm touches all three of its great rivals directly, with no neutral march between. China faces it across the northern river, Rome across the Euphrates and through the mountains of the Caucasus, and Egypt along the Levantine coast west of Mesopotamia and at the strait where the Red Sea meets the Arabian shore. Nowhere on its long land border does empty ground lie between the realm and a peer, and where the realm's ground ends, another great power's begins.

### People

The registered population of the realm stands on the order of ninety-five to a hundred million, the most populous dominion of any single throne. The great mass of it lives in the watered heartlands, the Gangetic plain, the Deccan, the Tamil south, and the Mesopotamian valley, while the vast dry reaches of Iran and Central Asia hold comparatively few. Reading and writing remain the possession of a small part of the people, perhaps one in ten at most, and those the men of the cities, the merchants, the officials, and the upper castes, for the mass of farmers has neither the need nor the means, and the tools that might spread letters more widely, cheap paper and the free printing of texts, the realm does not yet possess.

### Government

The realm is governed as it has long been, by the science of the *Arthashastra*, through appointed governors watched by overlapping networks of informers, with local custom and local faith permitted wherever they do not challenge the throne. Sanskrit is the language of administration everywhere, working alongside the local tongues, Iranian, Aramaic, Tamil, and Sogdian, in their regions. The court's quality remains the hinge on which the whole realm turns, as it has in every century.

### Military

The land army remains the largest and most experienced in the world, its elephant corps intact and its infantry seasoned across every terrain the realm holds. On the northern frontier stand fifty to eighty fixed gunpowder cannon, the cannon China demanded and the realm refused to remove. The realm has no firearm a single soldier can carry: its engineers reckon a crew-served piece for two men five to ten years off and a true single-soldier gun fifteen to twenty-five years at the least. At sea the realm is gravely weakened. The eastern fleet is destroyed, and the new naval cannon amount to six to ten vessels with green crews and powder always short. The gap between the realm's sea power and China's is the widest the realm has faced against any equal since China first showed its cannon.

### Economy

The realm remains the wealthiest in the known world, though several of its proudest revenues are ebbing. The Silk Road yields less as China's northern route draws off trade. The island-strait tolls are gone. The Kolar gold, worked for some seven centuries, gives less as its shallow ore runs out and its deeper ore demands far more labor to reach, its yield now assessed at some three-fifths to three-quarters of its peak. Against these declines, the land tax grows with population and better collection, the Rome trade holds and rises, and the state monopolies grow with the whole economy, so that the realm's total revenue is roughly steady, tilting very slightly down, even as its composition shifts from the glamorous trades toward the steady base of farm and salt. The realm's total wealth holds roughly steady in sum, while it loses the commercial and strategic pre-eminence that its ebbing trades represented, and the treasury's unease tracks that real loss.

### Diplomatic Position

The realm holds direct dealings with all three of the powers it knows. With China the great dispute has been settled, and settled hard. China, victor at sea, took the commercial terms it wanted: the realm rolled its Silk Road toll back to its old rate, granted free passage and market access in the eastern waters, and gave up the tolls it had claimed there. But on the one point that touched its survival the realm did not yield. It kept its cannon on the Syr Darya, and China, having pressed and failed, let the matter lie, in the way a great state overlooks a demand it cannot force. The peace is a wary one, the realm's sea power broken and its northern weapon kept, and the frontier is quiet again.

Rome is the steadiest tie the realm holds. Word passes both ways with honesty now, the realm having learned to weigh what it gives as Rome long has, and the two share what they see of Egypt and of China between them. Rome has told the realm that it has met gunpowder on its own northern river frontier, though never a firearm a single man carries, which the realm reads as a sign that China has shown that weapon only in the southern sea, or shows it only lately.

Egypt has given more in its last exchange than in any before it, confirming the realm's grasp of the galvanic force and pointing it toward the study of plants. Why Egypt, so close with its secrets for so long, should open even this far the realm does not know, and it watches the change without yet trusting it.

### Technology

The realm leads the world in steel, in mathematics now approaching the study of continuous change, in navigation, and in the organization of land war. It has developed, over the century, prototype and then deployed gunpowder cannon, working steam demonstrations not yet driving anything, administrative printing, the medical washing protocols, and the first accidental grasp of the galvanic force. It trails China in the breadth of gunpowder, in the firearm and the bursting shell and possibly in steam at sea, and it trails Egypt by an unknown span of generations in the copper network that carries messages across distance. Its path toward that last capability has only just begun, at the stage of testing plants.

A note on how these figures are reckoned and on the pace of the work. The weapons-program research staff is drawn straight from the program's budget. Materials, fuel, and equipment for this kind of work run about three times the wage cost of the researchers they support, so roughly one part in four of the program's funds paid researchers' wages. At a researcher's wage of about two thousand paṇas a year, the weapons-research budget of a strong reign supported on the order of seventeen to nineteen thousand trained researchers at its height, with a still larger body of craftsmen and laborers working alongside them. This scale is the reason the work advanced as fast as it did. A program running thousands of trials, castings, and powder mixtures at once, staffed by many trained hands, searches the space of workable designs far faster than a small circle of scholars could, and the pace of the realm's gunpowder, steam, and printing work rose and fell with the money and the people committed to it. Where the fielded arsenal still lagged the realm's wealth, the cause lay in the making of gunpowder itself, whose consistent-quality output scaled more slowly than either the research or the casting of the guns.

Why numbers and wealth quicken even work that seems to wait on a single stroke of insight. A problem still unsolved is a search: some worker must chance upon the right proportion, the right bore, the right treatment of the metal. The order of the steps is fixed, for a later step cannot be attempted until the earlier one is in hand, yet nothing fixes how many hands may work a given step at once. One scholar set to a hard step gives the realm his single stream of attempts. A thousand set to that same step, each trying a different variation, raise the odds that one of them strikes the answer within the season. The realm has seen this in its own workshops, where the same improvement is now and then reached in two places at once, neither workshop knowing of the other, a sign the discovery was ripe and waiting only for enough hands to reach for it. Wealth pulls two levers here. It keeps thousands of trained men at their forges and their studies as full-time specialists, whom a poorer realm would need in the fields to eat. And it pays for the great mass of failures, the burst vessels, the spoiled castings, the wasted powder, that are spent before a success is found, a cost a smaller treasury could not bear. What wealth cannot do is leap a missing prerequisite. It cannot buy the cannon before the burning of powder is understood, nor the message-bearing wire before the force within it is grasped. Resources govern how fast a step is reached, and whether it is reached at all, and they leave untouched the order in which the steps must come. The realm poured the wealth of the richest treasury in the world across gunpowder, steam, printing, medicine, and the galvanic study at one time, and advanced on all of them together, while the one task that no insight finishes, the making of gunpowder in bulk and of even quality, which grows only as fast as new production capacity is built, remained the slowest of all.

## Appendix I: Foreign Powers, as the Realm Assesses Them

**China.** A vast power on the far bank of the Syr Darya, first met in about 225 AD, which had expanded far westward looking for Parthia and found the realm instead. It holds gunpowder in a breadth the realm cannot match: heavy cannon, ship-borne guns, bursting shells, and the firearm a single soldier carries, and possibly ships driven without the wind. It has opened a northern trade route through the steppe that bypasses the realm and draws off Silk Road trade. Its intentions the realm judges to be commercial and self-interested rather than bent on conquering the realm's lands, though this is the court's reading and not a certainty. Relations at the century's close are strained and settling, the realm having refused to disarm its northern frontier and having used the Silk Road as its lever.

**Egypt.** The most technically advanced power the realm has met, holding the Red Sea, the strait at its mouth, the Levant coast, and the East African shore, and thereby every western sea route. It is ahead of the realm in printing, in fuel and lighting, in medicine, in canal engineering, in the standardization of made parts, in steam at sea, and above all in a copper network that carries messages across distance by a force the realm has only just begun to study. Egypt shares the function of its capabilities and never their construction. Its danger to the realm is commercial and technical, its exclusive capabilities compounding over time, and it threatens the realm's lands little by force of arms. The realm's estimate of Egypt's inner extent and workings remains thin.

**Rome.** The realm's steadiest partner and the great market of the west, a large and growing empire reaching, by the realm's estimate, from its Italian heart far to the north and northeast. It is the chief buyer of the realm's steel, textiles, spices, and gems, and a genuine ally in intelligence, more advanced in some arts, such as steam, than it had let the realm know. Its faith has become Christian across the fourth and fifth centuries, a change the realm marks as possibly altering its priorities in time.

**The Sogdians.** A merchant people rather than a state, dwelling in the Central Asian trade cities, speakers of the languages at both ends of the Silk Road, who for centuries have carried its goods and, at need, its messages. They served as the sole bridge between the realm and China at first contact, and their interest lies in the trade's continuance rather than in any one power's favor.

## Appendix K: A Note From Outside the History

*This appendix alone steps outside the voice of the chronicle. Everything before it is written from within the realm's world and refers only to what that world contains. The aim here is to answer a doubt a reader may fairly hold, whether a single power could invent as much as this history claims, and as quickly, and to answer it with reasoning and examples drawn from the reader's own world. A reader satisfied with the story can stop at the glossary. A reader who wants to know why the pace of invention is defensible will find the case set out here.*

**The doubt, stated plainly.** The history has the realm develop gunpowder weapons, steam machinery, printing, improved medicine, and the first grasp of electrical force across a few centuries, while committing a large and sustained share of its treasury and tens of thousands of trained people to the work. The natural objection is that invention waits on rare flashes of insight, and that no sum of money or crowd of workers can summon those flashes to a schedule. If that objection held, the realm's spending would be mostly theater, and its rate of progress would owe little to its wealth.

**Possible and fast are two different questions.** A chain of prerequisites governs what is possible. A working cannon cannot come before an understanding of how the powder burns and how to contain the burst, and a message-carrying wire cannot come before an understanding of the force that runs through it. Money buys no shortcut past a link in that chain. What money and people govern is the second question, how fast each link is reached and how reliably it is reached at all. The error beneath the objection is treating these two questions as one. They are separate variables, and resources act on the second.

**A single hard step is a search, and searches run in parallel.** Even where a step must precede the next, so that the work is sequential in its order, that order places no limit on how many people may attempt the current step at once. An unsolved step is a search for the right combination, proportion, or observation. One person searching yields one stream of attempts. A thousand people searching, each trying a different variation, raise the probability that someone lands the answer within a given span. The insight remains just as rare. The number of independent draws against that rare outcome grows with the number of searchers, and the expected time to the first success shrinks.

**Simultaneous discovery is the visible fingerprint of this.** When a field is ripe and enough capable people work it, the same discovery tends to be made in more than one place at nearly the same time, by people who do not know of one another. Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz developed the calculus independently in the late seventeenth century. Alexander Graham Bell and Elisha Gray submitted telephone filings to the same patent office on the same day in 1876. Joseph Priestley and Carl Wilhelm Scheele isolated oxygen independently within a few years of each other in the 1770s. Each case shows a solvable problem meeting a sufficient number of qualified attempts, at which point the result arrives, sometimes more than once over. The limiting factor was the count of capable attempts aimed at the problem, and raising that count pulled the discovery forward in time.

**Population and specialist numbers drive the long-run rate.** The economist Michael Kremer, in a 1993 study titled "Population Growth and Technological Change: One Million B.C. to 1990," argued that the pace of technological progress across the whole of human history has tracked the size of the population, on the reasoning that more people means more chances for someone to hit upon an improvement, and that a larger economy can support more full-time specialists in place of universal subsistence farming. A realm with a very large population and the surplus to keep tens of thousands at study and craft, rather than at the plough, is by this reasoning placed to invent faster than a smaller or poorer one, for reasons independent of any individual's brilliance.

**Concentrated funding has compressed real timelines against problems no one had yet solved.** The strongest cases are ones where the path was far from clear at the outset. The wartime effort that produced the first nuclear weapons ran roughly three years, and its teams did not begin knowing how to separate the material or how to build a device that would fire. They pursued several separation methods at once because no one knew which would work, and they solved the hard problem of a workable implosion device through the concentrated effort of many hands. The effort that first placed people on the Moon ran under a decade, and it faced a long cascade of problems none had solved before: how to bring two craft together in orbit, how to keep people alive across the journey, how to guide the craft in real time with the machines of the day, how to build an engine that did not shake itself apart, which was cured by firing test after test until the design held. In each, the foundational science was already in hand, and that is what told the planners the goal could be reached at all. Everything built above that foundation was a mass of unknown sub-problems, and money and people ground through them, some in sequence and many in parallel, far faster than a lean effort could have. This is the pattern that bears on the realm, and the realm's footing is firmer still. It has watched the very things work: it has seen the enemy's cannon fire, the western steam ships moving under their own power, and the identical printed sheets in Alexandrian hands. The possibility is settled by its own eyes before the first coin is spent, and what remains is the funded grind through the sub-problems.

**The honest limits, which the history respects.** Spending removes some constraints and leaves others standing, and the history is built around the ones it leaves standing. Returns to added researchers diminish, so that doubling the people does not halve the time, because attempts overlap and coordination grows costly as the crowd swells, and a mature field can demand ever more effort to advance the same distance. The foundational knowledge a result rests on is bought the same way, since discovering it is a search like any other, and more hands and more money reach it sooner and more surely. The one thing money cannot do is reorder that search. A later understanding that truly rests on an earlier one waits for the earlier one to exist, so the spending buys the chain in sequence, quickening each link as it passes through rather than skipping any, and many apparent dependencies dissolve on inspection into steps that can be worked at once. Here the realm holds a genuine advantage. It has seen the finished results in foreign hands, so it need not spend to learn whether a goal is possible, and it aims at a known target instead of searching open ground, which shortens the work at every step. And physical throughput sets a ceiling of its own, which is why the realm's fielded cannon stay few for so long: the bulk production of consistent gunpowder is a matter of manufacturing capacity that grows only as fast as new works are built, a limit of production rather than of discovery. The realm invents broadly and steadily because it funds broadly and staffs heavily, and it still arms slowly, because arming is a question of production capacity. That pairing is the deliberate and, on the evidence above, the realistic result of the realm's choices.
