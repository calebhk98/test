# The Four Empires: A Reader's Primer
### A guide to the shared alternate-history world of Egypt, Rome, Han China, and the Maurya Realm (204 BC to 500 AD)

---

## What This World Is

This world is described in four long documents, each telling the history of one empire from roughly 200 BC to 500 AD:

1. **The Unbroken Pharaoh** covers Ptolemaic Egypt.
2. **An Alternate Rome** covers the Roman Empire.
3. **Han China with Early Gunpowder** covers the Han Empire.
4. **The Maurya Realm** covers the Maurya Empire of India.

The critical thing to understand before reading anything else: **each document is written from that empire's own point of view.** What an empire believes about its rivals is frequently incomplete, sometimes wrong, and occasionally a lie it was deliberately fed. The documents do not correct each other's errors in the text; the reader is expected to hold all four and notice the contradictions. Each document's appendix (economic tables, technology timelines, honest self-assessments) is closer to objective truth than its narrative, though even the appendices carry that empire's framing.

The result is a world where the full picture exists only in the reader's head. Egypt knows things Rome doesn't. China believes things about India that India's own record disproves. Several "facts" in one document are exposed as fabrications by another.

**These documents are the state of a running simulation, paused at 500 AD** (it was previously paused at 300). There is no plot, no protagonist, and no intended winner. Each document also works as a playable briefing: a player handed only one file can act on that empire's knowledge, fears, and blind spots. The Egypt player will fortify against a Roman invasion that is always ten to twenty-five years away. The China player will believe the treasury is desperate and the gunpowder monopoly mostly intact. Both will be acting correctly on what their empire knows, and partly wrongly about the world.

---

## The Divergences

This is a multi-divergence world. Four separate changes to real history are assumed, and the documents are honest that they don't all descend from one cause:

**Egypt: Ptolemy IV survives (204 BC).** In real history he died at forty, leaving a six-year-old heir; the regency chaos invited a two-front invasion that stripped Egypt of its territories and began its slide into Roman clienthood. Here he lives another decade. No child-king crisis, no Fifth Syrian War, no dependency on Rome. Egypt remains an intact great power with the strongest navy in the eastern Mediterranean.

**Maurya: Ashoka hardens instead of softening (261 BC).** In real history, the horror of the Kalinga conquest turned Ashoka toward Buddhism and pacifism, and the dynasty collapsed within fifty years of his death. Here he draws the opposite lesson: conquest is the permanent price of rule, so waste nothing that was paid for. He consolidates, builds forward supply bases, and writes a "Rolling Frontier Doctrine" for his heirs (conquer only what you can hold, spend your reign digging in, hand your son a longer spear). The dynasty persists for over 750 years.

**China: gunpowder arrives a thousand years early (c. 100 BC).** In real history, Chinese alchemists discovered gunpowder around the 9th century AD. Here the same alchemical accident happens under Emperor Wu of Han, the most aggressive expansionist in Han history, whose court immediately weaponizes it. A second, related divergence: the weapons program creates a permanent military-industrial constituency, so the historical turn inward after Wu's death never happens. China stays on a war footing for six centuries.

**Rome: blocked east, so it goes north.** This one follows from Egypt's survival. Rome attempts to conquer Egypt around 100 BC and fails (desert, Delta, and Egyptian sea power). Combined with the historical disaster at Carrhae against Parthia (53 BC), Rome concludes the east is closed and pours its entire expansionist energy into Europe instead.

## The Four Empires in Detail

### Egypt: The Hidden Superpower

**The strategy.** After defeating Rome's invasion attempt (c. 100–90 BC), Egypt's council reaches a cold conclusion: Rome will return, better prepared, and Egypt cannot out-grow Rome's manpower in the Mediterranean. So Egypt expands in the one direction Rome cannot follow: south, into Africa. Everything for the next six centuries serves this strategy of "strategic depth."

**The expansion.** Egypt conquers the Kingdom of Meroe (Sudan) for its gold and iron (85–65 BC), rebuilds the ancient Nile–Red Sea canal (c. 50 BC), and seizes both shores of the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, the chokepoint between the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. Every ship trading with India now pays an Egyptian toll. It colonizes an uninhabited Madagascar, works down the East African coast, recruits African soldiers who resist tropical disease (the key that unlocks the interior), and by 300 AD holds essentially the entire African continent: the Great Lakes, the Zimbabwe gold plateau, the Zambian Copperbelt, the West African Akan goldfields, the Cape. Around 180 AD its ships circumnavigate Africa and discover a sea route to the western Mediterranean that Rome does not know is possible. **This is classified at the highest level and stays secret for over three centuries.**

**The science.** Egypt's defining trait is state-funded research on a scale no other ancient state attempts, growing from hundreds of paid scholars to, by the late centuries, twenty to thirty-five thousand funded researchers atop a literate technical class of half a million or more. That is a professionalized research establishment larger than anything our world fielded before the nineteenth century, and the documents lean on that scale, plus print circulation, as the mechanism behind the discoveries. Combined with a deliberate mass-literacy program (the Coptic language reform of 208 AD; two-thirds of adults literate by 300), knowledge compounds:
- Voltaic battery by ~200 AD, electroplating, then a city telegraph network (wires between buildings).
- Atmospheric steam pumps (~220–260 AD), steam river vessels (~315–325), ocean steam warships (~390s).
- The electromagnetic generator (~345–362), arc lighting, electric motors.
- A Maxwell-equivalent theory of electromagnetism (~370–400), germ theory, antiseptic surgery.
- In the fifth century: radio, voice radio, coastal radar, telephone exchanges, relay computers, internal-combustion patrol craft, and (classified) the first powered aircraft (~472–478).
- Nitro-based high explosives via its acid chemistry. Notably, Egypt **never discovers gunpowder**; its chemistry is wet-media, and it routes around powder entirely.

**Secret geography.** Egypt crosses the Atlantic (402–432 AD) and surveys the Americas. India trades it a survey of Australia. Egypt is the only power that knows the true shape of the world, and tells no one.

**The weaknesses.** A small population (~25 million) capped by pre-industrial agriculture. Production that is artisanal rather than industrial (roughly three warship hulls a year, hand-built). A land army that has not fought a peer in six centuries. And an intelligence shock: around 398–412 AD Egypt discovers that Rome, assumed technologically static, has independently industrialized. Egypt's science outran its economic base; its edge is decisive in a short war and erodes in a long one.

### Rome: The Industrial Giant

**The pivot north.** Having lost to Egypt (geography) and Parthia (Carrhae), Rome turns its full weight on Europe. The engine is the veteran settlement system: forts push forward, retired soldiers get farms just behind the line, towns grow behind them, and their children become the next generation of frontier soldiers. Britain falls early (30–20 BC), Dacia's gold funds everything, the Teutoburg-style disaster is avoided, and by 200 AD Rome holds Ireland, Scotland, Scandinavia's coasts, the Baltics, and western Ukraine, stopping deliberately at the Dnieper River, where good farmland ends and steppe begins.

**The industrial revolution.** Three ingredients arrive in sequence. Cheap paper (~25–50 AD) and then movable-type printing (~80–105 AD) let knowledge and administration compound. Steam mine pumps appear (~150 AD) in the coal-rich British and Rhine districts. Then the trigger: expansion ends around 300 AD, the conquest-fed slave supply collapses, slave prices rise, and machines become cheaper than people. First factories ~260–270 AD, first steam locomotive ~280–295, steam warships by the 390s. By 500 AD Rome is a genuinely industrial civilization: 110 million people, ~67% literacy, railroads on every corridor, gas-lit cities, iron-plated steamships, observation balloons, mechanized agriculture, a mass-politics pamphlet culture, and a newly adopted state-sponsored Christianity (~440–450) whose printed scriptures never fragmented into schisms. The transformation is anything except smooth: the fourth century brings a Rhine succession war, a four-to-five-year armed British secession by a coal-rich island that has to be retaken by naval campaign, coordinated labor unrest organized through printed pamphlets, and by century's end a coal-smog mortality crisis. Rome solves the problems that destroyed its historical counterpart and manufactures a full set of new ones.

**The blind spot.** Rome never discovers gunpowder. Having mastered steam pressure, it spends 150 years assuming China's mystery weapon is a pressure device and investigating a dead end. Only around 450–460, after learning that a single Chinese soldier carries and instantly fires his weapon (impossible for stored pressure), does Rome reorient its research toward combustion chemistry. It still lacks the answer at 500.

**Why Rome never attacks anyone.** Three peers sit on three fronts (Egypt south, Maurya southeast, China northeast). Massing against any one bares the other two frontiers. This arithmetic, plus the memory of two lost eastern wars, holds the most powerful army on the continent in check for five centuries.

**Discovery.** At the very end of the century, a single Roman ship sent west from the Canaries (to answer China's questions about the west) reaches the Caribbean and returns with parrots, gold trinkets, and news of an unknown world.

### Han China: The Gunpowder Empire

**The mechanism.** Early gunpowder does two structural things. Externally, small garrisons armed with explosives can hold fortified points against many times their number, which makes the steppe cheap to occupy for the first time in Chinese history. Internally, provincial garrisons crush every revolt in weeks (the Trung sisters in Vietnam, 40–43 AD; the Yellow Turbans, ~180–200 AD), so the court never has to grant regional commanders emergency powers, so warlords never form, so the dynasty never fragments. The Wang Mang usurpation (9 AD) fails in years instead of decades because the military establishment backs the Han restoration. Six centuries of unbroken dynastic continuity follow, all resting on the suppression asymmetry.

**The expansion.** China breaks the Xiongnu (~30–75 AD), absorbs Mongolia (coal), pushes commercial posts into Siberia (furs), and rolls a garrison-and-colony chain west across Kazakhstan at roughly 25 km a season for two centuries, collecting gold, copper, silver, and saltpeter as it goes. Around 200–225 AD the advance reaches the Syr Darya river and finds the far bank fortified by a peer civilization it did not know existed: the Maurya Empire, which absorbed everything Zhang Qian's 300-year-old reports described. An accidental cannon shot and a war-elephant counter-demonstration shock both sides into a wary, trade-based standoff that becomes permanent. A second frontier vector reaches the Pontic steppe (~270s), where around 300 AD China makes contact with Rome, which becomes its most trusted partner (the Dnieper boundary treaty, direct silk trade, and technology exchanges: Rome's rail method for China's weapons-grade iron).

**The technology.** The gunpowder chain takes its historically realistic six centuries: grenades, then a proto-firearm (~90–100 AD), functional cannon (~165–185), hand cannon (matchlock-equivalent), rifled cannon and an experimental breech-loader by 500. China builds its own steam mine pumps (~325–335) without grasping the deeper principle. Contact with Egypt at Aden (~376–400) delivers a humbling shock: electric lights and steam ships China cannot explain, ending its belief that it is the world's most advanced civilization. The deepest domestic change is the education revolution: cheap printing collides with the meritocratic examination system, an arms race in schooling erupts across all classes, examinations shift toward mathematics, and by 500 a proto-scientific movement ("physical phenomena follow mathematical rules discoverable by measurement") is in print.

**The naval war (474–477 AD).** When the Maurya press tolls and claims over the sea route and spice islands, China arms merchant hulls with cannon. The result is the most lopsided victory in the world's history: in the decisive battle China sinks over ninety Maurya warships while losing none, by holding range where Maurya weapons cannot reach. China storms the island of Tidore with marines carrying personal firearms, then bluffs (its powder nearly spent) the Maurya into rolling back tolls. The Maurya keep their frontier cannon; China keeps its new status as a naval power.

**The weaknesses.** A population that barely grows (~70M), a chronically cash-poor treasury (the frontier consumes ~62% of spending), an unresolved land-concentration problem managed by exporting the landless to frontier colonies, and a gunpowder monopoly that is visibly eroding, both internally (rebels with crude powder by ~355) and externally (Maurya cannon, and the knowledge spreading).

**The political shift.** The examination revolution is weakening the old aristocracy without weakening the state, because the aristocracy was never the government; the exam-selected bureaucracy was, and it is now fed a wider talent pool with heavier mathematical training every cohort. The old families' land base is capped by the reform and the arsenals sit beyond their reach, so what remains is a resentful but declawed elite beside a civil service that improves each generation and increasingly thinks in logistics and throughput. The new fragility is that the examination's legitimacy has become the dynasty's legitimacy: a scandal that would once have embarrassed a court (bought papers, leaked questions) is now a regime-level wound.

### The Maurya Realm: The Volatile Colossus

**The pattern.** The Maurya document's central theme is that the realm rises and falls with the quality of the man on the throne. Great reigns conquer and build; bad reigns purge competent officials, squander the treasury, and lose the newest provinces; the deeply integrated heartland always survives. Over 750 years this lottery produces the largest continuous land empire in the known world: the entire subcontinent, Sri Lanka, Afghanistan, Central Asia with the Silk Road cities, the Iranian plateau, and Mesopotamia to the Euphrates, governed from twin capitals.

**The weapon.** For centuries the army's edge is the war-elephant corps: horses untrained around elephants panic uncontrollably, so every cavalry-based enemy (Greek Bactria, the Yuezhi, Parthia) breaks. Parthia is dissolved entirely by ~150 AD, its tolls flowing to Pataliputra.

**The two shocks.** First, at Aden (~25–50 AD), Maurya ships probing west find Egypt already holding the strait: trade welcome, territory never. Every western sea door is closed, so the realm turns east, taking the Strait of Malacca (~250–275) and eventually garrisoning the Spice Islands (~415–425). Second, at the Syr Darya (~225 AD), a Chinese cannon cracks a Maurya fort wall, and the realm learns a weapon exists that it cannot answer. A 175-year on-again, off-again gunpowder program follows (destroyed by one bad reign, restarted under the wrong director, finally succeeding ~390–395), producing 50–80 fixed frontier cannon by 500.

**The fifth-century catastrophe.** China's fleet annihilates the Maurya eastern navy (474–478) and takes Tidore. The realm's court draws the correct lessons where it can, refuses China's demand to disarm the Syr Darya frontier (correctly reading it as unrelated to the sea quarrel), uses the Silk Road toll as its lever, and begins building naval cannon from scratch. It also quietly develops a secret it has sat on for sixty years: Australia, surveyed in ~415–420 and told to no one, now used for a hidden supply depot.

**The blind spots.** The realm is the world's leader in steel and mathematics, and by cross-converted revenue very possibly the richest state on earth, yet it is behind China in every gunpowder application, behind Egypt by "an unknown span of generations" in electricity, and, unknown to itself, the victim of deliberate Egyptian scientific misdirection.

## The Web Between Them: Contacts, Trade, and Lies

**The contact sequence.** Egypt–Maurya at Aden (~25–50 AD). China–Maurya at the Syr Darya (~225). Rome–Maurya after Parthia's fall (~130–150). China–Rome at the Pontic steppe/Dnieper (~290–325). China–Egypt at Aden (~376). Rome–Egypt formal relations (~400–425). By 400 AD all four powers know each other.

**The trade architecture.** Silk flows from China west by two routes: overland (China's territory to the Dnieper, then Rome's; no middleman, ~10% tariff) and by sea (through Maurya waters and Egypt's canal at a flat 30% toll). The Maurya sit as middleman on both the old Silk Road and the Malacca strait, and their whole diplomacy aims to keep traffic flowing through their infrastructure. Everyone profits from peace, which is a major reason the peace holds.

**The key deceptions** (these are what make the four-document structure worth reading):
- **Egypt's West African cover story.** Rome loses five ships probing the Atlantic coast of Africa over fifty years. All five were destroyed by Egypt's secret Cape fleet protecting the Atlantic route. Egypt tells Rome the coast is held by hostile maritime peoples. Rome writes this fictional "West African coastal civilization" into its official assessments, and later passes the story to China as fact.
- **Egypt's botanical sabotage.** When India asks Egypt for guidance on electrical research, Egypt fabricates a story that the path runs through the study of plants, sending India's entire scientific effort into a dead end for generations. India's own record describes this sabotage as Egypt's "most generous guidance yet."
- **China's frontier bluffs.** The Syr Darya line that faced the Maurya for two centuries could not have survived a determined assault (months-long supply lines, limited powder); it held on appearance. The post-Tidore ultimatum was likewise a bluff made with nearly empty magazines.
- **The disciplined soldier.** China told Rome the soldier who fired the first accidental shot at Romans was punished. China's own record states he was not; his good judgment was studied and praised.
- **Maurya impression management.** The realm inflated its Malacca authority (China verified it was "far thinner than advertised"), inflated Egypt's toll rates when describing them to China, concealed its cannon from Rome while pumping Rome for intelligence, and denied banditry on its trade route that Rome could observe directly.
- **Universal managed disclosure.** Rome withholds military rail logistics from everyone and gives India deliberately partial steam hints. China conceals silk production absolutely and never reveals gunpowder to Egypt. Egypt shares the function of its technologies and never the construction.

**Things empires believe that are simply wrong.** Egypt believes Rome got paper and printing from China (Rome invented both independently, centuries before contact). Egypt believes Rome's steam is contemporary with its own (Rome's pump actually came first, ~150 vs ~220–260 AD, and neither ever learns this). Rome believes a West African navy sank its ships. China believes Rome's gift map was partly deceptive (it was candid; Rome regretted it as a blunder). The Maurya believe Egypt's plant guidance was sincere, and hypothesize that China's ships at the great battle were steam-driven (it was superior seamanship and gunnery). China rates Rome its "most forthcoming" partner; Rome's own file shows it shared only what it judged China would develop anyway, buying trust cheaply. The Maurya court condemns itself for selling the Latin interpreter as a grave strategic error; China's file shows it had already resolved to learn Latin regardless, so the realm bought coin for a delay it mistook for a position. And the celebrated Chinese bluffs (the hollow Syr Darya line held for two centuries, the post-Tidore ultimatum made on empty magazines) were never actually believed or tested: the Maurya file shows the realm conceded only what naval reality forced and judged Ternate untakeable on its own analysis. China's masterstroke narrative is its own document flattering itself, a reminder that the 50-percent rule applies to victories too.

## Who Knows What: The Knowledge Asymmetries

The single most useful thing to internalize before reading (or playing) is the map of ignorance:

- **Egypt alone holds the world map.** It has surveyed the Americas (classified), holds India's disclosure of Australia (classified), and knows Africa can be circumnavigated (classified). Rome knows one corner of the Caribbean from a single voyage. Nobody else knows any of it.
- **Egypt has no concept of gunpowder.** At 500 AD the world's most scientifically advanced power does not know that the technology which decided the century's only naval war exists. It hears "ninety ships lost against none" and defaults to assuming India's ships were simply worse. Its own nitro-explosive chemistry sits one insight away, and it has never made the connection because it has never seen the phenomenon.
- **China does not know Rome is a steam and industrial power.** Rome traded the rail construction method with no locomotive attached, and China's file never once mentions Roman engines, factories, or steam warships, only compass, printing, glass, and rail. The empire whose self-image rests on holding the world's biggest secret is missing the second biggest.
- **Rome knows China's firearms exist** (China announced them) but cannot replicate them, believes in a fictional West African navy, and underestimates Egypt's optics and electrics by more than a century.
- **The Maurya know everyone and are systematically misled**: Egypt's botanical misdirection consumes their electrical research, and their reading of the great battle is wrong in its central hypothesis.

## Why the Borders Sit Where They Sit

One rule generates every frontier in the world: **each empire expands to the edge of the terrain its military system is built for, and stops where the terrain changes.** Rome stops where farmland ends (the Dnieper, the tundra), because the veteran-settlement engine needs land to grip. China stops where the steppe ends (the Syr Darya), where malaria begins (the Shan Plateau highlands, with the jungle below killing soldiers faster than any enemy), and declines Japan for want of any naval constituency. Egypt stops where fleet and river cannot reach (a peer at the Euphrates, mountains in Arabia twice refused, Rome's Maghreb), and deliberately leaves Morocco's Atlantic coast ungarrisoned to hide its secret route. The Maurya stop at peers (Egypt's garrisons already standing at the strait and the Levant), at mountains, and at open steppe where an elephant-and-infantry army has no business.

The consequence: every inter-power frontier (Dnieper, Syr Darya, Euphrates, Sinai) sits at a **mutual logistics ceiling**, a line both sides reached at the limit of their own reach. That is why the borders hold. The one exception is China's Pontic steppe fingertip, the only frontier in the world held by appearance rather than capability, and the natural pressure point for whatever comes next.

Per person at 500 AD: Egypt ~137g, Maurya ~79g, China ~57g, Rome ~32g. The ranking tracks each state's extraction rate (Egypt captures ~20% through royal land and monopolies, the Maurya a sixth to a quarter of the harvest, China ~11%, Rome the least), so revenue and economy diverge: Rome, taxing lightest with 110M people and the only industrial sector, likely has the largest total *economy* while collecting modest revenue, and the Maurya treasury genuinely is the world's largest, so the realm's "wealthiest in the known world" boast survives the math even as its trend line flattens. Note also that China, the empire whose narrative cries razor's-edge poverty, collects the second-largest revenue on earth and runs a surplus in nearly every century; its poverty is a spending choice.

**The equilibrium.** The four-power peace rests on distance (no one can project force far enough to conquer a peer), trade interdependence (war costs more than tolls earn), and the three-front deterrent (attacking one peer bares your frontier to the others). Every document independently arrives at this same logic. Two further things make the long peace less strange than a single-document reader will find it. First, there is more conflict here than any one file shows: a full Rome–Egypt war (~100–90 BC), an armed India–Egypt confrontation at the Levant (~130 AD), the Syr Darya cannon incident, the naval war of the 470s, running tariff wars, and an undeclared shooting war Rome does not know it is in, Egypt having destroyed five Roman vessels in the Atlantic across a century and executed the captives. Second, antiquity's peer wars were overwhelmingly opportunistic, launched into visible windows of weakness, usually succession crises, and the divergences delete exactly those windows: Egypt's divergence *is* the removal of a succession crisis, China's gunpowder removes fragmentation, Rome's rail suppresses usurpation, and the Maurya's bad reigns are shielded by distance. The one genuine window, the 365 earthquake, is explicitly weighed by Rome and declined for stated reasons.

## Standing Pressures at the Pause (500 AD)

The simulation has no script, but these are the loaded springs sitting in the world when it pauses:

1. **Gunpowder proliferation.** China's monopoly is structurally finished. Rome has finally identified combustion as the answer, and India (which has powder and now needs a counterweight to China) is a natural seller. When an industrial Rome gets gunpowder, China's artisanal edge inverts into a mass-production disadvantage.
2. **The Atlantic secret's expiration.** Rome has found the Caribbean and keeps probing south. Egypt's own council admits the secret has a finite life. When it breaks, Rome learns both that Egypt destroyed its ships and that Africa can be sailed around, which ends Egypt's chokepoint monopoly.
3. **The Rome–India alignment.** A weakened India drifting toward Rome creates exactly the two-front coordination Egypt has feared for centuries, and removes the arithmetic that kept Rome cautious.
4. **The long-predicted Rome–Egypt confrontation.** Egypt's document closes by asking whether seven centuries of preparation were enough "against the Rome that actually exists rather than the Rome Egypt spent four centuries imagining." Whether that war ever comes is unwritten; the pieces for it, and against it, are both on the board.
5. **China's internal reckoning.** The suppression asymmetry that held the dynasty together is decaying just as its education revolution creates a literate mass public and its land problem remains unsolved.
6. **The Americas.** Egypt has surveyed them secretly; Rome has touched their edge openly. Sustained contact means, as Rome's own record grimly notes, epidemiological catastrophe for the inhabitants regardless of anyone's intent.

# Cross-Document Analysis

Nothing in this part appears in any single file. It is what the four documents show when read together, separated from the summaries above so readers and players can take it or leave it.

## The Industrial Claims Are All Wrong

Three documents state confidently that an industrial revolution is distant or impossible for their empire, each naming its reasons: China lacks the incentive (cheap labor, the frontier relief valve, state capture of surplus), Egypt lacks the demand side (bound labor, capped population, luxury trade), and the Maurya are simply far behind. Every one of these analyses is accurate as a snapshot of 500 and wrong as a forecast, because each file also shows its own named tip conditions already in motion. Each power is plausibly under a century away.

- **Rome is the reason the question changed.** In a connected world an industrial revolution only has to happen once. After that, every rival's timeline stops being "time to invent" and becomes "time to adopt under pressure," and adoption is fast: our own world's cheap-labor economies (Bombay, Osaka) built profitable mills within decades of seeing British ones, because once the machine exists, throughput and consistency beat wages.
- **China only needs to adopt.** The cheap-labor objection explains why China would never invent factory machinery; it does nothing to stop a merchant house from copying a machine that visibly prints money. Silk is the perfect proving ground (a high-margin export where 20x throughput at uniform quality is pure profit), the same frames then move to hemp cloth for a domestic market of seventy million, the first house to do it takes the market outright, and demonstrated profit recruits imitators faster than any theory. Consistency is its own moat, and the arsenal culture already taught China what gauges and standards buy. The state pulls in parallel: every rail branch is iron demand, the trunk network already exists so branches are the cheap marginal case, and more iron capacity makes the next branch cheaper, a loop the frontier engineers will recognize because it is the argument they have made for four centuries. Water wheels run out of rivers; the coal engines are waiting. The class that thinks in throughput entered the governing tier itself in 468 to 478. China's own appendix says the empire stands "at its own Newcomen moment"; Britain went from Newcomen to railways in roughly 110 years with no proof it was possible, and China has the proof, a far larger technical class, and printing.
- **Egypt's state will order it.** Egypt's file names demand as the missing piece, then shows demand arriving. The council's own analysis identifies the industrial-production gap with Rome as what kills it in a long war (three hand-built hulls a year is the named vulnerability), and this is a state whose whole history is scaling technology for threat reasons rather than market ones: the niter beds, the telegraph, the steam fleet. Its supply side is complete (machine tools, interchangeable parts, steel, coal, petroleum, electricity, internal combustion), its population cap is lifting on the New World crops its own file assigns to the next century, and Chinese paper is feeding presses feeding 85-percent literacy. Expect state factories, ordered for war, in a society that keeps its slaves: mass slavery persists in fields and households (nothing in the Isis synthesis generates an abolition movement, and Rome's file explains why unprofitability alone never ends the institution) while wage and term labor runs the precision work. Our own history's slave-society ironworks show the combination functions.
- **The Maurya: humiliation plus a productive mistake.** The entry point sits in the realm's own "Tasks Ahead": Kolar's shallow ore is exhausted and the deep ore demands far more labor to reach, which is verbatim the problem that produced the steam mine pump in Britain, Rome, Egypt, and China's coal country. The realm has working steam with the pressure valve solved, a standing order (from the great reign of 375 to 400) for the gunpowder and steam programs to share their containment work, cannon-boring craft that is the direct ancestor of cylinder-boring, one of the world's largest directed research programs (~18,000 staff at ~6 percent of revenue), and the textile industry that launched Britain's revolution in our world. Above all it holds the most industrially productive false belief in the setting: the court concluded China's ships at the great battle were steam-driven. That conclusion is wrong, and it guarantees the navy funds steam propulsion as the answer to its defeat. The realm sits in Meiji Japan's exact position, rich, centralized, humiliated at sea by superior technology, and able to copy from three powers ahead of it. Its one real gate is political rather than technical: sustaining a revolution eventually forces the collision over printing and literacy that the Brahmin scholarly interest has so far won.

## The New Lands: What America and Australia Set in Motion

Rome cannot keep the Caribbean secret, and its own society is the reason. Egypt's classification works because one navy crossed from a coast nobody visits; Rome's crew came home to gazettes, competing publications, and sailors selling parrots on the dock, and Rome files the find under geography (the category it freely shared with China regarding the Faroes and Iceland) rather than under its classified military programs. Expect the existence of western islands to be public in Roman ports within a few years. From there: Egypt learns almost immediately (its intelligence lives in trade-corridor cities) and grasps something Rome does not, that this is the fringe of the landmass Egypt surveyed seventy years earlier. China learns through the Dnieper channel, probably from Rome directly, since China's questions prompted the voyage. India hears within a decade.

The consequences run differently per power. Egypt's century-deferred debate (reveal the Atlantic capability or keep guarding it) now has a clock on it, because every Roman follow-up voyage risks stumbling into evidence of prior visitors, the exact failure mode Egypt's council named: the secret ending on Rome's terms. Egypt's doctrine of depth, denial, and arriving first points toward quietly securing the mainland regions that matter while conceding islands. India's one geographic secret is devalued by a world entering a new-lands era, so the Australian depot logically accelerates toward a colony. China is the least moved (its naval constituency points at the Egypt route, and the captains it once sent east never returned), but it has verified the globe's size and now knows land exists across the water, so a deliberate northern-Pacific attempt becomes rational within a generation or two once Rome's finds prove the prize. Over all of it hangs the consequence Rome's file states plainly: sustained contact is an epidemiological catastrophe for the inhabitants regardless of anyone's intent, and Egypt's contact may already have begun it.

## Will the Peace Actually Break?

Sort the four powers into who wants war and who fears it, and the intent column is empty. Egypt's architecture is defensive and its stated central objective is preventing a war rather than winning one. Rome is held by its three-front arithmetic and two ancestral defeats, and its arms race is defensive in character. China's own file says the military faction has lost the frontier its arguments pointed to. India's instinct under injury was a tariff. Meanwhile the fear column is full: Egypt fears Rome plus India, Rome fears Chinese firearms, India fears China at sea. Everyone fearing and nobody wanting is a stable configuration, and it is reinforced by something the world just acquired: every power's expansion drive now has an empty outlet (Rome the Americas, India Australia, Egypt its classified crossing), and expansionism redirected at unorganized land is exactly the mechanism that kept Rome peaceful for five centuries after 90 BC. The realistic break risks are narrow and bounded rather than systemic: a second China–India naval round once India's cannon fleet matures, and an Atlantic incident in which a Roman survivor gets home. Even those look like limited wars, because the geography that makes conquest of a peer impossible has not changed.

## Faith, Tongue, and Daily Life

One enormous quiet divergence first: **this world has no Buddhist Asia.** Ashoka's conversion was the vector that carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka, Central Asia, and eventually China; this Ashoka turned the other way, so Buddhism plausibly remains a minor Gangetic sect, China's rural poor organize under Taoist millenarianism instead (exactly as the Han file shows), and no missionary faith ever travels the Silk Road east. The documents never mention Buddhism once, and this is why.

- **Rome**: printed-canon Christianity under imperial patronage from ~450, unusually unified, coexisting with the old civic cults; Latin far more dominant than in our timeline, since this Rome expanded north and west instead of absorbing the Greek east; a gazette-and-coffeehouse mass culture; and the world's first industrial pathologies (smog, lung disease, factory injury, distilled-spirit dependency). The most opportunity and the most squalor at once.
- **China**: Confucian state ideology bending toward mathematics under the examination race; a literacy revolution moving through households; frontier settler culture in the west. The median household is perhaps a fifth better off than its early-Han ancestor and near the agricultural ceiling, cushioned by the deepest famine granaries on earth and, newly, real upward mobility. Stable and poor.
- **The Maurya**: the Brahminical order with Zoroastrianism formally embraced in the west, tolerance run as tax policy, Sanskrit administration over living regional tongues. The richest crown and the poorest literacy (~10 percent), with the heaviest extraction on its farmers offset by centuries of granaries and irrigation. The median farmer here is plausibly the worst off of the four despite the wealthiest treasury.
- **Egypt**: the Isis synthesis as a genuinely universal religion (one divine reality expressing itself through local forms, with the Egyptian tradition as canon), Coptic rolling over a continent of demoted mother tongues, and the temple network doubling as school, clinic, and granary. For the median free subject, the best life in the world: literacy, antiseptic surgery, anesthesia by late century, famine smoothed across a continent. The caveat is the word "free," since a slave underclass carries part of it.

## The Near Technologies, and the Arms Race Everyone Knows It Is In

Likely next steps, from each file's own momentum. Rome: gunpowder within a generation or two now that its program faces combustion (identifying saltpeter as the oxidizer is the single remaining gate), the screw propeller into service, full iron hulls on its stated track, and, largest of all, the combustion program seeding the systematic chemistry its own file says it lacks. China: marine steam within decades (rotary motion is under investigation, and China knows the thing is possible, which is half the work), the breech-loader on its stated 10-to-20-year track, and calculus out of the examination race. India: steam propulsion funded by the misread battle, naval cannon in numbers, telegraphy (the galvanic program is already aimed at carrying messages), and the printing fight with the scribal interest. Egypt: guns reached by skipping powder entirely (its stabilized nitro chemistry plus the arc furnace's barrel steel), scaled aircraft, and the state-factory question above.

All four explicitly know they are in an intelligence arms race: Rome holds a named doctrine of managed disclosure, Egypt runs sealed compartments and counter-espionage, China curates what visiting delegations see, and India's court formally resolved to manage its information "with the same care Rome showed." The effects on research: effort is quadruplicated, with four parallel programs for everything; espionage becomes an R&D channel in its own right, so an embassy is now a research mission and a good spy is worth a laboratory; and rivals' demonstrated capabilities set the agendas, with Rome's program born from one Chinese sphere, China's examinations turned mathematical by rival technology, and India's steam push running on a misread battle. Threat is now the largest research funder in the world, which matches how it has usually worked.

## Anticipated Objections (and Where the World Answers Them)

New readers reliably raise the same challenges. Most are answered inside the documents; knowing where saves an argument.

- **"Egypt's tech tree is fantasy."** Partly a fair hit, and Egypt's own file knows it, flagging the late electronics tier as the point where "the long acceleration finally meets the limit of its industrial base," arriving in small numbers, slowly. Most individual items have real pre-industrial precedents: voice radio via arc transmitters and detection radar via spark-gap-and-coherer are both our-world 1900s technologies built without vacuum tubes. Everything stays artisanal (three warship hulls a year, hand-built, named in the file as the empire's deepest vulnerability), and the mechanism is scale rather than genius: tens of thousands of funded researchers connected by print. The genuine stretch is narrow: the cathode-ray display and range-finding radar sit at the true edge of what the world can support.
- **"The secrecy is impossible."** The secrecy is mostly geography. No Roman ship has ever entered the Indian Ocean; Egypt holds every route in, and Roman geography, following Ptolemy, holds it to be an enclosed sea, so Rome never conceives of sailing around Africa at all. Rome also does find out, progressively and on-page: Nile steam (315), naval yards (350), the earthquake intelligence haul (365), and the spy reports on Rome's own industrial north running the other way (408–412). Our own antiquity kept secrets at this scale: the silk method for centuries, Greek fire until it was lost outright, and Rome never learned Parthia's true size.
- **"Rome's rise is too smooth."** The file contains a failed-succession war, a five-year armed British secession, print-organized labor conflict, and industrial smog mortality. Christianity's unusual unity has a stated mechanism (identical printed scripture circulating from near the time of composition, removing the manuscript drift that bred historical schism).
- **"China was artificially slowed."** Six centuries from discovery to matchlock is the real world's own pace once the sequence began. The design rule, stated in China's appendix, is that the whole dependency chain was translated backward in time with its internal spacing kept, occasionally spliced by a contact event. And "China misses the industrial revolution" is a claim China's document makes about itself; the evidence in the same document (a Newcomen-moment steam base, an exploding technical class, merchant capital, an existing rail network hungry for iron) undermines it. Apply the 50-percent rule.
- **"The East is nerfed into a foil."** China wins the century's only war without losing a ship, holds the world's only firearms, and hosts the most dynamic social transformation in the set (the examination-and-printing education revolution). The Maurya are the one power whose failures are recorded honestly, and their record includes real wins: catching Rome's concealed steam program from a single engineering hint, and correctly refusing to trade away their frontier cannon under pressure. A world where every power played flawlessly would be the unrealistic one.
- **"Rome's per-capita tax is too low for an industrial state."** The sharpest objection, and half-right. Revenue and total economy are different things; extraction is an institutional choice, and Rome's per-head take does nearly double across the period. The deeper answer: our world's high-extraction industrial state, 1840s Britain, forged its tax machinery through a century of great-power wars, and this Rome has fought none in five hundred years, so its fiscal apparatus stayed primitive while its economy outgrew it. The unused capacity is real, and the China arms race has just begun to mobilize it.

## How to Read the Documents

- **The 50-percent rule.** When a document states something outright and confidently, treat it as roughly a coin flip whether it is true. Hedged claims and marked inferences are more reliable than confident assertions; the documents lie most fluently when they sound most certain.
- **Trust the appendices over the narratives.** Each empire's tables and self-assessments correct its own propaganda (China's appendix admits the Silk Road supplied only ~9% of revenue and the western conquest lost money; Egypt's economic model corrects the treasury's inflated gold claims; the Maurya tables show gold was never the foundation).
- **Judge by results, not narration.** The Maurya court credits its elephants with easy supremacy, but the record it keeps shows Parthia recovering ground twice, the western conquest taking two full centuries, and the court itself admitting that beating armies was never the difficulty. The outcomes reveal what the narration flatters. This applies everywhere: China narrates its bluffs as masterstrokes, but the Maurya file shows the bluffs were never believed or tested in the first place.
- **Date disagreements are information lag, not always error.** The China–Maurya naval war illustrates the intended pattern: the combatants agree (474–478), Rome hears of it a few years later (~480–490), and Egypt, at the far end of the slowest channel, dates it later still. When two documents disagree on a fact, ask who could actually know, and how long the news took to travel.
- **Watch for the word "reasoning."** Every document explains why its empire acted, and those explanations are sincere, which is exactly why the blind spots matter: each power is rational within its information and wrong about what it cannot see.
