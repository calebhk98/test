# Rome: Court Profile and Standing Assessment, 525 AD

Prepared for internal use. Sources are the consolidated history to 500 AD and the record of 500 to 525 AD. No other power's records were consulted, and this document does not pretend to know what those records contain.

---

## 1. The Other Three Powers: Trust and Danger

Ratings run 1 to 10. Trust means how far the court expects a stated position to hold and a stated fact to be true. Danger means how much harm the power could do to Rome if it chose to.

### Egypt

**Trust: 1. Danger: 10.**

Trust is at the floor because Egypt has now been caught in a concealment of the largest possible kind. For six centuries Rome held Egypt to be the Nile, Cyprus, the Levant, and a toll at Bab-el-Mandeb, and the southern survey of this turn found instead three thousand miles of planned stone cities, dozens of fitted harbours, a coastal signal net, foundries at work, subject populations beyond counting, and the largest fleet on the coast standing at the Cape. Worse than the size is the specific lie: Egypt spent a hundred years telling Rome that five Roman ships were destroyed off West Africa by reef, current, and canoe-peoples, and gave that account in the formal diplomatic channel Rome had opened in good faith. Rome cannot now treat any Egyptian statement as information rather than as an instrument.

Danger is at the ceiling because Egypt is the only power that is both adjacent to Rome and capable of ruining a Roman undertaking this generation. Its ships are beyond Rome's counting against Rome's twenty to thirty steam warships, it holds a propulsion method Rome has been trying to copy for fifty years and a communication method Rome has never identified, it knew of the Americas within months, and its yards went to a tenfold building surge that Rome has logged and cannot explain. The one theatre where Rome could contest Egypt at all is the New World, and that is exactly where Rome has just committed hundreds of thousands of colonists.

### The Han Empire (China)

**Trust: 6. Danger: 8.**

Trust is the highest of the three and it is earned by two centuries of conduct rather than by goodwill. China explained the weapon incident of 290 and said it had disciplined the soldier, honoured the Dnieper boundary without a military crossing since the agreement was made, published a silk price at the frontier that held, dealt through an auto-renewing treaty it has never moved to break, and volunteered the warning about its individual firearms when it had no obligation to and every military reason not to. It also asked four times for what Rome knew of the west, openly, instead of stealing it, and then explained why it had asked, having measured the sphere and reasoned the western land into existence before Rome sailed to it. Rome does not confuse this with friendship, since China withholds its weapons method exactly as Rome withholds its own, but a partner whose declared positions have held for two hundred years is the closest thing to trust the court has.

Danger is high and not at the ceiling. China can destroy a Roman army in the field, and the court accepts that: the shield wall assumes the enemy must be at arm's length to do harm, and individual firearms defeat that assumption before the formation moves. Against this stand three facts the Senate weighs. China has never crossed the river in two hundred years, the ground between is steppe that suits neither Roman farming nor Roman tactics, and India is about to hand Rome the complete firearm account for free, which begins to close the gap without a war. The Han rail now reaching the Syr Darya and the civilian towns thickening toward the far bank are the things that could change this rating, and the frontier watch has been redirected to count exactly that.

### The Maurya Empire (India)

**Trust: 2. Danger: 4.**

Trust is only just above Egypt's, and for a smaller kind of dishonesty repeated many times. India reported that the Mesopotamian route had no banditry while Roman merchants were being robbed on it, held land cannon on the Syr Darya frontier and never disclosed it while pressing Rome four times for what Rome knew of Chinese firearms, raised the transit tariff from twelve to twenty-four per cent without consulting a partner whose merchants paid it, professed not to understand steam engines while deducing correctly that Rome had them, and produced a claim of independently discovering a force running through metal at a moment that fell suspiciously soon after Egypt had shown it something. The pattern is a partner managing Rome's impression of it rather than a partner lying about one thing. Rome nonetheless intends to deal, because the swap on the table is worth more than the grievance.

Danger is the lowest of the three, and low in absolute terms. India lost ninety warships in one engagement for no loss to the enemy and has no navy to threaten Rome with. It shares with Rome a trade boundary at the Euphrates rather than a military frontier, no Indian army has ever moved against Roman ground, and the harm it can do is commercial and informational: a tariff, a false route report, an account of Chinese weapons padded to send Roman engineers down a wrong path for a generation. It holds cannon and it is about to hold Rome's hull engine, so the rating will need revisiting in twenty-five years, and Rome's engineers going openly into Indian yards are the means by which it will be revisited.

## 2. The Single Most Important Thing

That the empire's administration continues without a break, because everything Rome has that its rivals do not is the compound product of five uninterrupted centuries of records, rails, colonies, and standard specifications, and an interruption would cost more than any prize could pay back.

---

## 3. The Eight Concerns, Ranked as the Court Actually Ranks Them

1. **Defence of the core territory.** The military and frontier charge is 2,520 million of 4,000 million in revenue, sixty-three per cent, and it has been between sixty and sixty-eight per cent in every century on record. Nothing else in the accounts comes close, and no argument about any other priority has ever moved that line down.
2. **Military or technical advantage over rivals.** The entire industrial dividend of the last century has gone into the arms race, so that the free margin shrank in absolute terms at 500 AD while revenue stood at its highest ever. The gunpowder programme has run for two hundred and twenty-five years without producing a weapon and has not been cancelled, which is the clearest evidence in the accounts of where the court's real second priority sits.
3. **Revenue and the treasury.** Every decision in the record is gated on money, the reserves are a few months of revenue at most, there is no bond market to borrow in, and the state's emergency answers are the mint, confiscation, and special levies. The court attends to revenue constantly because it has no cushion and knows it.
4. **Internal order and the suppression of revolt.** Britain took four to five years to recover, the labour risings of the fourth century overlapped a usurpation, and print now carries a grievance across the empire faster than a provincial administration can answer it. The rail network was valued from the start as much for moving legions against a usurper as for moving coal.
5. **Survival of the dynasty or regime.** The emperor puts this first and the Senate does not, and the composite behaviour of the state is what is being ranked here. Rome has lost emperors to succession wars, to a Rhine general backed by industrial money, and to a British usurper, and the Dnieper frontier was untouched through all of it. The office survives its holders, which the holders resent and cannot change.
6. **Trade routes and access.** The money is real, seven hundred and twenty million in trade and customs and two hundred and eighty in luxury duties, and the grievance against Egypt's thirty per cent is six centuries old. Rome nonetheless paid that toll for six centuries, built the Maurya route as a partial answer, and accepted a doubled Indian tariff without retaliation, which places access below the four concerns above it.
7. **Standing and prestige among the other powers.** See below.
8. **Religious and ideological legitimacy.** See below.

**The two the court would claim to rank highly and does not.**

The first is standing and prestige. Rome speaks of itself as the first power of the world, trumpets the western crossing loudly and at length, and wanted an answer for China chiefly because it was embarrassed to have none after being asked four times. In practice it has absorbed every available humiliation without acting on any of them: an invisible Egyptian toll on Roman consumption for generations, a doubled tariff imposed by a partner without consultation, five ships destroyed off a coast Rome then agreed to treat as closed, and an Egyptian ship shadowing a Roman survey for three thousand miles while Rome's captains held their distance and turned for home. A state that ranked prestige highly would have fought over at least one of those.

The second is religious and ideological legitimacy. The court presents the Christian settlement of 440 to 450 as the empire's shared soul. The record shows it was adopted because an epidemic proved the civic cult had nothing to offer a sick factory worker's family and because a weak succession needed legitimacy, that the patriarchate was placed in Rome for the stated reason that Rome controls that appointment and Alexandria does not, and that the civic religion and the Senate's old prerogatives were left standing untouched. It is treated as an administrative instrument that works, which is not how the court describes it in public.

## 4. How This Rome Differs from the Rome of Our Record

The borders are the visible difference and the least interesting one. Three changes to the institution's character follow from being blocked in the east, turning north, and never collapsing.

**Rome learned that force has a ceiling, and it learned it early enough to build institutions around the lesson.** The historical Rome answered a lost army with another army and eventually got what it wanted; that habit is why it fought Persia for centuries without ever concluding it should stop. This Rome failed in the Delta around 95 BC and lost Crassus at Carrhae in 53 BC, two failures against two different opponents defeating two different Roman strengths, and it drew a general conclusion instead of a tactical one. The result is a state whose standard response to an unsolved problem is a survey, an intelligence operation, or a filed unknown, rather than a campaign. The southern survey of this turn was three ships with orders not to close the shore. The Yucatan foothold is a garrison whose true purpose is to measure the interior before committing to it. The historical Rome would have marched inland in the first season.

**Rome never absorbed a theoretical tradition, so its whole intelligence is in its hands and its files.** The historical Rome took the Greek east and with it Greek philosophy, mathematics, and the habit of asking why a thing is so. This Rome is overwhelmingly Latin, met Greek learning only as an import through Alexandria, and got its practical arithmetic from India as a convenience for ledgers. What it built instead is the largest body of applied craft knowledge in the world: four hundred thousand men in the technical workforce, compound engines of four hundred horsepower arrived at by trial with no thermodynamics behind them, standardised parts, and printed manuals carrying a working fix from one province to every other within weeks. The cost is exact and the court now sees it. Rome spent a hundred and eighty years assuming the Han weapon must be a pressure device, because pressure was the thing Rome had mastered, and the assumption held until a fact broke it rather than until a theory corrected it. Rome has no account of why its own machines work, which limits it to improvements it can reach by trial, and at least two of its rivals appear to hold things that trial alone will not reach.

**Rome has never been interrupted, so it has no idea how to operate without its accumulated apparatus, and it treats interruption as the worst thing that can happen.** There was no fall, no rebuilt west, no second capital, no Greek-speaking rival court, and therefore one language that never fragmented into daughter tongues, one body of law distributed in print, one rail gauge from the Irish sea to the Dnieper, and an archive that runs unbroken from the Republic. Institutional memory here is documentary rather than remembered. The Delta and Carrhae are consulted as filed reports, the error of handing the Han a complete infrastructure map in 300 AD is written down as an error and cited as doctrine, and this turn's two-hundred-ship count off Africa is logged as unresolved rather than as an armada precisely because the file says what happens when a telescope sighting is treated as a conclusion. The practical effect is that this court reasons from its archive where another court would reason from principle or from the character of its enemy, and that it will not put the archive and the apparatus at risk in a war it has not first measured.

## 5. The Voice of the Documents, and the Commitment

**What the two files do.** They are state papers rather than chronicles. The tense is the present, used throughout for past events, so that a decision of 365 AD and a decision of this turn read the same way. The person is the third, and the grammatical subject is almost always the institution: Rome resolves, Rome accepts, Rome logs, Rome reassesses, the Senate debates, the programme continues. Sections carry plain functional headings. Explanation runs from a physical constraint forward to the decision, so that water in the Sinai explains the failed invasion, cheap coal at the pithead explains the mine pump, the alphabet of twenty-three letters explains movable type, a coal range of six hundred nautical miles explains why the Atlantic crossing had to be made under sail, and a gauge explains why the Han rail will stop at the far bank of the river.

**What they record obsessively.** Numbers with units and honest ranges: population, revenue in sesterces broken out by source and charge, the size of the technical workforce, literacy by district, ship lengths in metres, engine power in horsepower, knots under steam and under sail, tons of coal, nautical miles of range, per cent of transit tariff, counts of tusks and ingots and modii. Dates as spans rather than points. Above all the provenance and status of every claim: what Roman eyes saw, what Rome infers from it, what Rome believes and cannot check, what Rome has decided to hold open. The files mark their own errors and keep them on the record. They also record what Rome decided not to do, and why, at the same length as what it did.

**What they barely mention.** Named individuals almost vanish after the Republic, and no emperor of the last two centuries is named at all. There is no rhetoric, no appeal to destiny, no description of a ceremony, no art, no literature, no architecture except as engineering, no interior life of anyone, and no moral judgement offered as a reason. Labour law is explained by productivity, the Christian settlement by legitimacy and epidemic response, and the restraint at the Maya city by the unknown extent of the interior rather than by any principle. The one place the writing loosens is in the sailors' report of the first crossing, and even there the strange details are entered because they are data.

**Correction to an earlier reading.** The 500 to 525 record was first described here as a present-tense staff paper. That was wrong, and the error came from reading the consolidated history's habits back onto the turn. The turn is a past-tense analytic narrative, reconstructed after the fact by men accounting for what was done and why, opening with the fleet already home and reasoning in continuous prose under titles that state what the section argues.

**Commitment.** Past tense throughout, events in order and accounted for afterwards. Descriptive section titles that carry the argument, in the manner of "Coal, and the Reassessment of the Northern Coast." The reasoning shown in long paragraphs rather than in flat findings, with the prior decision cited by date and the new conclusion drawn from it in view of the reader. Roman offices as actors where an office acted, the *magister classis*, the *curatores*, the *praefectus classis Britannicae*, with no man's minute quoted for effect and no private objection used as a device. Provenance and confidence attached to every claim, and physical quantities given wherever a decision turned on one. Where the reasoning was unsound it will be set out at its full length anyway, argued as the court argued it, because a hundred and eighty years on pressure vessels was a correct inference from a false premise and the record is worth nothing if it hides that.

### Three things that would identify the document as Rome's without the name on it

0. **It argues its way forward in the past tense, and it argues at length even where it turned out to be wrong.** A Roman section began from a decision already taken, cited the earlier decision and its date, and then worked to the new conclusion in front of the reader, so that an unsound step stayed visible instead of being tidied out. No other court's paper needs to preserve its own bad inferences, and Rome's does, because practice ran centuries ahead of theory and the working is the only thing that ever caught an error.

1. **Every claim carries its provenance and its confidence, in the sentence itself.** Rome's paperwork distinguishes what its own ships and observers saw, what its agents report at second hand, what a rival told it, and what Rome has inferred, and it says which is which as a matter of routine rather than for effect. Findings are filed under headings the other courts have no reason to use: settled, reliable in its core and loose in its interpretation, unresolved, quarantined, and reclassified from an exploration problem into an intelligence one. Rome will also write down the assumptions it has just discovered were wrong, name how long it held them, and say what they cost.

2. **Physical quantities decide arguments, and the quantities are always given.** A Roman page reaches for coal tonnage, engine horsepower, nautical miles of range, gauge in a single figure, per cent of revenue, and count of hulls, and the decision then follows from those numbers rather than from a judgement about anybody's intentions. Where a number is not known it is given as a range with the reason for the uncertainty attached. Where a rival's number cannot be checked, the document says it cannot be checked and proceeds anyway.

3. **Prior failures are cited by date as operative doctrine, not recalled as history.** The Delta, Carrhae, the map handed to the Han in 300, the five ships lost off West Africa, and a hundred and eighty years spent on pressure vessels are working precedents that appear in the reasoning for present decisions, in the same tone as a schedule or a specification. Attached to this is a habit no other court is likely to share: Rome writes down at the same length the thing it prepared and did not send, the ground it could have taken and deferred, and the conclusion it declined to draw.
