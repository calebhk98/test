# Turn 525–550 AD

Working paper of the strategic council. Alexandria.

Egypt's own figures are single values, taken from the navy list, the payroll, the manifests, the appropriations, the receipts and the works returns. Confidence classes are written into the sentence for estimates of foreign powers only: **firm**, **reported**, **rumor**, **assumed**. Any figure concerning a foreign power is a floor.

Dispositions are on a ten-point scale of trust, 1 meaning no reliance is placed on any undertaking that power gives and every statement of its is checked, 10 meaning reliance without verification. Egypt holds Rome at 1, India at 2 and China at 4, and 4 is the warmest figure in the register.

Standing figures at open: revenue 142,000 talents, reserve 263,000 talents, two-year reserve target with a floor at one year, population 26.9 million.

---

## Item 1. The reserve target is recomputed and the two-year rule is kept

Two bases are carried. Gross receipts include an imputed value for the copper Item 2 reclassifies to internal consumption. Reported revenue excludes it.

Gross receipts at 550 are 167,000 talents against 142,000 at open, built from seven streams. The agrarian and administrative base rises 16 percent to 95,500, tracking a population up 15.6 percent. This stream is the land tax together with the state monopolies on oil, salt, papyrus, textiles, beer and the rest, and it is the monopolies that carry most of the rise, since they scale with what the people consume rather than with Nile acreage. The land tax component grows little, the Delta being at its ceiling. The Red Sea and canal tolls rise 4 percent to 20,700, the overland competition for the light high-value trade continuing to hold canal earnings near level. Gold rises 2 percent to 14,500, output being held deliberately moderate. Copper stands at 15,600, of which 9,000 is consumed by the state and 6,600 sold. Ivory, timber, coffee and port customs rise 5 percent to 10,400. The western holdings yield 9,400: Plata wheat, hides and wine 2,400; Pacific copper 1,900; Pacific nitrate 1,800; the highland silver purchase margin of Item 8 1,400; tropical timber, latex and dyewood 1,300; southern coast wool and hides 600. Aluminum, plated goods and precision instruments sold abroad at monopoly rates yield 900. Reported revenue at 550 is 158,000.

The reserve target is computed on the gross basis. At open the target is 284,000 against a reserve of 263,000, so the reserve begins the turn 21,000 short, the target having risen with revenue since it was last met. At 167,000 the target is 334,000. Top-up is set at 2,840 talents a year and the reserve stands at 334,000 at 550.

The treasury proposed fixing the target as a sum, which holds it at 264,000. Refused. The sum required scales with the state.

Accepted cost: the top-up is drawn from works and colonial construction. Provincial harbour and road works in the older African provinces run three years behind schedule at 550.

---

## Item 2. The Copperbelt is reclassified from a revenue stream to a strategic input

Output rises and cost per unit of metal rises with it, the richest veins being worked out. Crown consumption has outgrown every other material demand, because wire is the bottleneck under telegraph, telephone, generators, motors and radio, and the drawing mills at the Copperbelt and the falls take their feedstock from the same ore.

The portion of Copperbelt copper the crown consumes is reclassified as a strategic input. Of a stream worth 15,600 talents a year, 9,000 is drawn by the wire mills, the arsenal and the electrical works and leaves the reported revenue figure while remaining in the state's means; 6,600 is still sold. Copper's share of reported revenue falls from 11 percent to 4 percent while the metal leaving the ground rises. The Pacific copper in Item 10 bears directly on this, arriving as the African veins thin.

The treasury's receipts side opposed, on the ground that the reserve target is computed on revenue and that removing a stream lowers the target. Resolved by computing the target on gross receipts including the imputed copper, as Item 1 sets out. The mine directorate's receipts office is folded under the arsenal's materials account.

Standing instruction: comparisons of Egyptian against foreign revenue use the reported figure of 158,000, which is money actually received. The gross figure of 167,000 is for internal budgeting and the reserve target alone, an imputed value being no part of what a rival's receipts would be measured against.

## Item 3. One note is sent to Rome concerning the Atlantic coast of Africa

Three sailing vessels flying Roman colours passed down the Atlantic coast of Africa in about 524, were shadowed from the Saharan works to the Cape with handoffs by radio, kept well off shore, used a balloon for elevated observation, and turned north (firm, continuous Egyptian observation). Egyptian ships held fire.

The note is sent because the presence is now open. Egyptian garrisons, works, ships and balloons on that coast lay within view of vessels that reached home (assumed). An armed presence that has never been stated is one a future encounter can read as an ambush, and the standing fear is a captain firing on an unwarned vessel and beginning the war over a coast.

The note states that the coast is under Egyptian administration, asks for advance notice of passages, and asks that vessels with no commercial business keep to open water. Standing orders continue: shadow, fire only if fired upon, issue no threat that will not be enforced.

The note carries two deliberate falsehoods and the council enters both.

The first is that the administration is lately come. Egypt has held that coast for generations. The second is the implication that other powers had some hand in how it came about; no other power had any part in it whatever, and the sentence is built so that Rome cannot tell whether it refuses a real arrangement or an empty one.

What each buys. The recency claim offers Rome an account of the garrisons that does not require seventy years of concealment, which is the reading Egypt least wants Rome to reach. The hint of other parties gives Rome a direction to spend effort in that leads away from Alexandria, and costs Egypt nothing to maintain because there is nothing there to find. Neither is dated, extended or numbered, so the letter gives Rome no figure to test even where it gives him something to disbelieve.

The council records that Rome may well disbelieve the first. Rome has its own file: five armed vessels lost on that coast across seventy years, and seventy years of Egyptian assurance through the formal channel that Egypt kept no naval presence there. Set against that, a claim of recent arrival is the kind of thing a careful clerk in Rome may reject. The council sends it anyway, and the reasoning is that the note's operative purpose is the standing order at the foot of this item, which works whether or not Rome believes a word of the timing: a Roman captain who has been told the coast is administered and asked to give notice is a captain an Egyptian squadron is less likely to fire on by mistake. A disbelieved lie leaves Egypt roughly where it already stands with Rome. A believed one buys a cheaper account of the coast than the true one. This is the same instrument that told India the electrical force runs through plants and ran twenty-five years, and that has sold an unsproutable coffee bean under the wrong country's name since the first century, and the council is content to lose some of them.

Both claims are entered as standing accounts under Item 4, with the ten-year expiry review and the written successor position that Item 4 requires. The internal record is that the coast has been Egyptian for generations and that no other power had any part in it, and no Egyptian paper is to adopt the letter's account.

The case Egypt survives is a sequential single front against Rome, through terrain and depth. The case Egypt does not survive is a coordinated two-front war against Rome and India at once. Preventing that coordination is the central diplomatic objective of the state, and the mechanism by which coordination forms is a grievance one power can name to another.

The council's read, assumed at best, being a judgement about a foreign court formed from no observation of it: the note and the western advance together raise the risk of coordination. The note converts part of an unaccountable grievance into an ordinary one, which cuts the other way, five vessels lost with no wreckage being a charge no channel can settle while a claim to a coast can be argued in writing for decades. The Caribbean advance in Item 6 is the larger movement, and it places Egyptian garrisons across the approach where the two spheres meet. The council makes both decisions with that read on the paper.

Standing instruction: the confrontation planners work to the case rather than to a date. The estimate last revised in about 408 to 412 is a century stale, and external intelligence is commercial and sees what merchants see in ports and corridor cities.

Accepted cost: refusing to discuss routes concedes that a subject exists, and a stated claim can be disputed where an ambiguity cannot.

Entered after the fact, the returns at Item 22 having reached the council at the close of the period and after the note had gone. Rome had already abandoned the reef-and-current account, already held that the coast has Egyptian masters, and already held that the account it believed for a century was given deliberately and was false. The recency claim was therefore sent to a court that was certain of the opposite and certain that Egypt manufactures accounts through this channel. It cannot have been believed and it will have been read as one more of the same. The claim is entered as failed under the Item 4 rule, and the successor position that rule requires is adopted now: Egypt says nothing further about when or how that coast came under it, to Rome or to anyone, and does not repeat the claim. The review board created at Item 4 approved the recency claim and was wrong to. The operative part of the note, the request for notice and the standing order behind it, is unaffected and stands.

## Item 4. Standing false accounts are restricted to matters a rival cannot visit

For roughly seventy years Egypt maintained an account, given to Rome through the formal channel, that the Atlantic face of Africa was held by capably hostile coastal peoples and that Egypt kept no naval presence there (firm, own file). In about 524 vessels flying Roman colours transited that coast with Egyptian garrisons and works within view (firm as to the transit, assumed as to what was seen).

The account failed because it described ground a rival could go and look at, and the decision on when to look lay with the rival. Doctrine follows, and it is narrower than a general rule against lying. What is barred is a standing account about the observable disposition of a coast, a harbour, a fleet or a garrison, because a rival settles those by sailing to them at a time of his choosing. On that class the instrument is refusal to discuss. What is not barred is an account of how a thing came about, when, or with whom, which a rival can weigh against his own papers but cannot settle by looking. Those may be told, some of them will fail, and each is entered with an expiry review and a written successor position so that its failure is planned rather than discovered. The coffee-origin account and the electrical misdirection continue on that basis. Every retained account carries a ten-year expiry review and a written successor position, so that its failure is planned rather than discovered.

The intelligence directorate opposed, arguing that the Atlantic account bought seventy years that refusal would not have bought. That is true and the practice is restricted anyway, because the same account then cost the state a grievance it cannot remove, at a moment it did not choose. The directorate keeps the two accounts it argued for. The section that drafted and maintained the Atlantic account loses supervision of standing accounts, which passes to a review board sitting with the council.

## Item 5. The Enterprise is funded on the Madagascar instrument

The instrument that filled Madagascar is applied whole to the western holdings: land grants at survey, exemption from the land tax for fifteen years, subsidised passage for settler families with livestock and tools, and crown-built harbour works, roads and cisterns raised in advance of arrival.

Transport tonnage is funded to match. The transport and river yards deliver 60 hulls a year against losses and condemnations running at 44, so the transport fleet grows from 240 hulls at open to 640 at 550. 340 of them are in the western passage trade; the rest work the African coast, the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean. 540,000 persons are landed across the turn. 135,000 are lost to death and return passage. Natural increase adds 87,000. Settled population at 550, garrisons excluded, is 602,000 against 110,000 at open. Garrison strength across the western holdings is 52,000.

Distribution follows survivability and volunteering.

The temperate south at the Plata holds 250,000. The estuary is a hundred and thirty miles of brown fresh water opening east, with the two principal harbours facing each other across it: one on the rocky point on the north shore, one on the flat mud shore opposite where the anchorage is poor and the hinterland is the best in the west. Behind it runs level treeless grass to the horizon on deep black soil, no timber anywhere, wheat and cattle taking to it immediately and horses running wild and multiplying. Nineteen towns: the two estuary harbours, and the rest strung up the two rivers feeding it, the Paraná and the Uruguay, as far as the confluence with the Paraguay some 900 miles inland.

The tropical Atlantic face from the Guianas through Brazil holds 180,000 across 34 fortified harbours and river posts, settled from the African provinces, whose people hold tropical lowland where Mediterranean bodies fail. The line runs from the Guiana river mouths past the Amazon delta, then south along a coast of reef and mangrove with rain forest behind it: the reef harbour at the eastern corner of the continent, the great enclosed bay of All Saints below it, the São Francisco mouth, and the deep landlocked bay 1,200 miles further south under a granite headland, which is the best harbour on the whole face. Dyewood, hardwood and latex come off it.

The Caribbean continental coast and the island arc hold 96,000. The Pacific valley districts hold 62,000. The Patagonian grazing coast holds 14,000.

Cost of the Enterprise across the turn is 78,000 talents, covering subsidised passage and land grants surveyed and registered across every western holding. Harbour works, roads, cisterns, garrisons and mineral plant are carried in the regional items and are not in this figure. Garrison pay and upkeep for the whole 52,000 runs 2,200 talents a year and is carried on the defence account. The continuing charges named in the regional items are works maintenance, stores, water carriage and administration, and carry no pay; no man's pay appears twice.

Radio reaches the western holdings continuously. Alexandria has broadcast a standard time signal heard at every receiver at once since about 460 to 490, and the West African and Cape shore establishment that carries the Atlantic patrol reaches across to the western shore, the working link being West Africa to the Guiana coast at under two thousand miles. Physical response from the West African depots to the Guiana coast is five days under steam, and from Alexandria three weeks.

## Item 6. The advance north is made slow and the advance south and inland made fast

The carried instruction is followed: the Enterprise is accelerated, an occupied coast beats an empty one, and the founding fear is a rival growing on uncontested ground. Speed is set separately for the two directions, because they run toward different things.

North is toward Rome. Egypt does not know how far off, and has no means of finding out short of sailing until it meets something. That governs the pace. From the Guiana coast the line is worked west and north along the continental shore, harbour by harbour, and each harbour is taken and held before the next is approached. Fortified harbours at the mouths of the Essequibo, the Demerara and the Berbice, at the Orinoco delta, and westward along the dry cactus coast beyond it to the gulf of Maracaibo and on toward the neck of land where the continent narrows. Garrisons are victualled and watered to hold three months cut off from the sea. Nothing is planted where a relieving squadron cannot reach it within a fortnight. Survey runs a full season ahead of settlement. The advance stops at the narrow neck of the continent, which is the westernmost ground taken this turn and is held as shoreline like any other.

The island chain running north from Trinidad is taken with the coast. Each island in that arc stands within sight of the last, so a squadron working up it is never out of sight of land and is doing what a coasting squadron does. Nine islands carry harbour posts, held on the Madagascar logic that an island cannot be attacked from the land and belongs to whoever holds the sea around it. The arc is where the northward advance halts, and no squadron is sent out past the head of it into open sea this turn.

Standing order to the Atlantic patrol command: survey squadrons work north and west of the island arc and north of the narrow neck, standing out from land where the current and the weather suggest ground beyond the horizon, charting what lies in that sea. They report by radio on sighting, enter no harbour showing works, vessels or people in occupation, attempt no contact, and hold position without closing. The purpose is to know what is in that water before a settlement is placed where it can be cut off.

South and inland there is nothing to be careful about, and the pace is set accordingly. Founding runs ahead of administration: good ground is taken and settled and the district organisation follows afterwards, rivers are ascended to the head of navigation before anyone asks what is at the top, and posts are planted wherever the land repays planting. Losses are accepted. The Plata, the Patagonian grazing coast, the Amazon ascent and the Pacific side are all done on that footing, and the settlement office is instructed not to hold a founding back for want of a survey where the only thing beyond the survey is more empty country.

The confrontation planners argued to hold at the Orinoco, on the ground that advancing creates the meeting with Rome early. The settlement office and the Atlantic patrol command carried the advance. The reversal is priced: 46,000 talents in works, harbours and garrison construction across the turn, passage and grants being carried in Item 5, with continuing charges of 700 talents a year for works maintenance, stores and harbour upkeep, the garrison pay being carried in Item 5, and the raised coordination risk entered in Item 3. The planners' position is recorded and their office keeps its standing.

## Item 7. The Amazon is ascended and the highland interior is entered

Method is the Congo playbook, the only method Egypt has for a great river system. Steam river vessels ascend from the mouth, posts stand at the major confluences, and authority is pushed outward from the posts. Founding runs ahead of administration here under Item 6, the country south and inland being empty of any rival.

Held at 550: 1,900 miles of ascent and 28 river posts with the frontage between them. The main stem needs no portage, running deep and unbroken far inland, so the posts stand at the great tributary mouths as they come in, the Xingu, the Tapajós, the Madeira, the Negro where the black water meets the brown, the Purus and the Juruá, and then on to the last reach a steamer can work. The portage railway of 31 miles is on the Madeira, the southern tributary, where a run of rapids blocks the way through to the country beyond, and it is built to the specification used at the Livingstone Falls in about 405 to 413 AD.

The highland interior is entered by three routes: up the westernmost reaches of the Amazon to where the rivers come down off the wall, east from the Pacific valley of Item 10 over the coast range, and up the Paraguay and its western tributaries out of the Plata system. The instruments are the ones the empire already carries: steam river craft, portage rail, wheeled exploration vehicles built to be shipped and set down inland since about 465 to 480 AD, aircraft for route survey over unmapped ground, and three centuries of tropical medicine. Twenty-two posts stand on the eastern flank of the wall at 550, on the cloud-forest slope between the last navigable water and the tree line, with a wagon road and a 40-mile rail link climbing from the navigable head to the highest of them at about 9,000 feet. The highland spine itself is surveyed and not held.

## Item 8. The highland finds

Four things come off the highland survey and the first is worth more than the other three together.

A bark of the highland eastern slopes breaks the intermittent fevers. The medical establishment tested it under the germ practice across 1,100 cases in the Congo, the Great Lakes and the Guiana posts, and it holds. This bears on every tropical province the empire owns and on every one it means to take, malaria having been the standing tax on African garrisons since the first century. Fourteen plantation stations are established in the East African highlands, the Ethiopian highlands and the Great Lakes. The first African harvest comes in 548. What is compartmented is the source and not the medicine: the wild stands on the highland slope and the siting of the plantation stations are held in the highest compartment on the coffee practice, which has sold a roasted bean that cannot sprout since the first century and named the wrong country as its origin ever since. The bark itself, its preparation and its dosage pass to China under Item 19.

A highland root crop yields heavily on cool ground at altitude. Trials run from 534 and provincial cultivation begins in 546 in the Ethiopian and East African highlands, at the Cape and in Lesotho, which are the empire's cool-ground provinces and were poorly served by every crop it held.

Silver runs in the highland ore at a grade the assay office rates above anything in Africa. No mine is opened, the spine being surveyed and not held, and the metal is bought worked and unworked at the approach posts under the trade of Item 9. The margin on that purchase is 1,400 talents a year at 550. The treasury takes it as silver and converts it to gold for the vault, the reserve being held in gold because gold pays distant armies and grain does not. A mine, with its workforce, smelting establishment and capital cost, waits on the conquest decision set for the next turn.

A highland pack animal carries loads at altitude where horses and mules fail. 4,000 head are moved to the Ethiopian highlands and the Great Lakes as transport stock.

## Item 9. The highland polities are logged as organised opposition

The survey met states rather than villages: terraced agriculture on engineered slopes, road systems, worked metal, granaries, and populations concentrated enough to field armies. Population across the highland states is 1.5 to 4 million (reported, survey estimate from terrace acreage and settlement counts, treated as a floor). Fighting strength is put at 20,000 to 60,000 in the field (reported, survey estimate scaled off the granaries and road capacity, treated as a floor).

This is the first organised opposition Egypt has met in the west, every coastal people to date counting its strength in hundreds. Doctrine on capable kingdoms inside the perimeter is settled and was applied to Himyar and Aksum between 150 and 200 AD. Doctrine on timing is also settled, and the Himyar precedent governs: Egypt took the Yemeni coast where the fleet was decisive and entered the mountains behind it only once the army held eighty to ninety years of highland and disease experience, the African-army solution dating from about 50 to 60 and the Rift campaigns running from about 70 to 150 before the highland conquests began about 150.

The council holds the highland approaches and the river and coastal ground below them, and opens trade at the approach posts for silver, bark, the root crop and pack animals, on the coffee practice of buying at the edge rather than occupying the source. The decision on conquest is set for the next turn, against an army with no experience of an organised opponent in the west and a supply line 4,000 miles from the nearest African yard.

Accepted cost: an organised power sits above Egypt's newest holdings while the council trades with it and builds the case for taking it.

## Item 10. The passage at the foot of the continent and the coast beyond it

Egyptian ships have rounded a stormy continental foot since the Cape in about 90 to 100 AD, and the method for the second one is the method for the first: stand well out, fix latitude from the stars, let the current carry, and hold the depots close behind.

The grazing coast comes first, being the near side of the same movement. South of the Plata the land dries out into a wind-scoured plain of low grey scrub and coarse grass, treeless, almost rainless, cut by a few rivers running east off the distant mountains. It carries almost no people. It is poor country for grain and good country for sheep, which is what it gets. Five harbour stations stand on it: at the wide shallow bay six days' sail south of the Plata where the grassland gives out, at the deep double gulf behind the whale peninsula, and at the mouths of the Deseado, the Santa Cruz and the Gallegos, each with its inland runs pushed forty to eighty miles up the river valley. 14,000 settlers, mostly from the Cape and the Highveld where the country and the work are the same. Wool and hides yield 600 talents a year. Cost 3,400 talents.

The passage lies 1,300 sea miles south of the Plata, six days under steam, and runs between the mainland and a large cold island beyond it. It is a strait rather than an open rounding, sheltered water for most of its length with two narrows and a bad westerly wind funnelling through it. The coaling and provisioning station stands mid-strait on the north shore at the second narrows, where the channel is a mile and a half across, the water there mined and covered from both shores. Heliograph and radio link it north to the Gallegos and thence to the Plata.

North out of the passage the coast is drowned and useless for 900 miles: a maze of channels, rain forest to the waterline, cliffs, glaciers coming down into the sea, rain most days of the year, and nobody living there worth the name. Egypt takes fuel stations at two sheltered channel mouths and nothing else.

Then the country changes altogether. Between the coast range and the great mountain wall inland lies a long enclosed valley of the same climate as Cyrenaica: winter rain, dry summer, snow-fed rivers coming down off the wall to water it. Wheat, grapes and olives take without coaxing. Four settled districts are laid out in that valley, on the rivers watering it, with harbours serving them at a bay open to the north 1,400 miles up from the passage, at the mouth of the Maipo, and at the mouth of the Bío-Bío where the valley meets the sea in the south. 62,000 settlers, Mediterranean and Alexandrian families, on the Item 5 instrument. This is the second core-population ground the west has offered and the council rates it with the Plata. Cost 9,800 talents, and 1,100 a year in administration, water carriage and works upkeep, the garrison pay being carried in Item 5, since every post on the dry northern run has its water shipped to it.

North of the valley the rain stops. Seven hundred miles up from the northern harbours the coast becomes the driest ground Egyptian officers have ever walked, a rainless shelf between the sea and a high desert plain, where water is shipped in to every post. It is worth holding for two things. Copper runs in the coastal ranges at grades above the Copperbelt's remaining veins, which matters exactly as the African veins thin under Item 2, and extraction begins at three sites, yielding 1,900 talents a year at 550. Nitrate lies in surface beds on that high dry plain inland, in quantity no maintained bed establishment can approach, assayed by the arsenal's officers in 541, and yields 1,800 talents a year at 550. Nitrogen has been the ceiling on every Egyptian explosive since the first nitration, and this is the first supply the state has found that does not have to be manufactured. Twelve harbour posts hold the whole run from the passage to the tropic.

The difficulty is the haul. Alexandria to the nitrate coast is 10,700 sea miles round the foot of the continent, which is six weeks under steam and puts a hard ceiling on what can be lifted in a season. Both workings are begun at the rate the present shipping supports rather than at the rate the ground would bear.

Held on this side is the shore, its harbours, the four valley districts and the mineral ground worked from them. Surveyed and not held is the coast north of the tropic, and the mountain wall inland of the valley, which rises to the same highland spine entered from the east in Item 7.

Standing instruction: any works, vessel, party or wreck not indigenous, encountered by patrol or survey on this coast or at the passage, is signalled to the council by radio on sighting, described as observed, and the station holds its position without closing.

## Item 11. The southern landmass survey reports, and the coast is held throughout

The expedition was funded on a judgement now shown to be wrong. The standing position, entered about 465 and after the coast was disclosed to Egypt, was that India had known it longer and had very likely set itself on it. The council set that aside on the destruction of India's eastern navy between 488 and 497 and the passing of the Malacca route under Chinese protection (firm, own file), reasoning that a power without an eastern fleet is less able to hold ground at that distance. Nine hulls sailed from Madagascar and the Cape at a cost of 1,400 talents, carrying no settlers, under orders to chart from seaward, to enter no harbour showing works, vessels or people in occupation, to attempt no contact, to signal by radio on sighting anything not indigenous and hold position without closing, and to withdraw rather than press a landing against opposition of any kind. Those orders were kept across twenty-one years, and they governed the whole survey, because there was almost nowhere the squadron could have gone ashore without breaking them. There was one landing, entered below.

The survey. Landfall in 528 at about thirty-four degrees south on the western face. Charted thereafter: that coast north to where it turns eastward, and the southern coast eastward for a long run. The squadron turned back short of both ends, so the extent of the landmass beyond those two points is unknown and no estimate is entered.

What the charting was done past. The coast is worked and occupied essentially wherever an Egyptian hull closed with it, on the western face, along the south, and east to the limit of survey alike. Not fewer than thirty-one separate works were counted along the surveyed run (reported, squadron returns, entered as a floor): walled positions of masonry and rammed earth with cistern-head, cleared approach and a beaten track inland; smaller unwalled stations between them; harbours with wharves and sheds; and behind several of them cultivated ground in regular holdings, fenced and under crop, which is settled country and not a post line. One harbour at about twenty-one degrees south carries a working yard, wharves, sheds, timber seasoning, and not fewer than nine hulls alongside and building. The works stand about thirty leagues apart where seen at all, and the council directs that a wider gap be read as a work the squadron did not see rather than as an absence. Water was taken at four places only, all of them stretches where nothing in occupation was in sight, and the squadron went short between them.

The landing. In 534 a hull struck a reef on the southern coast and was beached to be repaired, on a stretch showing no works, no vessels and no people, which is why the beaching was within orders rather than against them. The party lay there eleven days. It left behind a stone-lined hearth, cut timber, iron scale and offcuts of cut plating, all of it the ordinary refuse of a repair and none of it recoverable once the ship floated. Before sailing the party raised a cairn and set in it a squared timber cut with Egyptian characters, which is what Egyptian survey parties do on any coast to fix a position for the next ship. The council enters two things about it. The cairn was set as a navigational mark and not as a claim to ground, and Egypt asserts no claim there. And Egypt does not know whether it has been found, whether the refuse of the repair has been found, or what either would be read as by a court that holds that coast and reads a squared timber cut with foreign characters standing above a beach.

That single landing is why no Egyptian paper and no Egyptian letter states that nothing was put ashore on that coast.

What the works did. On eleven occasions, where an Egyptian hull lay in sight of a work more than a day, the same sequence followed: a standard raised, armed men brought out and drawn up on the open beach in view, and fires lit along the shore at intervals after dark. Counts were taken from deck and from balloon by three observers and are entered as floors: not fewer than 140 at the largest, 120 at a second walled work, and between 35 and 60 at the smaller ones. Cloth dress, arms at the shoulder, and horses at several in sections of about twenty. No boat came off, no hail was made, nothing was fired.

The council enters a caution against its own figures. The display followed an Egyptian hull lying still nearby on each occasion, and its plain effect is to have a passing ship count a garrison. A number a man is shown is not a number he has found, and whether the establishment is larger or smaller than the count offered cannot be told from a beach. The counts stand as floors and are not to be treated as measurements.

Small craft, from 536. Wooden, one and two masted, of a build not indigenous, working that coast between the works in ones and twos, some thirty sightings. They put in at the works, lay off them, and passed between them. On four occasions between 539 and 545 one altered toward the squadron, closed to a mile or two, kept company some hours and bore away without hailing. The council's read: those works are not separate posts but one system, supplied and connected by sea along its own coast, which can be reinforced faster than any land march and cut by whoever holds the water, as Egypt's own African coast has always been.

From 547. Three Egyptian hulls working before a walled work at about twenty-eight degrees south were approached by four vessels from the north: wooden, ship-rigged, with a row of shuttered ports cut low along each side whose purpose the officers could not name, and two of them carrying a funnel and making way with sail and smoke together, holding station in a wind that would have stopped the other two. They placed themselves between the squadron and the shore and stayed. The Egyptian squadron held and did not close, under orders. A boat came to hail, nothing was fired, and a paper was passed across. In 549 the same or a like force did the same at the yard harbour's approaches and the paper was passed again. Four vessels were counted at once across the whole period, from one deck on two days, and the figure is a floor.

On identification, and the council marks this as the correction its own officers volunteered. Egypt did not establish who it was dealing with by reading a seal. Both sides knew inside minutes of the boats coming within hail, and the recognition ran the harder way first. Egypt has held the East African harbours and traded through them for six centuries, and Egyptian hulls, rig, dress and speech are seen there constantly. The squadron was identified as an Egyptian squadron at once, and the paper was not handed to strangers. How that recognition was carried on the other side, and how widely it was held there, is not Egypt's to state and is not entered. Egypt's own recognition ran the same way and as fast, from the build and rig of the vessels and the dress of the boat's crew, and the seal on the paper confirmed a thing already understood rather than disclosing it.

The paper is in the council's hands and may be read, which makes the document itself firm. It is struck at a press and sealed, and is in three tongues, Coptic being none of them: Greek, Latin, and the script of India. Egypt's own speech is Coptic and has been since the language reform of about 208 AD, and Greek and Latin are carried as the tongues of trade and diplomacy, so the Greek was read at sea by a translator carried for that purpose and not by the ship's company. It states that the coast was found, charted, garrisoned, settled and taxed by the realm issuing it; that its harbours are open to any power for trade, water and repair on the same terms; and that no base, no garrison right and no ground will be granted upon it. The seal and style were known to several aboard as the throne at Pataliputra.

The council observes that the operative words of the last clause are Egypt's own rule at Aden and along the East African coast, returned in the same order. Egypt cannot dispute that formula there without weakening the formula it lives by at the Bab-el-Mandeb, where a published rate to all comers and no ground to anyone is what has kept the strait Egyptian without a permanent quarrel.

Three findings are logged permanently for the confrontation planners, each a floor.

First, India holds worked, garrisoned, settled and taxed coast around the whole of that landmass so far as Egypt has seen it, at a reach from its own base comparable to Egypt's reach into the Atlantic, and sustains it by sea. The council had not credited India with that and now must. The proper comparison is not a chain of outposts but Egypt's own western position, and India's is older.

Second, India fields steam at sea. Two of four vessels made way with sail and smoke together and held station in wind that stopped the sail-only pair, which is steam auxiliary and not a demonstration. This is the second power outside Egypt confirmed to have it, and the assumption that a rival's machinery is behind Egypt's is to be carried as a floor in every future estimate.

Third, a fleet destroyed is not a presence removed. The eastern navy went between 488 and 497 and the coast did not go with it, which is the error this expedition was funded on.

The same claim now stands twice. An Indian notice of holdings reached Alexandria in 543 by sea, claiming this coast, and Egypt did not reply. A sealed paper making the same claim was handed to Egyptian officers at sea in 547 and again in 549, backed by works Egypt's own officers counted. The 543 notice could have been a bluff. It is not one, and the council treats the claim as corroborated by its own eyes.

The notice's own text, now entered at INCOMING, carries three things the council did not have when this item was drafted. India dates its finding of the continent to 415 to 420 and the placing of a depot with shipwrights on the northwest coast to about 478, which is before the loss of its eastern navy and not after, so the reasoning this expedition was funded on was wrong twice over rather than once. India claims the great island north of that continent and the cold island south of it, which no Egyptian hull has approached and which stands on India's word alone. And India states in terms that it adopted the open-harbour rule because Egypt applied that rule to India at Aden and along the whole East African coast for five hundred years, which confirms as deliberate what this item had read out of the wording.

One thing in the notice cuts against the reasoning below. India says plainly that no grievance is raised requiring an answer and that none is expected. That weakens the argument that seven unanswered years are a slight, and the council records the weakening rather than dropping the sentence. The reply is sent on the two-front reasoning alone, which is unaffected by whether India expected one.

The reasoning for replying is entered, the council having given India nothing for a generation. It is not a change of disposition. India remains the party that leaks and embellishes, is rated 2 on trust, and the reply carries no fact Egypt would not publish. The reasoning is the two-front premise restated: the case Egypt does not survive is a coordinated war against Rome and India at once, preventing that coordination is the central diplomatic objective of the state, and the mechanism by which coordination forms is a grievance one power can name to another. A formal notice left seven years unanswered, and then a sealed paper handed over twice at sea by an armed squadron that fired nothing and took nothing, is precisely the sort of slight a court carries to the other party. The reply costs Egypt no ground, since it holds none there and proposes none, and it removes the only friction with India the council can currently name.

Decisions. No ground on that coast is entered as held and no settlement of any part of it is proposed. The survey squadron is withdrawn to Madagascar and the standing survey order for that coast is cancelled, the question it was sent to answer having been answered. The Atlantic and Cape commands keep the sighting-and-report order in force for any Indian vessel met in Egyptian waters. A reply is sent to the notice, at MESSAGES OUT. And the priority intelligence tasking for the next turn is set: the depth of the occupation inland, the eastern side of that landmass, and whether the steam seen there is built in India's own yards or bought.

What the report does not answer, and what no Egyptian paper is to fill in by inference: anything of the eastern side of that landmass; the depth of occupation inland beyond a track, a cleared approach and the fenced holdings behind a few of the works; the extent of the land beyond the two points where the squadron turned back; and the numbers, which are shown rather than found. Every figure in this item is a floor.

## Item 12. Contact parties are placed under quarantine discipline

Coastal populations in contact with Egyptian landing parties on the western shore have fallen heavily (firm, repeated observation at multiple sites). The physicians attribute this to crowd diseases carried by settlers reaching a population with no prior exposure (reported, medical office, consistent with the empire's own epidemic records).

Discipline imposed: a holding period before parties enter an unvisited settlement, exclusion of men showing fever or rash from first-contact duty, and separation of survey crews from garrison populations after contact. Reasons entered: a dead informant guides nothing and survey rate on unfamiliar ground rests on local knowledge more than on hulls; a collapsing population beside a garrison is an epidemic that can turn inward; and the medical office requires surviving populations to continue the series.

The discipline matters more now that the highland states of Item 9 are in contact, a collapse there costing Egypt the trade it has just opened and the labour of terraced ground it may later want.

Accepted cost: survey runs a sixth slower under the discipline.

## Item 13. The explosive programme reports and the nitrogen line is redirected

Held at open and still held: high explosive fit for mine blasting and special munitions, made and consumed near the place of use. Sought since the 320s: a nitrated explosive that survives carriage across a continent, sits quietly in store, and detonates when told.

The programme delivered the transport-and-storage version in 531. Handling deaths on the movement of explosive to minesites fall by four fifths. It is the qualified filling behind the moored mines at the canal mouths, the Sinai approaches and the colonial harbours, and behind the spar and drifting charges.

The residual is long life in a sealed store in tropical heat, a harder case than a mine laid and used within the season. The state now carries explosive nine thousand miles to the western holdings and keeps it at harbour posts through years of damp heat before it is wanted, and it hangs nitrated charges under aircraft that sit on tropical airfields between flights. 900 researchers continue on it. The programme held the largest allocation in the establishment before the 531 delivery and no longer does, standing fourth of twelve, the delivered problem having been the larger part of the work.

The nitrate reconciliation ordered of the assay office and the arsenal reports in 533 and the council adopts one figure. Refined nitrate from the cave deposits and the maintained beds runs at 4,200 tons a year at open. The bed establishment across the Nile, the Sahel and the highland provinces is expanded and delivers 9,000 tons a year from 545, of which mine blasting takes 6,600 tons and the magazine reserve 2,400. Works cost 3,100 talents, operation 340 talents a year. The Pacific surface beds of Item 10 stand above that figure and are worked at the rate the 10,700-mile haul allows.

The electrical line is redirected onto the chain that can reach nitrogen. Current through a solution yields no nitrogen that was not already in the vessel. Nitrogen independent of bed acreage is taken from the air, and the instrument is a high-energy arc burning in air. The skill is the arc: arc lighting demonstrated about 358 to 368, arc welding about 470 to 490, and the arc furnace producing steel of higher purity and more controllable carbon than any combustion process by about 480 to 510, the same furnace that pours the ship plate of Item 14. With it goes the atomic theory of fixed whole-number combining weights formalised about 490 to 500, which lets a chemist treat air as holding a combinable part. The downstream half is in hand, the assay houses having concentrated nitric acid in quantity since about 250 to 300, so the air-fixing step alone is wanting. The source is the Livingstone Falls plant, which holds electric current in a concentration greater than exists anywhere. The demand is that bed-independent nitrate lifts the ceiling on blasting explosive, on the nitrated charges the aircraft carry, and on field fertiliser.

Allocation 1,200 researchers and 1,900 talents a year, both raised this turn, with a bench process expected 545 to 585.

Standing instruction: the bed expansion and the Pacific workings proceed on their own schedule and their appropriations are not conditioned on any result from the arc programme.

## Item 14. The fleet is age-limited and the rate is short of requirement

A major steam hull serves about 60 years, carried through two general refits in which engines, boilers, fittings and much of the upper works are renewed. A hull is rebuilt rather than scrapped for as long as its frames and bottom will take the work, and only the earliest coal-fired construction, whose hull form was drawn around machinery three generations obsolete, is beyond refitting. Stock at open is 250 major steam hulls. 150 are built across the turn as standardisation and interchangeable components lift the rate from 3 a year to 9. 40 are condemned, being the last of the coal-era hulls. Stock at 550 is 360.

At 60 years of service a rate of 9 a year sustains a steady-state fleet of 540 hulls, so the fleet is well below the size the present yards would eventually carry and is growing toward it. The constraint is not the peacetime size. It is that 9 hulls a year cannot replace battle losses in a war of attrition against a rival producing iron at factory scale (firm, agent observation of Rome's northern provinces in about 408 to 412, treated as a floor). That inference is partly overtaken by the returns at Item 22, which count Rome laying down six warship hulls a year from 531 in eighteen of twenty years, below Egypt's nine. The council does not soften the requirement on that account, because Item 23 records that Egypt cannot say what a Roman hull now carries, and a rate comparison between fleets of unknown quality decides nothing. The requirement is put at 15 hulls a year and is derived rather than asserted. A fleet of 400 on a 60-year life needs 7 a year to stand still. To that the council adds the ability to make good a year of general action within five years, taking a year of general action at a tenth of the fleet, 40 hulls, which is a further 8 a year. Seven and eight is 15, which standardisation alone does not reach, and that is the requirement set for the next turn.

The refit cycle is the quieter cost. With 360 hulls on a 60-year life and two refits each, the yards carry 12 hulls a year in general refit before a single new keel is laid, which is where a third of the yard capacity goes.

Plate has been standard on new construction since 515 to 520. 175 hulls are plated to the designed standard at 550, being all construction since that date. The remaining 185 are older hulls, and plate is now added during general refit as well as on new construction, which brings the older stock forward without taking yard time from new keels.

Armament in service is the moored mine, the spar and drifting charge, the petroleum incendiary, and the aircraft of Item 15 operating from coastal and colonial bases.

The council records why that list is the whole of it, the question having been put this turn.

The confrontation planners asked for a means of putting explosive on an enemy at a distance from a fixed position, for the Sinai line and the canal approaches. The request was general and named no device, the planners having none to name. The council grants that the requirement is real and does not answer it by saying Egypt avoids open ground. A fixed position is not open ground. The Sinai system exists to make a Roman advance slow and costly enough to change Rome's calculation, which is attritional defence from prepared works, and the land army is drilled against simulated Roman formations precisely because the council expects to receive an assault on those works. The requirement stands and is met at present only in part, by water and the sluice gates, by mined approaches, by the incendiary aircraft that now reach hundreds of miles beyond the line, and by the torsion engines on the walls, which are as old as the walls and throw what they have always thrown.

Trials putting a nitrated charge into a torsion engine were made and stopped, the charge being worth more in a mine that waits under water than in an engine that must be laid, ranged and served in the open. The council notes what those trials did and did not test. They tested the charge as a load, the engine supplying the force. Nothing in them, and nothing anywhere in the establishment's work, touches making a charge yield its force in a controlled way inside a closed vessel. Egypt has never measured force inside such a vessel at the instant of ignition and has no instrument that would, has done no work on burning rates or on how the grain of a charge alters them, and has done none on the proportions such a vessel would need. The 531 delivery in Item 13 was a stabilisation for carriage and storage and says nothing about controlled burning. So the two things the record names as wanting, metal and nitrogen, are in hand, and a third body of work that no one has begun is not.

The decision is therefore to defer rather than to refuse, and the distinction is entered deliberately. Opening that work now would draw chemists from the long-store residual and from the arc-nitrogen line, both of which gate more than this does and both of which are already carrying transfers this turn. The planners' request is granted in substance and deferred in time, entered as the first candidate for the next turn's slate, and their office keeps its standing. Nothing in this item is to be read as a finding that the thing cannot be done.

## Item 15. Aircraft

The ceiling on this line was the want of a power-station-scale electrolytic smelter, and the falls plant removed it. Aluminum structural work is mature and airframes are metal-framed.

The electrical and precision works builds airframes to standard drawings in series. Production at 550 is 150 airframes and 230 engines a year, the surplus engines being spares. In service at 550 is 700 airframes against 60 at open.

The build path is given because the stock figure cannot be checked without it, and because the council had not totalled it before this paper. The works and the squadrons keep annual returns and nobody had added the middle. Across 525 to 534 the shops turned out about 10 airframes a year, the work still being hand assembly on the old footing, which is 100. Across 535 to 542, once the works was extended to airframes and building to standard drawings, about 45 a year, which is 360. Across 543 to 550, as the aluminum from the falls came through in structural quantity and the line settled, the rate climbed from about 70 to the present 150, averaging about 110, which is 880. Cumulative build across the turn is therefore about 1,340, which with the 60 at open and the 700 in service leaves 700 struck off.

Seven hundred struck off against roughly 1,400 airframes ever flown is half of everything the state has built, and the council enters the causes rather than the bare number. The squadron returns record a cause against each machine even though no one had totalled them: about 330 worn out and condemned, an airframe on tropical service lasting some eight years before its structure is past repair; about 210 lost in training, which is where the least experienced men fly; about 95 to engine failure away from a field; and about 65 to weather and ground accident at colonial stations. None to enemy action, there having been none.

The council does not treat the rate as an accident of bad handling. It follows from what the aircraft are used for and from conditions already on the record elsewhere in this paper. The heaviest use is route survey over unmapped ground in Item 7, flown from cleared strips with no charts, no prepared landing grounds and no second field to reach if the engine stops. The engine is stripped every 500 running hours, and the interval is short enough that a great deal of flying is done on machinery near the end of its run. The colonial stations sit in exactly the tropical conditions the medical and works returns describe as hard on everything the empire puts into them. A wastage of half is the price of using aircraft this way, and the council pays it because the survey they do could not be done otherwise.

The engine comes off the internal-combustion line: practical stationary engines from about 450, marine patrol craft at eighteen to twenty-two knots with long range from about 460 to 470, wheeled vehicles from about 465 to 480, and the first controlled flight in about 472 to 478 on a lightweight engine driving a screw propeller derived from naval practice. Fuel is Gulf of Suez petroleum distilled to light fractions, the supply the fleet converted to from coal beginning about 400 and standard on new construction by 440 to 450. A century of development puts aero engine overhaul at 500 running hours, the heavier marine patrol engines running four times that.

Aircraft fly from land. No vessel in the fleet operates them at sea, and none is designed to. The tethered balloon therefore remains the fleet's eye and is not superseded by anything in this item. Every major hull carries one, hydrogen-filled and winched, lifting an observer with a telescope and a telephone line to the deck, which raises the horizon far beyond the masthead and gives a squadron warning of smoke long before a lookout could find it. It was a balloon observer that first caught the Roman squadron of the 440s by its coal-smoke column beyond the horizon, and balloons carried the incendiary attack on massed troops and timber decks from about 330. The two capabilities divide cleanly. The balloon watches from above a ship, anywhere the fleet is. The aeroplane ranges hundreds of miles from a coast Egypt already holds and cannot follow the fleet into open water.

Operational radius on a reconnaissance loading is 750 miles, and 550 miles carrying incendiaries and nitrated charges. Basing is the Sinai and canal approaches, Cyprus, the Cape, the Atlantic, Caribbean and Pacific harbours, and the highland approach posts, where route survey over unmapped ground is the heaviest use.

Standing instruction: numbers, ranges, engine life and basing are held in the highest compartment.

## Item 16. Radar goes to sea, and the networks extend

Detection radar has stood on the Mediterranean coast, the Sinai approaches and Cyprus since about 450 to 460, with directional radar and range estimation from about 460 to 480. The electrical works now manufactures sets in series rather than building them by hand, and the compartment is opened to the fleet.

90 hulls carry detection radar at 550, fitted top down through the Mediterranean battle squadrons, the Atlantic patrol and the Cape fleet. The coastal chain grows to 68 stations, covering the Atlantic face of Africa, the western holdings and the Caribbean harbours. The cathode-ray display works alongside it as the tactical display, off the same vacuum-tube manufacture.

Radio sets in service are 1,240, covering every major hull, the shore stations and the colonial harbours, with encryption from the first transmission as it has been since the frequency authority was established about 460 to 465.

The sets themselves are not the sets of a century ago and the gains are worth stating, a hundred years of continuous work having gone into them since the first military deployment about 440 to 445. Transmission has moved off the spark gap onto a continuous wave generated by valve, which puts the whole of the power into one narrow frequency instead of splashing it across the band, so more stations work at once without fouling each other and the frequency authority can allocate finely. Receiving has gained multi-stage valve amplification, which lifts a signal far below what an unamplified detector could hear and roughly doubles the working range for the same transmitter. Shore-to-ship reliable working is 2,400 miles by day and further at night, and ship-to-ship 400 miles, against a fraction of that when ship-to-ship first went to sea about 470 to 490. Sets are sealed against salt and mounted on sprung frames against the shock of a heavy sea and the working of a hull, so a set now survives a commission instead of being nursed through one.

Two capabilities follow from the improvements rather than from more sets. A rotatable loop aerial gives a bearing on any transmitting station, so a vessel takes cross bearings on two shore stations and fixes its position in cloud, which is the first method Egypt has ever had that does not need a sky. And voice, in service since about 455 to 465 as a command tool, now carries clearly enough at squadron distance that keyed traffic is reserved for what wants a written record and for long-range work.

The telegraph carries formal empire-wide traffic and extends along the Caribbean, Plata, Patagonian and Pacific coasts, adding 4,100 miles of line on Copperbelt wire insulated with cultivated latex. Telephone exchanges, hand-connected, open in the nineteen Plata towns, the two Caribbean settlement capitals and the falls industrial city. Relay calculating machines, telegraph relays used as logical switches since about 460 to 475, extend from navigation and astronomical prediction to the settlement office's returns, the treasury's tables and the assay office's reconciliations.

## Item 18. Two stud establishments are founded, on ground Egypt holds

Cavalry has been the weakest arm since the Roman encounter and matured through seventy years of breeding in the East African highlands from about 275 AD, where the tsetse does not reach. The limit has been the extent of tsetse-free grazing rather than the skill, and the empire holds a great deal of such ground it has never used for the purpose.

The primary establishment is founded on the Highveld and Botswana grassland behind the Cape, the largest tsetse-free temperate grazing the empire holds, at 340,000 acres under stud and remount pasture. It is stocked from the East African highland studs and from Cyrenaica, with Arabian bloodstock by sea. Breeding stock in quantity by 552, remounts in militarily useful numbers by 566.

Cape to Alexandria is 6,100 sea miles and the Plata to Alexandria is 7,100, so distance alone barely separates them. A converted transport carries 250 head with fodder and water, so 40 sailings move 10,000 remounts in a season, which the transport fleet finds without touching the battle squadrons, and either coast could do it.

Three things decide it for the Cape. Stock moves to it overland from the East African highland studs, where seventy years of breeding sit, instead of crossing an ocean first. It stands inside the African fallback perimeter, which is the ground the whole strategy says Egypt fights from. And the Plata grassland is committed to settler wheat and cattle under Item 5, where 250,000 people are being fed and the land has a competing use. The Plata establishment is founded at a quarter the size and supplies the western holdings locally.

## Item 19. The China arrangement is honoured in full

Twenty of their scholars each decade sit inside Egyptian institutions by agreement, and Egypt's germ and tropical-disease body is open to them (firm, own file). It stays open, at full extent, on no schedule. The knowledge has passed and cannot be recalled, the paper stream rests on the arrangement, and a partner who finds an exchange quietly narrowed prices every later dealing accordingly.

Access under the exchange is defined as the germ and tropical-disease body together with the published optics and mathematics. The compartments in force are electrical engineering, vacuum work, radio, radar, aircraft, the Atlantic crossing, fleet dispositions, and the depth of the reserve.

A register office under the Library's branch administration records what each scholar is shown, on what date, at whose authority, and what was asked for and refused, at 40 talents a year. Its purpose is that a later estimate of foreign tropical capability starts from a known base. The record of the questions is collection obtained by paperwork.

The fever bark of Item 8 passes, and the council enters why. The undertaking covers the germ and tropical-disease body without reservation, the bark is the largest thing in that body Egypt has ever held, and China bought the exchange for exactly this: its southern frontier has stood at the Shan highlands for four centuries because the country below kills its soldiers faster than any army. Withholding it would also be detected, the register office recording on both sides what was asked for and refused, and a partner who finds an exchange quietly narrowed prices every later dealing accordingly. So the medicine passes whole. What does not pass is the source, held on the coffee practice at Item 8, and the council notes plainly that this is a narrowing China may in time detect and price.

The cost is entered rather than argued away. The premise that a power held out of tropical country by disease becomes capable of operating there is no longer a window running 545 to 585; it begins on delivery. The 537 letter at INCOMING has since carried that premise from assumed to firm, with sixty physicians posted and the quarantine widened in 534, and the three requests that letter makes are answered at Item 24. Egypt has removed the barrier in front of a capable power by its own hand, in exchange for paper, mathematics, the mirror principle, pendulum regulation and a consultation clause, and it keeps only the supply.

One further premise stands. Egypt's file records that China named silk production and weapons manufacture as matters it would not share, which is a category a partner stated and not a description of anything Egyptian eyes have seen; no estimate of foreign weapons manufacture rests on the carve-out's existence.

Three decisions on the consultation clause are entered, all in the letter at MESSAGES OUT.

The bark goes as medicine without its source, and the reservation is stated in the letter rather than left to be discovered, on the reasoning that a narrowing the register office would record on both sides is better admitted than found. Egypt sets it beside China's own reservation on the making of silk, which China stated at the outset rather than letting Egypt discover it, and which is the conduct that earned China the only 4 in the register.

Both falsehoods in the note to Rome are disclosed to China, and the cost is entered rather than argued away. Telling a partner that Egypt manufactures falsehoods through a formal channel teaches that partner to weigh Egyptian statements, and China will weigh them. Against that: one of the two implies unnamed other powers, China is a power, and if Rome repeats it before Egypt discloses it then China is left to wonder whether it was the party implied. The letter names China as no part of it. Disclosing one and withholding the other would have been worse than disclosing neither, since a partner shown a partial account and later given the rest reads the first telling as the deception. A partner who finds a thing out is worse than a partner who is told, which is the whole basis on which Egypt rated this power above the other two.

The survey squadron sent toward the southern landmass is disclosed, its approach lying near water under Chinese protection. Fleet dispositions are a standing compartment, and this is an exception the council authorises by name rather than one the drafting office takes: what is given is that a squadron went, that it has been withdrawn, that it made one landing to repair a damaged hull, and that it claimed no ground. Its strength, its stations and its routes are not given. Its standing orders are given, here and in the letter to India, because an order to make no landing where there are works, to attempt no contact and to fire only if fired upon is a statement of restraint whose whole value lies in the other party knowing it, and a compartment that hides restraint buys nothing. The reasoning is that a partner who protects the approach and learns from a third party that Egyptian hulls passed through it is a partner owed an explanation he did not get.

Nothing of Egypt's own western holdings goes in the letter. The compartments at Item 8 and in this item bind the drafting office as they bind everyone, and the report of Rome's crossing is given as it was given before, a crossing made and cargoes brought back, with nothing added from Egyptian ground.

The council's disposition toward China is 4 on trust and holds there. The Cape command logs the question of whether a power operating in tropical country would come west toward the East African coast, where the patrol line has stood at thirty degrees south since the 450 to 475 period.

## Item 20. Crops and population

Western stock arrived about 510 to 515 and was distributed for trial across a dozen climates. The Delta is unchanged, being already the richest grain land on earth. Everywhere else the food supply is rising, and it rises by two separate mechanisms which should not be run together.

The first is new ground carrying people the Nile never could. Cassava feeds villages on tropical soil in the Congo and the West and East African lowlands that grew almost nothing before, so land that was margin becomes farmland. The Plata, the Pacific temperate coast and the southern grazing runs add further ground outright, and the highland root crop of Item 8 enters provincial cultivation in 546 on the cool highland ground the earlier transfers left out. This mechanism raises the ceiling rather than the yield. Cultivated acreage in the African and western provinces stands 14 percent above the figure at open, and almost the whole of that increase is ground that carried no crop at all before.

The second is higher yields on ground already held, where the new crops rotate against the old and the marginal provinces now feed themselves instead of drawing grain from the Nile. Yields per cultivated acre stand 7 percent above the figure at open.

Put on one scope and one baseline, the aggregate will not settle the question either way, and the council states why rather than picking the answer it prefers.

The two mechanisms have to be weighted by the Nile's share of cultivated acreage, and the council does not know that share. What it has is the Nile block's share of receipts, 58 percent, and those two numbers are furthest apart precisely here, the Delta being the richest grain land on earth, which is a statement that it yields more per acre than anything else and therefore holds a smaller share of acreage than of receipts. Weighting by receipts at 58 percent gives empire acreage up 5.9 percent and food output up 13.3, which is 2.3 points below population. Weighting by a plausible acreage share of 40 percent gives acreage up 8.4 percent and output up 16.0, which is 0.4 points above it. The margin therefore runs somewhere between minus 2.3 and plus 0.4 points across twenty-five years, and the council cannot say on which side of zero it falls.

The reconciliation is regional and not aggregate, and the council enters it as such. What the crops changed is where food is grown. Provinces that drew grain from the Nile now feed themselves, and population grows fastest in exactly those provinces, which is what the provincial returns show. The empire-wide figures are consistent with that redistribution and do not by themselves account for the growth rate. The council holds no mechanism that converts a quantity of surplus into a rate of increase, and the population figure rests on the provincial returns rather than on any food calculation.

Together they lift growth above the old trend, which is what a ceiling being raised looks like. Population at 550 is 31.1 million against 26.9 million at open, an average of 0.58 percent a year.

The opening figure is reconstructed, and the method is stated because the answer moves with it. Only one revenue stream tracks population, the agrarian and administrative base, and the reconstruction uses that stream alone rather than total receipts, four of the seven streams tracking nothing of the kind and two not existing at open. That stream is the land tax together with the state monopolies on oil, salt, papyrus, textiles, beer and the rest, which is why it tracks population at all: the monopolies follow consumption, and consumption follows heads. It stands at 82,360 at open, being 58 percent of 142,000. Taking the same share at 500, which the record supports, the land tax having been about three fifths of receipts at 300 and the base at 500 being described as the broad agricultural one with trade, gold and copper layered above it, the agrarian stream at 500 was about 76,560. That is a rise of 7.6 percent across 500 to 525, so the population rose 7.6 percent from the fixed 25.0 million to 26.9 million, at 0.294 percent a year.

The other reading was tested and rejected. If the tolls, gold and copper were flat across 500 to 525, which is arguable on the record, the agrarian stream carried the whole increase, rose 13.8 percent, and would put the opening population near 28.5 million at 0.52 percent a year. The council rejects it because 0.52 percent is more than twice the 0.211 percent of 450 to 500 and falls almost entirely before the western stock arrived about 510 to 515, so it would require an acceleration with nothing to cause it. The figure carried is 26.9 million, and the council records that it cannot pin it closer than the two readings allow.

Against 0.211 percent across 450 to 500 and 0.294 across 500 to 525, the turn runs at 0.58, roughly double the interval before it.

The council is watching the early part of a change it will not see finished. The generational wave runs through the century following.

## Item 21. Freed term men are directed to colonial grants

Chattel slavery for mine workers was replaced over 352 to 368 by defined-term contracts ending in manumission and a settlement grant, with mechanical substitution required in the deadliest work. The reason recorded then governs still: large concentrations of indefinitely bonded men are the gravest danger inside the wall, and cutting the headcount is a war measure. The Copperbelt revolt of about 345, coordinated across three sites and retaken in eleven days by the Zambezi steam fleet, is the event the reform answers.

Freed term men owed grants are routed to the western holdings as a standing preference. 91,000 of the turn's landings are men out of term contract with their families, which lowers the bonded headcount at the concentrated African sites and supplies the Enterprise with settlers accustomed to tropical service.

The settlement office is instructed that term-contract labour is the instrument for colonial works, and the Pacific copper and nitrate works of Item 10 are opened on that basis. Reason entered: the 345 precedent. A concentrated bonded population 9,000 miles from the fleet, where relief runs weeks, is the worst arrangement available to the state.

Bonded labour on the great estates and in the Nile agrarian base continues under existing terms. ## Item 22. The returns from the Roman operation, and one question the council cannot close

The returns landed at the close of the period, after the note at Item 3 had gone. Several things the council carried as assumed or reported are now firm, and the movements are set out so they can be seen.

**What is now firm and was not.** Rome lays down six warship hulls a year from 531 and did so in eighteen of twenty years, counted on the ways. Against Egypt's nine a year and 150 built across the turn, Egypt out-builds Rome in warships, and the council records that this reverses the direction of its own standing worry. Item 14 has carried, since about 408 to 412, an inference that Rome produces at a scale Egypt cannot match, treated as a floor. On warship hulls the floor is now a counted figure and it is below Egypt's. The council does not extend that to what the hulls carry, which is Item 23.

**Rome is settling the far shore, and the founding fear is confirmed.** From 534 the Atlantic yards build six-hundred-ton ocean transports for the western run and nothing else, and from 526 emigrant embarkations were counted at the Iberian quays every season. The people going out are veterans with families and their gear. The whole Western Enterprise was launched on the premise that a rival growing on uncontested ground is the thing Egypt cannot survive, and that premise moves from assumed to firm. The council records that the Enterprise was right and that the confrontation planners who argued at Item 6 to hold at the Orinoco were wrong on this ground, whatever their standing on the other.

**The second tasking is answered and the answer is bad.** The reef-and-current account is dead on the Atlantic runs. Rome's returned crews describe a built and worked coast and the conclusion drawn everywhere is that its masters are Egyptian, said in the naval offices without argument as a matter no longer discussed. There is bitterness in it because Rome now holds that the account it believed for a century was given deliberately and was false. No shipowner will send a hull down that coast and the underwriters will not price it.

That is the exact grievance Item 4 said the seventy-year account had cost the state and could not remove, and it is worse than Item 4 assumed. Item 4 held that the account failed. It did not merely fail; it is known to have been management. The doctrine at Item 4 stands and is strengthened, and the council records that the section which drafted and maintained that account was more wrong than the paper said when it lost supervision.

**The first tasking is not answered, and the council splits.** Two returns bear on whether Rome believes Egypt is across the western ocean, and they point opposite ways. Both are in the same item.

Rome has kept a watch on the Canaries and Madeira since about 532, with glasses and a tethered balloon, under a standing order to count large hulls passing north and west, and its captains on the western run are instructed to report any such hull, its heading and its size. A power does not pay for a twenty-five-year watch for a thing it has not thought of. Against that, the watch's own people say they have counted none in twenty-five years, and no Roman document, printed or handled, states any conclusion about Egypt in the western ocean.

The intelligence directorate holds that a watch kept twenty-five years with a null and no document is a question Rome has open and unanswered rather than a belief Rome holds, and that Rome suspects without knowing.

The confrontation planners hold that a power which keeps that watch and then ships heavy pieces west for works its own clerks say are laid for ships and not for islanders is acting on a belief it has not troubled to write down, and that absence from documents shows only that the belief has not yet needed one.

The council does not resolve it and records the split. It refuses the comfortable reading of the null, as it refused the reassuring reading of the Roman balloon in the last turn: a null reported by the watched party's own men, which happens to flatter Egypt, is the weakest class in the packet and is not proof that nothing was seen.

**Why the null is probably real all the same, and what follows operationally.** The watch sits on the Canaries and Madeira, which is the door a Mediterranean power would use. Egypt's crossing departs the West African depots at the Senegal and Cape Verde latitude on the trade winds, twelve to fourteen degrees of latitude to the south of it, and a watch with glasses and a balloon on high ground sees tens of miles and not hundreds. Rome is watching the route it would sail itself. Standing instruction: the crossing departure stays where it is, no westbound traffic is routed within sight of the northern islands, and the Moroccan and Saharan coastal traffic of the last turn's build-out is to be reviewed against the watch's arc before the next sailing season, since that shore is the one part of the Egyptian position that lies under it.

**Rome's questions in the caravan trade.** From 548 the Roman gold-buying houses of the African provinces began asking the Berber factors where the interior routes run, what they carry and whether the volume has changed in two generations. Two returns, thin, treated as a floor. It is logged for the confrontation planners against the trans-Saharan corridor and no action is taken on two returns.

**The silence.** Rome has made no approach to Egypt on the lost ships or on the coast in twenty-five years, and has laid no depot, stocked no coal south of its Moroccan holdings, offered no charter, fitted out no probe and sent no hull at the African Atlantic coast in the same period. The council enters the two together. A court that believes it was lied to for a century and had five armed vessels destroyed, and that says nothing and prepares nothing for twenty-five years, has not dropped the matter. It has filed it.

## Item 23. The powder mills and the mounted pieces, and the reversal of Item 14

This came back outside the tasking and could not have been avoided by any network standing in Roman ports. It is firm, from handled papers and from Rome's own published law.

Rome has a state powder industry. A labour settlement of 540 extended collegium recognition to fourteen powder mills, naming them in published law, standing on the Rhine, in the British coal district, in Dacia, Campania, Noricum, Baetica and on the Danube. From 544 Roman warships in port carry mounted pieces.

The council sets down the limit of its own understanding before anything else. Egypt knows that fourteen mills make a powder, that the state recognised them together in one instrument, and that from 544 something is mounted on Roman warships. Egypt does not know what the powder is, what the mounted piece does, what it throws or how far, what it weighs, how many a hull carries, or whether the two facts are one thing or two. Nothing in Egyptian practice corresponds to either, and no Egyptian officer has seen a piece mounted or fired. The words in this item are Rome's own, taken from Rome's law and Rome's clerks, and the council uses them because it has none of its own.

**What the council held, and what it holds now.** Item 14 of this paper records that the confrontation planners asked for a means of putting explosive on an enemy at a distance from a fixed position, that the council granted the requirement was real, and that it deferred the work rather than refusing it, on the ground that the chemists were wanted more by the long-store residual and the arc-nitrogen line. That deferral was taken earlier in this same period, and this return reached the council before its close. The planners were right and the council was wrong, and the paper says so rather than rewriting the decision to have been wiser. The arsenal, which opposed opening the work as a diversion, was wrong with the council.

**The reversal.** The deferral is lifted. The work becomes the first charge on the next turn's slate and is not a candidate among others. No allocation is made inside this turn, the return having arrived at its close, so the research table in this paper is unchanged and shows the establishment as it actually stood across the twenty-five years.

**What is tasked.** The priority intelligence tasking already set at Item 11 is amended. The first question is now what a mounted piece is: what the mills make, what a piece does with it, what it throws, at what range, and what a hull carries. The southern-landmass questions stand behind it. The council notes that the answer may be readable in Rome's own published law and printed sheets, which is where this finding came from, and that it does not require the placement that failed.

**One thing not to be inferred.** Item 22 records that Egypt out-builds Rome in warship hulls. Nothing in that bears on this. A count of hulls says nothing about what is mounted in them, and the council forbids any comparison of the two fleets that rests on numbers alone until this question is answered.

## Item 24. The Chinese letter of 537 is answered, and a premise moves to firm

**The premise moves.** Item 19 revised the tropical-capability premise from a window running 545 to 585 to a thing beginning on delivery, and entered it as assumed. The 537 letter answers it. Sixty physicians trained on the body Egypt opened are posted to the southern provinces and the highland frontier, a record is kept of which remedy answers which fever and in what quantity, and the quarantine practice was widened in 534 to cover every ship returning from a long voyage, China's own squadrons included. The premise is now firm and dated: capability began, and the Shan barrier is coming down. The council made that trade knowingly and does not revisit it.

**The three asks are granted, all of them.** Physicians of Egypt's own go into residence at the southern stations for terms of years. The practice for keeping a ship's company in health on a voyage of many months, in water, in provisions and against the sickness of the gums, goes in full; Egypt has run the longest voyages in the world for four centuries and the practice is worth more than anything else it was asked for. The glasshouse practice for the slow cooling of a large piece goes in full, the chamber, the schedule by which the heat is let down, and the signs by which a piece is judged sound.

**The costs are entered rather than argued away.** The ship-health practice lengthens China's reach at sea, which is the second barrier Egypt has taken down for that court in two turns, after the disease body. The annealing practice bears on the size of glass a workshop can make sound, which bears on instruments, which bears on the mirror principle China itself gave Egypt. The council grants all three anyway, and the reasoning is the one it has used throughout: a partner who finds an exchange quietly narrowed prices every later dealing accordingly, the register office records on both sides what was asked and refused, and China asked in the open after saying plainly that the account between them is not level.

**The residency is also collection, and the council says so.** Egyptian physicians in residence at China's southern stations will see the country below the Shan frontier, the state of the frontier's health establishment and the pace at which it is moving south. That is observation obtained by an accepted invitation and not by an operation, on the same footing as the register office. The physicians carry no tasking and are not to be given one, because a physician in residence who is caught collecting costs Egypt the residency and the exchange together.

**What was delivered with the letter.** Two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, entered as a courtesy, the regulation itself having come to Egypt in the last generation and being already in the clock shops from 538. And the practice of the sealed deposit note, with its forms, its seals and the rules by which offices settle between themselves, which has carried that court's remittances and much of its merchants' trade for three centuries.

**The deposit note is adopted, and it is the largest thing in the packet that is not about Rome.** Egypt has had the letter of credit between banking houses since about 150 to 200 AD, so the instrument is not new. What is new is the office machinery behind it: a certificate stamped at one office and paid at another, with a settled rule by which the offices reckon between themselves afterwards, so that the coin does not travel at all. Egypt's treasury still moves specie, and it now moves it across an ocean to the western holdings, where a lost hull is a lost cargo of coin and where the garrison pay of Item 5 has to arrive in something. The treasury is instructed to raise the practice at the Alexandria, Khartoum, falls, Plata and Caribbean offices first, and to report at the next turn what proportion of the western remittance can be carried on paper instead of in metal.

## Item 26. The replies to the packet, and two decisions to send nothing

One letter is drafted. Two correspondents get nothing, and both are decisions taken rather than matters left undone.

**To China, and what it discharges.** Egypt undertook to send firm intelligence of Rome's western shore when it held it firmly and marked as such. Until this packet it did not, and the letter sent earlier in the period said so in terms. The returns now give it: transports of one size built for the western run in five named yards and nothing else built in them, embarkations counted at the quay every season across twenty-four years, veterans with families and goods rather than garrisons, two round voyages in three years to a hull. That is the promised thing and it goes.

What it buys. An undertaking discharged in the form it was given, marked firm where firm and reported where not, which is the form Egypt asked to be believed in when it conceded an overclaim to this court in the last generation. Against the finding at INCOMING that the traffic between the two courts runs one way, this is something Egypt can give that costs it no craft and no compartment, and the council notes that a partner permanently in surplus should look for such things.

What it costs. It tells China that Egypt's reach into Roman Atlantic ports is good enough to count embarkations by season across twenty-four years, which raises China's estimate of Egyptian collection. The cost is accepted, the alternative being to send a report so vague that it discharges nothing.

**What is withheld from that letter, and why.** The watch on the northern islands is withheld. A watch whose standing order is to count large hulls passing north and west implies a party expected to pass north and west, and the next question any reader asks is whose. Reporting it would put Egypt in the western ocean by inference in a letter that names Egypt nowhere.

The mills and the mounted pieces are withheld, and this is the harder call, so the reasoning is entered in full. Reporting them costs little on its face, standing as they do in Rome's own published law, which China can read and may already have read. Asking about them costs a great deal. A question is a measurement. A court that asks what a mounted piece is has told the party it asked that it does not have one, and Egypt's whole position rests on rivals being unable to weigh it. So they are neither reported nor asked, and the corollary is entered at Item 23: the answer is to be found in Rome, out of Rome's law and Rome's printed sheets, and not through a partner.

The provenance of the report is withheld, and this was corrected in the drafting. The undertaking given in the last generation was to mark what Egypt sends and not to source it, so the confidence class is the whole of the promise. An account of where the counting was done, whether at the quayside or inside the yards, and across how many hands it was corroborated, would describe this court's establishment in Roman ports as resident, multiple, yard-side as well as quayside, and unbroken for a generation. That is a larger thing than the reach, seasonality and duration priced above, it is owed to nobody, and it would be handed to a court that has recorded of Egypt that Egyptian statements are to be weighed. The letter marks the report firm and says nothing of how it was got.

Also withheld: everything on the standing list at Item 19, and every word that would let a reader place Egypt across the western ocean. The draft reports Rome and says nothing whatever of Egypt there.

**To Rome, nothing sent, and the council records that it weighed saying something.** Item 3 enters the recency claim as failed and adopts the successor position that Egypt says nothing further about when or how that coast came under it. That position binds here.

Four grounds. Rome already holds, on its returned crews and its own quaysides, that the coast has Egyptian masters and that the old account was given deliberately; a correction would move that from a belief Rome holds to a document Rome possesses in Egypt's own hand, which is worse. Any approach invites the question of the five vessels, which Egypt cannot answer truthfully and cannot lie about again to a court that has just caught the last one. Rome has made no approach in twenty-five years, and Egypt approaching now, hard on its own note, reads as a court that is worried. And there are no words available that lower the grievance without conceding the act.

The council states plainly that it is not obliged to correct its own falsehood and not obliged to maintain it, and that it does neither. It stops. What that costs is entered rather than argued away: the grievance stays where it is at its full weight, and it is the raw material of the coordination between Rome and India that this state fears above everything. The council accepts the cost because it can find no sentence that reduces it.

**To India, nothing further sent.** The reply already sent this period answered the notice of 543 and the sealed instrument, volunteered the beaching of 534 and the cairn unasked, and disclaimed any claim to ground. India said in terms that no answer was expected and none was required. Beyond that, every remaining subject between the two courts runs through the Atlantic and the five vessels, which Egypt raises with no one, and least of all with the court it rates 2 on trust and assumes carries what it learns to Rome.

**Standing instruction, generalising an error this court has made before.** No letter from this court to any power is to name a want of Egypt's own, and no request is to be made abroad in any domain where the request would disclose the want. The instruction of 525, that the drafting office check where Egypt already leads before asking, was the narrow form of this. The broad form is that Egypt does not ask questions that measure it.

## Technical assessments

Assessments of what physically arrived, or of what the returns disclose about a rival's technique rather than about his intent. Intent is at Items 22 and 23.

**The two Han pendulum clocks. Nothing learned, and that is the finding.** The clocks went to the Alexandria instrument shops and were taken down and measured against the shops' own work. The workmanship is good and the regulation is sound. Nothing in the escapement, the suspension of the bob, the train or the beat is a thing Alexandria did not already hold. The pendulum principle itself came to Egypt from China in the last generation and has been in the clock shops since 538, and Egypt has built escapement clocks since about 250 to 290 and carried them to sea since about 285 to 300 as guarded instruments. The shops report two differences of arrangement rather than of capability, in how the bob is hung and in the beat chosen, and judge both to be preference. China said as much in sending them, entering them as a courtesy and not as consideration and stating it did not propose to send the same knowledge twice. The council records a courtesy gift that taught nothing as an ordinary result and not as a slight.

**The sealed deposit note. The one thing of real technical value that arrived this generation, and it is not a device.** Egypt has held the parts for four centuries and has never assembled them. Deposit banking came from the old Ptolemaic state, private banking houses spread from about 100 to 150, the letter of credit came in from about 150 to 200 because a merchant could not carry a fortune to India and back, and the sea loan and the first shapes of marine insurance followed. What Egypt has never had is the office machinery: a certificate stamped at one office and paid at another, and behind it a settled rule by which the offices reckon with each other afterwards, so that the coin itself does not move at all. Egypt's instruments are bilateral and private, made between two houses that know each other. China's is a standing system with common forms, common seals and a settlement rule, and it has carried a continental state's remittances and much of its merchants' trade for three centuries, which is the part Egypt cannot get from its own practice.

The treasury's assessment. What it would displace is the bullion convoy. Egypt converts silver into gold for the vault and moves coin to pay distant garrisons, and it now moves coin across an ocean, where the garrison pay of Item 5 has to arrive in something and a lost hull is a lost cargo of metal. On the treasury's figures the western remittance is the costliest specie movement the state has ever run, and the practice bears on it directly.

Two objections were made and are entered. The bullion establishment, meaning the convoy and vault officers, objects that metal in a chest is a thing an enemy must take by force while paper is a thing he may forge, and that the state would be exchanging a risk it has managed for six centuries for one it has never carried. The private banking houses object that a crown paper network settling between its own offices will take the inter-port business their letters of credit now carry. The council overrules both, the first on the condition below and the second because the crown's own remittance is not the houses' business to begin with.

The condition. Forms, seals and the settlement rule go under the cryptographers, on the standing ground that a paper network is a network and that a certificate a rival can read or copy is worse than a chest he must board. Adoption is staged at the Alexandria, Khartoum, falls, Plata and Caribbean offices, with the proportion of the western remittance carried on paper to be reported at the next turn, as set at Item 24.

**Roman shipbuilding, from the operation's returns.** Two technical facts, both firm, both about capacity rather than intent.

Rome lays down six warship hulls a year under a standing authority and did so in eighteen of twenty years. Against Egypt's nine, Rome's warship yards are not the thing the council had assumed since about 408 to 412. But from 534 five named Atlantic yards build ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons for the western run and nothing else, which is a whole coast's yard capacity turned over to one standard hull. That is series construction of a single type, which is what Egypt did to its own yards this turn with interchangeable components, and it means Rome's Atlantic capacity should be read as a transport industry with a warship line beside it rather than as a warship industry that is smaller than Egypt's. The naval yard directorate is instructed to state Egypt's own western transport tonnage on the same footing, since the council cannot at present set six hundred tons against anything of its own.

**The Roman tethered balloon.** The watch on the Canaries and Madeira flies a tethered balloon from high ground as routine, and the clerks speak of it as routine. Egypt has flown tethered balloons since about 315 to 325 and married them to incendiaries from about 330, and Item 15 rests the fleet's over-horizon sight on them. The assessment is that the balloon is not an Egyptian advantage in kind and is not treated as one. Whether Rome flies them from hulls at sea is unknown and was not asked.

**The mounted pieces. A technical nil, and the most serious one in the packet.** Item 23 records what is known. Nothing physical arrived. No piece was seen, handled, measured or fired in Egyptian sight, no powder was sampled, and Egypt holds no drawing, no dimension and no account of what a piece does. The single largest technical fact of the period reached this court as two words out of a rival's published law, and Egypt cannot assess it at all. That is entered here as the state of the assessment rather than as a gap to be argued about, and the tasking that follows from it is at Item 23.

**What the traffic shows.** Set against each other, what came in and what went out this generation do not balance and have not for two turns. In came an institutional technique that Egypt could have built for itself at any time in four centuries and did not, and a pair of clocks that taught nothing. Out went the germ and tropical-disease body, the fever bark with its preparation and dosage, the practice for keeping a ship's company alive on a voyage of many months, and the glasshouse practice for cooling a large piece sound. China said first and in the open that the account is not level, and the register office will show the same from its own entries.

The council draws two things from that and neither is comfortable. Egypt has stopped learning the physical arts from anyone and is now learning only how other states arrange their offices, which is worth having and is not what an exchange was built to get. And a partner who is permanently in deficit stops asking, so the inflow Egypt values is sustained by China continuing to want things, not by Egypt continuing to be owed them. The greatest technical gains of this turn came off Egypt's own ground, the fever bark from the western highlands and the nitrate from the Pacific desert, and not from any power on earth.

## Handed over in person

**The sealed instrument concerning the southern coast.** Not posted to Alexandria. Handed to Egyptian officers at sea off that coast in 547 and again in 549, and entered here because the council holds the document itself, which is what makes it firm rather than reported. Struck at a press and sealed, in three tongues, Greek, Latin and the script of India, none of them Coptic, and read at sea by a carried translator. Nothing else was passed in either direction on either occasion and nothing was fired. Its terms and the council's reading of them are at Item 11, and the encounters in which it was handed over are recorded there as events rather than as correspondence.

Nothing else was handed to or by Egypt in person anywhere in the period.

In 534 an Egyptian hull struck a reef on the southern coast and was beached to be repaired on an empty stretch of shore, where the party lay eleven days. It left the refuse of a repair, and before sailing it raised a cairn and set in it a squared timber cut with Egyptian characters. That is what Egyptian survey parties do on every coast they chart, to fix a position for the ship that comes after. It marks a place and claims nothing, and Egypt gives notice of it here so that it is not taken for what it is not. If India would have it taken down, Egypt will take it down.
>

Egypt will hold to them where its ships touch that coast, and looks for the same at Egyptian ports, which India has long had.
>

>

>
> Alexandria.

## To China

> From the council at Alexandria to the court of the Emperor.
>
> Egypt writes under the consultation clause, and on three matters.
>
> First. Egypt has this year used the formal channel to Rome, which is old, on a subject new to it: the Atlantic coast of Africa, which Egypt administers, and the passage of Roman vessels along it. Egypt asked for notice of such passages and asked that vessels with no business there stand clear of its works. Egypt sought nothing else from Rome, was offered nothing, and made no undertaking to Rome touching any other power. China is told of it because Egypt undertook to tell.
>
> Egypt sets down the two things in that letter which are not true, since Rome may repeat either and a thing China hears first from Rome would read as concealment.
>
> The letter tells Rome that the coast has lately come under Egyptian administration. It has not. Egypt has held it for generations, and China is told the true thing.
>
> The letter also tells Rome that what Egypt has arranged with other powers in the matter of that coast is not Rome's business. There is no such arrangement. No power had any hand in how that coast came under Egypt, China no more than any other, and the sentence was put in to give Rome a direction to spend effort on that leads away from Alexandria.
>
> Egypt would rather China had both of these from Egypt.
>
> Second. Egypt promised firm intelligence of Rome's western shore when it held it firmly rather than by rumour, and marked as such. Egypt does not hold it firmly. What Egypt has of Rome across the western ocean remains what it was: a crossing made, and cargoes brought back. Anything further Egypt might now add would be inference, and Egypt undertook not to send inference dressed as knowledge. When it is firm it will come, so marked.
>
> Third. Egypt has kept its full germ and tropical-disease body open to China's scholars, and one thing in it is new and is the reason this letter does not wait. A bark breaks the intermittent fevers. Egypt's physicians have tried it over these years across eleven hundred cases of the fevers as they run in hot country, under the germ practice, and it holds. Preparation and dosage go to China's twenty with this letter and without price. Egypt understands what country lies below China's southern frontier and what has kept China out of it for four hundred years.
>
> Egypt sends the medicine and not the plant, and will not say where it grows. On that Egypt keeps its own practice, as China keeps its own on the making of silk, and Egypt would rather state the reservation than have it found.
>
> Fourth, and answering the letter of the year reckoned 537. All three things asked are granted and go with this letter or follow it within the year. Physicians of Egypt's own will go into residence at the southern stations for terms of years, which is where Egypt would have asked to send them had it been asked first. Egypt's practice for keeping a ship's company in health on a voyage of many months goes in full, in water, in provisions, and against the sickness of the gums, which Egypt has fought on the Cape route and the long southern passages for four centuries. The glasshouse practice for the slow cooling of a large piece goes in full: the arrangement of the chamber, the schedule by which the heat is let down over days, and the signs by which a piece is judged to have come through sound.
>
> China wrote that it has nothing of equal weight to set against what it asks and did not pretend the account is level. Egypt sets down that the sealed deposit note is worth more to Egypt than China appears to think, an empire that moves coin across an ocean having more use for a certificate than for the coin, and that Egypt does not regard the account as owed in either direction.
>
> Egypt also gives notice, as a courtesy and not as a request, that it sent a survey squadron to the southern landmass in these years, whose approach lies near water under China's protection. It has been withdrawn.
>
> Egypt sets down the orders it carried, since orders of this kind are worth nothing to either party if the other does not know them. It was to make no landing where there were works, vessels or people; to attempt no contact; to signal and hold position without closing on anything it did not expect; and to fire only if fired upon. Those orders were kept. It made one landing, to repair a hull damaged on a reef, on an empty stretch of shore where there were none of those things, and it claimed no ground.
>
> Alexandria.

# SPY TASKINGS

**Target.** Rome.

**Wanted, in order.** First, whether the Roman government holds that Egypt has crossed the western ocean and established itself on the far shore, or holds Egypt's presence to be confined to the African coast. Second, whether the Roman account of its vessels lost in Atlantic waters south of Morocco has hardened into a settled belief that Egypt destroyed them, and whether that belief is now attached to the garrisons and works observed on that coast.

**Cover.** Commercial, through the trade-corridor stations, reporting as a sideline to genuine trade. Officers carry no electrical apparatus, radio being held to fixed shore stations and warships.

**Who runs it.** The intelligence directorate, through the recruited-insider and Greek-Egyptian embedded channels in combination, reporting checked across two of the three channels before it reaches the council.

**Age of the network.** The commercial-cover network in the Roman ports is generations old. The dedicated military-technical placement in the northern provinces has stood since about 408 to 412, and this tasking is written to be answerable in the ports.

A report is expected at the next turn.

# TERRITORY

## Held

**Mediterranean and Near East.** The Nile Valley and Delta. Cyrenaica and Tripolitania. Cyprus. The Levantine coast. The Asia Minor coastal holdings. The Sinai. The Yemen coast, Aden and the former Himyarite highlands, holding the Bab-el-Mandeb. The Western Desert oasis chain and the forward Saharan stations.

**Africa.** Nubia and the Nile to Khartoum. The former Aksumite highlands. Djibouti and the Horn. The East African coast entire. The East African highlands to the Rift, the Lake Victoria basin and the Great Lakes. The Zimbabwe plateau. The Copperbelt. The Zambezi corridor and the Hwange coalfields. The Cape region and the Highveld and Botswana grassland. Namibia interior, Lesotho, Eswatini. The Congo basin through river control from the Livingstone Falls portage, with the industrial city and the aluminum plant 1,000 miles up the river behind the cataracts. The deep Congo interior and the southern Sahel margins. The Gold Coast and the Akan goldfields. The West African coast from the Congo mouth to Senegal. The trans-Saharan corridor and the integrated Sahel. Morocco's Atlantic coast and the Western Saharan coast.

**Indian Ocean.** Madagascar, a full province.

**Western Atlantic.** The Plata estuary and its two river systems: the two facing estuary harbours, one on the rocky north point and one on the flat mud shore opposite, and seventeen towns up the Paraná and the Uruguay as far as the Paraguay confluence some 900 miles inland, on level treeless black-soil grass. 250,000 settlers, wheat, cattle and wine. The tropical face: 34 fortified harbours and river posts from the Guiana mouths past the Amazon delta and south along the reef and mangrove coast, taking in the reef harbour at the continent's eastern corner, the bay of All Saints, the São Francisco mouth and the landlocked bay under the granite headland 1,200 miles further south. 180,000 settlers, dyewood, hardwood and latex. The Amazon to 1,900 miles of ascent, 28 posts at the tributary mouths, the Xingu, Tapajós, Madeira, Negro, Purus and Juruá, with a 31-mile portage railway past the rapids of the Madeira. Twenty-two posts on the cloud-forest flank of the mountain wall, the highest at about 9,000 feet, with a wagon road and a 40-mile rail link up from the navigable head.

**Caribbean.** The continental shore west and north from the Guiana rivers: fortified harbours at the Essequibo, Demerara and Berbice mouths, at the Orinoco delta, along the dry cactus coast beyond it, at the gulf of Maracaibo, and westward to the narrow neck of the continent, which is the westernmost ground taken. Garrisons victualled to hold three months cut off. The island arc north from Trinidad, nine islands under harbour post, each within sight of the last. 96,000 settlers across the continental coast and the arc.

**The grazing coast, the passage and the Pacific.** South of the Plata, the wind-scoured treeless scrub plain: five harbour stations, at the shallow bay six days' sail south where the grassland gives out, at the double gulf behind the whale peninsula, and at the Deseado, Santa Cruz and Gallegos mouths, with runs pushed forty to eighty miles up each valley. 14,000 settlers, mostly Cape and Highveld men, sheep and hides. The passage 1,300 miles south of the Plata, a sheltered strait between the mainland and a large cold island, with the coaling and provisioning station mid-strait at the second narrows where the channel is a mile and a half wide, the channel there mined and covered from both shores, linked by heliograph and radio north to the Gallegos. North of the passage, 900 miles of drowned channel coast, rain forest to the waterline and glaciers into the sea, held only at two fuel stations. Then the enclosed valley between the coast range and the mountain wall, Cyrenaican in climate: four settled districts on the snow-fed rivers, harbours at the north-open bay 1,400 miles up from the passage, at the Maipo mouth and at the Bío-Bío mouth. 62,000 settlers, wheat, grapes and olives. North of the valley, 700 miles on, the rainless shelf between sea and high desert plain, water shipped to every post: copper at three sites in the coastal ranges, nitrate from the surface beds of the inland plain. Twelve harbour posts hold the run from the passage to the tropic.

The western highland spine above the approach posts, surveyed by three routes, with the highland states in contact and trade opened at the approach posts.

The Amazon basin above the navigable head, by survey parties to the highland foot.

The Pacific coast north of the tropic, coastal survey only, and the mountain wall inland of the settled districts, which rises to the same highland spine entered from the east.

The Atlantic crossing route, in use, and discussed with no power.

The sea north and west of the island arc, and north of the narrow neck. Survey squadrons are ordered out under the standing order at Item 6 to chart what lies in that water before any settlement is placed where it could be cut off. Nothing there is known.

The southern landmass, surveyed 528 to 549 and found held throughout by another power. The western face charted from about thirty-four degrees south to where the coast turns eastward, and the southern coast eastward for a long run, both ends short of the limit of the land.
