# 525 to 550 AD

Kept by the office that holds these records. Internal. Nothing in this file is to be repeated in a letter.

---

## Standing at the Opening of the Period

The throne continues. No succession question arises in these twenty-five years, and the central weapons administration remains under the throne's hand. Garrisons are held and the doctrines settled in the previous period carry forward.

The completion of the Dnieper rail frees iron, construction gangs, surveyors and rail engineers rather than consuming them, and the contest of this period is over where that freed capacity goes. It goes to two places: the southern trunk line, and the coast that leads to the land across the eastern ocean.

---

## The Examination Cohort and the Offices That Absorb It

The registers of the examination offices count 900,000 sitting candidates per cycle at 550, against 75,000 in 450. The engine that produces them is the largest thing now happening inside the empire, it costs the treasury nothing beyond the examination itself, and it turns in a circle, so it is set out here as a circle.

**The wager.** The examination is the largest prize this state has to give and it is open to anyone who can answer the papers. A family that puts one son to letters while the others work the land is buying a ticket on an office that pays for life and lifts the whole line for generations. Printed texts made the ticket cheap. Families far below the official class therefore teach their children to read, in the countryside and the frontier settlements as readily as in the towns.

**The failures.** Most of them fail. About 1,800 candidates a cycle reach office, and the posts have not grown, so the number who fail rises with every cycle and the number who succeed does not. The 898,000 who fail do not vanish. They become tutors, clerks, scribes, letter-writers, land agents, accountants to merchant houses, works foremen and village schoolmasters. Teaching needs no capital to begin, so a failed candidate can start on the day he comes home.

**The price falls.** Where teachers are many they compete, and instruction gets cheaper every cycle. Cheap instruction puts more children in front of letters, including children whose families would never have paid the old price. Those children sit in their turn a generation later, which produces more candidates, more failures, and more teachers again, and the price falls again. The loop has now been running long enough that its second and third turns are visible in the registers, the sharpest rises in candidate numbers coming from the prefectures where teachers settled thickest two cycles earlier.

**The state's move inside the loop, and what it did.** A flood of candidates carrying cheaply printed classics cannot be separated by asking what the classics say, since a poor man with a borrowed text can memorise his way to a passing paper. The papers were therefore weighted long ago toward what printing cannot hand over: mathematics, survey, accounts, and reasoning from evidence to a conclusion. They are hardened again in 536 for the same reason and in the same direction. The effect is not the one intended. A harder paper makes a teacher who can actually teach mathematics worth far more than one who can hear a boy recite, so what teaching pays rises even as the general price of schooling falls, which makes teaching a better trade for a failed candidate, which brings more of them into it, which sends more children still. The state's instrument for thinning the field feeds the engine that fills it.

**Where it has reached.** Schooling is now cheap enough across the settled provinces that an ordinary farming or shopkeeping family sends its ablest child as a matter of course rather than as a gamble, and that is before counting what children pick up from literate parents, neighbours and shop tallies at no price at all. The state pays for the examination. The examination's failures have built everything else.

The state offices absorb 46,000 examination-trained men across the period, in these proportions:

- **The registration and audit establishment of the commanderies**, 12,000. The largest state absorption. Men trained in accounts are the instrument the estate ceiling has lacked for a hundred and seventy years.
- **The survey service**, 11,000. Rail survey for the southern trunk line, coast survey for the Northern Road, the charting of the far coast, and the resurvey of the frontier colonies' arable ground.
- **The arsenals and the central weapons administration**, 9,000. Gun founding, boring, gauging and the powder works.
- **The water works and the granary offices**, 8,000. The flood control establishment on the Yellow River and the famine granaries hold a seventh of all spending between them and have always been staffed thinly for their charge.
- **The rail and construction offices**, 6,000, on the trunk line and the Pontic works.

The merchant mills and the trading houses take a further 22,000 outside the state establishment, as clerks, foremen, machine keepers and accountants at wages the state does not match. The arsenals fill their establishment more slowly for it, and the iron and machine parts the works buy through the state monopoly are worth more to the treasury than the men are to the arsenals. The state and the merchants together take a small fraction of the cohort, and there is no prospect of the state ever employing the body the examination produces.

Cost and instrument. The additions are carried on the civil administration line, which stands at an eighth of revenue and rises with revenue. The audit and survey establishments together come to 44 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year by 550. The argument that carried the addition is that the register audit recovers land tax. The alternatives were a general rise in the land tax rate, refused because it falls hardest on the smallholders the reform exists to protect, and a second levy on the great families, refused because the estate tax already stands on that base and a new charge would have been fought as a novelty rather than as enforcement.

## Land Reform: the Register Audit of 528 to 546

The estate ceiling has been evaded since it was imposed in the 350s, chiefly by registering land under dependents and clients. The evasion holds for a reason of staffing. A commandery registrar is one official with a small establishment, sitting for years among the families whose registers he checks, and the clerks of a great estate outnumber and outlast him.

The instrument adopted in 528 is a circulating audit. Auditors are appointed from the examination cohort, posted for a fixed term of three years, never to the commandery of their birth, and required to reconcile three records the state already keeps separately: the household register, the land tax receipt by plot, and the poll tax and corvee buyout roll. A holding registered to a dependent household that pays no poll tax for the persons it claims, or that delivers grain no household of that size could farm, is marked for reassessment. In two provinces a fourth check is used, the salt monopoly's sales being written up there by household rather than by market, so that a register claiming forty persons against salt for four is marked. That is an arrangement of those two provinces and is not extended, keeping salt accounts by household costing more clerks than the check returns.

What it cost and who lost standing. The circulating auditors are made answerable to the censors rather than to the provincial registrars, which subordinates the registration officials in their own commanderies. Of the 1,100 registration officials in the audited commanderies, 190 are removed or transferred. The audit is suspended in two provinces for a year each on petition from the great families and resumed in both. The Confucian faction gains what it has asked for since the 350s. The military faction does not oppose, the recovered tax being revenue and the reform costing the arsenals nothing.

What it recovered. Audits are completed in 74 commanderies. Concealed holdings are found in 2,300 of the 9,100 estates examined. Recovered land and poll tax reaches 62 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year by 550, less than the memorial projected. The concentration itself continues, the audit reaching the register and not the market in land.

A further evasion appears in the 540s. Land is left registered to a smallholder while an estate holds a pledge against a debt the smallholder cannot clear, so that title, register entry and tax liability stay with the small household while the crop goes to the estate. All three audited records agree in such a case, nothing in them being false. Reaching it requires the debt contracts, which no office registers.

## The Frontier Colonies and the Shortening Season

Observed from the grain receipts the colony offices file and from the resurvey ordered in 531 and carried out by circulating surveyors from outside the colonies: deliveries from the garrison colonies on the dry northern and Kazakh margins fall by a fifth against their fifth-century figures, worst at the Minusinsk basin and the northernmost Mongolian posts. Sown area is little changed and yield to the unit has fallen. The colony offices report later frosts and shorter seasons in the same years, and the resurvey agrees with them.

The instruments applied:

- **Seed substitution**, ordered 533. The granary offices supply millet and short-season barley to the marginal posts in place of wheat, and hold the wheat allocation only where the resurvey shows the season still carries it. Millet yields less to the unit on good ground and ripens where wheat does not.
- **Consolidation**, 534 to 545. Forty-one of the smallest northern colony sites are closed and their households moved to surviving sites or to the Pontic grants. The garrison posts at those sites are kept and supplied by rail and river, the post being held for the ground and the colony having existed to reduce its supply burden.
- **Supplied share.** The share of the northern and Kazakh posts' food carried to them rather than grown by them rises to 22 percent by 550, a direct addition to the garrison line.

The cause lies outside anything an instrument reaches. The season shortens year on year and the dry steppe's rainfall stays what it has always been, so the colonies on that margin are managed as a standing charge.

## The Pontic Black Earth

The soil east of the Dnieper is the most fertile in the empire's reach and its barrier is the sod, heavy ox work to break at scale and beyond a thin garrison line to staff. The instrument adopted in 529 is a plough and team pool, enlarged in 535 and again in 542 when the returns show teams to be the binding constraint rather than settlers.

The pool holds 9,000 iron ploughs from the state foundries and 26,000 draft oxen at the rail towns, lent to settlement villages for the breaking season against a share of the first three harvests. Oxen are bought out of the heartland provinces, where the foundries and the rail make replacements cheap, and are moved west by rail. The whole is funded from the existing estate tax frontier settlement earmark, which the register audit has enlarged, so no new charge is raised.

By 550 the returns count 210,000 households settled along the Pontic rail towns since the grants began, three fifths of the surveyed grant land broken, and grain deliveries at 4 percent of the empire's grain receipts. The land carries more to the household than an interior farm does, the households being new and the soil deep. This is the first addition to the empire's food supply in six centuries that comes from better ground rather than from deeper extraction, and it is what carries the population above its old ceiling.

It also widens the relief valve. The landless of the heartland now have a destination worth walking to, which drains the pressure behind every revolt in the record. What it does to labour is a matter of where the poor end up rather than of what they cost. Two hundred and ten thousand households leaving the interior lift the wage a little in the counties they leave. What the valve determines is that the landless disperse outward to new ground instead of gathering in the mill towns, which bears on the order of those towns and on how large a working population the works can draw on, and does not bear on whether the works build machines, that being decided by what a machine returns rather than by what a hand costs.

## The Southern Trunk Line

The line runs from the northern and Mongolian coal country toward the Yellow River and on toward the Yangtze population centres. It was surveyed and begun in the previous period, justified as arsenal coal supply and as a road to the Vietnamese shipyards, and part funded by the Yangtze silk houses in exchange for its route passing their mulberry country.

The strategic case and the commercial case for this line point the same way, and the record should show both. The memorial argues arsenal coal and a road to the Vietnamese yards, and both are true and both are served. What the Yangtze silk houses are paying for is coal to their works and a road for their cloth to market, and the section they part-funded is the section that serves them. The court took the discount and knows what it bought with it.

It is completed in this period, reaching the Yellow River in 538 and the Yangtze mill counties in 548. Three things make it the cheapest line the empire has laid to the kilometre. The Dnieper completion released the gangs, surveyors and engineers who built it. Its route runs through settled heartland rather than across steppe, so it needs no garrison chain, no water posts and no colony to feed the work. And the silk houses carry part of the cost.

Rail spending rises to a tenth of revenue, 435 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year against 320 at 500. The military share gives up two points to pay for it and rises in absolute silver all the same. The military faction accepts the relative fall because the same line carries coal and iron to the arsenals, which is the argument it made for the line in the first place, and because nothing is taken from a standing garrison to fund it.

What the line does when it opens is put northern coal into the Yangtze counties for the first time. The three mill engine houses standing empty against its arrival are fed from 548, and the works begin converting from water wheels to coal-fired rotary engines, which frees a mill from the finite river sites. What the line changes is that the carriage constraint on the works comes off. Coal can reach a mill that is not on a river and cloth can reach a market that is not on the same river, and both were the binding limits on how large a works could usefully be. The state still holds the salt, the iron, the saltpeter and the arsenals, and still takes the surplus into the garrison system before it pools anywhere else, so the heavy industry of the empire remains the state's. What has changed is that a second kind of industry, in private hands, now has a road.

## The Mills

Observed from the licence rolls and the iron monopoly's sales books: 63 licensed works by 550, up from 9 at 525, across the Yangtze counties and the lower Yellow River. Hemp cloth works appear from the 530s, the machine and the method having been paid for by silk. The good river sites in the two most favoured counties are taken by 540, and the coal engines of 548 are the escape from that limit. Cloth from the works is more uniform than household weaving and undersells it where the works stand. Petitions from those prefectures report household weavers losing their trade, and are registered.

What the licence rolls record is no longer a tax question, and the cause of it should be set down correctly, because the court has more than once explained these works by the price of labour and that is not what drives them.

The sequence is this. A method of gathering the whole making of a thing under one roof was known to be working in Rome, through the ordinary talk that reaches traders. It was applied to silk. It produced more cloth, of more even quality, from fewer hands, and that is profit. Even cloth then took the market from household weaving because it is even, a bolt from a works being like every other bolt from that works where a household's bolts vary. Having paid for the method once, the houses carried it into hemp and will carry it into whatever else will take it. None of that turns on whether hands are cheap or dear. It would have been done at any price of labour, and it would have been done if the empire held ten million people or a hundred million, because the gain is in the throughput and the evenness rather than in the wage saved.

Two things follow. The works want people who can read a gauge, keep a tally and write a docket, and the examination is producing exactly that surplus, so the failed candidates are the clerks and foremen of the works. And the constraint that actually binds is neither hands nor money but carriage: coal to the works and cloth to the market. That is why the houses put their own silver into a trunk line the court had justified on arsenal supply and administration, and why, the moment it existed, they asked to run their goods on it. The merchants got a state railway built on a state argument and then asked to use it, and the court records that it understood the arrangement and took it.

Sixty-three mechanised works stand where there were nine, built with merchant money, and engine houses were raised at private cost against a line that had not yet opened, which is capital committed on a forecast. The beginning of an industrial change is being made in the Yangtze counties, and the merchant houses are making it rather than the state, which has licensed it, taxed it lightly, sold it its iron, and put in none of its own money.

The instrument adopted in 538 is a graduated licence fee assessed on the number of driven spindles and looms a works holds, inside the salt and iron framework the works were already licensed under. A tax on output was refused because output cannot be counted at the gate without an inspection establishment the state would have to build. A tax on profit was refused because the houses keep their own books. Closing the works, pressed by part of the Confucian faction, was refused because the iron monopoly revenue, the part funding of the trunk line and the cloth are all wanted.

The houses lose the weaver framing they won in the previous period, which is the concession the Confucian faction takes for tolerating the works. Spindles are then declared idle and machinery moved between works before assessment. From 544 the assessment is taken instead from the iron monopoly's own purchase records, a works that buys spindles and machine parts through the monopoly having already told the state what it owns. What survives the change is parts bought second hand or made inside the works from bar iron, so the assessment runs below the true count by an amount the monopoly's books cannot show. Licence fees and the monopoly's sales to the works together reach 2 percent of revenue by 550.

## The Question the Court Has Not Decided

Four things are moving at once in the Yangtze and Yellow River counties, and the court can see all four without yet knowing what they add to.

Northern coal now reaches the mill counties by rail, so cheap fuel and dense population sit on the same road for the first time. Sixty-three mechanised works stand where there were nine, built with merchant money. The houses that built them hold capital enough to part-fund a state trunk railway. And the literate population has risen sixfold in fifty years, so the clerks, foremen and accountants a works needs can be hired in any prefecture instead of being trained from nothing.

Against those, the arrangements that have held the empire steady for six centuries are still in place. The frontier and the Pontic black earth draw the landless outward instead of letting them gather in the manufacturing towns. The state holds the salt, the iron, the saltpeter and the arsenals, and takes the surplus into the garrison system before it can pool anywhere else. The works multiply regardless of either, being built for what they return rather than against the price of hands, so neither arrangement slows them and neither was designed to.

The court records that it has no settled view of which set of conditions prevails, and no policy has been framed for the question. What it can say is that the works were built without the state's money, that they are multiplying, and that the men who own them are now large enough to be a party to a public work rather than merely a subject of one. Whether that becomes a change in the nature of the economy or stays a rich trade in cloth is not answerable from 550, and the office is directed to keep the licence rolls, the iron monopoly's sales books and the works' engine counts in a single annual return so that whichever it becomes is visible early.

## The Syr Darya Line

The line was long held on appearance and could not have survived a long war. The far bank has held cannon since the 450s and twice refused to withdraw them in the exchanges of the 470s. The dispute remains open.

Observed by frontier observers from the north bank with the two-lens scope of the previous period: cannon positions counted at the river number three when first seen in the 450s and stand between twelve and thirty by 550, with drill observed at several and depot and road work behind two. Cold reading: a counted position is a displayed position, so the count is a floor and not a total, and a garrison that wishes a number seen can arrange to be counted. A mounted gun cannot be told from an empty mounting at that distance, so the upper end is the planning figure. Nothing in the count bears on that power's total artillery, which is a different quantity and is not observable from this bank.

A frontier that cannot fight a long war is one whose magazine empties before its supply arrives. The rail cuts resupply from months to weeks, and the remaining shortfall is the depth of powder held forward.

**The Central Asian powder works.** Between 527 and 541 the works at the two largest saltpeter deposits are enlarged and a second powder mill built behind the railhead. Powder needs three ingredients and one of them is a frontier product. Saltpeter, much the greatest part of the mixture by weight, is made at the deposits. Charcoal is burned in the wooded northern foothills of the Tian Shan, which the advance route has followed since the second century, and carried down to the mill. Sulfur has no worked deposit the survey has found on the western frontier and comes from the interior by rail. The saving is in tonnage rather than in independence, the mill drawing the bulk of its weight from within a few days of itself and staying tied to the line for the rest.

**The standard the magazine is measured against.** In 450 the far bank had no cannon at the river and the engagement to be provided for was short, so the stock the completed rail allowed satisfied the standard of that time. Since the far bank has cannon, the arsenals reckon in weeks where they reckoned in days. Output from the works raises the forward magazine to five weeks of sustained fire at the planning rate, and from 536 magazines are stocked and reported against a stated number of days rather than a weight of powder. Storage is outside the walls or in fireproofed works, under the policy adopted after the city fire of the 420s.

The assessment recorded for this office: the line can fight a longer engagement than at any time in three centuries, and the far bank's interior lies nearer the river than the empire's does, so a long war there is still a war of reinforcement the empire loses. The military faction pressed in the 530s for the dispute to be reopened by force. The throne spent less political capital refusing than in the previous period, the magazine programme giving the refusal something to point to.

## The Management of Appearances

Three centuries of the Syr Darya line were held on appearance, the Ternate demand was made with the powder nearly spent and no second fleet behind it, and the tributary order is kept as a form of words both courts know to be a form of words. Arranging to look stronger than the ledger is one of this state's standing instruments, and what it is doing under that head in this period is set down here so the true position is never lost behind the display.

**At the Syr Darya.** The court reads the far bank's counted positions as displayed positions and assumes the display is chosen. It arranges its own on the same principle. The powder works and the second mill are built where the road from the south bank can see them and the new magazines are sited on the visible faces of the forts rather than behind them. Gun drill on the north bank is run in the season when traffic on the river is heaviest. Relief columns are marched in by day and out by night, so that a garrison of a given size is counted more than once. What is actually behind it is five weeks of sustained fire and a line that still loses a long war of reinforcement, because the far bank's interior sits nearer the river than the empire's does.

**At sea.** Fifteen hulls carry a fully rifled broadside and nine a part-rifled one, and the fleet is worked so that no observer can tell which is which. Gunnery is exercised at the long range in waters where merchant traffic passes, always with the rifled hulls, and the guns are kept under cover in port. The intended reading is that the range belongs to the fleet rather than to fifteen ships of it.

**On the far coast, which is the bluff of this period.** The empire has seven stations of thirty-six men, relieved once a year, eight months of sailing from the nearest Korean port, with no ship of force stationed there and no means of sending one. That is the true position and it is worth stating flatly: any power that landed on that coast in strength would take the stations or ignore them, and the empire could do nothing about it inside a year and probably nothing after.

What is presented is a held frontier. The stations are built to the plan of a full commandery post, with the stockade, store range and flagstaff a garrison four times the size would carry, and the annual run lands its stores in daylight. Between the stations, stone markers are set along the shore at intervals a boat's crew can reach, each cut with the reign style and the name of the commandery the coast is entered under, so the ground reads as administered for the whole 1,900 kilometres rather than at seven points. The register enters the coast and the country behind it as claimed, on the footing of the northern forests, and the court has determined to answer for it in correspondence as settled territory of the empire rather than as an anchorage.

The instrument is the one used at the Syr Darya for three centuries and at Ternate in the 470s. A position that looks held is not probed, and the probing is what the empire cannot survive. The claim is asserted now because a claim made before anyone else is present costs seven stations and a letter, and the same claim made after another power has settled costs a war the empire cannot fight at that distance.

What would break it is a landing in force, or a foreign officer who counts the men at one station and reasons from it to the rest. Nothing can be done about either. The court's judgement is that the display is worth making anyway, on the same reasoning that kept the cannon forward on the north bank of the Syr Darya while the powder behind them would have lasted a fortnight.

**In the north.** The posted line lags the survey by about 900 kilometres and the claim runs to the full extent of the survey and beyond, as the claim to the Siberian forests has run for four centuries. No power is present to test it, which is the condition that makes the claim cheap.

The rule that governs all of this is the one the court has kept since the Syr Darya. A display is maintained only where it cannot be cheaply tested, it is never written into a letter as a figure a foreign court could later check, and the true position is written down here.

## The Naval Refit

The fleet stands at 26 purpose-built warships and 11 surviving armed escorts. Refit rather than addition is the programme, the fleet not being affordable to enlarge.

Rifled cannon are fitted through the period, limited by the boring machines and by the coal-fired steel that will hold the grooves. By 550, 15 purpose-built hulls carry a fully rifled broadside and 9 a part-rifled one, at an effective reach of 700 to 900 metres against 300 to 500 for the smooth bore. Chain and bar shot, the incendiary compound of sulfur, resin and pitch, and copper cladding below the waterline are standard on the long-range hulls.

Observed by the empire's own escort commanders and merchant captains on the Egypt run across twenty-five years: no engagement, and no foreign gun fired at sea in the eastern approaches. Cold reading: the escorts sail fixed routes in fixed seasons and would not see a drill held elsewhere, so the absence carries little. The planning assumption stays that the range advantage has a clock on it.

The range method requires favourable wind, and in calm water or a confined channel where range cannot be kept by manoeuvring it fails. The instrument adopted in 537 is the steam tender, on the reasoning that a becalmed gun ship needs moving rather than rebuilding. Four small steam hulls are built at the home and Vietnamese yards to tow a warship into or out of range, boiler pressure held low and a relief valve fitted, working within a day's run of a yard in home and Vietnamese waters.

## Research

**A light that burns from the electrical force, reached 541 to 549.** The force has run continuously since the 530s, when the generator was driven from a water wheel shaft and then from a mill engine shaft in place of a man's arm, with jars banked to even out what the shaft delivers. What the light then wanted was points that would not burn away, and that search had been run by arsenal fitters through the metals the arsenals stock and the minerals the alchemists keep, none of which endures more than minutes.

Charcoal is tried in 541. The empire has burned charcoal for the powder works for six centuries, knows its grades and hardness better than any material it handles, and has just built new burns in the Tian Shan foothills. Hard charcoal points endure for hours where metal endures for minutes, and burn back evenly enough that an attendant can close them by hand as they go.

By 550 the light stands at three installations: the boring shop of the principal arsenal, where night work on barrels was previously impossible; a second arsenal's gauge room; and the harbour head at the Vietnamese yards. Each needs a shaft already turning, an attendant to close the points, and a supply of dressed charcoal, and none can go aboard a ship or to a frontier post. The light is far brighter than any flame and painful to look at, which the arsenals turn to account by screening it and working by what it throws on the piece.

**A signal on a wire, reached 547 to 550.** The arc holds for hours, which means the shaft and jars deliver far more of the force than a signal needs, and a signal is the easier thing to make. The gauge room of the second arsenal keeps good compasses, made to the pivot standard Rome supplied two centuries ago, and in 547 the fitters record that a needle laid near the wire feeding the arc swings while the wire is live and returns when it is broken. Copper the empire has in quantity from the Kazakh workings, and Egypt long ago said that the force runs through certain metals and named copper among them.

From that a signal is worked out and put to use by 550. A wire runs from the boring shop to the gauge room, and a second runs the two li from the principal arsenal to its powder magazine outside the walls, with a compass and an attendant at each end and a code of long and short swings. It carries an order faster than a runner over that distance and it goes no further, for three reasons the fitters state: the swing weakens as the wire lengthens and is unreadable well before a useful distance; the wire must be kept off wet ground and out of weather, and no covering has been found that holds through a season outdoors; and each end must sit near a turning shaft. No line is laid between cities and none is proposed until the covering is solved.

Against what the empire's own scholars have seen at Aden, where a headland burns through the night with no attendant and wire carries word across a city, the gap narrows and does not close. Nothing has been reached about what the force is.

**A lens corrected for colour, reached 546.** The reflecting telescope's metal mirror shows no colour fringing and every glass instrument in the same observatory shows it, the difference lying in the glass bending the light. The scholar circles, which now hold that physical behaviour follows rules measurement can find, take from this that the bending of light and its splitting into colours are two behaviours rather than one, and that a glass might be found which splits differently for the same bending.

That reframes the melt search. Its object had been one better glass and becomes a pairing of two, worked against each other so the bendings add and the splittings cancel. The dense glass the search had already thrown off as a byproduct is tried against ordinary clear glass, and by 546 a paired lens is made showing only a trace of colour.

The yield is poor. The dense glass comes out uneven and only small discs grind true, so the instruments are small. Forty are made by 550 and go to the observatories, to four ships' masters and to the arsenals. The frontier scopes are not converted, there being nothing like enough good discs.

**The steam hull.** Work runs on boiler safety at sea, weight and the paddle wheel. Four low pressure tenders are in service in protected water. The boiler safe in a harbour is not trusted in a sea and the paddle wheel that works in flat water is damaged in ordinary swell, so the ocean-going hull is not built.

**The gas-tight breech, and the decision to issue it early.** Issue widens from trial units to 38 percent of frontier infantry between 531 and 548 while the failure rate stands above the standard the arsenals set for general issue.

It was pressed by the military faction and the western frontier commands, on the argument that the far bank has cannon and rate of fire is the answer the empire can field soonest. The arsenals' price of consent was three conditions: issue goes first to garrison troops at fixed posts where an armourer and a surgeon are inside the walls, every weapon is gauged at the post each season, and the muzzle-loader stays in the same magazines at full number.

The cost falls on the men holding them. The returns record burst breeches and hand and face injuries at 2.4 weapons in a hundred a year at the start of issue, falling to 0.9 by 550. Two western commands keep the older weapon in hand, and their returns show a rate of fire below what the new one gives.

The falling rate comes from gauging rather than from better metal. The breech block and its seat are cut to fixed sizes, as barrels have been since the second century, so wear can be measured against a gauge and a block condemned before it fails. A thicker breech ring is added in the same years. The limit on the improvement is how often a post can be visited to take the measurement.

**Fevers, barks and the closing of ports.** Sixty physicians and their assistants are trained at the capital and posted from 528 to the southern provinces and the highland frontier, keeping a record of which fever answers which bark and in what quantity. The quarantine protocol of the 470s is extended in 534 to all long-voyage arrivals, the northern and eastern squadrons' own hulls included. Recorded mortality at the highland and southern posts falls by a quarter against the previous generation's figures for the same posts in the same seasons. Cold reading: the establishment reporting the fall is the one funded by it, and the seasons compared differ. A measuring post below the Shan Plateau records mortality across two seasons in the 540s at a rate that keeps the plateau the empire's southern frontier.

## The Far Coast: the Expansion Axis of the Period

The crossing at forty degrees north in the westerlies is proven. The round trip is eight months of sailing, five out and three home by the far coast and the trade winds, which with the wait for the season keeps a hull away from its Korean port about a year.

Two charting voyages are made first. Four hulls sail in 532 and three return; a second crossing follows in 538. From 543 the crossing becomes a standing annual run out of the Korean ports, two hulls sailing each year and eight sailings completed by 550, the hulls working in rotation so that a year away from port costs the run nothing. The far coast is charted north of the landfall for 1,900 kilometres.

The instrument applied is the one the empire has applied at every frontier for six centuries, which is a post on the ground. Seven stations are planted between 543 and 550, at the landfall and at the viable anchorages north of it, spaced by what a boat can work between in a season. Each holds shelter, fuel, water, salt fish, a store, a boat and a garrison of 36, provisioned for two years and relieved on the annual run.

The stores arithmetic is what makes the annual run sufficient. Thirty-six men for two years is about thirty tonnes of stores, and all seven stations together about two hundred, which one hull of the ocean-going class carries with capacity to spare. So one of the two hulls each year carries the relief and the stores for the whole line, and the second carries the planting party, its timber and its ironwork to the next anchorage.

Each station trades with the peoples of its own stretch of coast for fur and fish, and the fur offsets part of the cost, which is the arithmetic that made the Siberian river posts pay in the second century. One part of that model is missing. On the Siberian rivers and in Mongolia a colony was planted beside the post to grow its food and cut the supply burden, and these stations grow nothing, so they cost more a man than any post the empire keeps and every pound they eat crosses an ocean. Garden ground is broken at the two southernmost stations in 548 to test whether the landfall latitude will carry grain. Nothing is delivered from it by 550.

The coast behind the stations is entered in the register as claimed, on the footing the empire already uses for the northern forests, where the hold is total in claim and thin on the ground and no other power is present to dispute it.

The reason for planting now rather than when weight can be put there is the reasoning that carried the western steppe for two centuries. Ground left uncontested is ground somebody else settles, and every decade of waiting is a decade in which a settlement on that coast grows into something that must be dealt with rather than merely noticed. A station costs the empire little and puts it on the ground before anyone else builds there, which is the whole of the reason for the timing. Fighting for that coast at this distance remains beyond the empire's reach.

**A standing order to the squadrons and the stations, 543.** Anything on that coast that is not of the coast's own peoples, whether a mark, a structure, a vessel, a beast or a manufactured thing, is to be recorded exactly as observed, with the distance, the season and the observer entered, and sent to the capital without the squadron or the station acting on it, taking possession of it, or drawing a conclusion from it in the field. The stations are not to be reinforced, moved or withdrawn on such a sighting until the capital has ruled. What the returns to 550 carry is the coast's own peoples, with whom the stations trade.

## The Northern Road

The Northern Road is the arm that reaches the far coast by land and ice, and the programme is now the closing of a single gap from both ends.

Twelve purpose-built hulls in two squadrons, heavily built for ice and rock, copper clad below the waterline, one working east from the Siberian river mouths and one north from Japanese waters through the island chain. Across fifty squadron-seasons the survey advances 2,800 kilometres of new coast, and 34 posts are established at viable landfalls with shelter, fuel, salt fish and a small garrison. Three hulls are condemned to ice, rock and rot against four budgeted at commissioning, and three replacements are built at the Vietnamese yards, the northern river posts having timber and no shipwrights, no ironworks and no copper. A replacement takes two seasons to reach its squadron after launch.

The posted line stands about 900 kilometres behind the head of the survey at 550, and the lag widens each season. Supply in that climate is the constraint that sets the pace, a ship being able to round and learn a coast far faster than the empire can turn that coast into a place a squadron works from. Each post trades fur, and each supplied cluster lowers the burden on the line behind it and makes the next post cheaper to plant, which is why the advance has never had to be given back anywhere the empire has run it. Cheaper per post does not make the posts arrive faster than they can be provisioned.

The gap between the head of this survey and the northernmost point charted from the far coast side stands at about 3,400 kilometres at 550. The northern squadrons deliver 112 kilometres a year between them, at 56 to the squadron-season, and the far coast side adds little more than ten, being worked from an annual run rather than a standing squadron. At those rates the gap closes in about twenty-eight years. The posted line reaches it later, on the lag above.

The Northern Road, the annual crossing run and the seven far-coast stations together cost 61 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year, about one and a half parts in a hundred of revenue, the annual run and the ocean stores being the greater part of the rise. The men and gangs come from the capacity the Dnieper completion released, and the charge is carried on the frontier and navy lines, which are not expanding anywhere else.

## Rome

The instrument adopted in 526 is a standing observation order. The Dnieper garrisons and the rail towns behind them return a fixed form each season carrying four counts: force present at the crossings within view, boat traffic on the river by season, depot and yard work observed across the water, and any inquiry received concerning the empire's interior distances, supply intervals or routes. The form comes in each season whether or not there is anything to enter, since a report filed only when something happens cannot show that nothing is happening.

What the returns hold by 550: force within view unchanged inside what the counts can distinguish, which is coarse, a garrison being reinforceable out of sight of a river bank; river traffic rising with the trade the completed rail carries; no inquiry of the fourth kind; depot work observed at two points and not distinguishable from ordinary commercial building at that distance. The court's confidence in Rome rests on two things and no others: twenty years of quiet frontier watched by its own garrisons from their own bank, and the rail method that secured the western frontier.

**The undertaking of transparency.** The agreements of the 340s to 370s closed with Rome asking for a standing transparency on developments it needed to know, naming maritime expansion among them, and the previous period told Rome the empire meant to sail toward the far land. The crossing has since been made and the stations planted. The court holds the undertaking to reach them, on two grounds: a voyage of this size cannot be kept from a power that keeps ports and merchants, and being found to have concealed it would cost the channel through which the rail method came. The letter of 527 reports the crossing and the stations.

What the letters carry is the fact of the crossing and of stations placed for water, timber and shelter. What stays out is the latitude of the crossing, the winds and currents that take a ship out and bring it home, the charts, the number of hulls, and any connection between these voyages and the Northern Road. Nothing in any letter renounces the coast or the ground behind it.

**A standing bar, entered 527, applying to every court.** The extent of the empire from north to south and from east to west, the distances between its posts, the intervals at which they are supplied, and any measurement or method from which those can be worked out are not subjects of correspondence with anyone.

## Egypt

The exchange runs at its full twenty scholars a side each decade. Confirmed across this period by the empire's own scholars with their own eyes: the headland light burns through a night without an attendant; hulls move without sail or oar and hold way against a wind; message wire carries word faster than a rider. Accounts of any third power, statements of intention and statements of what is being withheld are not confirmable by anything those scholars see, and are discounted.

The medical knowledge is the yield of the period and is worth more than anything the empire has taken from any court since the rail method. What the empire has to set against it is thin, and this office records the shape of the exchange plainly. The optical geometry the workshops use came from Egyptian instructors, who explained a relation between a glass's curve and its focal distance that Chinese scholars had recorded for decades without knowing why it held, and the grinding standards came from the same place. The measurement of the sphere was made by Egypt's own method, borrowed, and the figure was checked against Egypt's. Neither can be offered back as a gift. What remains the empire's own is the mechanical clock and the pendulum that regulates it, the deposit note, and the crafts the agreement already names.

The court applies to itself the rule it applied in 543 to the medical knowledge, when it decided that nothing received from Egypt would pass to a third court, on the ground that a partner who finds its gift in another capital narrows the channel. The rule holds in both directions: the channel is worth more than parity in it, and the court does not manufacture parity by offering back what it was given.

The electrical work, the arc light, the wire signal, the steam hull and everything of powder stay closed.

## The Maurya

The commercial terms settled after the war of the 470s hold. The overland toll stands at twelve, nothing is demanded at the strait, and merchants pass the island approaches at market rates. The cannon on the far bank of the Syr Darya remain an open matter, left to the frontier observers rather than to correspondence, a letter pressing the point inviting a test of a threat the empire cannot presently make.

The Sogdian merchant tasking is continued and is entered in the declarations below.

## State of the Realm, c. 550 AD

- **Population:** 74 million, against 70 million at 500. The Pontic black earth accounts for most of the rise, being the first new food supply the empire has gained from better ground rather than from deeper extraction. No general epidemic falls in the period.
- **Economy:** 38,500 tonnes of silver-equivalent.
- **State revenue:** 4,350 tonnes. Extraction 11.3 percent.
- **Revenue composition, in percent:** land tax 33; salt, iron and saltpeter monopolies 33; poll tax and corvee buyout 12; Silk Road tolls and silk export 9; frontier metals and fur 11; mill licences and the iron monopoly's sales to the works 2.
- **Spending, in percent:** military including garrisons, army, arsenals and navy 60; civil administration and court 13; water works 10; granaries and famine relief 4; iron rail 10; net surplus 3. The military share falls two points and rises in absolute silver to 2,610 tonnes. The rail rises to 435 tonnes, the trunk line being the cheapest the empire has laid and part funded by the silk houses.
- **Treasury:** the grain reserve is deep and the cash reserve thin. The surplus goes into the trunk line, the magazine works and the eastern programmes before it can accumulate. The empire is resilient against famine and fragile against any shock demanding mobile cash quickly.
- **Human capital:** literate population 41 million against 5 to 7 million at 500, which is about three in five of all people, near three in four in the towns and the mill counties and lower only in the remotest frontier. What has risen fastest is functional letters, the reading of common characters, contracts, notices, tallies and printed manuals, carried by households and by the failed candidates who now teach in every prefecture. The harder tier rises with it and more slowly, command of the literary language and of real mathematics not being something a literate parent can pass across the table, though the hardened papers of 536 have pushed more of the field into it than any earlier cycle. The investigation tier stands at 4,000 and the broad technical workforce at 220,000, both drawn from that harder tier rather than from the whole. The climb is steeper than the previous half century's because all three old brakes are off at once: books are cheap, the reward at the end of study is the largest the state can offer, and the first fully literate generation is teaching its own children at home.
- **Military technology:** rifled broadsides on 15 purpose-built warships and part-rifled on 9; the breech loader issued to 38 percent of frontier infantry at a failure rate of 0.9 in a hundred a year; mature hand cannon, mortars, rockets and the full explosive arsenal; tethered balloon platforms with the siege and frontier forces, unchanged since the 480s and still useless in wind; four low pressure steam tenders in home and Vietnamese water.
- **Instruments and works:** reflecting telescopes with metal mirrors at the observatories and two great halls, needing to be taken down and repolished as the mirror tarnishes; forty colour-corrected paired glass instruments, small and few; glass scopes at the frontier posts, still fringing; pendulum clocks in the observatories, great halls and yards; the arc light at three installations, each needing a turning shaft, an attendant and dressed charcoal; two wire signal runs at the arsenals, the longer of two li, each with a compass and an attendant at either end.

## Open Questions

- What the electrical force is. The light burns and the wire carries a signal two li, and no account of the force has been reached.
- A covering that holds on a wire through a season out of doors, and a swing still readable at the far end of a long line. Both stand between two arsenal runs and any line between cities.
- Whether the dense glass can be made even enough to yield discs for the frontier scopes, which is all that stands between forty instruments and general issue.
- The pledge evasion of the estate ceiling, which requires reaching debt contracts no office registers.
- The boiler at sea and the paddle wheel in swell, which stand between four harbour tenders and a hull that can cross.
- Whether the seven far-coast stations can be held once any other power is present on that shore, the empire being able to stand there and not to fight there.
- Whether the two garden plots at the landfall stations will carry grain, the far-coast posts costing more a man than any other the empire keeps while every pound they eat crosses an ocean.
- Whether the works, the coal, the merchant capital and the rising literacy amount to a change in the economy or to a rich trade in cloth. The court has no settled view and no policy. A single annual return of licence rolls, iron monopoly sales and engine counts is ordered so that the answer shows early.

## MESSAGES OUT

### To the court of Rome, sent 527, under the standing Han Rome Agreement

The rail from the interior to the frontier at the Dnieper is complete and in operation, and a transfer yard stands on the eastern bank with covered store, weighing house and standing labour.

The yard is open through the season in which the ground is passable, which in most years runs from the thaw to the first hard frost. It takes silk, lacquerware, worked steel and worked bronze outbound, and wine, oil, glass, tin, coin and horses inbound, at the tariffs of the standing agreement, which this court does not propose to alter. This court publishes no timetable and no tonnage. Consignments are to be arranged at the yard itself, season by season, between the yard's officers and whichever officer your court appoints to it, under the dispute officials the agreement already provides for, and any quantity your merchants wish to move in a season can be answered there.

One matter is reported, on the standing transparency your court asked for when the agreements were settled. Ships of this court crossed the eastern ocean, reached the far land, and have been working its coast on a standing yearly passage since. That coast is now a frontier of this empire. Posts are established along it and the shore is marked between them, the country behind is entered in the registers under a commandery of this empire as the northern forests have been entered for four centuries, and the passage out and home is held by this court's ships as a matter of course.

This court states, so that it is not later said it was left unsaid, that it will treat that coast and the land behind it as its own ground, and expects any court sending ships there to say so beforehand as this court has said it now.

It will keep to itself the latitude at which the crossing is made, the winds and currents by which a ship goes out and comes home, and the charts, as your own court kept its reckoning and said so plainly rather than let it be discovered. It is reported because it was told to you as an intention and has become a fact.

Sent as a gift are two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, which keep time more evenly than the weight and escapement clocks previously sent, with drawings of the escapement and the table relating the length of the pendulum to the beat. They are for the use of your scholars and your yards and are conditional on nothing.

One question is put, as a matter of farming practice. This court is settling farming households on the heavy grassland east of the river, whose turf resists the plough. If it is convenient to answer, this court would value your practice for breaking such ground: the form of the plough, the number of oxen in a team, the season in which the turf is best turned, and the number of years before such ground yields as ordinary field.

### To the court of Egypt, sent 529, under the scholar exchange

The knowledge of fevers and of the barks and plants that answer them is in use. Sixty physicians trained on it are posted to the southern provinces and the highland frontier, and a record is kept of which remedy answers which fever and in what quantity. The quarantine practice for ports is extended to all arrivals from long voyages.

Three things are asked, all within the exchange as it stands. First, that physicians of your own be received in residence for a term of years at the southern stations rather than at the capital only, so that they may see the fevers in the country where the fevers are. Second, your practice for keeping a ship's company in health on a voyage of many months, in respect of water, provisions and the sickness of the gums. Third, your practice for closing a port against a sickness arriving overland by a trade road, in the detail your earlier account offered.

Offered in return: two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, with drawings of the escapement and the table of the beat, this court's own work and its own to give. With them, the practice of the sealed deposit note, by which coin received at one office is paid against a stamped certificate at another and the coin itself does not travel, which has carried this court's remittances and much of its merchants' trade for three centuries, together with the forms, the seals and the rules by which the offices settle between themselves. The craftsmen the agreement provides for in paper, printing, iron and steel casting and farming continue as before.

This court is aware that what it sends is smaller than what it asks for.

## SPY TASKINGS

- **Target:** the Maurya Empire.
- **What is wanted:** whether cannon are mounted on hulls and fired at sea, as against hulls reinforced and carrying nothing; the volume of powder actually produced, judged by the price and availability of saltpeter in civilian markets and by the size of the works; and the depot, road and magazine work behind the cannon positions on the south bank of the Syr Darya.
- **Cover:** merchant. Commercial districts, dockyards and the caravan trades. No approach to military facilities, arsenals or garrisons.
- **Who runs it:** the frontier intelligence establishment of the western commanderies, through hired Sogdian factors, paid by the season and not resident.
- **Age of the network:** Sogdian intermediation on these routes is three centuries old and is a commercial fact rather than an asset of the empire. The tasked factors are a standing arrangement of the last fifty years, renewed each season.
- **Egypt:** eyes only, through the twenty scholar exchange, the representatives being irreplaceable and a failed attempt forfeiting the window itself.

## TERRITORY

**Held.** The two river valleys; the four Korean commanderies with tributary standing in the south of the peninsula; Vietnam, with the Shan Plateau the southern frontier; Mongolia, Manchuria and Inner Mongolia with the coal country; the Siberian river posts and fur stations, and the Minusinsk basin, contracting; the Kazakh steppe in administered depth with its three frontier cities and the rail; the Syr Darya north bank, railed and magazined to five weeks; the Pontic steppe east of the Dnieper, with 210,000 settled households, three fifths of the grant land broken and the completed rail.

New this period: the southern trunk line, complete to the Yellow River in 538 and to the Yangtze mill counties in 548; 34 posts on the northern coast; 7 stations on the far coast across the eastern ocean, planted 543 to 550 at the landfall and the anchorages north of it, with the coast behind them entered as claimed on the footing used for the northern forests; 41 northern colony sites closed, their garrison posts kept and supplied.

**Surveyed and not held.** 2,800 kilometres of new northern coast, the posted line about 900 kilometres behind the survey head and falling further back each season; 1,900 kilometres of the far coast north of the landfall, beyond the seven stations; the 3,400 kilometre gap between the head of the northern survey and the northernmost charted point on the far side, being worked from both ends; the interior behind the far coast, unentered and unknown.
