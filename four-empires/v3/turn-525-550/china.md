# 525 to 550 AD

Kept by the office that holds these records. Internal. Nothing in this file is to be repeated in a letter.

---

## Standing at the Opening of the Period

The throne continues. No succession question arises in these twenty-five years, and the central weapons administration remains under the throne's hand.

The doctrines settled in the previous period are carried forward unchanged and are not restated here. Conquest remains halted, garrisons are held, the navy is maintained and not expanded, and the single steam hull is kept in home water.

---

## The Examination Cohort and the Offices That Absorb It

By 550 the registers of the examination offices show, on the empire's own count and with the usual undercount in the frontier commanderies, something on the order of four to seven times as many sitting candidates per cycle as in 450. The governing tier the examinations select is not much larger, because the number of posts has not much changed. The layer beneath it is where the growth lands.

The offices that absorb the cohort in this period, in order of the numbers taken:

- **The registration and audit establishment of the commanderies.** The largest single absorption, described in the next section. Men trained in accounts are the instrument the estate ceiling has lacked for a hundred and seventy years.
- **The survey service.** Rail survey for the southern trunk line, coast survey for the Northern Road, and the resurvey of the frontier colonies' arable ground as the season shortens. Survey is the office that grows fastest in proportion, because every other programme in this file waits on it.
- **The arsenals and the central weapons administration.** Gun founding, boring, gauging, and the powder works. The arsenals take fewer men than they ask for, for the reason given below.
- **The water works and the granary offices.** The flood control establishment on the Yellow River and the famine granaries hold near a seventh of all spending between them and have always been staffed thinly for their charge. They receive clerks who can keep a stock account.
- **The mills.** The merchant works of the Yangtze counties take clerks, foremen and machine keepers at wages the state does not match. This is the first period in which a private employer competes with the arsenals for examination-trained men, and the arsenals lose some of that competition. The court records the loss and does not act against it, because the mills purchase their iron and machine parts through the state iron monopoly and the revenue from that purchase is worth more than the men.

Cost and instrument. The additions are carried on the civil administration line, which stands near an eighth of revenue throughout and rises with revenue rather than as a share. The audit and survey establishment together add on the order of a twentieth to a fifteenth of that line, which is to say something in the range of twenty-five to forty tonnes of silver-equivalent a year by 550. The argument that carried the addition is that the register audit recovers land tax, and the recovery is real and smaller than the argument claimed. The alternative instruments were a general rise in the land tax rate, refused because it falls hardest on the smallholders the reform exists to protect, and a new levy on the great families, refused because the estate tax already exists and a second charge on the same base would have been fought as a novelty rather than as enforcement.

## Land Reform: the Register Audit of 528 to 546

Concern ranked third in this court's standing is internal order, and the maintenance of it is never allowed to lapse. The estate ceiling imposed in the 350s has been evaded since it was imposed, chiefly by registering land under dependents and clients. The reason the evasion held is a matter of staffing. A commandery registrar is one official with a small establishment, sitting for years among the families whose registers he checks, and the clerks of a great estate outnumber and outlast him.

The instrument adopted in 528 is a circulating audit. Auditors are appointed from the examination cohort, posted for a fixed term of three years, never to the commandery of their birth, and required to reconcile three records that the state already keeps separately: the household register, the land tax receipt by plot, and the poll tax and corvee buyout roll. A holding registered to a dependent household that pays no poll tax for the persons it claims, or that delivers grain in a quantity no household of that size could farm, is marked for reassessment. The salt monopoly's sale records are used as a fourth check in the two provinces where those records are kept by household rather than by market, because a household register claiming forty persons that buys salt for four is a paper household.

What it cost and who lost standing. The great families resisted through petition and through the provincial officials whose standing rested on quiet relations with them. The circulating auditors are made answerable to the censors rather than to the provincial registrars, which subordinates the registration officials in their own commanderies and is the concrete loss of standing in this reform. Something in the range of one in eight to one in five of the registrars in the audited commanderies are removed or transferred across the period, and the audit is suspended in two provinces for a year each on complaint before being resumed. The Confucian faction gains what it has asked for since the 350s. The military faction does not oppose, because the recovered land tax is revenue and because the reform costs the arsenals nothing.

What it recovered. On the audit offices' own returns, marked as their own count and not independently checked against anything outside the state's registers, concealed holdings are found in something between a fifth and a third of audited estates, and the recovered land and poll tax comes to roughly one to two percent of total revenue by 550. That is less than the memorial projected. The mechanism the reform does not touch is the underlying concentration itself, which continues.

A new evasion is detected late in the period, in the 540s, and is not closed. Land is left registered to a smallholder while an estate holds a pledge against a debt the smallholder cannot clear, so that the title, the register entry and the tax liability stay with the small household while the crop goes to the estate. The audit's three records all agree in such a case, because nothing in them is false. Closing it requires reaching the debt contracts, which the state does not register, and no instrument for that is adopted in this period.

## The Frontier Colonies and the Shortening Season

Observed directly, from the grain receipts the colony offices file and from the resurvey ordered in 531: deliveries from the garrison colonies on the dry northern and Kazakh margins fall over the period by something in the range of one tenth to one quarter against their fifth-century figures, with the worst returns at the Minusinsk basin and the northernmost Mongolian posts. The resurvey finds the sown area little changed and the yield per unit fallen, and the colony offices report later frosts and shorter seasons in the same years. Cold reading: the colony offices have an interest in reporting a bad season, because a colony that cannot feed itself is supplied from the rail rather than closed. The resurvey was ordered by circulating surveyors from outside the colonies for that reason, and the resurveyed figures agree with the colony returns within the range given, so the fall is taken as real.

The instruments applied:

- **Seed substitution.** The granary offices are ordered in 533 to supply millet and short-season barley to the marginal posts in place of wheat, and to hold the wheat allocation only where the resurvey shows the season still carries it. Millet yields less per unit than wheat on good ground and ripens where wheat does not.
- **Consolidation.** Between 534 and 545, on the order of one in ten to one in six of the smallest and least viable northern colony sites are closed and their households moved to the surviving sites or to the Pontic grants. The garrison posts at those sites are kept and supplied by rail and river, because the post is held for the ground and the colony existed only to reduce the supply burden.
- **Supplied share raised.** The share of the northern and Kazakh posts' food carried to them rather than grown by them rises from roughly a tenth to somewhere between a fifth and a quarter. This is a direct addition to the garrison line, which is already the heaviest single charge in the budget.

The empire cannot lengthen the season and cannot make the dry steppe rain. The court records the limit in those terms and treats the colonies on the cold margin as a charge to be managed rather than a problem to be solved.

The offset is the Pontic black earth east of the Dnieper, which has rain enough and whose barrier is the sod. Breaking virgin grassland turf at scale is heavy work for ox teams, and a thin garrison line cannot staff it. The instrument adopted in 529 is a plough and team pool: iron ploughs from the state foundries and ox teams held at the rail towns, lent to settlement villages for the breaking season against a share of the first three harvests, funded from the existing estate tax frontier settlement earmark rather than from a new charge. Settlement grants continue from the same earmark.

By 550 the returns show something in the range of thirty to sixty thousand households settled along the Pontic rail towns since the grants began, and broken ground in the range of a fifth to two fifths of what the surveyed grant land could carry. The towns feed themselves and deliver a small surplus, on the order of one to three percent of the empire's grain receipts, first recorded in the 540s. The ceiling on the empire's population is better read as rising than as fixed, and it has not yet risen much, because sod breaking runs at the speed of ox teams.

## The Syr Darya Line

The court's own record states that the line was long held on appearance and could not have survived a long war, and that the far bank has held cannon since the 450s and refused four times to withdraw them. Nothing in this period changes the standing of the dispute, which remains open rather than settled.

Observed directly, by frontier observers using the two-lens scope of the previous period from the north bank: counted cannon positions on the far bank rise across the twenty-five years from the twenty to forty of the 460s to something in the range of sixty to a hundred and ten, with drill observed at several of them and depot and road work observed behind two. Cold reading: a counted position is a displayed position. The count is a floor and not a total, and a garrison that wishes a number to be seen can arrange to be counted. The observers also cannot distinguish a mounted gun from a mounting without a gun at that distance, so the upper end of the range is the safer planning figure.

What the empire does about it, and the mechanism first. A frontier that cannot fight a long war is one whose magazine runs dry before its supply arrives. The rail to the Syr Darya has stood since about 450 and cuts the resupply interval from months to weeks. The remaining shortfall is the depth of powder and shot held forward. Two instruments are applied:

- **The Central Asian powder works.** Saltpeter has been known in the Central Asian territories since the 120s to 150s and has been worked for local supply. Between 527 and 541 the works at the two largest deposits are enlarged and a second powder mill is built behind the railhead, so that frontier powder is made within weeks of the frontier rather than carried from northern China. Output is stated in the arsenals' returns as sufficient to raise the forward magazine from a few days of sustained fire to something in the range of three to six weeks, depending on the rate assumed for a general engagement.
- **The magazine standard.** From 536 the forward magazines are stocked to a stated number of days of sustained fire rather than to a weight of powder, and the frontier commands report against that standard each season. Storage is outside the walls or in fireproofed works, under the standing policy adopted after the city fire of the 420s.

What this costs and what is given up. Iron and coal go to the magazine works, the naval gun refit and the rail in the same years, and the foundries can be pushed only so far before one programme starves another. The southern trunk line from the northern coal country toward the Yellow River and the Yangtze, surveyed and begun in the previous period, advances more slowly than the survey allowed for, and its completion moves out of this period. The rail line remains near a twelfth of all spending and the trunk line is not finished.

The honest assessment recorded for the office's own use: the line can now fight a longer engagement than at any time in three centuries, and the court still does not believe it could be reinforced faster than the far bank can reinforce, because the far bank's interior is nearer to it than the empire's interior is. No offensive operation is undertaken and none is prepared. The military faction pressed again in the 530s for the cannon dispute to be reopened by force and was refused, at a smaller cost in political capital than the refusal of the previous period, because the magazine programme gives the refusal something to point to.

## The Naval Clock

Observed directly in this period by the empire's own escort commanders and merchant captains on the Egypt run: no engagement occurs, and no foreign gun is observed fired at sea in the eastern approaches across twenty-five years. Cold reading: that absence is weak evidence, since the escorts sail fixed routes in fixed seasons and would not see a gunnery drill held elsewhere. The paid Sogdian reporting of the previous period, discounted as an interested channel, stands unrevised. The planning assumption remains that the range advantage has a clock on it and that the clock is running.

The empire's answer is refit rather than expansion, because the navy is not affordable to expand and that finding is not reversed. Between 526 and 548:

- Rifled naval cannon move from the few in trials at 500 to something in the range of a third to a half of the purpose-built warships' broadside, limited by the boring machines and by the supply of coal-fired steel that will hold the grooves. Effective reach is recorded at 700 to 900 metres against the 300 to 500 of the smooth bore.
- Chain and bar shot, the incendiary compound of sulfur, resin and pitch, and copper cladding below the waterline are standard on the long-range hulls.
- Fleet numbers are held flat. Hulls condemned by age are replaced and not added to.

The named weakness in the method is not touched by any of this. The range advantage requires favourable wind, and in calm water or a confined channel where range cannot be kept by manoeuvring it erodes. Steam is the answer to wind dependence and the empire has one hull in home water trials, which is not a warship and not trusted at sea.

The instrument adopted in 537, on the reasoning that a becalmed gun ship needs to be moved rather than to be a steam ship, is the steam tender. Two to four small steam hulls are built at the home and Vietnamese yards to tow a sailing warship into or out of range in calm water and confined channels. Boiler pressure is held deliberately low, a relief valve is fitted, and the tenders are worked only within a day's run of a yard. By 550 the tenders are in service in home and Vietnamese waters and have not been sent to the eastern approaches, because a boiler failure at distance is a lost hull and a lost crew, and because a tender seen abroad teaches a watcher what the empire has.

## The Mills and the Tax Question

The previous period licensed the merchant silk works under the existing salt and iron framework, taxed them as an ordinary weaver's operation on the merchants' own argument that a works is one man with an unusual tool, and required them to buy iron and machine parts through the state iron monopoly. That framing was recorded as unresolved and certain to be tested by the scale of the profits. Twenty-five years is long enough that leaving it alone would itself have been a decision, and the court does not leave it alone.

What is observed, from the licence rolls and the iron monopoly's sales books, which are the state's own records: licensed works rise from a handful to something in the range of forty to ninety across the Yangtze counties and the lower Yellow River, hemp cloth works appear from the 530s because the machine and the method were already paid for by silk, and the good river sites in the two most favoured counties are taken by about 540. Iron monopoly sales to the works become a line worth watching in its own right. Cloth from the works is more uniform than household weaving and undersells it in the counties where the works stand. Petitions from those prefectures report household weavers losing their trade. The petitions are registered and no relief is granted beyond the ordinary famine and granary provision, because the displacement has not produced disorder and the garrisons stand where they stood.

The instrument adopted in 538 is a graduated licence fee inside the salt and iron framework, assessed on the number of driven spindles and looms the works holds, not on its profit and not on its cloth. No new legal instrument is created. The alternatives were considered and refused: a tax on output, refused because output cannot be counted at the gate without an inspection establishment the state would have to build and staff; a tax on profit, refused because the houses keep their own books and the state has no way to open them; and closing the works, pressed by part of the Confucian faction and refused because the iron monopoly revenue, the part funding of the southern trunk line and the cloth itself are all wanted.

Who lost and what it yielded. The merchant houses lose the weaver framing they won in the previous period and pay on a schedule that rises with the size of the works, which is the concession the Confucian faction takes in exchange for tolerating the works at all. The yield is small. Licence and iron monopoly revenue from the works together come to on the order of one to two percent of total revenue by 550, against the houses' profits, which the court cannot measure and does not pretend to. The evasion appears immediately and is recorded: spindles are declared idle, and machinery is moved between works before assessment. Assessment rests on inspection, the inspection establishment is thin, and no addition to it is funded in this period.

The mills still run on water wheels. The rotary steam engine is the escape from the finite river sites and it binds a mill to coal instead, which must be carried, and the southern trunk line that would carry it is unfinished. Three works build engine houses against the line's arrival. None of them is fed by rail coal by 550.

## The Northern Road

The programme is the outpost chain put on water, which is the model the empire has run for six centuries, and it is the only route by which the eastward crossing confirmed in the previous period can be supplied. Twelve purpose-built hulls in two squadrons, one working east from the Siberian river mouths and one north from Japanese waters through the island chain, heavily built for ice and rock, copper clad below the waterline.

Recorded from the squadrons' own returns:

- Surveyed coast advances at 50 to 150 kilometres a season where the season is worked, with idle months forced by ice at the northern end and by the typhoon season of roughly June to November at the southern end. Across the period this comes to something in the range of eight hundred to two thousand kilometres of new surveyed coast, taking the two squadrons together, which is at or a little below the one to a few thousand kilometres per generation the programme was costed against.
- Posts are established at viable landfalls, shelter, fuel, salt fish and a small garrison, not forts, because there is no cavalry confederation to deny ground to and supply points suffice. The posted line lags the survey by a wide margin, and the lag grows rather than closes.
- Hulls condemned or lost to ice, rock and rot over twenty-five years run in the range of two to four of the twelve, against an attrition of three to five budgeted at commissioning. Replacements are built at the Siberian river yards and at the Vietnamese yards.
- Steam does not go into these hulls. The standing rule holds, that a boiler failure on an unmapped frozen shore means a crew dies rather than drifts.

Why the treasury tolerates the charge. The programme is small, on the order of a third to two thirds of one percent of revenue a year, it is carried inside a navy line that is not expanding elsewhere, and the posts carry fur. Frontier metals and fur already stand near a tenth of revenue, and a post that trades fur offsets part of its own cost by the same arithmetic that made the Siberian river posts pay in the second century. The programme is not self-funding and is cheaper than its face cost.

Supply, not seamanship, remains the constraint. A ship can round and learn a coast far faster than the empire can make that coast into something a squadron can operate from. The estimate of two to three later periods, roughly fifty to seventy-five years, for the survey to reach the far shore is carried forward without improvement, and the capability to do anything on that coast still trails the survey by about a period.

## The Eastward Crossing

The court reasoned the far land into existence from the measured sphere before any ship of any power was known to have reached it, and the expedition of the previous period confirmed that the far land is real, that the low latitude band from ten degrees south to twenty degrees north is closed to an eastbound ship, and that the crossing runs in the westerlies at about forty degrees north with a return by descending the coast to about twenty north and riding the trades home.

What is funded in 532 is a charting voyage and not a settlement. Three to five hulls, restaged through Korean ports at the latitude of the crossing, provisioned against the proven five month outbound and three month return with the margin the first crossing showed to be needed, carrying compass, telescope, quadrant and the pendulum clocks of the previous period's clockmakers, which hold time better than the mechanical clocks the first expedition carried and therefore give a better reckoning of longitude by dead reckoning against a known rate.

The task set is narrow. Fix the landfall latitude and the coast north of it, chart northward toward the country the Northern Road survey is working south and east toward, and establish whether the two lines can be made to meet. The reason the treasury tolerates it is that the far coast is where the Northern Road is going in any case, so charting it from the sea side is survey work already inside the programme's purpose, and a charting voyage costs a fraction of a squadron.

Recorded outcome by 550: the crossing is made more than once, the landfall latitude is confirmed, and something in the range of one to two thousand kilometres of the far coast north of the landfall is charted, with the gap between that charting and the Northern Road's own surveyed line left unclosed. Voyage attrition is one hull in the range of the voyages made. No post is established on the far coast, no claim is made on it, and no timber, water or ground there is held. The court records that the empire is charting a coast it cannot yet reach with weight, and that by the time the posted line can carry weight there, any settlement another power has placed on that continent will have had decades to grow. That fact is recorded now, while the programme is still cheap to shape.

## Research

Every programme below runs on a skill the arsenals or the scholar circles already hold, a material the empire can source, and an office whose men depend on the programme continuing.

Men, funding and windows for each programme are in the RESEARCH block below and are not repeated here. What is entered here is what the programmes produced and, where they failed, the named cause.

**The steam hull.** Two to four low pressure tenders in protected water, described above. No ocean-going hull. The boiler that is safe in a harbour is not trusted in a sea, and the paddle wheel that works in flat water is damaged in ordinary swell.

**The lens that corrects colour.** Some thousands of recorded melts produce no correction of colour. The search fails on schedule and the money is not pulled, which is the arrangement it was funded under. Two byproducts are worth less than the object: a clearer glass that grinds with fewer flaws, which improves the telescopes and the frontier observers' scopes, and a denser glass useful for lamp chimneys and for the sight glasses on the tenders' boilers.

**A continuous source of the electrical force.** The step taken in the 530s is to drive the hand generator from a water wheel shaft and then from a mill engine shaft, which gives force for as long as the shaft turns, with jars banked to store it. This is enough for a bright spark between two points and for heating a wire, and not enough for a light that burns a night. Sustaining it fails for two reasons: no regulator the arsenals hold will keep a shaft at an even speed, and the points burn away in minutes. No account of what the force is has been reached.

**The gas-tight breech.** Issue widens from trial units to a fifth to a half of frontier infantry. The arsenals' returns record a failure rate falling across the period and still above the standard set for general issue.

**Fevers, barks and the closing of ports.** Physicians are posted to the southern provinces and the highland frontier from 528, and the quarantine protocol of the 470s is extended in 534 to all long-voyage arrivals, including the northern squadrons' own hulls. Recorded mortality at the highland and southern posts falls by a tenth to a third against the previous generation's figures for the same posts in the same seasons. Cold reading: the establishment reporting the fall is the establishment funded by it, and the seasons compared differ. A measuring post is placed below the Shan Plateau for two seasons in the 540s to record mortality under the new methods rather than to hold ground, and the mortality recorded is high enough that the southern limit is not moved and no lowland advance is proposed.

## Rome and the Standing Observation

The policy of no espionage in Roman territory is not changed. The four tests the court set for its rating of Rome being wrong are checkable from ground the empire already holds, and the instrument adopted in 526 is a standing observation order. The Dnieper garrisons and the rail towns behind them return a fixed form each season carrying four counts: force present at the crossings within view, boat traffic by season, depot and yard work observed across the river, and any inquiry received concerning interior distances, supply intervals or routes. The form is returned whether or not there is anything to enter, because a report filed only when something happens cannot show that nothing is happening.

Recorded by 550 from those returns and from nothing else: force within view unchanged inside the range the counts can distinguish; river traffic rising with the trade the completed rail carries; no inquiry of the fourth kind received; depot work observed at two points and not distinguishable from ordinary commercial building at that distance. Cold reading: no test is triggered and all four returns are weak instruments. The rating stands unchanged as credit rather than as a finding, and the order continues because it costs nothing beyond the paper.

The court decides in 543 not to pass the medical knowledge received from Egypt to any other power, on the reason that the exchange producing it is the only channel to knowledge the empire cannot reach by its own work, and a partner who finds its gift in a third court has reason to narrow the channel.

**The yard's lift is not published, ruled 527.** A tonnage per season together with the days on which trains arrive is the lift of the line behind the yard. The court's own planning against the far bank of the Syr Darya turns on that same quantity for the other side, which is the reason the figure is treated as one, and it falls under the standing bar on supply intervals and on figures from which they can be worked out. The instrument used instead is arrangement at the yard, season by season, against what is actually offered. The price of the ruling is paid by the silk houses and the frontier merchants, who wanted a published capacity and a timetable to plan a season's purchases against and who now have to send factors to the yard and settle there. Volume at the junction is expected to grow more slowly for it. The court accepts the slower growth, trade routes and access standing sixth among its concerns and the security of the frontier system standing above them.

## Egypt and the Exchange

The exchange is worked at its full twenty scholars per side per decade throughout. Confirmed across this period by the empire's own scholars with their own eyes: the flameless light burns through a night and is installed widely rather than at one showpiece; hulls move without sail or oar and hold way against a wind; message wire carries word faster than a rider. Not confirmed by anything those scholars have seen: any account of a third power, any statement of intention, any statement of what is withheld. Those stay discounted.

The medical trials are the yield of the period. Lens geometry and the pendulum clock stay open in the other direction. The electrical work, the steam hull and everything of powder stay closed.

**A ruling on the sphere, entered 529.** A measurement of the sphere taken from two stations is the latitude of both stations and the distance between them, so the method and the instruments together are the baseline, and the baseline is the empire's north to south extent with the working shown. That falls under the standing bar on interior distances and on any figure from which they can be worked out. The ruling is that the resulting figure may be sent and the stations, the baseline and the working may not. The cost of the ruling is small, because the method was Egypt's before it was the empire's and no instruction in it is being withheld, only the ground it was applied to.

**What the record does not settle.** The previous period's entry states that the empire opened the sphere and circumference as measured from its own posts. Whether the stations and the baseline went with the figure at that time is not recorded, and this office cannot establish it from anything it holds. The possibility that the extent was already given is therefore left open rather than assumed either way, and no attempt is made to recall it, a disclosure once made not being recoverable. From 529 the rule is explicit and applies to every court.

## The Maurya

No letter is sent. The commercial terms settled after the war of the 470s are working: the overland toll stands at twelve, no payment is demanded at the strait, and merchants pass the island approaches at market rates. The cannon on the far bank of the Syr Darya remain an open matter. The court does not reopen them by letter, on the reasoning that a letter pressing the point invites a test of a threat the empire cannot presently make, the fleet not being expanded, the marines not being enlarged, and the far bank's fortified island not having been attempted when the fleet was at its strongest.

The Sogdian merchant tasking is continued. It is the empire's one clandestine operation and it is placed in the declarations below.

## State of the Realm, c. 550 AD

Figures are the empire's own, from the census and tax registers, with the usual undercount in the frontier commanderies and the usual softness in any total reduced to silver weight. Ranges are left as ranges.

**Where the opening figures come from.** The last population figure this office holds is the one in its own data spine at 500 AD, about seventy million, against a peak of about seventy-two million at 450 that the epidemic of the 460s cut back. No figure is recorded for 525. The record of the previous period carries none, so the 525 baseline is itself reasoned from the 500 figure rather than counted, and every figure below inherits that softness. The same applies to the economy and revenue totals, whose last recorded values are about thirty-six thousand and about four thousand tonnes of silver-equivalent at 500.

- **Population:** 71 to 72.5 million. No general epidemic is recorded in the period. The rate assumed is the one the registers showed across 400 to 450, roughly two million in fifty years, which is what this empire's heartland has carried while farmed to the limit of its tools. Growth comes from the heartland and from the Pontic settlement towns and is offset at the northern and Kazakh margins, where the colonies are contracting. The figure approaches the 450 peak and cannot be said from these registers to have passed it.
- **Economy:** 37,000 to 38,000 tonnes of silver-equivalent, on the growth rate of the previous half century with a small addition for the mill cloth and the first Pontic grain.
- **State revenue:** 4,200 to 4,400 tonnes. Extraction near eleven percent, perhaps a little above it, the audit recovery and the mill licence being the additions.
- **Revenue composition, in percent:** land tax 33; salt, iron and saltpeter monopolies 34; poll tax and corvee buyout 12; Silk Road tolls and silk export 9; frontier metals and fur 11; mill licences and the iron monopoly's sales to the works 1. The monopolies and the land tax now stand level within the accuracy these shares carry, which continues a movement of six centuries and is not a decision of this period.
- **Spending, in percent:** military including garrisons, army, arsenals and navy 61; civil administration and court 13; water works 10; granaries and famine relief 4; iron rail 8; net surplus 4.
- **Treasury:** the grain reserve remains deep and the cash reserve remains thin. The surplus is committed to the magazine works, the trunk line and the northern squadrons before it can accumulate, which is the habit of every century in this record. The empire remains resilient against famine and fragile against any shock that demands large amounts of mobile cash quickly.
- **Human capital:** literate population 6.5 to 9 million, against the 5 to 7 million recorded at 500; urban and administrative literacy in the range of twelve to twenty percent; the deliberate-investigation tier 1,200 to 1,700; the broad technical workforce 60,000 to 80,000. The first fully literate generation of parents is now teaching its own children their first characters at no cost, which is the mechanism that lowers the price of the next cohort.
- **Military technology:** rifled naval cannon on a third to a half of the purpose-built broadsides; the breech loader in field issue on a fifth to a half of frontier infantry with a failure rate above the standard set for general issue; mature hand cannon, mortars, rockets and the full explosive arsenal; low pressure steam tenders in home and Vietnamese water; telescopes and scopes improved by the clearer glass; pendulum clocks in the observatories, the yards and the long-voyage hulls.

## Open Threads Carried Forward

- The ocean-going steam hull is not achieved. The boiler at sea and the paddle wheel in swell remain unsolved and funded.
- The lens that corrects colour has failed on schedule after some thousands of recorded melts, and the search continues at its funding.
- The continuous source of the electrical force is not reached. The force is now available while a shaft turns and cannot be sustained as a light, for want of a speed regulator and of points that do not burn away.
- The breech loader's failure rate is falling and has not reached the standard for general issue.
- The southern trunk line is not completed, iron having gone to the frontier magazine works and the naval refit. Three mill engine houses stand unfed by rail coal.
- The pledge evasion of the estate ceiling is detected and not closed. Closing it requires reaching debt contracts the state does not register.
- The colonies on the cold margin are a growing charge. The season continues to shorten and no instrument exists to change that.
- The Pontic black earth is a fifth to two fifths broken. The ceiling is rising slowly at the speed of ox teams.
- The naval range advantage remains on a clock. No foreign gun has been observed fired at sea, which is weak evidence.
- Counted cannon on the far bank of the Syr Darya have risen to sixty to a hundred and ten displayed positions, a floor and not a total. The dispute stays open.
- The Northern Road's posted line lags its survey by a widening margin, with fifty to seventy-five years still estimated to the far shore.

## MESSAGES OUT

### To the court of Rome, sent 527, under the standing Han Rome Agreement

The rail from the interior to the frontier at the Dnieper is complete and in operation, and a transfer yard has been built on the eastern bank with covered store, weighing house and standing labour.

The yard is open for exchange through the season in which the ground is passable, which in most years runs from the thaw to the first hard frost. It accepts silk, lacquerware, worked steel and worked bronze outbound, and wine, oil, glass, tin, coin and horses inbound. Goods will be delivered to the yard and taken up from it at the tariffs of the standing agreement, which this court does not propose to alter.

This court does not publish a timetable or a tonnage. Consignments will be arranged at the yard itself, season by season, between the yard's officers and whichever officer your court appoints to it, under the dispute officials the standing agreement already provides for. Any quantity your merchants wish to move in a given season can be answered at the yard, and this court expects the arrangement to grow by what is actually offered rather than by a figure agreed in advance.

Sent with this letter as a gift are two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, which keep time more evenly than the weight and escapement clocks previously sent, together with drawings of the escapement and the table relating the length of the pendulum to the beat. They are given for the use of your scholars and your yards and are not conditional on anything.

One question is put, as a matter of farming practice. This court is settling farming households on the heavy grassland east of the river, whose turf resists the plough. If it is convenient to answer, this court would value your practice for breaking such ground: the form of the plough, the number of oxen in a team, the season in which the turf is best turned, and the number of years before such ground yields as ordinary field.

### To the court of Egypt, sent 529, under the scholar exchange

The knowledge of fevers and of the barks and plants that answer them has been put to use. Physicians trained on it are now posted to the southern provinces and to the highland frontier, and a record is kept of which remedy answers which fever and in what quantity. The quarantine practice for ports has been extended to all arrivals from long voyages.

Three things are asked, all within the exchange as it stands. First, that physicians of your own be received in residence for a term of years at the southern stations rather than at the capital only, so that they may see the fevers in the country where the fevers are. Second, your practice for keeping a ship's company in health on a voyage of many months, in respect of water, provisions and the sickness of the gums. Third, your practice for closing a port against a sickness arriving overland by a trade road, in the detail your earlier account offered.

Offered in return and without condition: two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum with the drawings of the escapement and the table of the beat; and the geometry of lenses as this court's workshops now hold it, being the rule relating the curve of a glass to the distance at which it gathers light, with the grinding practice by which such curves are held true.

On the sphere, this court sends the figure it arrived at and nothing further. Its scholars reckon the circumference at about forty-five thousand kilometres by the measures of this court, and hold that figure to agree with yours within the margin the instruments of either allow. The stations from which the observations were taken, the distance between them and the working are not sent, this court's practice being that the extent of its territory is not a subject of correspondence. The method was yours before it was this court's, so nothing is kept from you in it that you did not already hold.

## SPY TASKINGS

**One operation is run in these twenty-five years.**

- **Target:** the Maurya Empire.
- **What is wanted:** whether cannon are mounted on hulls and fired at sea, as against reinforced hulls carrying nothing; the volume of powder actually produced, by the price and availability of saltpeter in civilian markets and by the size of the works; and the depot, road and magazine work behind the cannon positions on the south bank of the Syr Darya.
- **Cover:** merchant. Commercial districts, dockyards and the caravan trades only. No approach to military facilities, no approach to arsenals, no attempt on any garrison.
- **Who runs it:** the frontier intelligence establishment of the western commanderies, through hired Sogdian factors, paid per season and not resident.
- **Age of the network:** Sogdian intermediation on these routes is three centuries old and is a commercial fact rather than an asset of the empire. The tasked factors are a standing arrangement of roughly the last fifty years, renewed each season.
- **Egypt:** eyes only, through the twenty scholar exchange. No clandestine work. The representatives are irreplaceable and a failed attempt forfeits the window itself.
- **Rome:** none. The policy of no espionage in Roman territory is not changed. Observation is by the Dnieper garrisons from ground the empire holds, on the standing seasonal form.

## TERRITORY

**Held.** Unchanged in extent from 525 except as noted. The two river valleys; the four Korean commanderies with tributary standing in the south of the peninsula; Vietnam and the Shan Plateau as the southern limit; Mongolia, Manchuria and Inner Mongolia with the coal country; the Siberian river posts and fur stations, and the farmed pocket of the Minusinsk basin, contracting; the Kazakh steppe in administered depth with the three frontier cities and the rail; the Syr Darya north bank, magazined and railed; the Pontic steppe east of the Dnieper, now with settled farming towns along the completed rail and a plough and team pool.

Reductions inside the held line: one in ten to one in six of the smallest northern and Kazakh colony sites closed between 534 and 545, their garrison posts kept and supplied by rail and river.

Additions inside the held line: settlement grants and broken sod on the Pontic black earth, thirty to sixty thousand households and a fifth to two fifths of the surveyed grant land; new posts on the Northern Road at viable landfalls, the posted line lagging the survey.

No conquest. No territory taken from any power. Nothing held on the far coast across the eastern ocean.

**Surveyed and not held.** Eight hundred to two thousand kilometres of new northern coast, taken by the two squadrons together, the posted line well behind it. One to two thousand kilometres of the far coast north of the crossing landfall, charted from the sea, with no post, no claim and no ground held. The southern trunk line's route from the northern coal country toward the Yellow River and the Yangtze, surveyed and part built. The lowland below the Shan Plateau, measured for mortality across two seasons in the 540s and not advanced into.
