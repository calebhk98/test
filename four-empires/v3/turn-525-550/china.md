# 525 to 550 AD

Kept by the office that holds these records. Internal. Nothing in this file is to be repeated in a letter.

---

## Standing at the Opening of the Period

The throne continues. No succession question arises in these twenty-five years, and the central weapons administration remains under the throne's hand. Garrisons are held and the doctrines settled in the previous period carry forward.

The completion of the Dnieper rail frees iron, construction gangs, surveyors and rail engineers rather than consuming them, and the contest of this period is over where that freed capacity goes. It goes to two places: the southern trunk line, and the coast that leads to the land across the eastern ocean.

---

## The Examination Cohort and the Offices That Absorb It

The registers of the examination offices count 900,000 sitting candidates per cycle at 550, against 75,000 in 450. The engine that produces them is the largest thing now happening inside the empire, and it costs the treasury nothing beyond the examination itself.

**The wager.** The examination is the largest prize this state has to give and it is open to anyone who can answer the papers. A family that puts one son to letters while the others work the land is buying a ticket on an office that pays for life and lifts the whole line for generations. Printed texts made the ticket cheap. Families far below the official class therefore teach their children to read, in the countryside and the frontier settlements as readily as in the towns.

**The failures.** Most of them fail. About 1,800 candidates a cycle are placed in ranked office, and the ranked establishment is fixed by the number of graded posts the throne maintains, which has not grown. Unranked employment is a separate matter and has grown a great deal, the auditors, surveyors and clerks counted further down being hired on the civil line rather than placed in ranked office, and they are drawn from the failures rather than from the 1,800. So the number who fail rises with every cycle and the number who take rank does not. The 898,000 who fail do not vanish. They become tutors, clerks, scribes, letter-writers, land agents, accountants to merchant houses, works foremen and village schoolmasters. Teaching needs no capital to begin, so a failed candidate can start on the day he comes home.

**The price falls.** Where teachers are many they compete, and instruction gets cheaper every cycle. Cheap instruction puts more children in front of letters, including children whose families would never have paid the old price. Those children sit in their turn a generation later, which produces more candidates, more failures, and more teachers again, and the price falls again. The loop has now been running long enough that its second and third turns are visible in the registers, the sharpest rises in candidate numbers coming from the prefectures where teachers settled thickest two cycles earlier.

**The state's move inside the loop, and what it did.** A flood of candidates carrying cheaply printed classics cannot be separated by asking what the classics say, since a poor man with a borrowed text can memorise his way to a passing paper. The papers were therefore weighted long ago toward what printing cannot hand over: mathematics, survey, accounts, and reasoning from evidence to a conclusion. They are hardened again in 536 for the same reason and in the same direction. The effect is not the one intended. A harder paper makes a teacher who can actually teach mathematics worth far more than one who can hear a boy recite, so what teaching pays rises even as the general price of schooling falls, which makes teaching a better trade for a failed candidate, which brings more of them into it, which sends more children still. The state's instrument for thinning the field feeds the engine that fills it.

**Where it has reached.** Schooling is now cheap enough across the settled provinces that an ordinary farming or shopkeeping family sends its ablest child as a matter of course rather than as a gamble, and that is before counting what children pick up from literate parents, neighbours and shop tallies at no price at all. The state pays for the examination. The examination's failures have built everything else.

The state offices absorb 46,000 examination-trained men across the period, in these proportions:

- **The registration and audit establishment of the commanderies**, 12,000. The largest state absorption. Men trained in accounts are the instrument the estate ceiling has lacked for a hundred and seventy years.
- **The survey service**, 11,000. Rail survey for the southern trunk line, coast survey for the Northern Road, the charting of the far coast, and the resurvey of the frontier colonies' arable ground.
- **The arsenals and the central weapons administration**, 9,000. Gun founding, boring, gauging and the powder works.
- **The water works and the granary offices**, 8,000. The flood control establishment on the Yellow River and the famine granaries hold a seventh of all spending between them and have always been staffed thinly for their charge.
- **The rail and construction offices**, 6,000, on the trunk line and the Pontic works.

The merchant mills and the trading houses take a further 22,000 outside the state establishment, as clerks, foremen, machine keepers and accountants at wages the state does not match. The arsenals fill their establishment more slowly for it, and the iron and machine parts the works buy through the state monopoly are worth more to the treasury than the men are to the arsenals. The state and the merchants together take a small fraction of the cohort, and there is no prospect of the state ever employing the body the examination produces.

Cost and instrument. The additions are carried on the civil administration line, which rises by a full point across the period, 85 tonnes of silver-equivalent, and the whole of that rise is accounted for. The audit and survey establishments come to 44 tonnes of it. The physicians and their assistants take a further small charge, as does the saltpeter buyer registration extended in 535, which is licensing clerkwork in the interior commanderies and is carried here because there is nowhere else for it. The rest is the ordinary administration of new people and new ground: 210,000 households newly settled on the Pontic black earth need registrars, granary clerks, magistrates and tax offices where there were none, and the frontier and coastal stations need clerking of their own. The argument that carried the addition is that the register audit recovers land tax. The alternatives were a general rise in the land tax rate, refused because it falls hardest on the smallholders the reform exists to protect, and a second levy on the great families, refused because the estate tax already stands on that base and a new charge would have been fought as a novelty rather than as enforcement.

## Land Reform: the Register Audit of 528 to 546

The estate ceiling has been evaded since it was imposed in the 350s, chiefly by registering land under dependents and clients. The evasion holds for a reason of staffing. A commandery registrar is one official with a small establishment, sitting for years among the families whose registers he checks, and the clerks of a great estate outnumber and outlast him.

The instrument adopted in 528 is a circulating audit. Auditors are appointed from the examination cohort, posted for a fixed term of three years, never to the commandery of their birth, and required to reconcile three records the state already keeps separately: the household register, the land tax receipt by plot, and the poll tax and corvee buyout roll. A holding registered to a dependent household that pays no poll tax for the persons it claims, or that delivers grain no household of that size could farm, is marked for reassessment. In two provinces a fourth check is used, the salt monopoly's sales being written up there by household rather than by market, so that a register claiming forty persons against salt for four is marked. That is an arrangement of those two provinces and is not extended, keeping salt accounts by household costing more clerks than the check returns.

What it cost and who lost standing. The circulating auditors are made answerable to the censors rather than to the provincial registrars, which subordinates the registration officials in their own commanderies. Of the 1,100 registration officials in the audited commanderies, 190 are removed or transferred. The audit is suspended in two provinces for a year each on petition from the great families and resumed in both. The Confucian faction gains what it has asked for since the 350s. The military faction does not oppose, the recovered tax being revenue and the reform costing the arsenals nothing.

What it recovered. Audits are completed in 74 commanderies. Concealed holdings are found in 2,300 of the 9,100 estates examined. Recovered land and poll tax reaches 62 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year by 550, less than the memorial projected. The concentration itself continues, the audit reaching the register and not the market in land.

A further evasion appears in the 540s. Land is left registered to a smallholder while an estate holds a pledge against a debt the smallholder cannot clear, so that title, register entry and tax liability stay with the small household while the crop goes to the estate. All three audited records agree in such a case, nothing in them being false. Reaching it requires the debt contracts, which no office registers.

## The Suppression Margin

The third of the pressures carried into this century, beside the land question and the thin treasury, is the slow narrowing of the advantage by which this dynasty has put down every rising for five hundred years. The formula left state control in the 350s and the monopoly was declared lost in the previous period. What the saltpeter monopoly now protects is scale and quality rather than the secret, and no instrument exists to recover the secret.

What the returns show across the period. Crude powder is seized in the interior provinces in 41 separate instances between 528 and 549, chiefly in the northern commanderies near the older deposits, in quantities from a few catties to one cache of several hundredweight. Saltpeter appears in interior markets from sources the licensing rolls cannot account for, and the price of it in those markets falls slightly across the period, which is the reading the court trusts most, a price falling while licensed output is unchanged meaning supply from somewhere else. Cold reading on all of this: seizures measure the diligence of garrisons as much as the quantity in private hands, and a rising count of seizures in a period when the audit put more officials on the roads is not by itself proof that more powder exists.

The audit of 528 to 546 bears on the same question, and the court sets the two beside each other deliberately. The reform stripped 190 registration officials, was suspended in two provinces on petition, and reaches the register without reaching the concentration of land, so the great families end the period wealthier, more numerous in their dependents, and more aggrieved than they began it. They are also the only private parties in the empire with the means to make powder in quantity rather than in catties. No rising occurs in these twenty-five years and none is expected in the next few. The point entered here is that the arithmetic which made suppression cheap for five centuries, a garrison of fifty holding against many times its number, does not hold against an opponent with even crude powder, and the empire is manufacturing the grievance and losing the margin at the same time.

The instruments adopted are small, because nothing large is available. The first is carried on the civil administration line and the other three on the military line, which is unitemised and absorbs them without a visible change. Registration of saltpeter buyers is extended in 535 from merchants trading the substance to any purchaser above a stated quantity, which catches the careless and not the determined. The fire-siting rules made after the city fire of the 420s are extended in 539 to private stores of any powder found and forfeited, since the forfeited stock has to go somewhere. Interior garrison drill is rewritten in 542 on the assumption that an opposed force may hold crude powder and fight from cover rather than close in the open. And the forward magazine standard written for the Syr Darya, so many days of sustained fire rather than so great a weight of powder, is applied to the interior garrison magazines as well, so that a commandery can be told what it could actually fight.

The honest position. Suppression is no longer to be assumed to cost weeks. What the empire holds against an armed rising is no longer the possession of powder but the scale of its manufacture, the quality of its metal, rifling, range, and the rate of fire of a breech-loading weapon, which are advantages of degree that a determined opponent narrows rather than advantages of kind that he cannot obtain at all.

## The Frontier Colonies and the Shortening Season

Observed from the grain receipts the colony offices file and from the resurvey ordered in 531 and carried out by circulating surveyors from outside the colonies: deliveries from the garrison colonies on the dry northern and Kazakh margins fall by a fifth against their fifth-century figures, worst at the Minusinsk basin and the northernmost Mongolian posts. Sown area is little changed and yield to the unit has fallen. The colony offices report later frosts and shorter seasons in the same years, and the resurvey agrees with them.

The instruments applied:

- **Seed substitution**, ordered 533. The granary offices supply millet and short-season barley to the marginal posts in place of wheat, and hold the wheat allocation only where the resurvey shows the season still carries it. Millet yields less to the unit on good ground and ripens where wheat does not.
- **Consolidation**, 534 to 545. Forty-one of the smallest northern colony sites are closed and their households moved to surviving sites or to the Pontic grants. The garrison posts at those sites are kept and supplied by rail and river, the post being held for the ground and the colony having existed to reduce its supply burden.
- **Supplied share.** The share of the northern and Kazakh posts' food carried to them rather than grown by them rises to 22 percent by 550, a direct addition to the garrison line.

The cause lies outside anything an instrument reaches. The season shortens year on year and the dry steppe's rainfall stays what it has always been, so the colonies on that margin are managed as a standing charge.

## The Pontic Black Earth

The soil east of the Dnieper is the most fertile in the empire's reach and its barrier is the sod, heavy ox work to break at scale and beyond a thin garrison line to staff. The instrument adopted in 529 is a plough and team pool, enlarged in 535 and again in 542 when the returns show teams to be the binding constraint rather than settlers.

The pool holds 9,000 iron ploughs from the state foundries and 54,000 draft oxen at the rail towns, six to the team, virgin sod needing four to eight and the first enlargement having been ordered when teams rather than settlers proved binding, lent to settlement villages for the breaking season against a share of the first three harvests. Oxen are bought out of the heartland provinces, where the foundries and the rail make replacements cheap, and are moved west by rail. The whole is funded from the existing estate tax frontier settlement earmark, which the register audit has enlarged, so no new charge is raised.

By 550 the returns count 210,000 households settled along the Pontic rail towns since the grants began, three fifths of the surveyed grant land broken, and grain deliveries at 4 percent of the empire's grain receipts. The land carries more to the household than an interior farm does, the households being new and the soil deep. This is the first addition to the empire's food supply in six centuries that comes from better ground rather than from deeper extraction, and it is what carries the population past its old peak rather than merely back to it.

It also widens the relief valve. The landless of the heartland now have a destination worth walking to, which drains the pressure behind every revolt in the record. What it does to labour is a matter of where the poor end up rather than of what they cost. Two hundred and ten thousand households leaving the interior lift the wage a little in the counties they leave. What the valve determines is that the landless disperse outward to new ground instead of gathering in the mill towns, which bears on the order of those towns and on how large a working population the works can draw on, and does not bear on whether the works build machines, that being decided by what a machine returns rather than by what a hand costs.

## The Southern Trunk Line

The line runs from the northern and Mongolian coal country toward the Yellow River and on toward the Yangtze population centres. It was surveyed and begun in the previous period, justified as arsenal coal supply and as a road to the Vietnamese shipyards, and part funded by the Yangtze silk houses in exchange for its route passing their mulberry country.

The strategic case and the commercial case for this line point the same way. The memorial argues arsenal coal and a road to the Vietnamese yards, and both are true and both are served. What the Yangtze silk houses are paying for is coal to their works and a road for their cloth to market, and the section they part-funded is the section that serves them. The court took the discount and knows what it bought with it.

The line runs 1,150 kilometres from the pits to the mill counties. It is completed in this period, reaching the Yellow River in 538 and the Yangtze mill counties in 548, which is a pace near 50 kilometres a year against the 20 to 40 the western lines managed. The difference is iron delivered rather than iron made. On the western route every rail was hauled thousands of kilometres to the railhead and the pace was set by that haul; this line is laid between the pits it burns and the foundries it is made in, and the rail travels tens of kilometres to reach the works. Three further things make it the cheapest the empire has laid to the kilometre. The Dnieper completion released the gangs, surveyors and engineers who built it. Its route runs through settled heartland rather than across steppe, so it needs no garrison chain, no water posts and no colony to feed the work. And the silk houses carry part of the cost.

One smaller work belongs on the same line. The junction of the two rail systems proposed to Rome in the previous period was never settled, so between 526 and 531 the empire builds its own transfer yard on the eastern bank at the Dnieper railhead, with covered store, weighing house, standing labour, and from 544 a separate dry store for the western goods now coming up the road from Rome. It cost 9 tonnes of silver-equivalent to build and takes 2 tonnes a year to run, both found inside the rail line's 392 tonnes rather than added to it. A yard on the empire's own bank needs no agreement with anyone to build and none to use.

Rail spending stands at 9 parts in a hundred of revenue, 392 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year against 320 at 500. With the trunk line finished in 548 the establishment does not stand down. Three works carry it forward at 550: branch lines from the trunk into the mill counties and the mulberry country, part funded again by the houses on the same terms; the southern extension from the trunk toward the Vietnamese yards, which the navy has asked for since the yards became its only source of ocean hulls; and the relaying of the oldest Kazakh track, laid in the 400s and now worn past safe loading over long stretches. The military share gives up two points to pay for it and rises in absolute silver all the same. The military faction accepts the relative fall because the same line carries coal and iron to the arsenals, which is the argument it made for the line in the first place, and because nothing is taken from a standing garrison to fund it.

What the line does when it opens is put northern coal into the Yangtze counties for the first time. The three mill engine houses standing empty against its arrival are fed from 548, and the works begin converting from water wheels to coal-fired rotary engines, which frees a mill from the finite river sites. What the line changes is that the carriage constraint on the works comes off. Coal can reach a mill that is not on a river and cloth can reach a market that is not on the same river, and both were the binding limits on how large a works could usefully be. The state still holds the salt, the iron, the saltpeter and the arsenals, and still takes the surplus into the garrison system before it pools anywhere else, so the heavy industry of the empire remains the state's. What has changed is that a second kind of industry, in private hands, now has a road.

## The Mills

Observed from the licence rolls and the iron monopoly's sales books: 63 licensed works by 550, up from 9 at 525, across the Yangtze counties and the lower Yellow River. Hemp cloth works appear from the 530s, the machine and the method having been paid for by silk. The good river sites in the two most favoured counties are taken by 540, and the coal engines of 548 are the escape from that limit. Cloth from the works is more uniform than household weaving and undersells it where the works stand. Petitions from those prefectures report household weavers losing their trade, and are registered.

What the licence rolls record is no longer a tax question. The court has more than once explained these works by the price of labour, and that is not what drives them.

The sequence is this. A method of gathering the whole making of a thing under one roof was known to be working in Rome, through the ordinary talk that reaches traders. It was applied to silk. It produced more cloth, of more even quality, from fewer hands, and that is profit. Even cloth then took the market from household weaving because it is even, a bolt from a works being like every other bolt from that works where a household's bolts vary. Having paid for the method once, the houses carried it into hemp and will carry it into whatever else will take it. The gain is in the throughput and the evenness rather than in the wage saved, so the decision to build a works does not wait on hands becoming dear. What the supply of hands does bear on is how large a works can grow once built, and there the frontier settlement tells against the works by drawing outward the people who would otherwise be at their gates.

Two things follow. The works want people who can read a gauge, keep a tally and write a docket, and the examination is producing exactly that surplus, so the failed candidates are the clerks and foremen of the works. And the constraint that actually binds is neither hands nor money but carriage: coal to the works and cloth to the market. That is why the houses put their own silver into a trunk line the court had justified on arsenal supply and administration, and why, the moment it existed, they asked to run their goods on it. The merchants got a state railway built on a state argument and then asked to use it, and the court records that it understood the arrangement and took it.

Sixty-three mechanised works stand where there were nine, built with merchant money, and engine houses were raised at private cost against a line that had not yet opened, which is capital committed on a forecast. The beginning of an industrial change is being made in the Yangtze counties, and the merchant houses are making it rather than the state, which has licensed it, taxed it lightly, sold it its iron, and put in none of its own money.

The instrument adopted in 538 is a graduated licence fee assessed on the number of driven spindles and looms a works holds, inside the salt and iron framework the works were already licensed under. A tax on output was refused because output cannot be counted at the gate without an inspection establishment the state would have to build. A tax on profit was refused because the houses keep their own books. Closing the works, pressed by part of the Confucian faction, was refused because the iron monopoly revenue, the part funding of the trunk line and the cloth are all wanted.

The houses lose the weaver framing they won in the previous period, which is the concession the Confucian faction takes for tolerating the works. Spindles are then declared idle and machinery moved between works before assessment. From 544 the assessment is taken instead from the iron monopoly's own purchase records, a works that buys spindles and machine parts through the monopoly having already told the state what it owns. What survives the change is parts bought second hand or made inside the works from bar iron, so the assessment runs below the true count by an amount the monopoly's books cannot show. Licence fees and the monopoly's sales to the works together reach 13 tonnes of silver-equivalent by 550, which is three parts in a thousand of revenue and does not show as a whole point in the composition below. A works buys a few tonnes of iron in a year, worth tens of kilograms of silver, so the monopoly side was never going to be large, and the licence is assessed to be borne rather than to be felt.

## The Question the Court Has Not Decided

Four things are moving at once in the Yangtze and Yellow River counties, and the court can see all four without yet knowing what they add to.

Northern coal now reaches the mill counties by rail, so cheap fuel and dense population sit on the same road for the first time. Sixty-three mechanised works stand where there were nine, built with merchant money. The houses that built them hold capital enough to part-fund a state trunk railway. And the literate population has more than trebled in fifty years, so the clerks, foremen and accountants a works needs can be hired in any prefecture instead of being trained from nothing.

Against those, the arrangements that have held the empire steady for six centuries are still in place. The frontier and the Pontic black earth draw the landless outward instead of letting them gather in the manufacturing towns. The state holds the salt, the iron, the saltpeter and the arsenals, and takes the surplus into the garrison system before it can pool anywhere else. Neither arrangement stops a works being built, since a works is built for what it returns rather than against the price of hands. Both bear on how large the works can grow: the valve keeps the labour they can draw on dispersed, and the monopolies keep the iron and the fuel in the state's gift.

The court records that it has no settled view of which set of conditions prevails, and no policy has been framed for the question. What it can say is that the works were built without the state's money, that they are multiplying, and that the men who own them are now large enough to be a party to a public work rather than merely a subject of one. Whether that becomes a change in the nature of the economy or stays a rich trade in cloth is not answerable from 550, and the office is directed to keep the licence rolls, the iron monopoly's sales books and the works' engine counts in a single annual return so that whichever it becomes is visible early.

## The Syr Darya Line

The line was long held on appearance and could not have survived a long war. The far bank has held cannon since the 450s and twice refused to withdraw them in the exchanges of the 470s. The dispute remains open.

Observed by frontier observers from the north bank with the two-lens scope of the previous period: cannon positions counted at the river number three when first seen in the 450s and stand between twelve and thirty by 550, with drill observed at several and depot and road work behind two. Cold reading: a counted position is a displayed position, so the count is a floor and not a total, and a garrison that wishes a number seen can arrange to be counted. A mounted gun cannot be told from an empty mounting at that distance, so the upper end is the planning figure. Nothing in the count bears on that power's total artillery, which is a different quantity and is not observable from this bank.

A frontier that cannot fight a long war is one whose magazine empties before its supply arrives. The rail cuts resupply from months to weeks, and the remaining shortfall is the depth of powder held forward.

**The Central Asian powder works.** Between 527 and 541 the works at the two largest saltpeter deposits are enlarged and a second powder mill built behind the railhead. Powder needs three ingredients and one of them is a frontier product. Saltpeter, much the greatest part of the mixture by weight, is made at the deposits. Charcoal is burned in the wooded northern foothills of the Tian Shan, which the advance route has followed since the second century, and carried down to the mill. Sulfur has no worked deposit the survey has found on the western frontier and comes from the interior by rail. The saving is in tonnage rather than in independence, the mill drawing the bulk of its weight from within a few days of itself and staying tied to the line for the rest.

**The standard the magazine is measured against.** In 450 the far bank had no cannon at the river and the engagement to be provided for was short, so the stock the completed rail allowed satisfied the standard of that time. Since the far bank has cannon, the arsenals reckon in weeks where they reckoned in days. Output from the works raises the forward magazine to five weeks of sustained fire at the planning rate, and from 536 magazines are stocked and reported against a stated number of days rather than a weight of powder. Storage is outside the walls or in fireproofed works, under the policy adopted after the city fire of the 420s.

The assessment recorded for this office: the line can fight a longer engagement than at any time in three centuries, and the far bank's interior lies nearer the river than the empire's does, so a long war there is still a war of reinforcement the empire loses. The military faction pressed in the 530s for the dispute to be reopened by force. The throne spent less political capital refusing than in the previous period, the magazine programme giving the refusal something to point to.

**What the returns of 536 to 546 show behind the far bank, and what it means.** The mountain road that feeds that frontier has been cut wider and metalled in its bad stretches, with new stone relay houses and water, and a caravan crosses in noticeably fewer days than before and loses no animals. Three walled towns are new on the road, each with a supervised market. Four great grain stores stand on the road, two new and two enlarged, with grain going in at a season when it should be going out, and a store clerk says the standing order is now that the store be full through the winter and stand full into the next season where in his father's time it emptied by spring. Cart traffic runs nearly all one way and nearly all on state hire. Behind the bank, stone houses worked by a service that lodges apart from the line troops, and more of them than there were.

The empire has spent this period curing exactly the same defect on its own side, by rail, by a local powder works and by a magazine standard counted in days of fire. The far bank is curing it by road, relay, granary and magazine. Read together, the two frontiers are being made able to hold a longer war than either could hold before, and the side that finishes first is the side whose interior is nearer, which is not this one. This is the most serious change on that river since the cannon appeared on it, and it is the first military problem of the next period.

The honest entry on the faction question. The military faction pressed in the 530s to reopen the dispute by force and was refused, and on the facts as they now stand that faction had the better reading of where the balance was going. The refusal was still correct, because the empire could not have won a war of reinforcement in the 530s either and the magazines were not yet built. What the refusal bought was peace and what it cost was position, and the court records both rather than crediting itself with foresight it did not have.

## The Management of Appearances

Three centuries of the Syr Darya line were held on appearance, the Ternate demand was made with the powder nearly spent and no second fleet behind it, and the tributary order is kept as a form of words both courts know to be a form of words. Arranging to look stronger than the ledger is one of this state's standing instruments. What it is doing under that head in this period follows, with the true position under each.

**At the Syr Darya.** The court reads the far bank's counted positions as displayed positions and assumes the display is chosen. It arranges its own on the same principle. The powder works and the second mill are built where the road from the south bank can see them and the new magazines are sited on the visible faces of the forts rather than behind them. Gun drill on the north bank is run in the season when traffic on the river is heaviest. Relief columns are marched in by day and out by night, so that a garrison of a given size is counted more than once. What is actually behind it is five weeks of sustained fire and a line that still loses a long war of reinforcement, because the far bank's interior sits nearer the river than the empire's does.

**At sea.** Fifteen hulls carry a fully rifled broadside and nine a part-rifled one, and the fleet is worked so that no observer can tell which is which. Gunnery is exercised at the long range in waters where merchant traffic passes, always with the rifled hulls, and the guns are kept under cover in port. The intended reading is that the range belongs to the fleet rather than to fifteen ships of it.

**On the far coast, which is the bluff of this period.** The empire has seven stations of thirty-six men, relieved once a year, five months' sailing out from the nearest Korean port and eight in the round trip, with no ship of force stationed there and no means of sending one. Any power that landed on that coast in strength would take the stations or ignore them, and the empire could do nothing about it inside a year and probably nothing after.

What is presented is a held frontier. The stations are built to the plan of a full commandery post, with the stockade, store range and flagstaff a garrison four times the size would carry, and the annual run lands its stores in daylight. Between the stations, stone markers are set along the shore at intervals a boat's crew can reach, each cut with the reign style and the name of the commandery the coast is entered under, so the ground reads as administered for the whole 1,900 kilometres rather than at seven points. The register enters the coast and the country behind it as claimed, on the footing of the northern forests, and the court has determined to answer for it in correspondence as settled territory of the empire rather than as an anchorage.

The instrument is the one used at the Syr Darya for three centuries and at Ternate in the 470s. A position that looks held is not probed, and the probing is what the empire cannot survive.

The display is put to Rome and put now for three reasons. Rome is the only power known to reach that landmass, so a claim not stated to Rome is stated to nobody. A claim made before a foreign settlement is grown costs a letter, and the same claim made after costs a war at eight months' distance. And the form chosen is the Dnieper article extended, notice before crossing in force, offered in both directions, which is a courtesy between courts rather than a demand and is answerable without either side losing standing. The risk accepted is plain: naming the coast tells Rome exactly where to look, and the position could not survive being looked at closely. The claim is asserted now because a claim made before anyone else is present costs seven stations and a letter, and the same claim made after another power has settled costs a war the empire cannot fight at that distance.

What would break it is a landing in force, or a foreign officer who counts the men at one station and reasons from it to the rest. Nothing can be done about either. Rome's letter, received in this period, states that Rome keeps soldiers, colonists and an administration on the same landmass, so the power that could land in force is present on that ground already and is no longer a possibility but a neighbour of unknown distance.

**The claim is narrowed on that account, and the display is not.** The draft reply to Rome claims the shore where the posts and the marks stand and drops the country behind, which this empire has never entered. What is given up is any written assertion over that interior, to the one court able to contest it, and the interior is thereby left to whoever reaches it first. What is kept is a claim that survives being asked about. The stations themselves are still built to the plan of a post four times their garrison, the marks still run the length of the charted shore, and the annual run still lands its stores in daylight, so the display on the coast is unchanged and so is the true position behind it, which is 252 men who could be taken or ignored by any power that landed in strength. The court's judgement is that the display is worth making anyway, on the same reasoning that kept the cannon forward on the north bank of the Syr Darya while the powder behind them would have lasted a fortnight.

**In the north.** The posted line lags the survey by about 900 kilometres and the claim runs to the full extent of the survey and beyond, as the claim to the Siberian forests has run for four centuries. No power is present to test it, which is the condition that makes the claim cheap.

The rule that governs all of this is the one the court has kept since the Syr Darya. A display is maintained only where it cannot be cheaply tested, it is never written into a letter as a figure a foreign court could later check, and the true position is written down here.

## The Naval Refit

The fleet stands at 26 purpose-built warships and 11 surviving armed escorts. Refit rather than addition is the programme, the fleet not being affordable to enlarge.

Rifled cannon are fitted through the period, limited by the boring machines and by the coal-fired steel that will hold the grooves. By 550, 15 purpose-built hulls carry a fully rifled broadside and 9 a part-rifled one, at an effective reach of 700 to 900 metres against 300 to 500 for the smooth bore. Chain and bar shot, the incendiary compound of sulfur, resin and pitch, and copper cladding below the waterline are standard on the long-range hulls.

**The clock has run out, and the court held the wrong instrument to hear it.** For twenty-five years the empire's own escort commanders and merchant captains on the Egypt run reported no engagement and no foreign gun fired at sea, and this office treated that absence as weak evidence and kept the planning assumption. The absence was worth even less than it was credited. The escorts sail fixed routes in fixed seasons and were never going to see a gunnery drill held in a yard's own roads, and the returns of 542 record a hull standing off and firing eleven or twelve times in a morning, and a piece run out at a shuttered port and run back in, on the same class of hull a yard hand had described as merely stiffened with iron. Guns are mounted and are fired at sea. The reading of the previous period, that hulls were being reinforced and might be carrying nothing, is wrong and is withdrawn.

What the empire keeps is the rifled reach of 700 to 900 metres on 15 hulls and the part-rifled reach on 9, against an opponent now able to answer at some range rather than at none. That is a narrower margin than the one the fleet was built around and it is not nothing. The lesson on method is entered plainly: a fleet watching the sea lanes is not an instrument for counting an opponent's guns, and the merchant network on the opponent's own quays is, and the court had both and believed the wrong one.

The range method requires favourable wind, and in calm water or a confined channel where range cannot be kept by manoeuvring it fails. The instrument adopted in 537 is the steam tender, on the reasoning that a becalmed gun ship needs moving rather than rebuilding. Four small steam hulls are built at the home and Vietnamese yards to tow a warship into or out of range, boiler pressure held low and a relief valve fitted. They are worked within a day's run of a yard, which is not where the problem they were built for arises, so the calm-water weakness in the eastern approaches stands exactly where it stood. What the four are actually doing is harbour and yard work, training crews to a boiler, and giving the engine the running hours it needs before anything of the kind is trusted at distance. The single hull of the previous period, the first the empire built, remains in home water as the trials boat, and the four were built from what it taught.

## Research

**A source that runs continuously, reached 538 to 541.** The work of this period on the problem was work on leakage, which is what stood in the way. A machine turned by hand is worked in short bursts and can be dried and rested between them. A machine belted to a shaft runs on, the rubbing surfaces heat, the frames and supports take up damp from the air of any workshop, and the charge bleeds away through the mounting as fast as the machine makes it. Nothing the arsenals held would hold a charge under continuous running: lacquered wood took up water, waxed cloth softened as the bearings warmed, and ordinary glass was too full of flaws to be cut into a support of any size.

What removed it came out of the glasshouse, and it came late because of size. The melt search had thrown off its hard dense glass and its clearer glass by 529, and small pieces of both were available from that year, which is why a corrected lens pair could be ground in 531 from discs a hand's breadth across. A generator frame wants rods and cylinders a forearm and more in length, and a piece that long cools far more unevenly than a disc and cracks or holds a strain, which is the same annealing failure that caps the lens work. Two things separate that work from the lens work. The rods are drawn from the clearer glass rather than the dense, the sluggishness that defeats the annealing belonging to the dense glass alone, and the frames want a few dozen sound rods a year where the lens programme wants thousands of sound discs, so a yield that is fatal in the one is tolerable in the other. Sound rods of the length the frames need are drawn from 537, and the machines follow at once. Mounted on glass, run in a closed housing kept dry with quicklime trays, and belted to a mill shaft, the generator holds its charge while the shaft turns. That is reached between 538 and 541, with jars banked to even out what the shaft delivers.

**A light that burns from that source, reached 545 to 549.** What the light then wanted was points that would not burn away, and that search had been run by arsenal fitters through the metals the arsenals stock and the minerals the alchemists keep, none of which endures more than minutes.

Charcoal is tried in 545. The empire has burned charcoal for the powder works for six centuries, knows its grades and hardness better than any material it handles, and has just built new burns in the Tian Shan foothills. Hard charcoal points endure for hours where metal endures for minutes, and burn back evenly enough that an attendant can close them by hand as they go.

By 550 the light stands at three installations on two sites: the boring shop of the principal arsenal, where night work on barrels was previously impossible, and that same arsenal's gauge room; and the harbour head at the Vietnamese yards. Each needs a shaft already turning, an attendant to close the points, and a supply of dressed charcoal, and none can go aboard a ship or to a frontier post. The light is far brighter than any flame and painful to look at, which the arsenals turn to account by screening it and working by what it throws on the piece.

**A signal on a wire, reached 549 to 550.** The arc holds for hours, which means the shaft and jars deliver far more of the force than a signal needs, and a signal is the easier thing to make. The gauge room of the principal arsenal keeps good compasses, made to the pivot standard Rome supplied two centuries ago, and in 549 the fitters record that a needle laid near the wire feeding the arc swings while the wire is live and returns when it is broken. Copper the empire has in quantity from the Kazakh workings, and Egypt long ago said that the force runs through certain metals and named copper among them.

From that a signal is worked out and put to use by 550. A wire runs from the boring shop to the gauge room, and a second runs the two li from the principal arsenal to its powder magazine outside the walls, with a compass and an attendant at each end and a code of long and short swings. Only the sending end needs the shaft, the receiving end wanting nothing but a needle and a man to watch it, and the magazine outside the walls has no shaft of its own. So the second run works one way: the arsenal can tell the magazine and the magazine answers by runner as it always has. It carries an order faster than a runner over that distance and it goes no further, for three reasons the fitters state: the swing weakens as the wire lengthens and is unreadable well before a useful distance; the wire must be kept off wet ground and out of weather, and no covering has been found that holds through a season outdoors; and a line can be worked only outward from a place that already turns a shaft. No line is laid between cities and none is proposed until the covering is solved.

Against what the empire's own scholars have seen at Aden, where a headland burns through the night with no attendant and wire carries word across a city, the gap narrows and does not close. Nothing has been reached about what the force is.

**Both searches are redirected on the returns of 546, and the earlier reading of the Maurya is withdrawn.** This court judged in the previous period that the Maurya had lied about mastery of the force while feeling toward something real. That judgement was wrong. They have a message line in service on the northwest road out of their capital, running many days' travel on a raised drained causeway, carrying drawn copper wrapped in baked cloth inside glazed earthen pipe, read at the far end by a hung needle under glass, spelling out words, and beating a rider by two days against an hour.

Two things in that are worth more to the arsenals than the line itself. The first is that nothing turns it. There is no wheel, no beast, no water and no mill at any of the four station houses, and the force comes from earthen jars holding plates of two metals standing in a liquor, carried out spent and replaced by cart like any stores. The limit this office entered as hard, that a line can be worked only outward from a place that already turns a shaft, is therefore not a property of the force but of the empire's own machine, and a source that consumes stores instead of motion exists and has been seen. The second is the conduit. The empire's covering problem was posed as a search for a varnish or a wrapping that would last a season outdoors, and what is in the ground on that road is a wrapping inside a pipe on a drained causeway, which is a work of civil engineering rather than a material. That the line still dies for weeks in the heaviest rains says the wet is reduced and not beaten even by that.

The metals, the liquor and the cloth were not obtained and are not guessed at here. The searches are accordingly redirected from open-ended to directed, which is a cheaper and faster kind of search: pairs of metals standing in liquors, tried across every metal the arsenals stock and every acid, brine and lye the alchemists keep, looking for a pair that gives the force without anything turning; and a conduit of glazed pipe on a drained bed, which the empire can build tomorrow out of its own kilns and gangs. Both are ordered in 547 and neither returns inside this period. Any result belongs to the next.

**A lens corrected for colour: the idea in 529, the manufacture unsolved.** The idea cost almost nothing to reach. A reflecting telescope with a metal mirror shows no colour fringing and every glass instrument standing beside it in the same observatory shows it, and the comparison was there to be made any day a scholar walked between them. The circles that now hold that physical behaviour follows rules measurement can find drew the obvious conclusion in 529: the fringing lives in the glass bending the light rather than in the light itself, so the bending and the splitting are two behaviours, and a glass that splits differently for the same bending could be set against ordinary glass so that the bendings add and the splittings cancel. The dense glass the melt search had already thrown off was the candidate, and a first corrected pair was ground and proved in 531.

Everything since has been a manufacturing problem, and it is not solved. Across 531 to 550 the glasshouses pour about 21,000 discs of the dense glass for pairing. Some 1,400 come out of the annealing without strain or trapped bubble. Some 620 of those grind to figure. About 310 pair true with a clear element and become a corrected objective, and 260 are in service at 550, the rest broken, strained in use or withdrawn.

The rejects have four named causes, and the first of them is much the largest. The dense glass is more sluggish than ordinary glass and cools unevenly, and no glasshouse in the empire can hold a disc of any size at an even heat long enough to anneal it, so nineteen discs in every twenty crack, strain or keep their bubbles before they ever reach a grinder. Of those that survive the annealing, better than half are lost at the wheel, the dense glass being softer than ordinary glass under the abrasive, so that it takes a scratch which will not polish out and a disc carried past its figure cannot be brought back. The two glasses must then be matched melt against melt, batches varying enough that a good dense disc is wasted when no clear disc of the right behaviour is on hand that season. And the two figures must agree closer than the gauges can comfortably check, so pairs that measure well still show colour when tried against a star.

The 260 go to the observatories, to the western frontier gun positions, to ships' masters and to the arsenals. General issue to the frontier scopes waits on yield and on nothing else, the empire keeping thousands of posts and the glasshouses having made 310 objectives in twenty years.

**Two methods received from Rome, neither yet in service.** The gas method, being the drawing of a lighting gas off coal with its retorts and mains, and the pneumatic method, being the carriage of written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air, arrive as enclosures late in the period. Both are put to the arsenals for trial rather than to the treasury for building, and neither is built inside these twenty-five years, so neither appears in the accounts at 550.

The assessment entered on them is short. The gas does what the empire's own arc cannot, which is light a place that has no turning shaft, and the empire has the coal, the rail to move it and the foundries to make retorts and pipe, so this is a question of building rather than of learning. The tubes want a pump and a tube, and the empire has run pumps in the coal country for a century and a half. Both were given free, against a measurement of the sphere sent in the last generation, and the office notes without comment that the two methods arriving by letter are worth more in immediate use than the arc the empire spent two decades reaching.

**The steam hull.** Work runs on boiler safety at sea, weight and the paddle wheel. Four low pressure tenders are in service in protected water. The boiler safe in a harbour is not trusted in a sea and the paddle wheel that works in flat water is damaged in ordinary swell, so the ocean-going hull is not built.

**The gas-tight breech, and the decision to issue it early.** The seal itself was settled in the previous period and is not in question. A paper cartridge swells under the firing pressure and closes the breech, and a new weapon made to gauge seals as well as anything the arsenals have built. The weapon has been in the field since then.

What was not settled is what the seal does after a few hundred rounds. Where the closure is imperfect by even a little, the escaping gas cuts the metal of the breech face and the block, and the cut widens the gap, which lets more gas past, which cuts faster. A weapon that sealed when issued stops sealing, and it does so at an accelerating rate rather than a steady one, so the failures fall on weapons that have been in hand a season or two rather than on new ones. That is a wear problem in the metal rather than a fault in the principle, and it is why the arsenals hold a general-issue standard the weapon does not yet meet. Issue widens from trial units to 38 percent of frontier infantry between 531 and 548 while the burst rate stands above that standard.

It was pressed by the military faction and the western frontier commands, on the argument that the far bank has cannon and rate of fire is the answer the empire can field soonest. The arsenals' price of consent was three conditions: issue goes first to garrison troops at fixed posts where an armourer and a surgeon are inside the walls, every weapon is gauged at the post each season, and the muzzle-loader stays in the same magazines at full number.

The cost falls on the men holding them. The returns record burst breeches and hand and face injuries at 2.4 weapons in a hundred a year at the start of issue, falling to 0.9 by 550. Two western commands keep the older weapon in hand, and their returns show a rate of fire below what the new one gives.

The falling rate comes from gauging rather than from better metal. The breech block and its seat are cut to fixed sizes, as barrels have been since the second century, so wear can be measured against a gauge and a block condemned before it fails. A thicker breech ring is added in the same years. The limit on the improvement is how often a post can be visited to take the measurement.

**Fevers, barks and the closing of ports.** Sixty physicians and their assistants are trained at the capital and posted from 528 to the southern provinces and the highland frontier, keeping a record of which fever answers which bark and in what quantity. The quarantine protocol of the 470s is extended in 534 to all long-voyage arrivals, the northern and eastern squadrons' own hulls included. Recorded mortality at the highland and southern posts falls by a quarter against the previous generation's figures for the same posts in the same seasons. Cold reading: the establishment reporting the fall is the one funded by it, and the seasons compared differ. A measuring post below the Shan Plateau records mortality across two seasons in the 540s at a rate that keeps the plateau the empire's southern frontier.

**The bark, received late in the period.** Egypt sends the preparation and dosage of a bark it states breaks the intermittent fevers, tried by its physicians across eleven hundred cases of the fevers as they run in hot country, and sends it without price and unasked. If it holds on this empire's own ground it reaches the thing that has stopped this empire at the Shan Plateau for four hundred years, which no weapon, no rail and no garrison has ever reached.

The court orders trial and orders nothing else. The physicians already posted to the southern provinces and the highland frontier are put to running the bark against the empire's own fevers under the recording method that came with it, and the results will belong to the next period. No advance below the plateau is ordered, no force is moved and no plan is drawn, because a foreign case series is a reason to test and not a reason to march, which is the same rule the court applied to the strait claim before it would sign. The court records that it has been given something it could not have bought and that it does not yet know whether it works here.

Egypt sends the medicine and not the plant and will not say where it grows. The court reads the reservation exactly as Egypt frames it, being the same reservation this court keeps on the making of silk, and it accepts it without complaint while entering what it costs: every dose the empire uses comes from Alexandria, so a supply this empire cannot make is a hold Egypt has on any southern movement the bark would make possible. Finding the plant is entered below as a thing to be worked at.

## The Far Coast: the Expansion Axis of the Period

The crossing at forty degrees north in the westerlies is proven. The round trip is eight months of sailing, five out and three home by the far coast and the trade winds, which with the wait for the season keeps a hull away from its Korean port about a year.

Two charting voyages are made first. Four hulls sail in 532 and three return; a second crossing follows in 538. From 543 the crossing becomes a standing annual run out of the Korean ports, two hulls to the run, and eight such annual runs are completed by 550, which is sixteen hull voyages, the hulls working in rotation so that a year away from port costs the run nothing. The far coast is charted north of the landfall for 1,900 kilometres.

The instrument applied is the one the empire has applied at every frontier for six centuries, which is a post on the ground. Seven stations are planted between 543 and 550, at the landfall and at the viable anchorages north of it, spaced by what a boat can work between in a season. Each holds shelter, fuel, water, salt fish, a store, a boat and a garrison of 36, provisioned for two years and relieved on the annual run.

The stores arithmetic is what makes the annual run sufficient. Thirty-six men for two years is about thirty tonnes of stores, and all seven stations together about two hundred, which one hull of the larger ocean-going sort carries with room to spare, those hulls running near 300 tonnes against the 150 the smaller ones take. So one of the two hulls on each run carries the relief and the stores for the whole line, and the second carries the planting party, its timber and its ironwork to the next anchorage.

Each station trades with the peoples of its own stretch of coast for fur and fish, and the fur offsets part of the cost, which is the arithmetic that made the Siberian river posts pay in the second century. One part of that model is missing. On the Siberian rivers and in Mongolia a colony was planted beside the post to grow its food and cut the supply burden, and these stations grow nothing, so they cost more a man than any post the empire keeps and every pound they eat crosses an ocean. Garden ground is broken at the two southernmost stations in 548 to test whether the landfall latitude will carry grain. Nothing is delivered from it by 550.

The coast behind the stations is entered in the register as claimed, on the footing the empire already uses for the northern forests, where the hold is total in claim and thin on the ground and no other power is present to dispute it.

The reason for planting now rather than when weight can be put there is the reasoning that carried the western steppe for two centuries. Ground left uncontested is ground somebody else settles, and every decade of waiting is a decade in which a settlement on that coast grows into something that must be dealt with rather than merely noticed. A station costs the empire little and puts it on the ground before anyone else builds there, which is the whole of the reason for the timing. Fighting for that coast at this distance remains beyond the empire's reach.

**A standing order to the squadrons and the stations, 543.** Anything on that coast that is not of the coast's own peoples, whether a mark, a structure, a vessel, a beast or a manufactured thing, is to be recorded exactly as observed, with the distance, the season and the observer entered, and sent to the capital without the squadron or the station acting on it, taking possession of it, or drawing a conclusion from it in the field. The stations are not to be reinforced, moved or withdrawn on such a sighting until the capital has ruled. What the returns to 550 carry is the coast's own peoples, with whom the stations trade.

## The Northern Road

The Northern Road is the arm that reaches the far coast by land and ice, and the programme is now the closing of a single gap from both ends.

Twelve purpose-built hulls in two squadrons, heavily built for ice and rock, copper clad below the waterline, one working east from the Siberian river mouths and one north from Japanese waters through the island chain. Across fifty squadron-seasons the survey advances 2,800 kilometres of new coast, and 34 posts are established at viable landfalls with shelter, fuel, salt fish and a small garrison. Three hulls are condemned to ice, rock and rot against four budgeted at commissioning, and three replacements are built at the Vietnamese yards, the northern river posts having timber and no shipwrights, no ironworks and no copper. A replacement takes two seasons to reach its squadron after launch.

The posted line stands about 900 kilometres behind the head of the survey at 550, and the lag widens each season. Supply in that climate is the constraint that sets the pace, a ship being able to round and learn a coast far faster than the empire can turn that coast into a place a squadron works from. Each post trades fur, and each supplied cluster lowers the burden on the line behind it and makes the next post cheaper to plant, which is why the advance has never had to be given back anywhere the empire has run it. Cheaper per post does not make the posts arrive faster than they can be provisioned.

The gap between the head of this survey and the northernmost point charted from the far coast side stands at about 3,400 kilometres at 550. The northern squadrons deliver 112 kilometres a year between them, at 56 to the squadron-season, and the far coast side adds little more than ten, being worked from an annual run rather than a standing squadron. At those rates the gap closes in about twenty-eight years. The posted line reaches it later, on the lag above.

The Northern Road, the annual crossing run and the seven far-coast stations together cost 61 tonnes of silver-equivalent a year, about one and a half parts in a hundred of revenue, the annual run and the ocean stores being the greater part of the rise. The men and gangs come from the capacity the Dnieper completion released, and the charge is carried on the frontier and navy lines, which are not expanding anywhere else.

## Rome

The instrument adopted in 526 is a standing observation order. The Dnieper garrisons and the rail towns behind them return a fixed form each season carrying four counts: force present at the crossings within view, boat traffic on the river by season, depot and yard work observed across the water, and any inquiry received concerning the empire's interior distances, supply intervals or routes. The form comes in each season whether or not there is anything to enter, since a report filed only when something happens cannot show that nothing is happening.

What the returns hold by 550: force within view unchanged inside what the counts can distinguish, which is coarse, a garrison being reinforceable out of sight of a river bank; river traffic rising with the trade the completed rail carries; no inquiry of the fourth kind; depot work observed at two points and not distinguishable from ordinary commercial building at that distance. The court's confidence in Rome rests on two things and no others: twenty years of quiet frontier watched by its own garrisons from their own bank, and the rail method that secured the western frontier.

**The undertaking of transparency, and the timing of the report.** The agreements of the 340s to 370s closed with Rome asking for a standing transparency on developments it needed to know, naming maritime expansion among them, and the previous period told Rome the empire meant to sail toward the far land. The court holds the undertaking to reach the crossing, on two grounds: a voyage of this size cannot be kept from a power that keeps ports and merchants, and being found to have concealed it would cost the channel through which the rail method came.

The crossing is made in 532 and the report is not sent until 549, which is a deliberate delay and is recorded as one. A report of a crossing with nothing standing on the far shore is an invitation to go and look, and looking is the one thing the position cannot survive. A report of a posted coast, with the stations built, the shore marked and the register entered, is a statement of a frontier rather than a notice of an opportunity. So the court waited until there was something to report that would not be tested by the reporting of it, and the letter goes in 549 with the seven stations standing. The undertaking was stretched by seventeen years to do it. That is the price paid and the court paid it knowingly, holding that the alternative was to hand a rival the coast while advertising that nothing was on it.

What the letters carry is the coast answered for as settled territory of this empire rather than as an anchorage: the crossing made, the posts established, the shore marked between them, and the country behind entered in the register under a commandery. What stays out is the latitude of the crossing, the winds and currents that take a ship out and bring it home, the charts, the number of hulls, and any connection between these voyages and the Northern Road. Nothing in any letter renounces the coast or the ground behind it.

**Rome's answer, and the standing arrangement it touches.** Rome's letter reaches the crossing at some point in this period which the document does not fix, so this office cannot say whether it crossed the 549 letter, preceded it, or followed it. That uncertainty is entered because it bears on courtesy: if the letter came first, this court's 549 letter answered none of it.

On the rails Rome refuses, and refuses for the reason this court would have given. Its track is one width for three thousand miles and it will relay none of it, and it holds that a rail running from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army. This court accepts the refusal without argument, having built its own yard on its own bank in 526 to 531 precisely because the junction proposal stood unanswered, and notes that the two yards together are the transshipment Rome describes. The yard this court built is therefore not wasted and is one half of the arrangement; Rome proposes to build the other half on the Roman bank at Rome's cost, under Roman garrison and Roman law, with Han factors in it protected as Roman merchants are protected here.

The court accepts, and the acceptance is owed rather than sent, this court having spent its letter to Rome for the period. Word will go in the next period. The terms are judged good on their face: Rome pays for its own bank, the tariffs are unchanged, the standing agreement continues of itself, and the protection offered to Han factors is the reciprocal of what Roman merchants already have here. The one thing the court weighs against it is that Han factors will stand under Roman law on Roman ground, which is the ordinary condition of any merchant abroad and is accepted as such.

**A decision this letter shows to have been wrong.** This court withheld the telescope from Rome in the last generation, treating it as a weapon, and this period declined to ask Rome about the annealing of large glass on the ground that the question would disclose the optical programme. Rome has now sent the observed phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter, confirmed by its own glasses, with the printed argument current among its natural philosophers. Glasses that show the moons of Jupiter are better than anything the frontier scopes of this empire carry. The thing withheld was already held, the premise of the decision was false, and the empire spent a question it needed on the wrong court for a reason that did not exist. The annealing question went to Egypt instead and may be answered there; that does not make the reasoning behind it sound.

**What Rome states of the western land, and what follows for the far coast.** Rome states that it holds the islands, holds a stretch of the mainland coast, has taken the inland centres behind it, and keeps soldiers, colonists and an administration there. It names what it withholds rather than letting it be found: soundings, charts and surveys of any western coast or harbour, the fixed position of any station or holding, anything of their strength, and the method by which its ships fix their place at sea. It gives the imbalance of the first embassy as its reason and says it expects this court to read those refusals as Rome would read them arriving from here, which this court does.

This is a court dealing straight and it is also the single worst piece of news in the packet. The reasoning that planted seven stations on the far coast was that ground left uncontested is ground somebody else settles. That reasoning was sound and it was late. Rome was established on the other face of the same landmass with soldiers and an administration while this empire was still charting 1,900 kilometres of coast with thirty-six men to a station. Neither court knows how far the land runs between them, this empire having entered nothing of the interior, and Rome having declined to say where anything of its own stands. The claim this court asserted in 549 is therefore a claim made against a power already present on the same land rather than against an absent one, which is a different and much weaker thing than the court believed when it made it.

**The supplementary letter, and what a court that corrects itself is worth.** Rome writes a second time to say that its first letter was in three respects a paper its own files had overtaken: it proposed a transshipment yard as though none existed, when this court had built one and a Roman officer had been arranging consignments in it for years; it thanked this court for a clock already in Roman hands; and Rome states that three of its papers have now been found in that condition. It asks that the accurate conclusion be drawn, which is that it is slow to read itself rather than slow to answer.

The judgement this office makes on that habit changes how Roman letters are handled rather than how Rome is regarded. A court that hunts its own stale papers and reports them unprompted is a court whose statements can be taken as its honest belief at the moment of drafting. What they cannot be taken for is its position at the moment of arrival. The failure mode disclosed here is staleness and not deceit, and the two want different handling. Every Roman paper is therefore to be read against its drafting date rather than its delivery, and where a Roman article and a Roman act disagree, the act is preferred and the article treated as overtaken rather than as false. What Rome is doing is the practice this office keeps for its own record, which is that a thing entered wrong is entered again rather than quietly mended, and it is the reason the channel is worth what it is worth.

**The first independent check on a Roman statement in six centuries.** Rome states that it holds and intends to keep settlements in the west. Egypt, which is not Rome and has no interest in flattering it, reports in the same season and marked firm that Roman Atlantic yards have built nothing but six hundred ton ocean transports since 534, that embarkations have been counted at the Iberian quays in every season from 526, and that those going out are veterans with families rather than garrisons. The two accounts were prepared independently and agree on the substance. Egypt further declines to give any figure for how many crossed, on the ground that a count of those setting out is no count of those arriving, and a source that refuses the number it cannot establish is worth more on the numbers it does give.

This court has held Rome on credit extended rather than on anything gathered, gathering nothing in Roman territory by policy. On this one matter that is no longer so. Rome's account of its western settlement is corroborated from outside Rome, and this office marks it firm as to the fact and the character of the programme, and unknown as to its size, its position and its distance from anything of this empire's.

**The third holding, and what it does to the far coast.** Rome names what it holds by kind and not by position, and the third item is new. Two large islands with the lesser ground about them, and a stretch of mainland coast with the inland centres behind it, were both in the first letter. Settled country on the rivers of another coast was not, and is named now because Rome states that an article of this kind cannot protect ground neither court has named.

Another coast is not further defined and this office will not guess at it. What can be said is that this empire's seven stations stand on a coast of that landmass which is not the coast Rome first described, that Rome has now disclosed a holding on a coast which is likewise not the one it first described, and that neither court has said where either lies. The two may be a continent apart and may not. Until that is known the far coast is to be read as a position with a possible neighbour rather than a certain one, and the annual run's captains are to report any river settlement they meet under the standing order of 543 without approaching it.

**Rome's device answers an objection this court raised.** This court retired its own notice article as unperformable because neither party would say where its ground stood. Rome's answer is to name by kind rather than by position, which gives the article something to bite on without either court surrendering a survey. That is a better answer than this office had, and it sits inside the standing bar of 527, which forbids extent, distances and supply intervals and does not forbid saying what kinds of thing the empire holds.

**The narrowest undertaking in the packet.** Rome undertakes that it says nothing of the Han to anyone, and expressly declines to claim any general practice of carrying nothing about third powers, stating that it writes of third powers to others and did so in the same season. This office rates a small undertaking that is checkable above a large one that is not, and marks this one untested, to be watched over the coming periods through whatever this court hears of itself from other quarters.

**A small technical note.** Rome sets the pendulum clocks as its standard against the works that keep its legal and factory time. That Rome runs works on kept time is a detail this office had not held, and it bears on the merchant works of the Yangtze counties, whose method came out of Roman practice at second hand.

**A standing bar, entered 527, applying to every court.** The extent of the empire from north to south and from east to west, the distances between its posts, the intervals at which they are supplied, and any measurement or method from which those can be worked out are not subjects of correspondence with anyone.

## Egypt

The exchange runs at its full twenty scholars a side each decade. Confirmed across this period by the empire's own scholars with their own eyes: the headland light burns through a night without an attendant; hulls move without sail or oar and hold way against a wind; message wire carries word faster than a rider. Accounts of any third power, statements of intention and statements of what is being withheld are not confirmable by anything those scholars see, and are discounted.

**The undertaking on Rome's western shore is answered by Egypt itself.** The register under it stood empty and Egypt now states why: it does not hold the thing firmly, what it has remains a crossing made and cargoes returned, and it undertook not to send inference dressed as knowledge. The court accepts that as an answer and closes the thread. The empire has meanwhile put its own people on that landmass, and Rome has told this court directly what Egypt could not.

**The undertaking on the western shore is discharged in full.** Egypt reported earlier in these years that it did not hold the thing firmly and would not send inference dressed as knowledge. It has now sent it, marked firm, and marked separately as not firm the one part it could not establish. The thread is closed and closed well. This office notes that the discharge cost Egypt nothing to withhold and that Egypt sent it anyway, asking nothing and setting no condition.

**The annealing question was granted before it was asked a second time.** The request of 537 was granted in full and without condition, and the grant and this court's second asking crossed on the road. This court is therefore a court that asked twice for a thing already given. The cause is eight thousand kilometres and nothing else, no register here failed, and the matter needs no procedure built around it. What is worth keeping is the character of the grant. It was complete, it carried no condition, and it was made before there was any second asking to answer, so nothing was drawn out of Egypt by the repetition and nothing is owed on account of it.

**Egypt discloses two falsehoods it told Rome, and the court reads the disclosure two ways.** Egypt reports having told Rome that the Atlantic coast of Africa lately came under its administration, when it has held it for generations, and that it has arrangements with other powers concerning that coast, when there is no such arrangement and the sentence was placed to send Roman effort away from Alexandria. Egypt says it would rather this court had both from Egypt.

The first reading is that Egypt is dealing straight with this court at real cost, since a court that confesses a lie shows a peer exactly what it is willing to do. The second is that the confession was cheap, both falsehoods being spent already and worth nothing once Rome might repeat them. The court does not choose between the readings and does not need to, because the operational conclusion is the same under either: Egypt puts untrue sentences into formal correspondence with a peer when it suits its purpose, deliberately and with a purpose in mind. This court's standing rule on Egypt, that what Egyptian scholars show is believed and what Egyptian letters report is discounted, is not softened by the disclosure. It is confirmed by it, and the confirmation comes from Egypt's own hand.

**An omission beside an observation.** Egypt's letter discloses its falsehoods to Rome, its survey squadron and that squadron's orders, and its reservation on the bark, and says nothing whatever of any Egyptian holding across the western ocean. In the same twenty-five years the scholars in residence watched settler families embark from Alexandria in numbers, with livestock and tools, every sailing season, year after year. A man walking past that harbour cannot miss it. The omission is a fact about the document and the embarkations are a fact about the port, and the court declines to join them into a conclusion it cannot support. Settlers may be going to the Atlantic coast Egypt administers, to the southern landmass its squadron went to look at, to holdings this court knows nothing of, or to some mixture. Where they go is entered below as unknown.

That question is sharper after the second letter, and the sharpening comes from juxtaposition rather than from any disclosure. Egypt has now shown that it can count embarkations at another power's quays across twenty-five seasons, tell veterans with families from garrisons, identify what a foreign yard builds and what it has stopped building, and mark firm and reported apart in the same paper. That is a careful instrument turned on a rival's harbours. In three letters across the same period Egypt has said nothing whatever of the embarkations from its own harbour, which this court's scholars in residence have watched every sailing season for twenty-five years. This office draws no conclusion from the pairing, records that the pairing exists, and notes that it required nobody to disclose anything, being the kind of thing a compartment loses to over time without anyone opening it.

**The survey squadron, and a gap in this empire's own watch.** Egypt gives notice that it sent a survey squadron toward the southern landmass by an approach lying near water under this empire's protection, that it has been withdrawn, and that its orders were to make no landing where there were works, vessels or people, attempt no contact, hold position and fire only if fired upon, with one landing made on an empty shore to repair a hull and no ground claimed. The court takes the disclosure as a courtesy and cannot verify a word of it, no vessel, station or captain of this empire having reported the squadron at all. That is the finding worth keeping. A foreign squadron worked near waters under this empire's protection across some part of these years and this court learned of it by letter from the power that sent it. The watch on those approaches is thinner than the court had supposed and is entered as a thing to be repaired.

The court applies to itself the rule it applied in 543 to the medical knowledge,

The medical knowledge is the yield of the period and is worth more than anything the empire has taken from any court since the rail method. What the empire has to set against it is thin, and this office records the shape of the exchange plainly. The optical geometry the workshops use came from Egyptian instructors, who explained a relation between a glass's curve and its focal distance that Chinese scholars had recorded for decades without knowing why it held, and the grinding standards came from the same place. The measurement of the sphere was made by Egypt's own method, borrowed, and the figure was checked against Egypt's. Neither can be offered back as a gift. What remains the empire's own is the mechanical clock and the pendulum that regulates it, the deposit note, and the crafts the agreement already names.

**Where the annealing question was sent, and why not to Rome.** The one question the empire most wants answered from outside is how a large piece of glass is brought down from heat without cracking or straining, that being what caps the corrected objectives at 310 made in twenty years. It was drafted for Rome, which sent the double-walled glass furnace in the last generation and would be the natural place to ask. The draft it stood in was the Rome letter begun in the early 530s and held back, the report of the crossing having been kept until there was a posted coast to report, so that draft sat by for years and was rewritten more than once before it went in 549. The question was struck from it and put into this letter instead.

The reason is what the question tells the reader. A court with observatories and glasses of its own, asked for large discs, annealed, with colour mattering, has been told what the asker is building. The telescope was withheld from Rome in the last generation as a weapon, by decision, and asking Rome this question gives up by inference what that decision withheld. Egypt is differently placed. Egypt taught this court to grind at all, set the standards its workmen still work to, and has known for a hundred years that the empire works glass to Egyptian measure, so the question tells Egypt nothing about the empire that Egypt does not already hold. The cost of asking is that it deepens an account this court has already told Egypt it cannot level. That cost is accepted and the plain statement of it stands in the letter.

Before the letter of 537 went out, the office set the register of what has passed each way side by side. It found the pendulum and its escapement already sent to Egypt in the previous generation, and struck the drawings and the beat table from the letter's offer, leaving two clocks as an ordinary courtesy. Counting a gift twice would have been caught, Egypt keeping its own register of what is asked and what is given, and a partner who catches a court padding an account discounts everything that court sends afterward. What went instead was the deposit note, which has not been sent before, and a plain statement that the account is not level.

The court applies to itself the rule it applied in 543 to the medical knowledge, when it decided that nothing received from Egypt would pass to a third court, on the ground that a partner who finds its gift in another capital narrows the channel. The rule holds in both directions: the channel is worth more than parity in it, and the court does not manufacture parity by offering back what it was given.

The electrical work, the arc light, the wire signal, the steam hull and everything of powder stay closed.

## The Maurya

The commercial terms settled after the war of the 470s hold. The overland toll stands at twelve, nothing is demanded at the strait, and merchants pass the island approaches at market rates. The cannon on the far bank of the Syr Darya remain an open matter, left to the frontier observers rather than to correspondence, a letter pressing the point inviting a test of a threat the empire cannot presently make.

**The powder question, and an instrument that could not answer it.** The tasking asked for the volume of powder produced and named the price and availability of saltpeter in civilian markets as the measure. The returns of 531, 537 and 545 show the price standing within a little of the same at every visit and the same as in earlier years, the stuff sold openly in baskets in quantity by villagers who scrape it off their fields after dry weather, with no forward dealing and no house holding it against a rise. The same factor found three new walled compounds off that road with kilns burning, and carters hauling charcoal in great quantity, sulphur up from the south in sacks, and timber and cooperage, to those compounds and reportedly to more of them south on the plateau roads. He could not reconcile the two and returned both, which is the correct thing to have done.

The reconciliation is that the measure was wrong. Price measures scarcity, and that country's saltpeter is not scarce and cannot be made scarce, since it comes up out of the ground on the fields and any man who wants it walks out and takes it. A flat price there is consistent with any level of consumption whatever, including a very large one, so the court chose an instrument that could not have moved no matter what it was measuring. What does carry information is the compounds, the kilns, and above all the charcoal and sulphur haulage, since those must be carried and cannot be picked up outside a town.

The previous reading, that the Maurya hold the formula but not the production volume and that this is a manufacturing gap of the kind that closes, is no longer supported. Powder works are being built and fed. No figure of output was obtained and none is claimed here.

The Sogdian merchant tasking is continued and is entered under the spy taskings below.

## State of the Realm, c. 550 AD

- **Population:** 74 million, against 70 million at 500. About half the rise is ordinary recovery from the epidemic of the 460s back to the old peak near 72 million, which the registers would have shown with or without any new ground. The remainder is the Pontic black earth, which is the first food supply the empire has gained from better ground rather than from deeper extraction and is what carries the figure past the old peak rather than back to it. No general epidemic falls in the period.
- **Economy:** 38,500 tonnes of silver-equivalent.
- **State revenue:** 4,350 tonnes. Extraction 11.3 percent.
- **Revenue composition, in percent, rounded to the whole:** land tax 33; salt, iron and saltpeter monopolies 34; poll tax and corvee buyout 13; Silk Road tolls and silk export 9; frontier metals and fur 11. Mill licences and the iron monopoly's sales to the works come to three parts in a thousand and do not reach a whole point.
- **Spending, in percent:** military including garrisons, army, arsenals and navy 60; civil administration and court 13; water works 10; granaries and famine relief 4; iron rail 9; net surplus 4. The military share falls two points and rises in absolute silver to 2,610 tonnes. The rail rises to 392 tonnes, the trunk line being the cheapest the empire has laid and part funded by the silk houses.
- **Treasury:** the grain reserve is deep and the cash reserve thin. The surplus goes into the trunk line, the magazine works and the eastern programmes before it can accumulate. The empire is resilient against famine and fragile against any shock demanding mobile cash quickly.
- **Human capital:** literate population 21 million against 5 to 7 million in 70 at 500, which was a fourteenth to a tenth of the people then and is a little over a quarter of them now. The figure is built by age band rather than assumed, because the loop has been turning since about 500 and a man's letters depend on when he was a boy. Those under twenty-five were schooled inside a mature loop with two turns of the price behind it, and 39 million of them stand on the registers, of whom about two in five have letters, since an ordinary family sends its ablest child as a matter of course and its ablest child is one of several. Those between twenty-five and fifty, 25 million, were schooled between 500 and 525 when about one parent in ten could read and teachers were still thin outside the towns, and about one in five of them has letters. Those over fifty, 10 million, read at the rates of their own boyhood, about one in twelve. The three bands give 15.7, 4.8 and 0.8 million.

  The candidate register has grown faster than letters have, twelvefold from 75,000 sitting in 450 to 900,000 in 550, against a quadrupling of readers across those same hundred years and a trebling across the fifty since 500, because entering the examination has become a normal ambition faster than reading has spread. The register measures the ambition and not the literacy, and most who learn letters never sit.

  What has risen fastest within the whole is functional letters, the reading of common characters, contracts, notices, tallies and printed manuals, carried by households and by the failed candidates who teach in every prefecture. Command of the literary language and of real mathematics rises more slowly, not being something a literate parent can pass across the table, though the hardened papers of 536 have pushed more of the field into it than any earlier cycle. The investigation tier stands at 5,000 and the broad technical workforce at 260,000, both counted against the whole literate population as they have always been counted. Those are a fortieth of one percent and one and a quarter percent of that population. At 500 the same fractions, taken across the whole of the recorded ranges, ran from a ninetieth to a fiftieth of one percent and from four sevenths to one percent. Both stand above the top of their old range at 550, so the selection is sharpening on any reading of the old figures and not merely on the middle of them. The climb is steeper than the previous half century's because all three old brakes are off at once: books are cheap, the reward at the end of study is the largest the state can offer, and the first fully literate generation is teaching its own children at home.
- **Military technology:** rifled broadsides on 15 purpose-built warships and part-rifled on 9; the breech loader issued to 38 percent of frontier infantry at a failure rate of 0.9 in a hundred a year; mature hand cannon, mortars, rockets and the full explosive arsenal; tethered balloon platforms with the siege and frontier forces, unchanged since the 480s and still useless in wind; four low pressure steam tenders in home and Vietnamese water.
- **Received and not yet in service:** the gas method and the pneumatic tube method from Rome, and the preparation and dosage of the fever bark from Egypt, all arriving late in the period. Nothing built and nothing marched. None of the three touches the accounts above, the charges falling after 550.
- **Instruments and works:** reflecting telescopes with metal mirrors at the observatories and two great halls, needing to be taken down and repolished as the mirror tarnishes; 260 colour-corrected paired glass instruments in service, at the observatories, the western gun positions, the ships' masters and the arsenals; glass scopes at the frontier posts, still fringing; pendulum clocks in the observatories, great halls and yards; the arc light at three installations on two sites, each needing a turning shaft, an attendant and dressed charcoal; two wire signal runs at the arsenals, the longer of two li, each with a compass and an attendant at either end.

## Open Questions

- What the electrical force is. The source runs, the light burns and the wire carries a signal two li, and no account of the force has been reached.
- The metals of the plates and the liquor in the jars that work the Maurya line without anything turning. The existence of such a source is now established by observation and its composition is not, and a directed search stands ordered from 547.
- Whether a glazed pipe on a drained causeway will keep a wire working through this empire's own wet seasons. The Maurya line dies for weeks in the heaviest rains even so.
- An annealing that will hold a large disc of the dense glass at an even heat, which is what caps the corrected objectives at 310 made and 260 in service, and is all that stands between them and general issue to the frontier.
- The pledge evasion of the estate ceiling, which requires reaching debt contracts no office registers.
- The boiler at sea and the paddle wheel in swell, which stand between four harbour tenders and a hull that can cross.
- Whether the seven far-coast stations can be held, a power with soldiers, colonists and an administration being present on the same landmass already, and the distance between its holdings and this empire's coast being unknown to both courts.
- How far the far land runs between this empire's charted coast and Rome's, neither court having entered the interior and neither having told the other where anything of its own stands.
- Where the settler families embarking from Alexandria every sailing season are going, which the scholars have watched for twenty-five years and which Egypt's letter does not mention.
- Whether the fever bark holds on this empire's own fevers, and where the plant grows, Egypt having sent the medicine and withheld the plant and its country.
- The capacity of the magazines behind the Maurya bank and the number of pieces on it, both still past the limit line the factors are not permitted to cross.
- The watch on the southern approaches, a foreign survey squadron having worked near waters under this empire's protection without any vessel, station or captain of this empire reporting it.
- Whether the two garden plots at the landfall stations will carry grain, the far-coast posts costing more a man than any other the empire keeps while every pound they eat crosses an ocean.
- The suppression margin, which narrows while the land concentration that produces risings continues and the audit adds to the grievance of the only families able to make powder in quantity. No rising falls in this period and the arithmetic that made suppression cheap no longer holds.
- Whether the works, the coal, the merchant capital and the rising literacy amount to a change in the economy or to a rich trade in cloth. The court has no settled view and no policy. A single annual return of licence rolls, iron monopoly sales and engine counts is ordered so that the answer shows early.

## INCOMING

Material delivered to this court from outside is entered here and nowhere else. A letter is the sending court's statement and not a verified fact, and is marked so where it matters.

### Documents received

**1. From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans.** Delivered at the Dnieper crossing by the Roman frontier officer. The year is not stated in the document and this office cannot fix it inside the period, which matters because this court's own letter went in 549 and the office cannot say which crossed which.

- Rome accepts the joining of the traffic at the river under the standing agreement and does not move to change the agreement, which continues of itself.
- Rome refuses the joining of the rails. Its track is of one width for some three thousand miles from the western sea to the Dnieper and it will relay no part of it. It proposes transshipment instead: eastern cars stop on the eastern bank, Roman cars stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods cross where wheels do not. Its stated reason is that a rail running from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army, and that neither court would lay the other's track inside its own territory.
- Rome proposes a yard on the Roman bank, built by Rome at Rome's cost, with sheds, cranes and quays, under Roman garrison and Roman law, with Han factors in it protected as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory. Tariffs unchanged. Rome will begin the works on word that the yard as described is acceptable.
- Rome returns, against the measurement of the sphere sent in the last generation, the observed phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter, confirmed by Roman glasses, with the printed argument now current among Roman natural philosophers that the old order of the heavens is no longer defended by the men who look through the glass.
- Rome sends two methods of its own: the lighting of streets and workshops by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains; and the carrying of written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air.
- Rome acknowledges the mechanical clock and escapement of Han making as received in the last delivery and in Roman service since, and corrects its own draft in the open rather than mending it quietly.
- Rome declines the making of paper, having made paper from linen and flax for five hundred years and printed from cast type for four hundred.
- On the western land Rome states that what has changed since its earlier disclosure is possession and not geography: that it holds the islands, holds a stretch of the mainland coast, has taken the inland centres standing behind that coast, and has soldiers, colonists and an administration there which it intends to keep. It states this so that this court has it from Rome rather than from a merchant.
- Rome names what it withholds rather than leaving it to be found: soundings, charts and surveys of any western coast or harbour; the fixed position of any Roman station, garrison or holding in the west; anything of their strength or defensive state; and the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea, which it declines even to describe on the ground that describing the assembly would be the disclosure. It gives one reason for all of it, being the imbalance at the first embassy when it gave a full survey against a sketch without scale, and states that it expects this court to read those articles as Rome would read them arriving from here.

*Enclosures received as stated: the observations of Venus and of the lights near Jupiter with the printed argument; the gas method with its retorts and mains; the pneumatic tube method.*

**2. From the council at Alexandria**, under the consultation clause. The year is likewise not stated.

- Egypt reports having used its own old channel to Rome on the Atlantic coast of Africa, which it administers, asking notice of Roman passages and that vessels with no business there stand clear of its works. It states it sought nothing else, was offered nothing, and made no undertaking touching any other power.
- Egypt then sets down two things in that letter to Rome which it says are untrue: that the coast has lately come under Egyptian administration, when Egypt has held it for generations; and that Egypt has arrangements with other powers concerning that coast, when there is no such arrangement and the sentence was put in to send Roman effort in a direction away from Alexandria. Egypt says it would rather this court had both from Egypt.
- On Rome's western shore Egypt states it does not hold firm knowledge, that what it has remains a crossing made and cargoes returned, that anything further would be inference, and that it undertook not to send inference dressed as knowledge.
- Egypt states that a bark breaks the intermittent fevers, tried across eleven hundred cases of the fevers as they run in hot country under its germ practice, and sends preparation and dosage to the twenty scholars without price. It states plainly that it understands what country lies below this empire's southern frontier and what has kept the empire out of it for four hundred years.
- Egypt sends the medicine and not the plant and will not say where it grows, setting the reservation down rather than letting it be found, and likening it to this court's own reservation on the making of silk.

**3. From the council at Alexandria, drafted 550.** The discharge of the undertaking on Rome's western shore.

- Egypt states it now holds the thing firmly and sends it so marked, having reported earlier in these years that it did not.
- Marked firm by Egypt: that Rome is settling the far shore beyond the western ocean and that the settling is a programme of the Roman state rather than a venture of merchants; that from 534 the Atlantic yards of Rome build ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons for the western run and build nothing else in those yards; that embarkations have been counted at the Iberian quays in every season from 526 to 550; that those going out are veterans of Rome's own service with their families and their goods rather than garrisons; and that a hull makes two round voyages in three years.
- Marked by Egypt as reported and not firm: any figure for how many have crossed, a count of those who set out being no count of those who arrived, and Egypt states it will not send a number it has not established.

**4. From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans, drafted 550, supplementary to the letter already held.** By the Dnieper crossing.

- Rome states that its earlier letter and this court's letter of 549 crossed on the road, that this letter is not a withdrawal of the first, and that it corrects two things in it and answers this court's.
- Rome states that its earlier letter proposed a transshipment yard as though none existed; that the terms in it were settled in Rome in 525 and were not read again before the packet was sealed; that in the years while it lay in Rome this court built its yard and a Roman officer has been arranging consignments in it; that the terms stand as terms for the Roman bank and none are withdrawn; and that the arrangement proposed is the arrangement already working. It states that the same letter thanked this court for a clock already in Roman hands, that three papers of that court have now been found carrying a position their own files had overtaken, and that the accurate conclusion is that Rome is slow to read itself rather than slow to answer.
- Rome states that the two pendulum clocks arrived, keep a beat more even than anything Rome has made, and are set as the standard at its observatories and against the works that keep its legal and factory time, and that it will not pretend the exchange was even.
- Rome accepts the extension of the article on the far land, undertaking that this court will have word before any ship of Rome goes where this court's own is settled, and taking the same undertaking from this court.
- Rome names what it holds by kind and not by position: two large islands with the lesser ground about them; a stretch of mainland coast with the inland centres standing behind it; and, named for the first time, settled country on the rivers of another coast. It states that an article of that kind cannot protect ground neither court has named, that it makes no claim to ground it does not hold, and that it asks the same naming from this court, which it says this court's letter has already given in substance.
- Rome states it keeps what the earlier letter said it keeps and no more, adds nothing to that list, does not withdraw what it sent at about the turn of the century, and notes that this court kept its own charts, winds and latitude and said so plainly.
- Rome states that it carries nothing to this court about any third power in this letter or the next, declines to claim that as a general practice because it is not one, states that it writes of third powers to others and has done so in the same season, and undertakes the narrower thing instead, which is that Rome says nothing of the Han to anyone, adding that this is checkable over time.

*Enclosures with these two letters: none. Neither names any object, drawing or person as travelling with it.*

**5. Established separately, text not held by this office.** The request put to Egypt in 537 for the practice of cooling a large piece of glass slowly was granted in full and without condition. The grant was made before this court's second asking of the same question, drafted 550, could have been read at Alexandria. The two crossed on the road, which is the ordinary working of eight thousand kilometres and not a failure of any register here. Worth entering beside the fact is the shape of the grant: it was full, it carried no condition, and it was made before there was any second asking to answer, so it was not given under pressure of any kind.

### Intelligence returns

Marks as the service uses them: **firm**, the factor saw, handled, counted or heard it himself; **reported**, someone told him; **inferred in the field**, his own supposition and flagged as such.

**Operation 1, the Maurya realm, western commanderies, continued.** Hired Sogdian factors, merchant cover, the self-imposed limit line against arsenals, garrisons and military ground held throughout.

*Guns upon ships.* Firm, 534 at the fitting quay: hulls with square ports cut low in the sides and shuttered, four to a side on the one side seen whole. Reported, same quay: a shipwright's man said the heavy iron in those hulls was for stiffening and was not a thing fired from the deck. Firm, 542, two mornings in the roads: firing from a hull standing off, eleven or twelve reports counted the first morning; on the second, smoke from her side and a piece run out at a port and run back in, on the same class of hull he had been told carried stiffening iron only. He does not undertake to reconcile the two accounts. He was put off the outer quay that day while barrels went out to that hull under escort. Firm, 548: an iron piece the length of a man craned onto a timber carriage on small solid wheels and run inboard, with three finished carriages lying by awaiting hulls. Reported, second-hand and the man drunk: that the far southern coast yards are busier than the eastern and are cutting ports too. Inferred in the field: three or four guns to a side, and a yard with three spare carriages is building for more hulls than it has ready. Nil: the number of armed hulls, their stations, their orders, anything of a fleet as a fleet.

*The white salt, its price, and the works.* Firm, 531, 537 and 545, two markets each time in both seasons: the price stood within a little of the same at every visit and the same as in earlier years, the stuff there in quantity every day, sold in the open in baskets by villagers who scrape it off the fields where it comes up white after dry weather, the buyers being dyers, a man curing meat, and apothecaries in small lots. Reported, 537: a broker said there is no forward dealing and no house holds it against a rise, because a man who wants it walks out beyond the town and takes it up himself. Firm, 537 and 545: three walled compounds new to him on a road he has ridden for years, set back off it, brick kilns burning beside two and gangs on the walls of the third, which stood finished and gated on his return. Reported, 545: carters said their season's hire had been hauling to such places, charcoal chiefly and in great quantity, sulphur brought up from the south in sacks, timber and cooperage; one had made the run six times and never been inside a gate; another had it from his brother that there are more such places away south on the plateau roads. Inferred in the field, and flagged by the factor as something he cannot reconcile: that where a thing lies cheap and unmoving and no house troubles to hold it there is no great buyer for it, which he cannot make agree with the carts and the compounds, and he returns both as he found them. Nil: what is made inside any of those walls, the output of any works, any figure of powder whatever.

*The northern road and what stands behind it.* Firm, 536 from a second man: the mountain crossing no faster than of old, the upper road broken and under work. ### Technical assessments

**The Maurya message line is the most important thing in the packet.** It exists, it works, it carries spelled words over many days' travel, and it does it faster than a rider by a margin of two days against an hour. Two of its parts answer questions this office had entered as open. The source is jars of two metals standing in a liquor, consumed and replaced like stores, and nothing turns it, so a message line need not be worked outward from a place that already turns a shaft, which this office had entered as a hard limit on its own two-li run. And the conductor is drawn copper wrapped in a baked cloth and laid in glazed earthen pipe on a raised drained causeway, which is an answer to the covering problem in the shape of a conduit rather than a varnish. Neither composition was obtained. The metals, the liquor and the cloth are all unknown, and the line still fails for weeks in the heaviest rain, so the wet problem is reduced and not solved even there.

**Rome's gas lighting is better suited to this empire than this empire's own arc.** The arc needs a turning shaft, an attendant to close the points and a supply of dressed charcoal, and stands at three installations. Gas drawn off coal needs a retort, mains and coal, and this empire has coal in quantity, the rail to move it and the foundries to build retorts and pipe. It will light where the arc cannot, which is everywhere the shaft does not reach.

**Rome's pneumatic tubes are immediately buildable.** Written messages carried between the offices of a city on driven air need a pump and a tube, and this empire has run steam pumps in the coal country for a century and a half and has drawn pipe for its own works. It answers the intra-city case without any electrical knowledge at all.

**Rome's observations of Venus and of the lights near Jupiter can be checked here.** This empire holds reflecting telescopes and 260 corrected objectives, and the check costs nothing but clear nights. The observations also establish that Rome's glasses reach Jupiter's moons, which bears on a decision recorded below.

**Egypt's trial method may be worth more than the bark.** Eleven hundred cases across three regions, with the design, the recording and the standard of a result all passed to the scholars, is a way of settling whether a thing works that this empire has not had. It is applicable to every empirical chain this office funds, the melt search and the jar search included.

### Reply to Rome, drafted 550

**What is outstanding.** Rome asks for word that the yard as it describes it is acceptable and will begin the works on receiving it. That word is owed and this court has nothing to gain by delaying it further, its own yard on the eastern bank having stood since 531 waiting for a counterpart.

**The undertaking this court cannot perform.** In 549 this court offered Rome the Dnieper article extended to the far land, being word beforehand rather than after, and said the same of itself: that Rome would hear from this court before any ship of this court went where Roman ground is settled. Rome's letter withholds the fixed position of every station, garrison and holding it has in the west, and does so for a reason this court accepts. The two things do not fit together. A promise to give notice before entering ground cannot be kept by a party that does not know where the ground is, and the same holds in the other direction, this court having withheld its own latitudes. The offer as made was therefore unperformable when it was made, which this court did not see at the time and sees now.

The substitute proposed is conduct on contact rather than notice in advance, since contact is the moment at which both parties know where they are. It costs neither court a position and both can perform it. That is the whole of the change and it is proposed as a repair of this court's own article rather than as a new demand.

**The claim, narrowed.** In 549 this court told Rome that the far coast was a frontier of this empire and that the country behind it was entered under a commandery. The posts and the marked shore are real. The country behind is a thing this empire has never entered and cannot describe. Continuing to claim it against a power with soldiers, colonists and an administration on the same landmass invites the one question this court cannot answer, and a claim that collapses when asked about is worth less than a smaller claim that does not. The claim is therefore narrowed in this draft to the coast where the posts and the marks stand.

The price of narrowing is real and is entered here rather than glossed. It gives up, in writing and to the one court that could contest it, any assertion over the interior behind that coast, and it invites settlement of that interior by whoever reaches it first. What it buys is a claim this court can maintain under inspection, and the removal of a sentence that would have read, to a court that keeps colonies of its own, as a large assertion by a party unable to describe what it was asserting. The display on the coast itself is unchanged and its true position stands where it stands in this record.

**What is offered.** The sealed deposit note, which has gone to Egypt and not to Rome, and the results of this court's own check of Rome's observations against this court's own instruments. The second discloses that this empire holds glasses that will show the phases of Venus and the lights near Jupiter. That is judged an acceptable cost, Rome having just demonstrated the same capability and being likely to assume it in a court that measured the sphere and built the pendulum clock. The account between the two courts is not level and the draft says so, this court having received the rail method, the letter-casting, the coal-glass furnace, the compass work, two disclosures of the western ocean unasked, and now the gas and the tubes.

**Kept out.** The latitude of the crossing, the winds and currents out and home, the charts, the number of hulls, the strength of any station, the cadence of the annual run, the connection between the eastern voyages and the Northern Road, and everything of powder, steam, the electrical work and the making of silk. Also kept out, deliberately, is anything Egypt has told this court, which includes what Egypt has said of its own correspondence with Rome. The rule adopted in 543 for Egypt's gifts is applied to Egypt's confidences: a partner who finds its words in a third capital narrows the channel, and this court will not spend the Alexandria channel to buy credit in Rome.

**Checked for disclosure by inference.** Narrowing the claim tells Rome the claim is thin. That inference is available and is accepted as the price named above. Nothing else in the draft permits a distance, an interval, a strength or a route to be worked out.

### No reply to the Maurya court, and the ground for it

Nothing was received from that court in the period and nothing is outstanding between the two. The commercial terms settled after the war of the 470s hold, the toll stands at twelve, and merchants pass the island approaches at market rates.

Two reasons stand against writing anyway. The cannon on the far bank remain an open matter, and a letter pressing them invites a test of a threat this empire cannot presently make, which was the reason for silence before the packet and is a stronger reason after it. And this court now knows a great deal it did not know: that guns are mounted and fired at sea, that powder works are being built and fed, that the road, the relay houses and the granaries behind the far bank are being made ready to carry something larger, and that a message line runs on the northwest road. Every one of those was obtained by factors working under merchant cover, and any letter that touched any of it, however obliquely, would tell that court it is watched and would put the network at risk. The network is the instrument that answered four tasks this period, including two the empire's own fleet and its own scholars could not.

Silence is therefore the answer, and it is chosen rather than defaulted to.

**Held.** The two river valleys; the four Korean commanderies with tributary standing in the south of the peninsula; Vietnam, with the Shan Plateau the southern frontier; Mongolia, Manchuria and Inner Mongolia with the coal country; the Siberian river posts and fur stations, and the Minusinsk basin, contracting; the Kazakh steppe in administered depth with its three frontier cities and the rail; the Syr Darya north bank, railed and magazined to five weeks; the Pontic steppe east of the Dnieper, with 210,000 settled households, three fifths of the grant land broken and the completed rail.

New this period: the southern trunk line, 1,150 kilometres, complete to the Yellow River in 538 and to the Yangtze mill counties in 548; the transfer yard on the eastern bank at the Dnieper railhead, built 526 to 531 with dry store added 544; 34 posts on the northern coast; 7 stations on the far coast across the eastern ocean, planted 543 to 550 at the landfall and the anchorages north of it, with the coast behind them entered as claimed on the footing used for the northern forests; 41 northern colony sites closed, their garrison posts kept and supplied.

**Surveyed and not held.** 2,800 kilometres of new northern coast, the posted line about 900 kilometres behind the survey head and falling further back each season; 1,900 kilometres of the far coast north of the landfall, beyond the seven stations; the 3,400 kilometre gap between the head of the northern survey and the northernmost charted point on the far side, being worked from both ends; the interior behind the far coast, unentered and unknown.
