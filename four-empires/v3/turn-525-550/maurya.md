# Part XIII: The Southern Consolidation (525 to 550 AD)

### The Ruler and the Inheritance

A near-great ruler, assessed at seventy-six, took the throne in about 525.

He inherited the largest position the realm has ever held and the thinnest reserve it has held in three centuries. The reserve stood at 1,400 million in 500 and at 600 million at the accession. Revenue at the accession was 2,300 million.

The 6.25 billion paṇas the previous reign is recorded as having spent on the southern continent is the venture's whole cost and not the treasury's outflow, and the treasury sets out the difference here because a later officer reading the one figure will not otherwise find the other. Of the 6.25 billion, about 2,400 million was requisition, chiefly of merchantmen out of the Tamil and Bengal ports and of provincial carriage and stores charged to the provinces that furnished them, and never passed through the central treasury at all. About 1,150 million was raised on the ground by settlers on their grants, by the merchant houses at the harbours their trade uses, and by endowed temple establishments. About 1,900 million was met out of the surplus of the years and the trims to the household and temple lines. The remaining 800 million was the draw on the reserve, which is the fall from 1,400 to 600.

The fourth of those legs is the one the treasury cannot break down, and it says so. Revenue over the previous quarter century moved from 2,280 million to 2,300, which is twenty million and no help at all, so the whole 1,900 rests on the surplus and the trims together, and the previous reign's yearly returns were not kept in a form that separates them. Nor is that the only charge the same surplus carried: the weapons line rose from 150 million a year to 230 across the same years, which above a flat baseline of 150 is an average increment of 40 a year and so on the order of a thousand million more across the quarter century. Either the trims to the household and temple lines were far deeper than the present lines suggest, or the surplus ran well above the 112 million a year the register gives for 500, and the treasury does not know which. Nor can it say how deep the trims were or whether any was still in force at the accession, because the previous reign's returns do not carry those two lines at all. The register holds 68 million of temple patronage and 70 of household at 500, and this reign closes at 77 and 68. Neither pair says anything about where the lines stood in 525, and the second of them shows the household line still below its figure of 500 rather than restored above it.

Against that charge the previous reign left fifty major harbours and three hundred lesser stations on a foreign continent, the eastern coast and the northern belt settled, the arid northwest lined with fourteen posts, and two island groups taken by force. It left a Han hand cannon with the taking apart still in hand, a marine engine of the realm's own building that was heavy for its power and thirsty and trusted in fair season only, a one-man firearm fielded from about 518, a paper craft from 513 that made sheets by the hundred thousand and not by the million, the galvanic force understood and carrying nothing, and the eastern fleet unrebuilt forty-seven years after its destruction.

> **The reasoning.** The ruler put the position to the council in the terms of the Rolling Frontier Doctrine, which holds that a reign takes only what it can hold and supply and then spends itself turning that new ground into the platform from which the next reign pushes again. Ashoka's own consolidation pushed granaries, armouries, and garrison towns progressively closer to the frontier, and it was that forward building and not any conquest of his own that made his son's campaigns possible. By that reading the previous reign had done the taking and none of the turning. Fifty harbours on a coast are a claim. Fifty-seven harbours with roads inland, interior stations behind them, supplied waypoints on the sea road, granaries and engine works on the ground, and a fleet that can reach them in any season are a platform. The reign was directed at the second, and it was paid for out of the growth of the revenue and not out of the reserve, which stands within twenty million of where it stood at the accession.

### Dakshinadvipa: The Coast Made a Platform

Nothing founded was given up. The fifty major harbours the previous reign raised were kept and filled to their establishment of eight hundred registered persons each. Of those fifty, five stood not on the continent but on the islands, their founders having taken ship for better ground, and they are counted with the islands below and not with the continent. Forty-five stood on Dakshinadvipa. Twelve further major harbours were founded, bringing the continental total to fifty-seven: four on the eastern coast south of the existing anchors, three at river mouths on the northern belt, and five on the middle coast that the previous reign had held thinly. The lesser stations, markers, wells, and small garrisons rose from three hundred to four hundred and twenty.

The arid northwest, the shore a fleet crossing from Africa would reach, was thickened rather than thinned. Its line of posts rose from fourteen to twenty-two and the interval between them fell from about two hundred and fifty kilometres to about a hundred and sixty. Eight of the twenty-two were raised from posts of fifty men to walled stations of two hundred with cisterns, at the points the survey had marked as the only landings on that coast where a large fleet could water.

Roads were cut inland from all fifty-seven harbours to their nearest hinterland. The optical signal-tower relay of about 480 was carried along the eastern coast and the northern belt, seventy-one towers, so that word from the southernmost harbour reaches the establishment's seat at Ratnakula within the day in clear weather, where it took three weeks by ship, and goes blind in fog and rain as the towers do everywhere.

The people were carried to match. Some hundred and sixty thousand had been landed alive by 525. The seasonal convoy carried a further sixty-two thousand across this reign, about twenty-five hundred a year, and the registered population of the establishment stands at 250,000 at 550, counting the settlers and their families, the farmed hinterland behind the harbours, the garrisons, and the enslaved. Of that number about seventy-five thousand are the establishments of the harbours, stations, posts, and yards, and the rest hold and work the ground behind them.

### What the Establishment Costs

The previous reign reckoned a major harbour at roughly fifty million paṇas apiece across build and upkeep. Of that, about twenty million was the crown's works, the fort, the yard, the granary, the wharf, and the road; about twenty-three million was the passage and first stores of its people; and about seven million was standing charge. Those harbours were raised across the previous quarter century and stood on average twelve or thirteen years inside it, so the seven million is a standing charge of about five hundred and sixty thousand a year and not of a quarter million.

**The standing charge at 550**, built from that rate and from the rates of the lesser works, in millions of paṇas a year: fifty-seven major harbours at 0.56, thirty-two; four hundred and twenty lesser stations at 0.045, nineteen; the fourteen northwest posts at 0.35 and the eight walled stations at 0.75, eleven; forty inland stations at 0.055, two; seventy-one signal towers at 0.03, two; seven waypoints at 0.45, three; nine archipelago stations at 0.28, two and a half; thirteen island stations at 0.40, five; eleven yards and the engine works, eleven; roads and works repair on the continent, six; the seasonal convoy at its reduced rate, fourteen; the establishment's administration, its two seats, and the overseas informers, fifteen; presses, schools, and mortality registers, two. That is 125 million a year.

**The building across the reign**, in millions: twelve new major harbours at 20 of crown works, 240; a hundred and twenty new lesser stations at 2, 240; seven waypoints at 8, 56; eight walled stations at 1.5, 12; eight new fifty-man posts on the northwest line at 2, 16; forty inland stations at 3, 120; seventy-one towers at 0.15, 11; nine archipelago stations at 1, 9; eight new island stations at 1.5, 12; twenty-two survey parties at 0.5, 11; the engine works at Ratnakula, 30; roads inland from fifty-seven harbours at 1.5, 86; and the carriage of sixty-two thousand settlers and their stores at about twelve thousand paṇas a head, 744.

That last rate carries near half the ledger and it is the establishment's own working figure, drawn from the convoy accounts at the Tamil and Bengal ports and not audited against the previous reign's shipping. The only comparator this record holds is the 23 million that covered the passage and first stores of eight hundred people at a new foundation under the previous reign, which is 28,750 a head. The two are not strictly comparable, since that figure carried the first stores of a place that had none, while most of this reign's sixty-two thousand went to harbours already standing and already growing part of their own food. Against that, the southern road now waters and repairs a hull five times on the passage and the engined transports keep the season. The treasury has not proved the reduction and states its size: at the previous reign's rate the carriage would be 1,783 million rather than 744, the building ledger 2,626 million rather than 1,587, and the southern line's average across the reign 211 million a year rather than 170. The building comes to 1,587 million, or 63 million a year averaged across the reign.

The two together put the southern line at about 213 million in the heaviest years, at 170 million averaged across the reign, and at 125 million at the close, the waypoints having run from 531 to 545, the survey from 529 to 549, and the harbour founding from 528 to 546. Against the 125 the establishment returns 62 million a year from the continent and the southern road and 22 million from the counted districts of the great northern island, so its net charge on the treasury at 550 is 41 million and falling as each place comes to feed itself from its hinterland and forge from the iron of three interior districts.

The crown built the forts, the yards, the granaries, the roads, and the wells and nothing else. Land was granted on the eastern coast and the northern belt on the realm's ordinary terms, and the settlers cleared their own fields, raised their own villages, and dug their own tanks upon it. The merchant houses of the Tamil and Bengal ports built their warehouses, wharves, and counting houses at the eleven harbours their trade passes through. Temple establishments endowed on granted land keep schools and rest houses at seventeen of the fifty-seven. None of that was ever a charge on the treasury and none of it offsets one. It is recorded because it explains why more stands on that continent than the crown's line would suggest, and for no other reason.

### The Interior Walked

The interior had never been entered. Survey parties went in from 529: twenty-two parties across twenty years, each of thirty to fifty men with Malay and Sumatran guides and the realm's own surveyors, provisioned for a hundred and twenty days and carrying the mechanical clocks the realm's brass-workers had brought into use at sea for reckoning east and west.

They mapped some 480,000 square kilometres. Behind a narrow belt of good soil the country is dry grazing land. Two large river systems run inland from the eastern coast and a third from the north, and none of them reaches the far side. They found hardwood stands on the eastern rivers, iron showings in three districts and copper in one, and grazing enough to carry the establishment's own herds instead of shipping meat from the Tamil ports.

Forty inland stations were planted along the three river systems, sixty to two hundred kilometres from the coast, each a well, a granary, and twenty to sixty men. The interior peoples were met and offered the trade terms rather than the sword: the realm buys, pays, and settles no inland ground without agreement. That is the arrangement that held on the Malay and Sumatran coasts for three centuries. It is not the arrangement the previous reign used on the islands it seized, and the difference was deliberate.

### The Southern Road Made a Supplied Corridor

The road from the Strait of Malacca to the northern belt was a passage ships crossed and no ground the realm held. Seven fortified waypoint stations were planted along it between 531 and 545, at anchorages the realm's pilots had used since the Spice Island trade: Dvipasetu, Jalaghata, Payonidhi, Varunapura, Setubandha, Timiratirtha, and Anilagupta. Each holds a hundred to two hundred men, a cistern, a granary, and a careening beach. A hull leaving the Strait now waters and repairs on the realm's own ground five times on the passage instead of crossing open water in one run. They cost eight million paṇas apiece to raise, fifty-six million in all across the fifteen years, and about four hundred and fifty thousand a year each to keep, all of it carried in the southern establishment's line. They are why that establishment can be reinforced in any season.

### The Eastern Islands from Ternate and Banda

The garrisons on Ternate and Banda had been holding on borrowed time since 478, twelve hundred men and seven hundred, with powder for forty to sixty rounds between the two islands and no ship able to bring more through waters China's fleet watched. The southern road answered that. Their powder was raised to three hundred and fifty rounds at each island, their garrisons filled, and their harbour works repaired, all by the southern route.

From that restored base the reign worked outward through the archipelago by trade and not by landing. Nine trading stations were established between 536 and 549 on islands east and north of Banda whose peoples had sold to whatever trader came, each a warehouse, a wharf, a well, and thirty to eighty men, with the realm buying clove, nutmeg, mace, and sago at prices set by agreement and renewed each season. Two of the nine sit on nutmeg-growing islands outside the Banda group that the realm had not known of before its own pilots charted them. No island was taken by force in this reign.

### The Northern Platform

The frontier facing the power the realm rates most dangerous is the Syr Darya, and it was supplied through one mountain pass whose road an earthquake broke in about 320. The pass road was rebuilt between 531 and 539 at forty million paṇas a year within the works line, with two new relay stations.

Behind it the reign did what Ashoka's reign did behind its own frontier. Five forward granaries were built between the Hindu Kush passes and the river, each holding a hundred and twenty days of ration for the frontier establishment where the stock had stood at sixty. Three garrison towns were founded on the Bactrian and Sogdian road at Ashvasthala, Girivraja, and Uttarakota, each with an armoury, a smithy, and a walled market, so that the frontier draws its arms and its grain from ground three hundred kilometres behind it rather than from a capital four thousand kilometres away. The fixed cannon on the river rose from between fifty and eighty pieces to 240, and the powder magazines behind them from four to eleven. The building and the upkeep of all of it sit in the military line's fortification charge, which rose from 104 million a year to 130.

The realm did not cross the river and did not intend to. The platform is for a successor.

### The Works and the Granaries

The works line rose from 228 million a year to 258, which is near ten parts in a hundred of revenue. The granary and famine relief line rose from 46 million to 57, the increase going to stock rather than building, the granaries having stood at four fifths of establishment.

Four charges sit inside the works line and are named because they are its largest items and not additions to it. The Hindu Kush pass road, forty million a year from 531 to 539. The Tigris and Euphrates embankments and canal silt clearance, thirty million a year from 532 to 545, those works having broken under neglect before 450 and the flood that followed having cost several hundred million paṇas in ruined harvests and interrupted traffic on the Rome road. The Deccan and central reservoirs and wells, twenty-five million a year throughout, with the African drought-hardy sorghum, millet, and cowpea pressed further into the dry northwest and the northern Deccan. And the Mesopotamian stretch of the Rome road, twelve million a year from 528 to 540, doubling the waystations and putting permanent mounted patrols on the long runs between them, which Rome asked of the realm in about 415 and which the answer of that reign thinned without ending. Three of those four had ended before the reign closed, and only the Deccan reservoirs still ran in 550. So the four named charges take 107 million of the line in the peak years and 25 million at the close, and the balance is the standing charge for roads, rest houses, bridges, wells, and canal upkeep across the realm, which is about 151 million in the peak years and 233 at the close. The line's figure of 258 is the figure at 550 and not a peak.

That balance is the part of this reckoning the record should not pass over. The standing charge for routine works across the realm stood at 228 million at the accession, fell to about 151 through the years the four projects ran together, and came back to 233 by 550. It is a cut of about a third in ordinary maintenance held for the better part of a decade. The cause is arithmetic and not policy: the line rose thirty million between 528 and 532 and reached its ceiling there, while the four named charges came to 107, so seventy-seven million had to be found inside the line and it was found in the routine work.

What was given up was road widening and new rest-house building in the settled interior, deferred from 532 to 545, and the pace of bridge and canal repair on the Gangetic and Deccan roads, which slowed rather than stopped. The provincial establishments of the interior petitioned against it in those years and the merchant houses that use those roads petitioned with them, and both were refused. The court enters the charge against itself plainly. The realm's own record holds that the Tigris and Euphrates works broke before 450 because routine upkeep had been let slip, at a cost of several hundred million paṇas in ruined harvests and interrupted traffic, and this reign spent thirty million a year for fourteen years repairing exactly that damage while taking a third off routine upkeep everywhere else to pay for it. It was a smaller version of the same error and it was made knowingly.

### The Fleet Rebuilt

The eastern fleet was destroyed in about 478 and had not been rebuilt. This reign rebuilt it without returning to the waters where it was lost.

Navy crew rose from 48,000 to 76,000, and the garrison and second-line infantry fell from 500,000 to 465,000 in the interior provinces that hold no frontier, with the field infantry down 5,000 beside them. That transfer pays for less than half of the new charge. At the standing rates, 35,000 garrison men released is 12.3 million a year and 5,000 field infantry 3 million, while 28,000 sailors added is 30.8 million. The balance, with the 26 million of new fortification and the 4 million of new command, comes to 45.5 million of new charge, and it is met by the growth of the line's components. Those components at 550 sum to 936: field 255, garrison 163, horse 120, elephants 24, navy 84, command 52, weapons and consumables 108, fortification 130. At 500 the same components sum to 890, so the growth against them is 46 and not 36. The appendix gives 900 as the total for 500, ten million above the sum of its own parts, and the difference between the 36 and the 46 is that rounding and nothing else. Consumables stand at 108, exactly where they stood at 500, which the removal of forty thousand men from the establishment makes possible and which will not hold if the firearm stands and the frontier cannon keep growing. Cannon-armed warships rose from between thirty and forty to about a hundred and ninety, of which ninety carry engines. The eastern fleet was re-established at a hundred and ten hulls based on the realm's port at the eastern mouth of the Strait and in the Bay of Bengal, the Gulf, home, and Nauvandha squadrons holding the other eighty, of which the Nauvandha six are the standing force on the southern continent's western side. Building was done at the Tamil and Bengal yards and, for the first time, at the eleven yards on the southern continent working local hardwood.

> **The reasoning.** The previous reign judged that rebuilding into the eastern waters was the lunge the doctrine warns against, and it was right at the time, the realm then having no gun that reached and no hull that held its speed. Two things changed the case. A fleet based at the Strait and in the Bay sits on the realm's own ground at a gate it already holds rather than in open sea beyond it. And a fleet with cannon and engines can refuse a fight at a range it cannot answer, which the fleet of 478 could not do. The standing order is to hold the Strait, the Bay, and the southern road, and to enter the waters beyond them only under escort.

### Steam

The marine engine programme took fifty-one million paṇas a year and 1,180 million across the reign, and it moved the realm from one heavy engine to a standard pattern built in numbers.

The yards kept a printed failure register from 528, every burst vessel, failed seal, and fuel trial entered by hull and struck yearly for all eleven yards to read. Weight per unit of power fell by half against the engine of about 520 and fuel burn per day's steaming fell by a third. Three engine works were built, on the Tamil coast, at the Bengal yards, and at Ratnakula, casting cylinders and boilers to one pattern.

At 550 the realm holds two hundred and ten engined hulls: ninety warships and a hundred and twenty transports and packets. The engine is carried as auxiliary power beside the sail and not in place of it, which is enough for a hull to hold its station and its speed against a wind that would stop a sailing ship, and that was the whole of what the Chinese line did to the realm's fleet in 478. The southern packet runs the year round, through the monsoon under reduced power, where in 525 it ran in fair season only. No hull of the realm's yet moves without sail.

### The Galvanic Line

The programme took twenty-seven million a year and 620 million across the reign, and it put the force to carrying messages.

Three things had to be built. Copper wire drawn fine and long came in quantity by 535 from the Rajasthan and Central Asian workings. A covering came by 538: cloth wound on the wire and soaked in the realm's pressed plant oils and the petroleum compound its fire ships were packed with, then baked hard, the botanical programme having left the realm a wide catalogue of extracts to try. A reader came by 541: a magnetised needle of the kind the realm's navigators have floated since about 250, hung beside the wire, swinging when the circuit closes and returning when it breaks. The galvanic report of about 440 had already recorded a third piece of metal pushed and pulled in ways lodestone behaviour did not explain, and this is that observation turned into an instrument. The spelling convention was taken unchanged from the optical signal towers of about 480, so no new code was needed.

The covering fails when wet, and burying the line in the bed of the Ganges road put it in ground that saturates for three months of the year. Glazed earthenware conduit is watertight along its length and leaks at every joint, which is where Rome's hint of about 385 was brought to bear. Rome's words were that such vessels fail first at the joints between their parts, where metals expand and contract unevenly under repeated heat and force, rather than in the middle of a solid wall. Rome said nothing of earthenware and nothing of wet. The realm carried the hint across to a fired clay pipe in wet ground by its own analogy, on the reasoning that the principle named is the meeting of parts and not the metal, and the analogy is the realm's and is recorded as the realm's. The joints were therefore sleeved in the same baked oiled cloth and the conduit carried on a raised drained causeway rather than in the roadbed, at some eleven million paṇas a year across the building.

That works, and not everywhere. The trunk runs 260 kilometres from Pataliputra along the raised sections of the northwest road, with four relay stations and fresh cells at each, and 60 kilometres of branch line to the arsenals and the mint. A message crosses it in under an hour where a rider takes two days. On the low ground the line goes dead for two to four weeks in the heaviest monsoon and the optical towers carry the traffic until it dries. Egypt was described to the realm in 462 as binding its administration across the whole of its reach, and the realm has 320 kilometres of line that works ten or eleven months in the year.

### Paper, Printing, Schools, and Letters

The realm has had paper since 513 and the Brahmin printing settlement since 525, under which the Brahmins hold the plates and seal every sacred text struck. Under that settlement the press became the instrument of their authority rather than a threat to it, and this reign spent the settlement instead of reopening it. What the realm did not have was sheets in quantity, the making of paper having stayed a craft of small workshops. Materials and not presses were the binding constraint, and the reign attacked the materials.

Thirteen paper mills were built between 528 and 544, eleven on the Ganges and its tributaries and two on the Tamil coast, each with water-driven stampers to beat the fibre where hands had beaten it before, working rag, hemp, and rice straw. Output rose from a few hundred thousand sheets a year to about forty million. The mills and the presses together took twenty million paṇas a year within the research line.

On that supply the presses rose from twenty-nine to four hundred and ten, at every provincial seat, every district town of consequence, the temple schools, the arsenals, and the yards, with fourteen more in the southern establishment. Funded district schools rose from about six hundred to two thousand four hundred, and the schools, libraries, and scholarship line from 46 million a year to 76, which carries the great schools of Pataliputra and Taxila and the work on the mathematics of the continuous done in them.

What was printed, beyond the tax codes, law codes, military orders, and medical texts the realm had printed since 395: a school primer, struck in some 1,200,000 copies across the reign; the medical corpus carrying the Alexandrian washing protocols and the antiseptic and analgesic findings; and the mathematical corpus, printed for the schools with the Brahmins' seal on the sacred portions and no seal needed on the rest. The Brahmin establishment took the printing of school mathematics as it had taken the printing of law, the plates being theirs and the authority with them.

Reading and writing reach about one person in eight at 550, against the one in ten the register has carried in every century since the founding. The caste order still fixes who is taught and the realm did not attempt to change it. What moved was the cost of a sheet, the number of places that hold a book, and the number of schools.

### The Delivery to Rome

In about 501 the realm asked Rome for the working method of a marine engine and offered the complete account of the Chinese hand-weapon. Rome set out its own half in full in advance, required one delivery and nothing less, and gave notice that it would find the seams in a padded account. The realm did not then have what it had offered. An example reached its engineers only at the close of the previous reign, and nothing was sent.

This reign finished it, and the dates are entered because the previous reign's failure was one of years passing. The example was reassembled in the realm's hands in 524 and was on the bench at the accession. It was measured and drawn through 525 and 526. Barrels were cast to its dimensions and proved through 527 and 528, that firing being what stood between a description and a maker's account, since a maker needs to know what the thing does and not only what it measures. They were cast one at a time and each was checked against the example itself, bore by a plug turned to the example's own bore and every part offered up to the example's part, which is copying by direct comparison with the object in the room. That is the only method the realm had in 527. It is not the method of the master gauges of 536, whose whole purpose was to carry a dimension to an arsenal that does not have the object. The account was written in 528, struck and sealed at the capital's press in 529, and carried west in 530. Four years is what the proving cost. It is not what the previous reign spent, which was twenty-five years of the matter not being raised.

The decision to send was taken by the throne on the council's advice in 528, and it was a decision and not a lapse. The account was ordered sent complete, with no request attached, and the realm's own firearm disclosed in the same letter. The arsenal and the western frontier command opposed sending it, holding that the complete construction of such a weapon arms a neighbour across the Euphrates and through the Caucasus. They were overruled on three grounds: that a realm which asks and does not perform is worth less at every future asking; that Rome's containment hint of about 385, given for nothing at the time, is what unlocked the realm's first working gunpowder weapon; and that the realm had answered Rome's questions upon that weapon in about 478 with what the survivors of Tidore could say and what its engineers reasoned of its reach and its loading, which was thin, had then built the weapon itself by 518, and had sent no word, so that a partner given a thin answer once and no word at all afterwards would read the silence badly if he learned of the weapon by another road.

The volume was struck at the capital's press and sealed, so that Rome's copy and the realm's are the same to the letter. Three limits on its figures stand in it beside the figures they touch. No powder came with the example, so every figure of range and rate was got firing the realm's own powder in that barrel. The example is one piece and could not bear the firing the observed effects required, so a small number of proving shots were fired with it and the effects on timber, on stone, and on men were got with barrels cast in the realm's shops to the example's dimensions, every figure marked according to which piece produced it. And the example came out of material an arsenal had set aside as unserviceable, so the realm cannot tell whether it holds a current Han pattern or a superseded one. The arsenal objected that this last hints at how the piece was got. The throne judged that the words describe the condition of the weapon and not the road it travelled, and that an account passing off a scrapped piece as current manufacture is the kind of seam Rome had given notice it would look for.

### The Coast Order

The realm has held ground on Dakshinadvipa since the depot of 478 and has had settled districts on it since 500, and until this reign it had no standing instruction for what a man on that coast does when a hull he does not know comes over the horizon. The council put the want plainly: a coast is only held if the men holding it know beforehand what they are to do, because the distance from Ratnakula to the northwest line is a fortnight and the distance to Pataliputra is a season, and a post commander who must send for orders has already answered by the time they reach him.

Two further grounds were entered for writing them when the reign did. The realm gave the location of that continent to Egypt in the exchange of about 462, and the previous reign had judged on that account that a foreign coast-guard rule might be applied there within fifty to eighty years. And the previous reign had downgraded the secrecy of the venture in favour of speed, so that the realm's presence on that continent is no longer a thing it can expect to keep quiet. A coast that will be visited needs orders before the visit and not after.

There was also a fourth ground, and it was the immediate one. Between 530 and 533 six reports of foreign hulls came in from three posts and one coasting master, in no common form and mostly without bearing, distance, or build, so that nothing in them could be set against anything else. The Order was written to make such reports comparable before it was written to do anything else.

The standing orders were therefore issued by the throne, on the advice of the council, through the southern establishment at Ratnakula. There are three instruments. The Coast Order of 535 is the first. The Amendment of 545 is the second, and it was made in answer to the years before it. A landing had been found overland at a bay below the last post in 544. The Second Degree had been reached at a post in 544 and at Nauvandha in 545. And transports and despatch boats had closed upon foreign hulls on their masters' own judgement four times since 539. Its additions answer the first two of those and carry word faster for the third. The masters it did not answer at all, and that fault stood open five years more until the Second Amendment of 550, which is the longest any fault found on that coast has been left standing. The Second Amendment of 550 is the third, and what it changes is set out with the returns below. All three were struck at the capital's press and issued to every post, station, harbour, yard, and patrol on Dakshinadvipa, on its islands, and on the southern road, and to every hull of the southern squadron.

**The Coast Order of 535.** It is a ladder of five degrees, and a commander acts at the degree the other party's own act has reached and at no degree above it.

*First, sight and record.* Every foreign hull is entered in the post's foreign-hull register with the date, the hour, the bearing, the distance, the number of hulls, the build, whether sail was set, and whether it moved without sail. Word goes by signal tower where a tower stands and by rider where none does. At this degree no post hails, shows itself, or fires.

*Second, show the line manned.* If a hull holds station within sight of a post for more than a day, the post shows itself: the standard raised, the garrison drawn up on the beach in view, and fires lit at night at intervals along the shore. The purpose is that a rival counting this coast counts a garrison and not an empty beach.

*Third, shadow and state the position.* If a patrol vessel is within reach it closes to within hail, keeps company, and does not fire first. Every hull and every post holds copies of a written statement struck at the capital in Greek, Latin, and Sanskrit, which says that this coast was found, charted, garrisoned, settled, and taxed by this realm, that its harbours are open to any power's ships for trade, water, and repair on the same terms, and that no base, no garrison right, and no ground will be granted upon it. The statement is handed over. Nothing further is said, and no commander is to negotiate, since no commander on that coast has authority to grant or refuse anything beyond what the statement contains.

*Fourth, water and go.* A foreign party landing for water, wood, shelter, or the burial of its dead is permitted, watched, and provisioned if it asks. The post commander offers water from the post's own cistern rather than allowing a party to dig, a well dug being a work raised. The statement is handed over again. Nobody is seized, nobody is detained, and nothing is taken.

*Fifth, the acts that are not permitted, and what is done about them.* A foreign party may not raise a marker, cairn, post, wall, hut, beacon, or any standing work; may not remain ashore past one night; and may not land within the walls, roads, cultivated ground, or watering places of any of the realm's own places. Against those acts the commander warns in writing and by interpreter where one is present. If the work continues he removes it, with his own hands and his own men, and rebuilds nothing in its place. If the party resists the removal he takes and holds them, feeds them, harms them not, and sends word to Ratnakula and thence to the capital. He does not fire upon men who are not firing upon him. If fired upon, he answers, and thereafter he is at war on that beach and will be judged on whether he held the ground.

The order states as plainly what the realm will not do. It will not fire on a vessel standing offshore. It will not board a vessel that has landed nothing. It will not pursue beyond sight of the coast. The reason entered is that the realm's whole position on that continent is to be found already in possession, and a shot fired at a hull that has taken nothing hands a rival the grievance and gains the realm nothing it does not already hold.

**The Amendment of 545.** Four additions.

Any party moving inland from a landing place beyond the beach is followed at a distance by mounted men from the nearest station, kept in plain sight, not engaged, and escorted back to its boats. Each of the eight walled stations of the northwest line keeps a mounted section of twenty for that purpose.

Every post sends its foreign-hull register entire to Ratnakula each season, so that the establishment can tell one hull from many and a return visit from a first.

A standing squadron of six cannon-armed hulls, two of them engined, is based at Nauvandha from 546, the first naval force the realm has kept on that continent's western side. Its orders are the same ladder, with one addition: it places itself between a foreign hull and any harbour, yard, or settled district of the realm, and stays there.

And the establishment at Ratnakula is to hold, at all times, one engined packet at readiness to carry word to the Strait and thence to the capital outside the convoy season, so that a landing in force is known at Pataliputra in months and not in a year.

> **What the orders do not cover, and the court says so.** The ladder is written for a ship's company against a post of fifty or a walled station of two hundred, which is the size of party a coast of that kind ordinarily brings. It does not say what a post commander does if a foreign power lands in strength and begins to build, because at that point the question is no longer one of orders to a post but of what the realm will spend, and the court has not decided it. The realm holds fifty-seven harbours on that continent, twenty-two posts, eleven yards of its own upon the coast, a squadron based at Nauvandha, and a hundred and ninety cannon-armed warships of which ninety are engined. What it does not hold is any experience of contesting a landing at all, and the nearest force that could reinforce the squadron lies a season away at the Strait.

### The Coast Returns, 530 to 550

These are the returns of the southern establishment as they reached Ratnakula and thence the capital. The facts stand first and the establishment's interpretation stands after them, marked.

**Before the Order, 530 to 533.** Six reports, from three posts and one master of a coasting craft, in no common form. Bearing, distance, and build are mostly not given. Nothing in them can be measured against anything else. The date of the first foreign hull upon this coast cannot be fixed from the realm's own papers, and that is the plainest argument for the printed register the Order imposed.

**First Degree, 535 to 545.** Twenty-nine entries at nine posts, six of them made by masters of the realm's coasting craft, despatch boats, and transports at the post they next made. One hull at nineteen entries, two at seven, three at three, never more than three at one post at one time.

The entries agree on the build. Sides plated in iron, riveted, flush, with no timber wale anywhere along the length. No mast that would carry sail, and light poles for signals. One warm pipe amidships, its smoke thin and near colourless. Boats hung outboard on iron arms. In the side, at every entry and at every range, the nearest look being three hundred metres: no port, no opening, no lid, no shutter. On deck, nothing any master or post officer could name as a weapon, the things seen being winches, a drum with a line, casks, covered iron cases, a boom, and lengths of hose. Sail set at any entry: none. Moving without sail: every entry, and at fourteen of the twenty-nine against the wind. The hulls worked slowly, stopped, lowered boats, and sounded.

**Vessels of the realm keeping company, four occasions, in 539, 541, 543, and 545.** A transport or despatch boat on passage altered toward a foreign hull, closed to two to four kilometres, held part of a day, and broke off. No hail was made on any of the four, a transport master being no patrol vessel and holding no authority upon the ladder. Nothing was fired. No foreign hull altered course, closed, or made any sign.

**Second Degree, five occasions, in 537, 538, and 544 at posts, in 545 at Nauvandha, and in 548.** The standard was raised, the garrison drawn up in view, and fires lit along the shore at night, as the Order directs. At 537 and at 544 a grey ball larger than a boat was held above the after part of a hull on a line, with a man in a basket beneath it, up in the forenoon and down at dusk. At 538 two boats pulled to half a kilometre, lay on their oars, and put about without landing.

**A landing below the line, 543 and 544.** People of the country trading at the well of Post One gave word of boats and strangers at a bay some days' walk to the south, with a fire kept many days. A party of ten went down overland in 544 and found the place months cold. There were the marks of two large boats hauled up; a stone-lined hearth; cut timber, shavings, and iron scale; a length of the same plating seen on the hulls' sides, cut and discarded; and a cairn of stones with a squared timber set upright in it, cut with characters nobody present could read. The characters were copied and the copy sent to Ratnakula and thence to the capital. There was nothing else there and nobody.

The Fifth Degree of the Order provides for a warning in writing and for removal if the work continues after the warning. There was nobody to warn. The commander recorded, measured, and copied, left the work standing, and referred the question to Ratnakula, that bay lying below the last post and so in no commander's charge. No instruction had come back to him at the close of 550. The squadron found the work still standing in 549, untouched, the characters legible, the hearth and boat marks weathered out, with no fresh marks and no other work within ninety kilometres either side.

**547, the Third Degree applied for the first time.** Four hulls of the southern squadron, two of them engined, went out to three foreign hulls lying before Walled Station Five, placed themselves between those hulls and the shore, and held. The foreign hulls did not close, did not withdraw, and did not alter station. The squadron then closed to hail and sent a boat across. The written statement in Greek, Latin, and Sanskrit was handed over and was taken. Nothing further was said, nothing was asked, and nothing was fired.

At four metres the boat's officer entered what he saw. Sides plated, riveted, flush, with no wale and no rubbing strake. No port, no opening, no lid, no shutter anywhere from stem to stern. Two light poles and one warm pipe. Boats on iron arms and a steam winch forward. Aft, a drum carrying a great length of line, and a stand of iron flasks lashed upright and coupled by pipes, with the grey cloth of the ball folded and covered beside them. Amidships under tarpaulin, long spars with an iron fitting at one end, and racks of iron cases with cable and weights. Hose on drums and a hand pump. Nothing that the boat's officer or the squadron's gunner could name as a gun. Forty to sixty men on the nearest deck, fair and dark both, in white and blue cloth. No answer of any kind was made. The paper was taken and carried below.

**The identification, entered as fact from 547.** The writer carried in the flag's boat had served four years with the realm's factor at the harbours of the East African coast. He knew the speech he heard upon that deck for Greek, and named it before the boat had put off. He knew the dress and the cut of the coats for the ships' companies he had stood among at those quays, and he said the hull answered those lying there in its plating, in its want of a wale, and in its pipe. Upon his recognition the establishment enters as fact that these are Egypt's ships, and sets down the ground of it here so that the court may weigh the ground and not only the conclusion. No name was given by any party. Nothing was told and nothing was shown. Before 547 the matter stood as a reading only, upon build and bearing.

**549, the Third Degree applied a second time**, at the Nauvandha approaches. The statement was handed over and taken. Nothing was fired. The hulls stood away seaward the same day.

**550.** Two First Degree entries early in the season, and nothing after.

> **The establishment's interpretation, entered as interpretation.** Nothing nameable as an armament has been seen at any range, alongside at four metres included. These hulls are not to be reported unarmed, and no officer of this coast is to report them so. No officer here has seen a hull of this construction, and a flush side may be a side whose ports have not been found. The squadron placed itself between those hulls and the shore twice under its standing addition, and on neither occasion could any officer aboard say what stood on the other side of the water. That will be true again the next time, and the order to interpose stands unaltered regardless, because a coast is not held by ships that give way to what they cannot identify.

> **A second matter for the court.** Word of the landing came within the season, and it came from the people of the country and not from any man of the realm's own, there being no post on that coast to hear it from. The inland terms of 529, under which the realm buys and pays and settles no ground without agreement, bought that word. It is the first return the realm has had on them and it came from ground the realm does not garrison.

**What the returns showed about the Order itself.** Four faults, all found by use.

The Fifth Degree assumes a party present to be warned. It has no degree at all for a work standing on the realm's ground with nobody at it.

The Order's geography stops at the last post. The bay of the landing lay in no commander's charge, and the referral has stood six years unanswered because there was no officer whose business it was to answer it.

Masters of transports, despatch boats, and coasting craft made six of the twenty-nine First Degree entries and all four of the approaches, and the Order gives them no authority and no instruction. They are the most numerous eyes the realm has upon that coast.

And the reports of 530 to 533 were loose enough that the realm cannot say from its own papers when the first foreign hull came.

**The Second Amendment, issued 550.** The throne answered the four on the council's advice, and the instrument went south in the last season of the reign. It had not reached the northwest line at the close, the passage being a season.

A Sixth Degree is added, for a work found standing with nobody at it. The work is not to be thrown down. It is to be recorded, measured, drawn, and its characters copied; a marker of the realm's own is to be set beside it, of stone, carrying the same statement in the same three tongues with the date of the realm's finding, its depot, and its settlement cut into it; and the place is to be garrisoned. The reasoning entered is that a marker thrown down destroys the evidence of what was claimed and when, and buys a grievance for nothing, while a marker set beside it and dated contests the claim by the same custom that made the realm's own claim, which is being there and being able to prove when. The realm has spent a century and a half objecting that it was walled out of coasts by powers that arrived first, and it will not now be the power that answers a stone with a crowbar.

The Order's geography is extended to the whole coast of Dakshinadvipa and its islands, whether or not a post stands there, and the southern squadron is given charge of every stretch below and beyond the posts, so that no future referral falls to nobody.

Masters of transports, despatch boats, and coasting craft are bound into the First Degree, with the printed register form issued to every master sailing south, and are forbidden the higher degrees, including the closing to two to four kilometres that four of them made on their own judgement.

A post is ordered for the bay of the landing, of fifty men with a cistern, which extends the northwest line by one and closes the gap at its southern end.

### The Notice to Egypt

A notice of holdings went to Egypt in 543, eight years after the Coast Order was issued and two years before it was amended, the court judging that a rule the realm means to apply on that coast is better stated to the one power that already knows the coast exists than discovered by that power at a beach. It asked no question and reported nothing the realm had seen. It carried the dates of the realm's finding, depot, and settlement of the southern continent, and the rule that the realm's harbours there are open to Egypt's ships for trade and closed to foreign basing. It carried no establishment figures.

It also stated that those coasts are garrisoned along their length, and that is an overstatement the court made knowingly. The eastern coast, the northern belt, the middle coast, and the arid northwest are garrisoned. The western and southern coasts are charted by ship and held by nobody. The purpose of the notice was to raise a rival's estimate of what a landing would cost, and a coast described as garrisoned in part is a coast with its gaps advertised. The cost of the overstatement is recorded with it: a power that tests the claim finds the gap, and the landing found overland in 544 lay in precisely the stretch the notice covered with a word. The Second Amendment of 550 exists to make the statement true, and until its post is raised at that bay the notice is ahead of the fact.

> **The reasoning.** A claim of first finding is worth what a power can make it worth, and there is no court between powers to hear one. What a letter can affect is a rival's estimate of what a landing on that coast would cost. The notice was cut to what raises that estimate, and the scale of the establishment was kept out of it, a rival needing the scale to judge which coast is worth contesting and which is thin.

The notice was answered. A letter from the council at Alexandria reached this court within the reign, undated by its sender, and its substance is entered under INCOMING and worked through below. It asserts no claim to that coast and disputes none of the realm's. The overstatement the court made knowingly in 543, that those coasts are garrisoned along their length, was neither tested nor challenged.

### China

Nothing was sent to China in this reign, neither message nor probe. The realm holds in its own workshops a hand cannon taken from a Han frontier arsenal and now fields a firearm of its own, and an exchange invites a question about where the second came from. The Silk Road toll stayed at twelve parts in the hundred while transit revenue ebbed from 255 million to 235 under the northern steppe route, on the judgment that a higher toll drives more traffic onto that route. The northern river frontier was quiet throughout, and the reign's answer to it was the northern platform rather than any letter.

### Rome, After the Delivery

Rome sent nothing in answer to the delivery of 530 between that year and the close of 550, and the realm has not asked again. That the silence is real and not a letter lost on the road is now established: nothing whatever reached this court from Rome in these twenty-five years. It asked for nothing in the letter and it will not now dun a counterparty it kept waiting twenty-nine years. Rome's half of the bargain of 501, the working method of the marine engine with its seals and fuel figures and its engineers into the yards, has not come, and the realm built its engine without it.

The harder question was raised in 548, when the establishment's identification of the previous year made the southern coast a matter of Egypt. The two courts have traded what each sees of Egypt for a hundred and fifty years, and by that practice the returns should have gone west. The court decided not to send them, and the decision is recorded with its reasoning because it departs from a long habit.

> **The reasoning.** An account of foreign hulls on the southern coast cannot be given without saying which stretches were watched and which were not, and the realm's western and southern coasts are held by nobody. Rome keeps a warm hand with Egypt and sent it a subsidised relief fleet in 365 without telling the realm, which the realm learned through its own merchants. The court weighed the standing exchange against the disclosure and held. It records the cost: Rome is the steadiest tie the realm has, the exchange runs both ways, and a partner who is not told a thing this large may reasonably tell the realm less in his turn. The matter is left where the next reign must take it up, when there is a post at that bay and the gap is closed.

One thing bears on that decision and it came in late. Rome's silence had by 548 already run eighteen years, and the realm's withholding followed it rather than began it. The court records that in its own favour and records the harder half beside it: the realm now holds, from its own operation, that Egypt has been settling ground beyond the Cape for twenty-one years and that a bark on the East African coast has stopped the fevers killing men there. Those are exactly the two things the standing exchange with Rome exists to trade, the second of them worth more to a power with northern frontiers than anything the realm has ever sent west, and the realm has not offered either. A partner told nothing that large twice over may fairly conclude that the exchange has ended, and the court cannot say that Rome would be wrong.

### The Mortality Registers

The realm's own record of the Spice Islands garrison states that disease killed men there at a rate the realm never counted, and that the capital's ledgers counted the establishment and never the graves. The southern venture then carried some hundred and sixty thousand people across an ocean, and this reign carried sixty-two thousand more.

From 528 a mortality register was required at every garrison, yard, and settlement outside the subcontinent, entered by month, by cause where a physician could name one, and by condition of person, free or enslaved. It is the first time the realm has counted its enslaved anywhere but as a share of the whole. The registers are printed forms, which is what made the requirement cheap enough to impose everywhere at once.

Three measures came out of the first ten years of returns. Inoculation against the pox, practised in the realm since about 150, was made a condition of boarding for every southern convoy. The washing and antiseptic protocols were printed and issued to every southern and island station. Convoy crowding was cut by a fifth, at four million paṇas a year in extra sailings. Deaths in passage on the southern route fell from about one in nine of those embarked to about one in thirty-two.

### What Arrived in 550, and What It Overturned

The last usable product of the East African operation reached the capital in 550, and the letter from Alexandria reached it in the same reign. Between them they bear on the largest judgement this realm has made in a century, and the court sets out plainly what it held, what it holds now, and who was right.

**What the court held.** In the first years of the previous reign the realm's agents on the East African coast reported bulk provisioning for over a hundred ocean hulls, past two hundred thousand tons. The court read that as Egypt preparing to reach the southern continent first and to line its coast harbour by harbour, as it had lined Africa, and to turn to the realm's ships the same answer it had turned there. The previous reign committed 6.25 billion paṇas on that reading. Its own record says the decision was reached by a court that was miffed to the point of grief and moving at the speed of injured pride, and that the calm language of the Arthashastra was laid over it afterwards.

**What the returns establish.** No second provisioning of that character occurred at those harbours in twenty-one years. That is a nil, and a nil of twenty-one years' residency is not the nil of a season. A season's nil says the agent did not see it. Two decades of resident factors buying in the same market, watching for one thing across twenty-one sailing seasons, says the thing did not happen where they were standing. The court enters that as the most valuable single line the operation produced and rates it above any positive item in the packet.

**What the returns do not establish, and the court adopts the operation's own caution rather than the comfortable reading.** It is a nil upon a spike and not a nil upon a programme. What runs at those harbours is a continuous flow of transports loading people and not cargo, families with stock and tools, contract men out of their term with their households, and garrison drafts, sailing south toward the Cape every season for twenty-one years, many not returning within the season and some not returning at all, with transport shipbuilding rising, horses and highland breeding stock going south in the later years, and the older provincial harbour works standing unfinished. Egypt is settling ground somewhere beyond the Cape and has been doing it continuously for at least two decades. The realm does not know where.

> **The court's reading, marked as the court's.** The provisioning of 500 to 505 was most probably the opening sailing of that programme and not an expedition aimed at Dakshinadvipa at all. Three things support it and none of them proves it. The programme runs continuously in the same direction, south and round the Cape, and the realm's continent lies east. The harbour talk of a land across a western ocean is inconsistent between tellers and uncorroborated, and the realm sets no weight on it by itself, but it agrees with the word a Roman merchant gave the realm in the previous reign that Rome too had found a new land and begun to colonise it, and two weak signals pointing the same way are worth more than either alone. And the older Egyptian harbours are visibly going short of the works money that a power not stretched elsewhere would spend on them. The realm may be wrong about all of this. It was wrong before on the same evidence read the other way.

**So the account of the venture.** The realm spent 6.25 billion paṇas, and this reign a further 1,587 million of building on top of it, racing a fleet that on the present evidence was never coming. The treasury called the venture reckless and was overruled, and on the narrow question of the Egyptian fleet the treasury was right and the throne was wrong. That is set down without qualification.

What is also true is that the realm holds a continent, sixty-two thousand more of its people are settled upon it, its harbours return 62 million a year against a charge of 125 and the gap closes each decade, and the ground was worth having on its own account. The case for holding it now rests on the ground and not on the race. Nobody at this court should read the second sentence as cancelling the first.

**The letter from Alexandria.** Egypt asserts no claim to that coast, disputes none of the realm's, states that its squadron carried orders to make no landing where there were works, vessels, or people and has been withdrawn, and gives notice unasked of the cairn its party raised in 534, offering to take it down.

The court weighs it as a court's statement and not as a verified fact, which is the discipline the realm applies to its own exports of knowledge. Two parts of it can be checked and both hold. The realm's own party found at that bay the marks of two large boats hauled up, a stone-lined hearth, cut timber and shavings and iron scale, and a length of hull plating cut and discarded, which is the refuse of a repair and not of a founding, and no other work within ninety kilometres either side. And the realm's returns for 550 carry two entries early in the season and nothing after, which is what a withdrawal looks like from a beach. On the one point the realm can test independently, the letter is corroborated, and that raises the standing of the rest without proving it.

**The one thing that does not reconcile, entered because it does not.** Egypt puts the beaching in 534. The realm's country contacts gave word of boats and strangers at that bay in 543, and the party of ten that went down overland in 544 judged the site months cold. Ten years and months are not the same. Three readings are open. The party of ten were ten men on an overland march with no craft in dating a beach, and may simply have judged it wrong, which the court thinks the likeliest of the three. The country people's word was not dated to the event and may have carried an old thing late. Or there were two beachings at that bay and Egypt has given notice of one. The realm cannot settle it from anything it holds, and it does not treat the discrepancy as evidence of bad faith, a wrong date being the commonest thing in any register including this one.

**A second thing the court notes without calling it a contradiction.** Egypt states orders to make no landing where there were works, vessels, or people, and the realm's returns record a party of about forty landing at the watering place below Post Seventeen in 544 and a party of about eighty working inland between Posts Two and Three in 548. The realm's posts on that line stood at intervals of about a hundred and sixty kilometres in those years, and both landings fell between posts. A party could come ashore on that coast without seeing a work or a man, and at Post Seventeen the party re-embarked as soon as fifty men came down onto the beach, which is what an order of that kind produces when the order meets a garrison. The two accounts can both be true and the court reads them so.

**The cairn, and the answer to the offer.** The Second Amendment of 550 had already directed that a work found standing with nobody at it is not to be thrown down, but recorded, measured, drawn, its characters copied, a dated marker of the realm's own set beside it, and the place garrisoned. The letter does not change that direction and strengthens the reason for it. A foreign marker whose sending court has stated in writing that it marks a place and claims nothing is a better thing to have standing on the realm's coast than a bare beach, because the statement stands with it. The realm will therefore decline the offer to take it down, will leave it, and will set its own dated marker beside it as ordered. The answer was written in the last season of the reign and had not gone when the reign closed. It is entered in full in the file so that a successor need only send it.

**The fever bark.** This is the most consequential item in the packet and it is the one the realm can do least with. Egyptian crews and garrison men on that coast stopped dying of the intermittent fevers from the later 540s and a bark preparation is issued to them. The identity of the bark, its preparation, and its dosage were not obtained, and the operation returned a total nil on where the plant grows or is grown.

The realm's own history is the reason this matters more to it than to most. Its Spice Island garrisons were killed by climate at a rate its ledgers never counted and had to be refilled by drafts from home for a century and a half. Its southern establishment now holds 250,000 people on a foreign continent, its island stations sit in equatorial country, and its own mortality registers since 528 are the first instrument it has ever had for measuring exactly the thing this bark answers.

Two things were ordered in 550, both to run from 551 within the standing tenth of revenue and neither touching the figures of this reign. The medical establishment is to test barks against the intermittent fevers by the method that produced the guard against the pox, which is trial, record, and keep what works without knowing why, drawing on the whole botanical range the realm now holds from the Deccan to the Malay peninsula, the archipelago, and Dakshinadvipa. And the factors on the East African coast are to buy the preparation itself as a trade good if it is ever offered in a market, since the realm has learned twice now that it can buy a plant more cheaply than it can find one.

> **The court's reading, marked as the court's.** This is the second plant Egypt holds that the realm cannot reach, and the shape is the same as the coffee. The realm hunted coffee down the whole East African shore for two reigns, lost two ships to the sea below it, and failed because every road to the plant's country ran through ground Egypt held. It should expect the same here and should not spend two reigns learning it again.

**The masts with wires.** Tall masts carrying wires stand guarded and attended at the Egyptian harbour posts and have stood as long as anyone in the factories remembers. The operation states the function was not obtained and does not guess at it. The court will guess, and marks the guess as a guess. Egypt told the realm in 462 that copper carries word between points faster than a rider and that it binds its administration by that force. The realm has since built a wire, a covering, a reader, and 320 kilometres of line. A guarded mast carrying wire at a harbour post is very probably the same thing, carried in the air upon poles rather than laid in the ground.

If that is right it is worth more to the realm than anything else in the packet, because the realm's line fails for exactly the reason an overhead line would not. The realm buried its trunk in glazed conduit to keep the wet out of a covering that softens when wet, sleeved every joint, carried the conduit on a raised drained causeway, and still loses the low ground for two to four weeks in the heaviest monsoon. It has been solving the wrong problem at some expense. The galvanic programme is directed from 551 to raise a trial line upon poles between Pataliputra and the first relay station, sixty to seventy kilometres, and to compare it season by season against the buried trunk beside it.

**The root crop and the highland tuber.** A new root crop has come into the coastal villages of that shore and a new highland tuber began coming down to market from about 546. The realm's factors buy coastal grain in those same markets and can buy both without any change of cover. They are ordered to. The precedent is exact and recent: the coffee hunt failed and brought home the African sorghum, millet, and cowpea bred for drought and heat, which now feed the dry northwest, the northern Deccan, and the arid ground of Dakshinadvipa, and which the realm would not have had if it had bought only what it went for.

**The operation itself, and what is now asked of it.** The tasking of 529 is answered in every part it was given. The realm knows what was bought, what sailed, in how many hulls, in what season, whether the hulls returned, and whether a second provisioning was under way. It also knows what the operation could not reach, and the list of nils is longer than the list of finds: nothing of Egypt's industry, fleet, or treasury, nothing beyond those harbours, nothing of the bark, nothing of where the hulls go.

The court marks one further thing. The identification of the hulls on the southern coast in 547 came from a writer in a squadron boat who had served four years as a clerk with the realm's factor at those same harbours and knew the speech and the coats when he heard and saw them. It did not come from the operation. Twenty-one years of paid residency produced the most valuable nil the realm has ever bought, and the single fact the realm most needed on the day came from an ordinary man who had once kept accounts in the right place. The court draws no rule from this beyond the obvious one, that men who have served in a foreign harbour are worth keeping track of after they come home, and it has ordered a register of them kept.

**What the court now holds about Egypt.** It held that Egypt would do to Dakshinadvipa what it did to Africa. Egypt reached that coast, found the realm upon it, made no claim, kept its stated orders so far as the realm's own returns can check them, gave notice unasked of the one work it left, offered to remove it, and withdrew. That is the opposite of what this court predicted, and the prediction was the whole ground of the largest commitment in the realm's recorded history.

The court will not read it as a change of Egyptian character. Egypt came to that coast and found fifty-seven harbours, twenty-two posts, a garrisoned line, and a squadron. Africa it reached first and empty. The realm cannot tell from this whether Egypt forbears by policy or by arithmetic, and it notes that the previous reign's spending is what made the arithmetic, so that the venture may have been unnecessary and may equally have been the thing that made itself unnecessary. Both readings fit every fact the realm holds. The court sets them down side by side and does not choose.

On the standing marks: the trust the realm places in Egypt rises from the floor, for the first time in a hundred and sixty years, because Egypt volunteered a thing that told against it when silence would have served it better. It does not rise far. The judgement that the galvanic counsel of 495 was given to waste the realm's effort is untouched by anything in this packet. The danger the realm assesses in Egypt does not fall. On the southern coast it falls sharply. Everywhere else it rises, because the realm now knows that Egypt has been moving its people, its horses, and its breeding stock south by sea without pause for twenty-one years to ground the realm cannot find, and that it has a medicine that stops the fevers killing garrisons in the country where the realm's own garrisons die.

### The Answers, and the One That Is Not Sent

Two letters were drafted in the last season of the reign and one correspondent gets nothing. The reasoning is entered here and the drafts stand in their own section below. They had not gone when the reign closed.

**To Egypt, and what it buys.** An offer was made to this realm in writing and an offer in writing is answered. The realm declines the removal of the cairn, and the reason is the one the Second Amendment already gives: a foreign work whose sender has stated in writing that it marks a place and claims nothing is a better thing to have standing on the realm's coast than a bare beach, because the statement stands with it and the beach would not. The letter also tells Egypt that the realm will set its own dated marker beside it and keep a post at that bay.

The draft as first written went further and told Egypt that neither the marker nor the post yet stood and that both were ordered. That sentence was struck before the draft was entered, and the reasoning that put it there is set out here because it was wrong in three ways and the register should carry the correction rather than the error alone.

It was priced as telling Egypt where the realm had been thin, which Egypt already knew from having lain eleven days at that bay undisturbed. That is the wrong tense. Egypt knew the bay was empty in 534. The sentence would have told Egypt it was still empty in 550, sixteen years unbroken, and that the answer to it was an order and not a post. Set beside this reign's own record that the Second Amendment had not reached the northwest line at the close, the passage being a season, it would also have told Egypt how long this realm takes to carry an instrument from the capital to that coast.

It would have falsified a formal notice after the fact. The notice of 543 stated that those coasts are garrisoned along their length. The court entered that as a knowing overstatement and its whole purpose was that a coast described as garrisoned in part is a coast with its gaps advertised. The struck sentence would have advertised the gap, named the bay it lies at, and handed a court that rates this realm near the floor a demonstrated instance of this realm overstating in formal correspondence. That is a discount applied to every assertion this realm makes afterwards, and it does not expire.

And the justification was void on what the realm itself holds. The argument was that saying it plainly is cheaper than being found out twice, which presumes Egypt finds out. Egypt's own letter, in this realm's hands, states that the squadron has been withdrawn and that Egypt keeps no vessel upon that coast, and the realm's returns for 550 carry two entries early in the season and nothing after. There is no instrument by which Egypt discovers it. The gap stays unadvertised and the letter says that a marker will be set and stops.

**One question is asked and it is a question of fact.** Egypt puts the beaching in 534, and the realm's record does not agree. The draft as first written said that the realm's people came to that bay ten years after and judged the refuse months old. That was repriced and cut. Its stated cost had been that Egypt learns the realm had a party there in 544, and the true cost was larger: it discloses that a foreign party can beach below this realm's post line and lie there ten years before this realm arrives, which is a measure of how blind that stretch of coast is and is worth more to a rival than the answer to the question is worth to this realm. What the letter now says is that the realm's own record does not agree with the year, that the realm holds its own record likelier to be at fault, and that it asks for confirmation. That keeps the courtesy, keeps the question, and gives away neither the latency nor the way the realm dates a site.

**What is withheld from Egypt, and why.** Nothing is said of the bark. To ask Egypt for it would tell Egypt three things at once: that the realm knows of it, that the realm watches those harbours closely enough to see a change in how often men die there, and that the realm's own garrisons die of those fevers. The last is a military weakness and the realm does not hand one over for a plant it will not be given. The realm's whole experience of the coffee is that Egypt sells the product and kills the seed, which means the preparation can be bought and the plant cannot be asked for, and the realm has already ordered its factors to buy. Buying gets the same thing and discloses nothing. Nothing is said of the transports going south, of the masts at the harbour posts, or of anything the realm holds about Egypt beyond that coast.

**To Rome, and why now.** The register has already named this debt against itself. The standing exchange with Rome, a hundred and fifty years old, exists to trade what each court sees of Egypt. The realm now holds two things squarely inside it and has offered neither, and its own record says a partner told nothing that large twice over may fairly conclude the exchange has ended.

The letter therefore carries two items and asks for nothing. That Egyptian transports at the East African harbours load people rather than cargo and sail south, families with their stock and tools, contract men with their households, garrison drafts, horses and breeding stock in the later years, with many hulls not returning. And that a preparation of a bark is issued to Egyptian crews and garrisons on that coast, and that the intermittent fevers have stopped killing them there at the rate they formerly did.

**What that costs, checked sentence by sentence for what it tells by inference and not only by statement.** Both items are things a merchant who calls at those harbours every sailing season would see from the quay and the anchorage, and the realm has traded at those harbours by Egypt's own permission since about 40 AD, which Rome knows. The letter gives no count, no year, no season, and above all no duration, because the duration is the source: a statement that a thing has run for twenty-one years can only come from someone who has stood there twenty-one years. The realm's judgement that the hulls and the shipbuilding are one programme is not sent, that being reasoning built on counts and the counts being the thing that would give the residency away. The observation that the older harbour works stand unfinished is sent; the realm's reading of it, which is that a power is stretched somewhere the realm cannot see, is not.

**Two sentences of the Rome draft broke the realm's own rule and were struck.** The rule the court set was no count, no year, no season, and above all no duration, the duration being the source. The draft then said that horses and breeding stock came in more recent seasons, which cannot be said by anyone who has not stood there long enough to tell an early season from a late one, and that some hulls come back after two or three years, which cannot be said without three continuous years on the ground and a record kept hull by hull. Both were removed. The horses and the breeding stock stay, without the season. The non-return stays, without the count of years. The letter's own closing claim that it gives no seasons and no span of years was untrue of itself while those two sentences stood in it, and is true now.

**A cost in the Rome letter that the first reasoning did not weigh, and it is the one that changes the sending.** The realm's plan for the bark is quiet purchase by factors who already buy gum, hides, and coastal grain in those markets, which works only while the preparation is an ordinary issued good that nobody is asking after. Handing Rome the existence of an Egyptian fever specific invites Rome to collect against it, and the realm has a precedent from its own files for what follows: when its agents in Rome asked quietly after the source of the coffee, they found themselves marked by Egypt's own watchers in a market that was not Egypt's. A power that spends men abroad to guard a plant will tighten around a second one. Rome collecting could close the buying route this reign has just ordered.

The court weighed that against the debt and did not cancel the letter. What it did instead was set the order of the two. The Rome letter is not to go until the factors have bought the preparation and shipped a working quantity home, which the re-tasking of 550 already directs them to do without enquiry after its source. A season or two of delay costs the exchange little after twenty years of silence, and it removes most of the risk, because the realm cannot be shut out of a market it has already bought in. This is the same discipline the court applied to the gift and the request, which is that the order of things is worth more than the things.

**What is withheld from Rome, and why.** Dakshinadvipa is not mentioned, nor the coast returns, nor the Coast Order, nor that Egypt's hulls came to the realm's own southern coast and were met there. Rome does not know from this realm that the continent exists, and a third power learning of it buys the realm nothing and may cost it a coast. The masts carrying wires at the Egyptian harbour posts are not mentioned, and neither is the realm's own work upon that force, which is the most valuable secret the realm holds and the one thing it has never offered to anyone. That the realm has ordered a trial of barks is not mentioned. The letter states instead, as the report's own limit, that the realm has not obtained the plant, its name, or its country. That is not a request and Rome may do with it what it likes.

**And nothing is asked.** The realm asked in 501, was given terms in full, failed to perform for twenty-nine years, delivered in 530, and heard nothing for twenty more. A letter that gives two things and asks for one in the same breath makes the gift look like a payment, and the realm has already learned at Rome's hands to weigh what a gift will grow into. The request the realm still wants, which is the seals and where they fail and the fuel figures, is held for the letter after this one. It has been written since 533 and it is in the file. The standing directive that has kept it there, that it was not to go until Rome had answered the delivery of 530, is discharged by this decision, Rome having not answered in twenty years and the realm having judged that waiting further is a way of doing nothing while appearing to have a policy. The court sets the sequence down plainly so that it is not lost again in the way the matter of 501 was lost for twenty-nine years: the gift goes first and alone, and the request goes in the letter after it, whether or not this one is answered.

One condition is set upon that request before it goes. It is to be read against anything that arrives from Rome first, and against the realm's own record of the bargain of 501, before a word of it is sent. The realm's record says Rome's half never came. The realm has just been shown, at a cost of 6.25 billion paṇas, that a thing it held for a generation on its own reading can be wrong. And it knows from its own files that an obligation can sit in one court's papers unraised while the other court counts it outstanding, that being exactly what happened at Pataliputra between 501 and 530. To ask a partner for a thing he believes he has already given would cost this realm more than the seals and the fuel figures are worth.

**To China, nothing, and the ground for it.** Nothing came from the court of the Han in these twenty-five years and nothing goes back. The realm holds in its own workshops a hand cannon taken by bribery from a Han frontier arsenal and now fields a firearm of its own build, and any exchange invites a question about where the second came from that the realm cannot answer without giving up the first. Nothing in what arrived this reign bears on that. The court records the thing that will eventually force a letter and does not force it now: the nine trading stations the realm planted through the archipelago between 536 and 549 stand in waters where the settlement of 478 grants China free passage and market access, the realm has said nothing of them, and the day China remarks on them the realm will be answering rather than proposing.

### The Unresolved Matters of the Reign

The Coast Order answers a ship's company and does not answer a landing in force, and the court has not decided what the realm would spend to contest one. That is the largest question the reign leaves open, and it will be settled at a beach by a squadron commander out of Nauvandha reading an instrument written fifteen years earlier, with the nearest reinforcement a season away at the Strait.

Egypt's ships were on that coast from at least 530, and from 547 that is entered as fact and not as a reading. The realm handed over its written statement twice, in 547 and 549, and had no answer at sea either time. The answer came instead by letter to Pataliputra within the reign: no claim asserted, the squadron's orders stated, the squadron withdrawn. The realm still does not know what those hulls carried. Nothing nameable as a weapon was seen at four metres and the establishment stands ordered not to report them unarmed on that account, and that order is not relaxed by a letter.

A foreign work stands on the realm's ground at a bay below the northwest line, and the realm now has its sender's written account of why it is there, which is a repair party's position mark and no claim, with an offer to remove it. The realm declines the offer, leaves the work standing, and sets its own dated marker beside it. Neither that marker nor the post of fifty men existed at the close of 550, the Second Amendment carrying them having gone south only in the last season, and the letter declining the offer had not gone either.

The premise of the whole southern venture is overturned. No second Egyptian provisioning of the 500 to 505 kind occurred in twenty-one years of watching, and Egypt asserts no claim to that coast. What stands in its place is a harder question the realm cannot answer: Egyptian transports have carried people, horses, and breeding stock south round the Cape every season for twenty-one years, to ground the realm has no means of finding.

A bark on the East African coast has stopped the intermittent fevers killing Egyptian crews and garrisons since the later 540s. The realm holds the fact and not the plant, its name, its preparation, or its country. Its own garrisons have died of those fevers for a century and a half, and it has 250,000 people on a foreign continent and stations in equatorial country. A programme of trials is ordered from 551.

Guarded masts carrying wires stand at the Egyptian harbour posts and have stood beyond memory. The court reads them as the copper network carried in the air, marks the reading as a guess, and has ordered an overhead trial line on poles from 551 against the buried trunk that loses its low ground every monsoon.

The reserve stands at 620 million against 600 at the accession. The reign spent the growth of its revenue on the platform, and the treasury is no stronger than it found it, though the establishment that was pure charge in 525 now returns 84 million a year against a charge of 125 and the gap closes each decade.

The southern continent is held to a depth of two hundred kilometres on its eastern and northern coasts and along a line of twenty-two posts on its arid northwest. The island peoples of the great northern island and the cold southern island were brought in by force in the previous reign and hold terms offered in this one, and the debt the realm's own history names, that garrisons without integration breed resentment, is part paid.

The nine trading stations through the archipelago stand in waters where the settlement of 478 grants China free passage and market access, and the realm does not know how China reads them. Nothing has been said on either side.

The engine runs the year round under sail and steam together and does not yet drive a hull without sail. The galvanic line runs 320 kilometres along one road and goes dead on the low ground for two to four weeks a year. Rome has been sent what it was promised. The shipping seen provisioning on the East African coast in the previous reign is still unaccounted for.

**One document, from the council at Alexandria, addressed to the throne at Pataliputra. The year is not fixed. It falls within 525 to 550 and the road it came by is not entered in the register.** Its substance, in the order it sets it out.

- Egypt sets down unasked that in 534 an Egyptian hull struck a reef upon that coast and was beached to be repaired upon an empty stretch of shore, where the party lay eleven days. It left the refuse of a repair, and before sailing it raised a cairn and set in it a squared timber cut with Egyptian characters. Egypt states that this is what its survey parties do upon every coast they chart, to fix a position for the ship that comes after; that it marks a place and claims nothing; and that it gives notice of it so that it should not be taken for what it is not. Egypt offers to take it down if the realm would have it down.

## Technical assessments

**Physically received with the letter from Rome: twelve Roman engineers and two master fitters, and the working drawings for the hull engine.** They travel with the convoy that carries the letter, to work in whatever yard this court names, and Rome states they are not to be paid by this court.

This is the first construction knowledge ever delivered to this realm by a foreign power. The realm's standing condition since 365 has been that it holds the fact of a foreign capability and the making of none: Egypt has given it function and never construction across a hundred and eighty-five years, and the realm has built its cannon, its firearm, its printing, its engine, and its wire by reasoning from a result it had only seen. That condition is broken here and it is broken by the one power the realm rates a partner. The drawings are paper and the men are the greater part of the delivery, Rome's own stated reasoning in 525 having been that paper builds nothing without hands that have already ruined a few.

What the realm holds bearing on technique and did not get a method for remains as before: a bark preparation issued on the East African coast has stopped men dying of the intermittent fevers there, and tall guarded masts carrying wires stand at the Egyptian harbour posts. It has the fact of each and the making of neither.

## To Rome, sent in the year reckoned 530, by the Mesopotamian road to the Syrian frontier, with one printed and sealed volume

From the throne at Pataliputra to the throne of Rome.

In the year reckoned 501 this realm asked Rome for the working method of an engine that moves a hull, and offered in exchange the complete account of the hand-weapon carried by China's soldiers. Rome set out its own half in full before receiving anything, and required one delivery and nothing less. The volume accompanying this letter is that delivery, complete. Nothing is asked in return for it.

The delay was twenty-nine years and the reason does not flatter this realm. At the time of the offer this court held the men who survived that weapon at the island of Tidore. Those men could describe being shot at. They could not describe how the thing is built so that another maker may copy it, which was the substance of what Rome required. A witness's account had been offered where a maker's account was needed, and the difference was not understood at Pataliputra until Rome's terms arrived. An example of the weapon was then sought. That took the better part of two decades and cost more than is set down here. It reached the engineers of this realm in the year reckoned 524 and was still on the bench when the present ruler came to the throne. It was measured and drawn across the two years following. Barrels were then cast to its dimensions and fired through two years more, that proving being the difference between a description of a weapon and an account a maker can build from. Each such barrel was checked against the example itself, part offered up to part, the example being in the room. The account was written in the year reckoned 528 and struck and sealed in 529.

The volume gives, under separate heads: the weight and the length; the manner of carrying and of holding; the construction part by part, with barrel, lock, and stock drawn and measured, and the metals and their treatment so far as the metalworkers of this realm could determine them; the means of ignition; the loading sequence; the assessed killing range and the range at which aim fails; the time between shots for a trained man with his charges made ready; the effect observed on timber, on stone, and on men; and the full reasoning of the engineers.

Three plain warnings about the figures, all set down in the volume beside the figures they touch.

The example arrived with no powder. Every figure of range and of rate of fire was got by firing this realm's own powder in that barrel, and this realm's powder is made from the saltpetre of its own plains to its own proportions. Range and rate are the two figures most moved by the charge. A different powder would change both, and by how much the engineers of this realm cannot say, having never had a grain of China's to weigh against their own.

The example is one piece and could not bear the firing the observed effects required. A small number of proving shots were fired with the example itself, and the effects upon timber, upon stone, and upon men were observed with barrels cast in this realm's shops to the example's own dimensions. Every figure is marked according to which piece produced it. Those copies were made to the Chinese pattern and are not the weapon this realm has since built for its own soldiers, which the volume does not describe.

The two points on which the engineers of this realm remain uncertain, and say so in the volume, are these. The first is the powder, as above. The second is whether the example represents what China now makes. It came out of material an arsenal had set aside as unserviceable, and a piece so set aside may be a current pattern discarded for a fault or an older pattern discarded for being old. Nothing about the piece settles which. If Rome finds elsewhere a weapon of this class that differs from the drawings, the difference may lie there and not in any error of the measuring.

There is no padding in the volume. Where a thing is not known, it states that it is not known.

Two further matters, given for nothing.

First, this realm has since built a weapon of the same class by its own casting work, and has had such weapons in its soldiers' hands since the year reckoned 518. In about 478 this court answered Rome's questions upon that weapon with what the survivors of Tidore could say and what its engineers reasoned of its reach and its loading, which was thin, and it has sent no word since. Rome should have this from this letter rather than from another quarter.

Second, a warning, the two courts having traded what each sees of Egypt for a hundred and fifty years. In the year reckoned 495 Egypt gave this realm guidance on the force that runs in metals joined in salt water, the force by which Egypt binds its administration through copper lines. Egypt's counsel was that the road to it lies through the study of plants, and that this realm should look for the force in its trees and grasses and grain. The counsel was taken gratefully, every botanist of the medical tradition was set to it, and the programme ran the whole twenty-five years of the last reign and was closed in the third year of this one, thirty-two years from the giving of the counsel. It never produced the understanding of the force it was pointed at. It did return other things: the behaviour of the force in salted plant matter, an effect of metal plating, and a twitching of dead tissue under live current which served this realm as a second confirmation of the force from another angle, arrived at against the design of the programme. None of that was the thing sought, and the road Egypt named did not lead to it. This court judges the counsel to have been given in order to spend the effort of this realm where it would not arrive. The purpose is this court's reading and may be wrong. The result is not in question. If Egypt has ever offered Rome guidance of that shape, this is what it was worth here.

Nothing is asked of Rome in this letter.

## To Egypt, sent in the year reckoned 543, by sea through Egypt's own waters

From the throne at Pataliputra to the throne of Egypt.

This notice concerns the great dry southern continent that lies beyond the eastern islands, and the islands to its north and to its south. It asks nothing and offers nothing.

That continent was found, and its northwest coast charted, by a survey expedition of this realm in the years reckoned 415 to 420. A depot with shipwrights was placed upon that northwest coast in about 478 and has been kept there since. Its coasts are settled, administered, and taxed by this realm, are garrisoned along their length, and are governed under this realm's law from seats established upon the continent itself. The great island to that continent's north and the cold island to its south are held by this realm also.

Egypt has known of that continent since this realm told it, in the exchange of about the year 462, when the charts of the eastern seas, the harbour notes, and the continent's location were given, and the account of what the copper lines along Alexandria's walls are for was received. That bargain is raised here not as a complaint but to record that Egypt learned of the ground from this realm, that this realm was upon it first, and that the dates above may be tested against Egypt's own memory of the exchange.

One matter of practice. The harbours of this realm upon that continent and its islands are open to Egypt's ships for trade on the same terms as the ships of any other power, and will grant no base, no garrison right, and no ground. That rule is adopted because it was applied to this realm at Aden and along the whole length of the East African coast for five hundred years, from the first meeting of the two powers at that strait, and because five hundred years of being turned from those harbours showed this court that the rule works and keeps a coast in one hand. No grievance is raised that requires an answer, and none is expected.

## Draft, to Egypt. To go by sea through Egypt's own waters, as this realm has approached Egypt since its first embassy.

From the throne at Pataliputra to the council at Alexandria. Written in the year reckoned 550.

The letter sent by the council to this throne has been received, and the sealed paper handed to Egyptian officers off the southern coast is confirmed to have been given by this realm's own order and to say what this realm meant it to say.

Upon the cairn and the timber. This realm will not have it taken down and thanks the council for the offer. Egypt has stated in writing that the work marks a place and claims nothing, and a work standing with that statement beside it is of more use to this realm than an empty beach would be. It will be left where it stands, unharmed and unaltered, and a marker of this realm's own will be set beside it, of stone, cut with the dates of this realm's finding of that coast, of its depot, and of its settlement.

Upon one matter of fact, and this realm sets out its own likeliest answer first so that the question is not read as anything worse. The council puts the beaching in the year reckoned 534. This realm's own record of that bay does not agree with the year, and this realm holds it a great deal likelier that its own record is at fault than that the council's is. It asks the council to confirm the year notwithstanding, because if there were two beachings at that bay rather than one, this realm would rather know it than not, and the council's papers can settle in a line what this realm cannot settle at all.

Upon the terms. They are as the council states them and this realm holds to them. The harbours of this realm upon that continent and its islands are open to Egypt's ships for trade, water, and repair on the same terms as the ships of any power, and grant no base, no garrison right, and no ground. This realm has long had trade at Egypt's ports and says plainly that it has, and it looks for nothing at those ports beyond what it has.

This realm asks nothing further of Egypt.

## Draft, to Rome. To go by the Mesopotamian road to the Syrian frontier.

From the throne at Pataliputra to the throne of Rome. Written in the year reckoned 550.

These two courts have set before one another what each sees of Egypt for a hundred and fifty years. This realm has lately come by two things that belong plainly within that practice and has not sent them, and it sends them now. Nothing is asked in return for either.

The first. At the harbours of the East African coast, Egyptian transports load people rather than cargo and sail south. They carry families with their stock and their tools, men out of the term of their contracts with their households, drafts for garrisons, and horses with breeding stock. A large part of those hulls does not come back within the season, and some do not come back at all. Shipbuilding at the hardwood yards of that coast has increased and the building is of transport pattern. Alongside this, the moles and wharves at the older Egyptian harbours of that coast stand begun and unfinished.

This realm sends the observation and not a conclusion drawn from it. It does not know where those hulls go. What it has heard on that coast of a land beyond a western ocean came second hand, differed between the men who told it, and is not corroborated, and this realm sets no weight upon it and would not have Rome set any either.

The second, and this realm holds it the more valuable of the two. Egyptian crews and garrison men on that coast have stopped dying of the intermittent fevers at the rate they formerly did, and a preparation of a bark is issued to them. This realm has not obtained the bark, nor its name, nor the country it grows in, nor how it is prepared or in what quantity it is given. It has the change and the fact of the issue and nothing further, and it sets down the limit of what it has so that Rome does not take the report for more than it is.

Both of these are things a merchant standing at those quays in the sailing season may see, and this realm trades at those harbours by Egypt's permission and has since the two powers first met at the strait. It gives no numbers, no seasons, and no span of years, and Rome will understand why a court does not set down the shape of its own looking.

This realm asks Rome for nothing in this letter.

# SPY TASKINGS

**Ordered 529.** The tasking has run twenty-one years and stands open at the close of 550. The establishment notes, without drawing a conclusion from it, that the identification of the hulls in 547 came not from this operation but from a writer carried in a squadron boat who had served four years as a clerk with the realm's factor at those same harbours and knew the speech and the coats when he heard and saw them.

- **Who.** Two resident trading factors of the realm's merchant houses on the East African coast, one Gujarati and one Omani, with the ship-masters of the Oman gum, hide, and grain trade as carriers. With them from 529, one officer of the realm's own service under a factor's cover, the first man of the service placed on that coast.
- **Cover.** Buying gum, hides, and coastal grain for the Oman market. Both factories have traded those harbours by right since before 500. The officer travels as a junior man of the Gujarati house learning the accounts.
- **Target.** The harbours where bulk provisioning was seen in about 500 to 505. What was bought and in what quantity, what sailed, in how many hulls, in which season, whether those hulls have returned, and whether a second provisioning of the same kind is under way. Timber, cordage, and water orders in particular, the original report having been assembled from provisioning alone.
- **Network age.** The two merchant factories are twenty-five to fifty years established on that coast and have not been used for the realm's business before. The realm's own officer is new to the coast as of 529 and has no line of communication independent of the seasonal ships, so a report takes a season to reach Oman and two to three weeks more to reach the capital.

**Re-tasked 550, the same residency, no new operation and no new men.** The tasking of 529 is answered in every part and is closed. Three things are asked instead.

- **Who and cover.** Unchanged. The two factories and the officer under the Gujarati factor's cover, twenty-one years in place and not detected.
- **Struck from the tasking.** All questions touching crown victualling quantities and crown contract tonnage. The operation's own assessment names that interest as the one a buyer of gum and hides has no commercial reason to hold, and one direct question about a contract's tonnage as the thing that would burn the officer. The realm has its answer on provisioning and will not risk twenty-one years of residency to refine it.
- **Asked instead.** The bark preparation issued against the intermittent fevers: to be bought as a trade good wherever it is offered in a market, in any quantity, without enquiry after its source, its name, or its maker. The new coastal root crop and the new highland tuber: living seed and living stock, bought in the ordinary way of the factors' grain trade and shipped home to be grown out. The seasonal count of embarkations on the southward transports, continued as it has been kept.
- **Not asked.** Where the southward hulls go, where the bark grows, and anything beyond those harbours. The operation has returned a total nil on each across twenty-one years, and asking again buys nothing and costs the cover.

# TERRITORY AT 550

## HELD, SETTLED OR GARRISONED

**The subcontinent and its seas.** The whole subcontinent to its southern tip with Sri Lanka. The capital at Pataliputra. The Kolar gold fields, worked deeper on steam pumping and still yielding less each decade. The Zawar zinc workings. The Punjab salt range. Thirteen paper mills on the Ganges, its tributaries, and the Tamil coast.

**The northwest and Central Asia.** The Afghan lands. The Hindu Kush passes, road and two new relay stations rebuilt 531 to 539. Five forward granaries between the passes and the river at a hundred and twenty days of ration. Three new garrison towns on the Bactrian and Sogdian road at Ashvasthala, Girivraja, and Uttarakota. Bactria with its river gold. Sogdia with Samarkand and the Silk Road cities. The Syr Darya line, garrisoned, with 240 fixed cannon and eleven magazines.

**Iran, the Caucasus, Mesopotamia.** The Iranian plateau with Khorasan and Media. Armenia and the Azerbaijan highlands. Mesopotamia to the Euphrates, governed from the second capital by the Tigris, embankments and canals rebuilt 532 to 545, the Rome road's waystations doubled and patrolled 528 to 540.

**The Gulf and the Arabian shore.** Oman at the mouth of the Persian Gulf. The tributary trading ports of the gulf's Arabian shore. A tributary hold on eastern Yemen.

**The eastern seas.** The Strait of Malacca with the realm's trading port at the eastern mouth and the eastern fleet based upon it. Ternate, 1,200 men, and Banda, 700, supplied by the southern road, powder raised from forty to sixty rounds between the two islands to three hundred and fifty rounds at each. Nine trading stations through the archipelago east and north of Banda, thirty to eighty men at each, established by agreement between 536 and 549, two of them on nutmeg-growing islands outside the Banda group.

**The southern road.** Seven fortified waypoint stations between the Strait and the northern belt: Dvipasetu, Jalaghata, Payonidhi, Varunapura, Setubandha, Timiratirtha, Anilagupta. A hundred to two hundred men, a cistern, a granary, and a careening beach at each.

**Dakshinadvipa, the southern continent.** Fifty-seven major settlements, 250,000 registered persons.
- *Northern belt:* Uttaradvara, Somatirtha, Vetravati, and twelve further anchors, three founded this reign at river mouths.
- *Eastern coast:* Ratnakula, the establishment's seat, with Nauvinda, Gopatha, Shankhatirtha, Tamraghata, Suvarnamukha, Ambupura, Prabhasa, and twenty-five further anchors, four founded this reign to the south. Eleven shipyards and one engine works.
- *Middle coast:* Rukshatirtha, Madhyaghata, and six further anchors, five founded this reign.
- *Arid northwest:* the depot and yard at Nauvandha, founded about 478; the line of twenty-two posts at about a hundred and sixty kilometre intervals, eight of them walled stations of two hundred men with cisterns.
- *Inland:* forty stations along the two eastern river systems and the northern river, sixty to two hundred kilometres from the coast.
- Four hundred and twenty lesser stations, markers, wells, and small garrisons. Seventy-one signal towers along the eastern coast and the northern belt.

**The great northern island.** Nine coastal stations including Dvipamukha, Nagatirtha, and Vanatirtha. Coastal districts assessed at 900,000 persons and taxed. Terms of local governance in force from 540 and extended along two river systems inland. It has not been checked against a second count, the establishment gives it as nine hundred thousand within a hundred thousand either way, and it is entered here as the establishment's return and not as a figure the treasury has audited.

**The cold southern island.** Four stations including Himatirtha and Dakshinaghata, 400 men, terms of local governance offered from 546.
