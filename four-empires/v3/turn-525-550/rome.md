## 525 to 550 AD: Powder, and the Price of the Western Commitment

### The Silence from Pataliputra, and Why the Powder Programme Was Not Allowed to Wait for It

The single message of the previous turn went east to the Maurya court, offering the complete working method of the marine steam engine, boiler through transmission, against a maker's-standard account of the Chinese hand-firearm, both delivered whole rather than in instalments, with Rome stating that it would test what arrived before providing engineers. Twenty-five years passed. Nothing was received. No reply came. No Roman engineer went to any Indian yard, and the doctrine recorded in 525 of shifting from espionage toward open presence in Indian yards remained a plan that was never executed. That is the settled fact of the exchange at 550 and it is the fact the rest of this turn had to be arranged around.

The Senate and the *curatores* of the weapons programme argued four readings of the silence, and the record keeps all four because none was eliminated. The first was that the message never arrived. This is not a weak reading. Rome's own record for the fifth century has persistent banditry on the Mesopotamian leg of the Euphrates route, reported by Roman merchants and denied by the Maurya at the time, and a single sealed packet moving on a route that loses cargo is a packet that can be lost. Against it stands the fact that Roman merchant traffic continued to move both ways across the whole twenty-five years and that no return message of any kind arrived on any of it, which a single theft does not explain.

The second reading was that the message arrived and the account is being assembled. A maker's-standard account of a weapon means drawings with dimensions, the metal and its working, the charge and its proportions, and the failures. Rome knows from its own programmes what that costs to produce, because the standardised-parts catalogue of the military steam programme took from about 390 to 460 to reach the form it now has. A court that has to gather such an account from its own engineers and its own survivors of an engagement it lost might take a decade. It would not take twenty-five years, and a court taking a decade could have said so in one line inside the first two.

The third reading was that the offer was declined and the decline was not stated. The fourth was that the interval was itself the object, and that the eastern court preferred an open Roman offer of the hull engine standing unanswered to either taking it or refusing it, because an unanswered offer commits Rome to nothing it can withdraw from with dignity while leaving Rome hoping. The court's disposition toward the Maurya, formally recorded since about 490 as a commercial partner of value and a strategic partner of limited reliability, weighs the third and fourth above the first and second. That disposition rests on the concealed Syr Darya cannon held back while Rome was pressed four times for what it knew of Chinese firearms, the route reports that denied robberies Roman merchants were suffering, the tariff raised from twelve to twenty-four per cent without consultation, and the claim of independently discovering a force running in metal that arrived conveniently soon after contact at Alexandria. A court with that record behind it does not get the benefit of the innocent readings. Filed unresolved, weighted toward the third and fourth.

What followed from the silence was the important part. The reorientation of the powder programme from stored pressure to combustion, resolved about 485 and confirmed in 525, had been expected to be steered by the Indian account. The 525 record says so plainly, that India's incoming firearm account was the one thing likely to pull Rome's engineers off the emplaced launcher before a generation was spent on it. The account did not come. The *curatores* accordingly put the programme on the footing of receiving nothing, on the reasoning that a programme which has run two hundred and twenty-five years without a weapon cannot be paused a further twenty-five for a packet whose arrival cannot be dated. This is the whole of why the weapon Rome now holds is the weapon Rome reasoned its own way to, with the errors that came with reasoning alone, set out below.

### The Working Mixture, and Where the Supply Had to Come From

The search for a stable ratio, reckoned in 525 to stand about six years off, closed in 531. Three materials go into it. Charcoal and the yellow stone of the Sicilian and Campanian workings were both long familiar and cost the programme nothing to obtain. The third had to be found, and how it was found is worth setting down, because nothing in Roman practice pointed at it.

After the reorientation of about 485 the programme's standing instruction was to test any substance reported to make a fire burn fiercer, and the instrument used to gather them was the one Rome always reaches for, which is print. A circular went out through the provincial offices in 487 and was reprinted twice, asking for reports of such substances from anyone who worked with fire. Several hundred came back over the following decades and almost all were worthless, being resins, pitches, oils, and the ordinary mineral salts. One from a tanning district in the 490s described a white bloom that gathers on the walls of old cattle byres and reported that a handful of it thrown on a charcoal bed made the bed flare up. That report is why anybody scraped a byre wall, and the programme's own file credits it, though not by name, the collector having been paid the standard bounty and not recorded further.

Against the bloom stood the obvious candidate, the *nitrum* of ordinary commerce, the mineral salt bought in bulk through Alexandria for glass and for washing, and the programme spent between eleven and fourteen years before 531 on it. Batches made with trade *nitrum* burned and did not drive. Batches made from the wall bloom and the earth under old byres drove hard. The two are alike enough in appearance to be confused in a store, and the men who handle both report that they do not taste alike, so the programme can tell them apart. What it cannot do is say why one drives and the other does not, or predict from anything observable which of two unfamiliar salts will behave which way, and it did not obtain that in this turn. The working rule is therefore by source rather than by property: the salt is what comes off ground or walls that have stood under animals, and nothing bought as *nitrum* is to be substituted for it at any price.

Getting from a scraping to a usable salt took its own five years. The scrapings arrive as earth and rubbish and are perhaps a fortieth part salt by weight. They are steeped in water in vats, the liquor is drawn off and strained, boiled down in iron pans, and left to cool, and the crystals that come out of it are washed and boiled a second time. The first salts to form on cooling were found by trial in 528 to be useless and are thrown away, which cost the programme two years of poor batches before anyone thought to separate what formed early from what formed late. Wood ash entered the process at the straining step, and it entered by accident: a contractor in the British district strained his liquor through a bed of wood ash to clear it of dirt, and the salt that came out drove measurably harder than his previous deliveries. The programme adopted ash in the leach on that trial alone. When the *curatores* began building deliberate beds from about 538, walls and pits of dung, straw, and ash wetted and turned on a schedule, the ash was put into the beds as well, on the reasoning that a thing which improves the salt in the vat may improve it in the wall. No trial supports that step. It is recorded here as an untested extension of a tested finding, and the beds laid with ash and those laid without have not been compared, which the court should regard as a gap and not as a result. The beds were built at all because collectors reported that a scraped wall yields again in two or three years, so a wall could be built to be scraped. They are slow. Those laid in 538 came into first yield around 543 and are not yet at the yield projected for them.

That supply chain set the size of the armament, so the *curatores* costed it, and the figures are the load-bearing ones in this turn. In its best year, 549, the four mills together delivered between twelve and fifteen tons of finished powder, which is the treasury's figure and is the least reliable number in this section, because output was repeatedly interrupted by the losses that come with grinding the material and eleven mill buildings were rebuilt across the period as an expected charge rather than as accidents. Output before 543 was a fraction of that, on the order of two to four tons a year once the collection contracts were running and almost nothing before them. Cumulative production from 531 to 550 was between sixty and eighty tons, of which the greater part was consumed by proving, by the destruction trials of 540, by the grain trials, by the hand-weapon trials from 546, and by the practice allowance.

Against a service charge of about two Roman pounds, which is near two thirds of a kilogramme in the measures the engineers use for boiler work, fifteen tons is about twenty-two thousand charges. The practice allowance fixed by the *curatores* is forty rounds a piece a year, so one hundred and eighty pieces consume about five tons a year in practice, proving takes about three, and what is left over accumulates. The stock standing at 550 is between twenty-two and thirty tons, or something over thirty-five thousand charges, which will keep sixty pieces in continuous action at sixty to a hundred rounds a day for six to ten days and no longer.

The consequence was stated to the Senate in exactly these terms and accepted. Rome did not arm its field legions with powder in this generation and could not have. A thousand pieces on campaign at five hundred rounds each would want something over three hundred tons in a single year, which is more than everything the mills have made since 531 and more than twenty years of present output, and it cannot be made to exist faster than the beds mature. The foundries were never remotely the limit. The ordnance shops of the Rhine and British districts are the same shops that bore engine cylinders and roll hull plate, and the *curatores* reckoned they could deliver five hundred pieces a year on existing plant if ordered to; five hundred pieces at the practice allowance alone would consume the whole annual output of powder and leave nothing for proving or for stock. Rome has the capacity to found guns it has no means of serving.

### The Branch That Turned, and the Eight Years Lost Inside It

The court's standing instruction is that the working is the only thing that has ever caught a Roman error, so the working is set down here in full, including the part of it that was wrong.

The premise that broke in 485 was that the Han weapon must store pressure, held from about 300 for a hundred and eighty years, and it was a correct inference from a false premise rather than carelessness. Rome had mastered pressure, Rome's own launchers accelerated a projectile with expanding gas, and the flying sphere reported in the incident of the 290s looked like a launch from a better pressure engine than Rome had. What broke it was one fact from the Chinese warning of the 480s: a single soldier carries the weapon and fires it on the instant. Rome knows from its own two centuries with boilers that stored pressure requires a heavy vessel and time to raise it, and that its own emplaced launchers must be pre-heated. A weapon a man carries and fires at once has nowhere to keep pressure and no moment in which to raise it, yet plainly looses great force with flash and smoke at its mouth. Therefore the force is made at the instant of ignition by something that burns and expands violently. That step is settled and 531 confirmed it, because a mixture Rome lit did exactly that.

The step that turned inside this turn follows from the same fact and was not drawn until 532. If the force is made in the instant, the vessel is required to hold only for the instant. Rome's boilers are heavy because they must hold for hours against a pressure that is continuously renewed, and Roman practice had never had cause to distinguish the weight a vessel needs from the duration it must last. Therefore the weight of the vessel is set by duration and not by the force alone, and a vessel that must hold for an instant can be far lighter than boiler practice would size it.

The *curatores* did not act on that in 532. They sized the first weaponised pieces by boiler proportions, wall thickness carried in ratio to the bore as the naval boiler programme had established it, and produced pieces three to four times heavier than the ones now in service. Eight years went into those. The correction was empirical and came in 540, when the programme began firing deliberately thinned tubes to destruction to find where the proportion actually failed, and found that tubes at a third of the boiler wall survived charges the boiler-proportioned pieces were built for. Rome cannot say why boiler proportion overestimates the requirement so far. One line of argument inside the programme holds that a pressure applied in an instant is borne differently by the metal than a pressure sustained, and that the metal has some capacity to take a blow that it does not have to take a load, and that line is presently the majority view among the programme's engineers. It is recorded here as the programme's explanation and not as a finding, because nothing was measured that distinguishes it from any other explanation, and because the same body of engineers held the pressure premise for a hundred and eighty years on reasoning that was equally tidy.

Two further results stand as reliable in their core and unexplained. Lengthening the tube increased the force delivered up to a ratio of about eighteen to twenty times the bore and gave nothing measurable beyond it, and Rome has no account of why there should be a limit at all. Working the finished powder to a coarser and more even grain rather than a fine dust increased the force and reduced the number of pieces lost in firing, which the programme found by accident in 536 when a badly sieved batch outperformed a good one, and which it standardised without understanding.

The small tube followed from the light vessel by argument and did not follow in practice. Trials of a tube a man could carry and fire ran from 546. At one hundred paces the results were worse than a crossbow in accuracy, the loading was slower than a crossbow's, and the ignition failed often in wet weather. Whether the fault lies in the tube, in the grain of the powder, or in the means of ignition is unresolved. The programme knows perfectly well how to vary one thing against a fixed rest, having done it in 536 with two grain batches against one piece and in 540 by firing thinned tubes to destruction at a fixed charge and bore, and both of those gave clean answers. The difficulty here is that the three do not stay separate. A coarser grain alters the force and also alters how readily the charge takes fire, so a failure to fire and a failure to drive cannot be assigned to different causes; a narrower tube alters the force and also the fouling that spoils the next shot; a change of ignition alters where in the charge the burning starts, which alters the force again. The programme can vary one and observe three effects, which is not the same as being able to attribute them, and it has no means of measuring the pressure inside a tube at the instant of firing, only of seeing whether the tube burst and how far the ball went. What the court holds against its own failure is a rival's demonstrated practice: soldiers plainly carry such weapons and fire them, so the thing can be done and the fault is Roman. Funding continued on that reasoning alone.

### What the Powder Pieces Replaced, and What They Did Not Change at Sea

By 550 one hundred and eighty pieces stood in service, emplaced or on carriages, throwing a solid iron shot of five to six Roman pounds. They replaced the fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers position by position at the river crossings and the fortified points, and the replacement was not completed. Of about one hundred and forty launchers, sixty-two were withdrawn and the rest were left standing, because a launcher that must be pre-heated is worthless in a surprise and adequate in a prepared defence, and powder was short. The *praefecti* of the frontier districts were instructed to keep both until the mills allow otherwise.

The one hundred and eighty pieces are disposed as follows. One hundred and fifty-two stand at frontier and river positions in Europe, fourteen in the masonry batteries on Hispaniola and Cuba, and fourteen mounted afloat.

The fourteen afloat are carried by nine ships out of a steam fleet of twenty-eight hulls, and because the figure nine appears twice in this turn's papers it is worth separating. Nine hulls are gun-armed. Nine hulls are in western waters. They are not the same nine. Thirteen hulls in the fleet are iron-plated, and the nine gun-armed ships are drawn from those thirteen; of the nine, five are in home and Mediterranean waters and four are in the west. The western squadron of nine is made up of those four iron-plated gun-armed ships and five wooden hulls carrying no pieces, all nine rigged for sail with engines banked, since no steam warship can cross three thousand nautical miles on a bunker good for six to seven hundred. So nine of twenty-eight hulls are committed west, not eighteen.

Pieces were fitted only to iron-plated hulls, and the reason is narrower than the practice looks. The trials of 542 and 543 mounted pieces on existing wooden hulls with iron reinforcement and worked their frames loose, which is a finding about retrofitting ships that were framed for a ram and a ballista and not about what a wooden hull could be built to do. Rome's shipwrights could frame a wooden vessel for the recoil of a five-pound shot without difficulty. The *curatores* of the naval programme considered laying down purpose-built wooden gun vessels and declined, for a stated reason: iron plate is already the standard for new construction, adopted in the 480s and 490s against the shallow-draft swarming attack described to Rome in about 420, and starting a second new class in wood at the same time would divide yard capacity that is already short. Pieces go on the hulls Rome is building anyway.

The naval assessment against Egypt was not altered by any of this. Nine gun-armed hulls out of twenty-eight is a real gain measured against Rome's own fleet of 525 and it is measured against nothing on the other side, the Egyptian count being unknown, so it changes nothing that can be written down. The assessment of 525 stands: attacking Egypt is harder than ever and impossible anywhere near Egyptian waters.

The full iron hull has slipped and the slippage should be recorded, since the projection made around 500 was twenty-five to thirty years from then, which is 530 at the earliest, and 550 finds Rome still riveting plate over wooden frames. The plate is not the problem, the rolling mills having made consistent sheet since the 480s. The frames are. An all-iron hull needs iron framing in heavy sections, and the mills roll flat plate well and heavy angle and bar badly, so the sections have had to be forged and are expensive and inconsistent. Behind that sits yard capacity, which has gone to plating existing hulls and to the western run, and behind that sits the screw programme, which the *magister classis* has held at higher priority since Egypt's quiet vessels were first observed in the fifth century. The first all-iron hull was laid down in 548 and is expected in service between 556 and 560.

### The Western Commitment Cost More Than the Survey Forecast, and the Senate Chose to Keep Paying

The commitment of 500 to 525 was argued on three facts, that the land was real and rich, that the veterans owed land had somewhere to go, and that Egypt could not be assumed idle. All three still hold. What the argument had not costed correctly was the standing charge of keeping a presence three thousand nautical miles out, and the second twenty-five years is where that showed.

Four things ran over. First, hulls. Dozens of ocean-going sailing hulls dedicated to the western run are hulls doing nothing else, and by 550 the run absorbed one hundred and ten to one hundred and thirty of them permanently against the seventy the *magister classis* had projected in 520. A hull on the western run makes roughly one round voyage a year, and the Iberian and African grain and oil trades had to be re-let to make up the tonnage withdrawn from them. Second, food. The island colonies did not feed themselves in their first fifteen years. Grain went west in quantity through the 530s, and grain shipped three thousand nautical miles is the most expensive food the treasury has ever bought. Cassava and maize plantings on the islands closed that by about 542 and it should not recur. Third, the garrisons. Fifteen to twenty-five thousand troops went out for the conquest and about eleven thousand remained as garrison, paid at frontier rates with a transport charge on every relief. Fourth, and least forecast, the labour.

Before the labour is dealt with, the western population as a whole was recounted in 549 at the Senate's order, because the figures the treasury had been using were plainly too small to be consistent with the shipping it was paying for. The recount is set out here because it changes how the western charge should be read.

The colonist side can be reckoned two ways and both give the same order. By shipping: one hundred and ten to one hundred and thirty hulls on the run at roughly one round voyage a year is two thousand seven hundred to three thousand two hundred voyages across the twenty-five years, and although a great many of those carried stores, troops, timber, and returning cargo rather than people, the *praefectus* of the western fleets returns an average of sixty to ninety emigrants a voyage across the whole period, which gives something between one hundred and seventy and two hundred and eighty thousand landed in this turn alone. By roll: the allotment registers and the tax rolls of the island province, which are the better source because a veteran with an allotment is a man on a list, carried four hundred and sixty to five hundred and forty thousand colonists and their children at the recount of 549, the greater part of them born in the west or landed in the previous turn, when the run was already moving colonists in the tens of thousands a year. Ordinary mortality is in those figures and it is ordinary; the crossing and the island fevers were assessed in the previous turn as not severe, and nothing found since has changed that.

The subject side is counted far worse, by district heads rather than by roll. Hispaniola stands at one hundred and twenty to two hundred thousand at 550, Cuba higher at one hundred and eighty to two hundred and eighty thousand, the lesser islands together at forty to eighty thousand, so the subject and enslaved population is somewhere between three hundred and forty and five hundred and sixty thousand.

The western total is therefore about eight hundred thousand to one million one hundred thousand, and the colonist share is the larger of the two and rising, which is the reverse of what the treasury's papers said before the recount. Two populations are moving in opposite directions: one is added to by ship every season and by birth every year, and the other is falling. The court should read every western figure in this turn with that in mind, including the charge per head, which is lower than the old papers implied because the population is larger, and including the labour argument, which is the next matter.

Hispaniola's tributary and enslaved population fell from a first estimate of three to five hundred thousand at the earliest count to the one hundred and twenty to two hundred thousand given above, with Cuba following a similar course some years behind. The first count was made by district heads and is not reliable, so the ratio is worth more than the absolute figures, and the ratio is a fall of somewhere near half or worse in twenty-five years. The cause is unresolved. Rome's physicians argued three explanations in the printed medical journals: the labour in the gold creeks, which does kill and cannot account for the deaths among those not set to it; the change of diet under Roman requisition; and the air of newly worked ground, on the old and much-used analogy of the Delta marsh fevers of the campaigns before 90 BC. A minority held that the sickness came off the ships with the colonists, on the observation that villages nearest the landings emptied first. The *legatus* was accordingly ordered from 544 to land new colonists at a distance from tributary villages. Whether that helps is unresolved, and the court should not expect it to, because no Roman physician has any theory of what is being carried or how.

The fiscal instrument was argued in 533 and settled in 535. The *curatores* of the treasury proposed extending the existing luxury import duty to western cargo at the ports of landing, chiefly Gades and Olisipo with Ostia for the re-export, at twelve per cent. The land tax was not raised, because it is the largest line in the accounts at about thirty-four per cent of revenue and falls on the veteran colonies that are the whole recruiting base of the northern army, and because raising it to pay for land in the west while taxing men settled on land in the east is an argument no *legatus* wanted to make to his own colonies. Debasement was proposed by a minority and rejected on the standing objection that the mint is the instrument of last resort in a war and is worth less each time it is used. The Imperial Commercial and Industrial Council opposed the duty on raw cotton and hides, on the ground that the Rhine and British mills consume the cotton and that a duty on a raw material taxes Roman manufacture rather than western luxury. That objection was accepted in the manner of the standardisation compromise of 480, which is the court's usual way of settling with the Council: twelve per cent on cacao, tobacco, dyestuffs, and worked gold, four per cent on raw cotton and hides. The shortfall of about forty million a year against the *curatores*' target was covered by advances from the shipping companies against the following season's landings, which is a further step in a practice Rome has used since it took advances from tax-farmers and large suppliers, and which is not borrowing in any form the treasury will admit to, since there is no bond market to borrow in.

The duty and the advances together did not cover the western charge, which the treasury put at one hundred and sixty to two hundred million a year by 550 against western revenue of about ninety to one hundred and ten million, most of it gold. The fiscal conservatives who lost the commitment argument in the previous turn moved again in 536 to cap the charge and lost again, and the office that gained standing from both defeats is the *praefectus* of the western fleets, whose command now disposes of more tonnage than any other single naval command in the empire. The Senate's reasoning for continuing to pay is not that the west pays for itself. It is that the alluvial gold of the islands is thinning at the first workings, that the veteran land pressure the commitment relieved has not gone anywhere, and that the one theatre where Rome could contest Egypt at all is the New World, where neither power holds fortified home ground. That last reason is doing most of the work and it rests on an Egyptian disposition Rome has logged as unresolved. The court is aware of that and paid anyway.

### The Coal of the Northern Coast

The prospecting parties sent up the navigable rivers behind the northern seaboard in the previous turn continued through the 530s, and the finding of the turn came off the river the surveys number as the fourth navigable opening south of the great bay. Coal in workable seams was reached about fourteen Roman miles above the head of navigation. The engineers burned it under a standing pump engine at the river station and recorded it raising steam within a tenth of the poorer British grades, which is adequate for a ship's bunker and for an engine and is not good coal. Reliable in its core, since Roman engineers tested it themselves under a Roman engine. Unresolved as to quantity, because one basin proved at one working face is not a fuel supply and no Roman has surveyed the extent of it.

A tramway of fourteen miles was laid from the pit to tidewater between 543 and 549, built of local timber faced with imported iron strip rather than of rolled rail, because rolled rail shipped three thousand nautical miles costs more per mile than the tramway earns and the northern coast has excellent timber. This is the first fuel Rome has found on the far side of the ocean and it changes the western position more than the gold does, because a steam warship carries forty to fifty tons of coal for a range of six hundred to seven hundred nautical miles and the crossing from the Canaries is near three thousand. No Roman steam warship crossed or can cross under steam. The nine that went west went as sail-rigged hulls with engines banked, and a squadron in western waters fights only as long as its bunkers last and then is a squadron of poor sailing ships. Coal at tidewater on the northern coast is the beginning of the answer to that, and the *magister classis* was instructed in 549 to plan a coaling station there rather than on the gold islands.

### The Yucatan Reconnaissance Answered Its Question, and the Conquest Was Deferred Again

The station on the Yucatan coast was established to measure the interior before anything was committed to it, and it did that. Balloon sightlines from the coast and probes inland to about sixty Roman miles counted eleven or twelve substantial walled centres with cleared and cultivated ground between them, and no single authority was observed over them. Reliable in its core for the near interior. Unresolved as to how far the settled country runs inland, whether the centres stand subject to one another, and whether those further in are larger, because no probe went beyond sixty miles and there is still no interpreter.

The fractured case is therefore the more likely, and on the programme set in 525 the fractured case is the affordable one, taken one centre at a time. The conquest was nonetheless deferred, and the reasons are fiscal and not military. The eleven thousand troops in the islands cannot be drawn on until veteran settlement relieves them, which the *legatus* puts at fifteen to twenty years at present migration rates. The powder pieces are wanted at home first, and there are one hundred and eighty of them. The western charge already runs eighty million a year ahead of western revenue. The court's own record of the Delta before 90 BC and of Carrhae in 53 BC is that Rome loses when it commits a force it cannot supply into country it has not measured, and sixty miles is not the measure of an interior. The authorisation in principle stands. Nothing was committed.

### Egypt Remained Unresolved, and the Senate Declined to Resolve It by Inference

The southern survey of the previous turn produced the largest single military reassessment in the record and two items logged as unresolved: the shipbuilding surge in the home yards, and a northbound coastal column of large loaded ships counted past two hundred before the counting stopped. Neither was resolved in this turn, because nothing Roman observation produced in twenty-five years bore on them, and the Senate declined three times to convert the count into a finding.

The argument for converting it was that a column of the largest ships on that coast moving north toward the latitude from which a fleet crosses west, in the same decades in which Rome commits hundreds of thousands of colonists to the west, is an armada by any plain reading. The argument against it, which prevailed, is the one the previous turn set down: the same pieces fit an Atlantic gold artery running metal north that was invisible only because no Roman had sailed the coast, with the return leg run offshore exactly as Rome's own survey ran its own return, and the ships were never seen going south. Behind that stands the operative precedent, which is that Rome has twice ruined itself by treating a telescope sighting as a conclusion and has written both instances down. The finding remains unresolved. The Alexandria network was kept standing with its silent branch quarantined and untouched, as ordered in 525, and no replacement was walked into it.

One decision followed from the reassessment rather than from any new observation. The harbour works on Hispaniola and Cuba were fortified against ships rather than against islanders, with masonry batteries laid to take the new powder pieces from 546. Fourteen pieces of the one hundred and eighty are in the west. That is a small provision and it is what could be spared.

### The Junction at the River

The matter of joining the rail lines at the Dnieper, standing unanswered in Roman hands since 525, was answered in this turn. The terms sent are those the Senate settled in 525 and are set out in the letter below. They give a transshipment yard on the Roman bank rather than a joining of the lines, because Rome's single gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and would not be relaid, and because a rail running from one empire onto the other is a road for an army. Rome builds the sheds and cranes at its own cost, wanting the volume of direct rail-to-rail silk more than the argument about who pays.

The letter also answers the question Rome was asked four times in the previous century and could not answer. What the letter says is that a mainland exists in the western ocean, that its coast has been followed for more than a thousand miles, that it is inhabited, and that Rome holds ships, soldiers, and colonists on it. What it gives beyond that is nothing.

Two passages were struck from the draft between 546 and 549, and the striking is the part of this business worth recording, because the draft as first written contradicted its own refusal and nobody noticed for three years.

The draft of the seventh article said that the land lay west of the Canary Islands, at about six weeks under the trade wind. The eighth article then refused positions, soundings, and charts. The *magister classis* was asked to review the packet before sealing and reported that the seventh article had already given away everything the eighth article claimed to be withholding. A departure point, a named wind that fixes the bearing, and a passage time are sailing directions, and sailing directions are the only part of a chart a court without the coast needs in order to go and find it for itself. Soundings and harbour outlines are what a fleet wants once it is already there. On that reading the draft withheld the cheap part of the packet and handed over the expensive part in one sentence, and would have done it in a letter that cited the map of 300 AD as the reason for withholding anything at all. The whole clause was struck. The court accepts that the error stood in a sealed-ready draft for three years and that it was caught by a naval office reading for its own interest rather than by the secretariat that wrote it, which is the ordinary way Roman errors have been caught.

The draft also said that the peoples of the west work no metal. That was struck on the same reading. Rome rates the Han the dominant military power of the known world and rates the danger from it high, and there is no version of Roman interest served by informing such a court that there is a metal-less continent to be had. The letter now says the land is inhabited and says nothing about what its peoples possess.

The third striking was the largest and it was argued for the better part of two years. The draft offered, against an account of how the sphere of the earth was measured, both Rome's own observations of the heavens and the Roman method of fixing a ship's place at sea, which is the mechanical clock keeping reference time worked together with stellar sights through the glass. The argument for offering it rested on the criterion Rome has used for disclosure to the Han since the agreements of the late fourth century, which is that Rome shares what it judges the Han would develop without Rome in any case, and Rome shared its compass calibration, its type-casting, and its double-wall glass furnace on exactly that test. A court that has measured the sphere of the earth and that makes escapements has both halves of the method already and wants only to be told they go together, so on the plain application of the criterion the disclosure costs Rome little. The natural-philosophy patrons pressed hard for it, because an account of the measurement of the earth is worth more to a state with no theory of anything than any tariff in the letter, and Rome's own heliocentric case was settled in the previous turn by observation without any account of the earth's size to set beside it.

The *magister classis* and the *curatores* of the naval programme carried the refusal, and the Senate accepted it on two grounds. The first is a difference in kind between the two disclosures the letter was making. A chart tells a rival where Rome has been. A method of fixing position lets a rival go anywhere on the ocean and come back, repeatedly, without ever having been shown a Roman route, which makes the method the more consequential of the two by a wide margin and makes refusing charts while handing over the method incoherent. The second is Rome's own record of what the method did. The crossing of the 490s succeeded and returned because the ship carried a compass, a glass, and a good clock, and could write down its position every night for four months across water no Roman had ever crossed. Before that instrument existed, Rome had sailed the African coast for two generations laying coal depots season by season and had lost five ships doing it. The whole of Rome's presence in the west rests on the one technique the draft proposed to give away, and it rests on Rome having it and the reachable alternatives not obviously having it.

A fourth striking followed from the third and is smaller. The draft had offered, among the things Rome was willing to part with, the iron conductor that has been fixed to tall buildings since the fourth century to carry off the stroke of lightning. The *curatores* struck it on the ground that Rome cannot say why the device works, only that buildings carrying it are struck to their ruin less often than buildings without it, which is how it came to be built and is the whole of what Rome knows about it. A method Rome cannot explain is a method Rome cannot set down in a form another court could build from, and offering it invites a question the eleven men of the enquiry into the force in metal have spent sixteen years failing to answer. What went in its place was the pneumatic message tube, which Rome can describe completely because Rome designed it from its own aqueducts and bellows, and whose loss would cost Rome nothing, being a convenience of the administrative offices and, on Rome's own reckoning, an inferior reproduction of something at Alexandria that Rome has never identified.

Against that the court set down honestly what refusing costs. Rome may well get no account of the measurement of the earth, having offered coal-gas lighting and a message tube in place of the thing that was asked for in all but name, and the letter says as much rather than dressing the offer up. The natural-philosophy patrons lost the argument and said so in print, and the court's own ranking is what decided it, since standing and prestige and the satisfaction of curiosity sit low in the order and technical advantage over rivals sits second. The doctrine of managed disclosure was not set aside in this letter. It was applied twice against the wishes of the men who most wanted the exchange.

### What the Rest of the Revenue Bought

The frontier charge held at about sixty-five per cent, up from sixty-three, the rise being the powder mills, the western fleets, and the western garrisons. Of the remainder, the items worth recording are these.

Two thousand two hundred miles of new line were laid and about nine hundred doubled, at the *curatores*' figure of one and one tenth to one and four tenths million a mile on easy ground. The Dacia to Dnieper corridor was doubled throughout. The second Alpine crossing was deferred for the fourth time, because the *curatores* cost it at up to three million a mile and the existing crossing has not yet reached its capacity.

Four purpose-built steam ferries of about five hundred tons, able to take twelve loaded cars each, were put on the Gesoriacum to Dubris crossing of some thirty Roman miles of water between 537 and 544, with rail-height quays built at both ends. The *curatores* reckon a legion with its stores can now be put across in two days against eight to twelve before. The limitation was stated when the works were approved and stands: the ferries help Rome reinforce a loyal Britain and do nothing at all against a Britain that has already gone, because a Britain in revolt holds the far quay. Britain remains the permanent breakaway risk and its fiscal autonomy under the settlement of 362 remains the instrument that manages it.

Maize is established in the African provinces, Sicily, Baetica, and southern Italy, at yields the agricultural *curatores* return as half again to twice wheat on the same ground where the season is long enough. Northern adaptation is not achieved and the court has been told to expect two further generations of selection. The staples are still not traded to any peer. The pneumatic tube network was extended to four more administrative centres. The chimney and setback legislation of about 500 was enforced and the relocation fund spent, and the printed gazettes were correct that the rules moved the smoke without reducing the coal burned.

### Why Fifteen Men Went to the Emplaced Piece for Every One on the Hand Weapon

The programme's establishment at 550 is about nine hundred men on powder and pieces against about sixty on the hand weapon. Set against the court's own record, which says that the disclosure of individual firearms in the 480s changed Rome's military assessment more than anything since the first cannon sighting of the 290s, that split needs defending or admitting, and the Senate was asked for it in 547.

Part of it is not a comparison at all. Of the nine hundred, something over six hundred are the four mills, the collection contracts, and the two proving grounds, which are plant and supply rather than design, and they would be needed at the same size whatever the powder was fired out of. The design establishments compare at about two hundred and fifty against sixty, which is four to one and not fifteen.

Three arguments were given for the remaining four to one. The first is that the hand weapon cannot presently absorb men, because its three variables interact as described above and there is no instrument for measuring what happens inside the tube, so adding two hundred men to a badly posed question produces two hundred men's worth of inconsistent results. The second is disposition. The emplaced and carriage pieces defend the Dnieper line, the river crossings, and the fortified positions, which is the first item in the court's order of concerns and has been in every century on record, while a hand weapon re-equips a field army that Rome has no present intention of marching against any peer and every intention of not marching. The third is that the *praefecti* of the frontier districts asked for pieces and did not ask for tubes, and the Senate funds what its officers ask for.

The court also recorded the objection, because the men who lost the argument put it in print and it is not a weak one. Rome spent from about 300 to 485 on pressure vessels because pressure was the thing Rome had mastered, and it is now spending fifteen to one on the emplaced piece because the emplaced piece is the thing Rome has mastered. If the two errors have the same shape, the second will take a century to become visible in the same way the first did. Nothing was changed in the establishment on that objection. It is set down so that whoever reads this file next can see that it was made and see who ignored it.

### The Labour Settlement of 540

The fourth-century labour law was framed on productivity and had grown inadequate against a workforce that reads, organises by pamphlet, and has had a century of Christian argument about human dignity in circulation among it. The settlement of 540 raised the injury compensation schedule, fixed a shorter maximum shift in the deep workings, extended collegium recognition to the powder mills and the rail yards, and required the mine operators to certify shaft timbering to a printed standard. It was argued to the Senate and passed entirely on output and on the risk of a disaffected industrial workforce in a region that can be separated, which is how the risings of 338 to 358 are recorded and how this law is recorded. The Council and the British and Rhine mine operators opposed the certification requirement and lost it. What the provincial governors lost is discretion, since a recognised collegium brings a dispute to a court rather than to the governor, and several said so in print.

### The Enquiry into the Speed at Alexandria

A small enquiry was funded from 534, of eleven men, into why Rome's pneumatic message tubes do not achieve the speed Roman agents reported for the Egyptian offices at Alexandria across the fifth century. It was previously carried in the papers as the enquiry into the force in metal and has been retitled, because that name states the Maurya's hypothesis rather than Rome's question, and an office should not be named after a proposition it was set up without crediting. It was not funded on the Maurya claim about a force running in metal, which Rome declined in the previous century and has no more reason to credit now that its author has been silent for twenty-five years. It was funded because Rome built the pneumatic system as the explanation it could build for itself and the explanation does not account for the observed speed. The enquiry produced nothing in sixteen years. It has no way to specify what it is looking for, and the court accepts that an enquiry that cannot state its question cannot be expected to answer it. Funding continued at eleven men because the sum is small and the gap is real.

### Prepared and Not Sent, Deferred, and Declined

A second message to Pataliputra was drafted twice, in 534 and again in 546, and was not sent either time. The 546 draft would have restated the offer and asked whether the first had arrived. It was withheld on this reasoning. The value of the firearm account has fallen since 525, because Rome now holds a working mixture of its own and the account would confirm details rather than set the direction of a programme. The value to Rome of the offer being taken up has meanwhile risen, since Egypt is now assessed as a far greater naval power than was thought and anything that occupies Egyptian ships in the Red Sea and the Gulf is worth more to Rome than it was. Both of those are reasons to want an answer. Against them the *curatores* set one objection and the Senate accepted it: restating an offer to a court rated a partner of limited reliability tells that court the offer matters to Rome, and prices it up in whatever exchange eventually happens. The offer stands as made in 525. Rome will not restate it and has not withdrawn it.

Two letters to Alexandria were prepared and not sent. The first was a protest over the account Egypt gave in about 420 of the loss of five Roman ships off the West African coast, which the survey of the previous turn reduced to a fiction. It was withheld because a protest states what Rome has concluded and gains nothing, and the only thing Rome presently wants from the formal channel opened in the 400s is continued access for its permanent staff. The second would have proposed extending the mutual naval notification arrangement from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic and the western islands. It was withheld because Rome would have had to state where Roman ships are in order to receive statements about Egyptian ships that the court has already reclassified as instruments rather than information.

A second survey down the African Atlantic coast was proposed in 541 and refused. The precedents cited were the two ships lost in the 390s, the three lost about 420, and the fact that the three ships of the survey of the previous turn were shadowed in relay for the whole three thousand miles and were allowed to go home. A fourth expedition would learn less than the third did and would be a further five ships risked on a coast the court has already classified as denied.

The interior conquest of the Yucatan hinterland was deferred, as set out above. The conversion of the two-hundred-ship count into a finding of an armada was declined three times. Florida and the northern seaboard south of the coal river remain charted and unconquered, there being nothing on them worth a legion.

**State of the empire, c. 550 AD.** Population about 122 million in the old territory, with eight hundred thousand to one million one hundred thousand in the west on the recount of 549, of which four hundred and sixty to five hundred and forty thousand are colonists and their children counted off allotment and tax rolls, and three hundred and forty to five hundred and sixty thousand are subject and enslaved islanders counted by district head and counted badly. The colonist share is the larger and is rising. Revenue about 4,700 million HS, of which military and frontier about 3,050 million or sixty-five per cent, administration about 560 million, infrastructure about 420 million, social about 280 million, research about 180 million, and free margin about 210 million. The free margin fell again in absolute terms from about 280 million at 500, at the highest revenue in Roman history, and the western charge and the powder mills are where it went. Industry stands near twenty-seven per cent of revenue and land tax near thirty-four. Technical workforce about 575,000, or 0.47 per cent.

Literacy is reported here as the returns are actually kept, which is by district, because the single empire-wide number is not a measured quantity. The figure of sixty-five to seventy per cent carried for 500 AD, and the sixty-seven per cent that appears against 500 in the summary tables, were both constructions weighted from district returns rather than counts of anything, and the construction is dominated by whichever weighting is chosen for the frontier countryside, which is the largest population with the least reason to read. The returns at 550 stand as follows. In the major industrial cities of the Rhine valley, Britain, and Rome itself, where a man cannot hold a place in a mill or a rail yard without reading a schedule and a safety notice, eighty-eight to ninety-two per cent of working-age adults. In the administrative cities, seventy-eight to eighty-seven. In the settled rural provinces, fifty-eight to sixty-eight. In the frontier countryside, thirty-eight to fifty-four. A construction on the same weighting the treasury used at 500 gives sixty-eight to seventy-two per cent, and the court is advised that the movement of three or four points since 500 is inside the error of the method and should not be reported to anyone as growth. What can be said with more confidence is that no state school was built in this turn either, that the rise where there is one came from the army teaching its recruits, from the fee-charging town schools that printed primers made cheap, and from the powder mills and rail yards adding trades that will not hire an illiterate, and that the frontier countryside moved least because nothing there pays a man to read. Technology: powder in service in one hundred and eighty emplaced and carriage pieces, nine ships mounting pieces at sea, the fixed steam launcher being withdrawn where powder allows, no serviceable hand weapon, compound steam to four hundred horsepower, iron-plated hulls with the screw still not in fleet service, coal gas, rolling mills, balloons, reliable longitude, pneumatic tubes, and no electricity, no chemistry, and no theory of any of it. Territory unchanged in Europe. Reserves remain thin at a few months of revenue.

## MESSAGES OUT

**To the court of the Han, delivered at the Dnieper crossing by the frontier legate.**

From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans to the Emperor of the Han and his ministers.

Rome answers late and states so plainly. The matter of the lines at the river was put to Rome in the last generation and Rome's answer was settled and not sent. The fault is Rome's.

1. Rome accepts the joining of the traffic at the river under the standing agreement, which continues of itself unless one party moves to change it, and Rome does not move to change it.

2. Rome does not accept the joining of the rails themselves. Rome's track is of one width from the western sea to the Dnieper, some three thousand miles of it, and Rome will not relay any part of it to another width. Rome proposes instead a transshipment yard: cars from the east come to the eastern bank and stop, Roman cars stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods cross where wheels do not. Rome states the reason without dressing it, because it is the same reason on both sides. A rail that runs from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army, and neither court would lay the other's track inside its own territory.

3. The yard stands on the Roman bank, built by Rome at Rome's cost, sheds, cranes, and quays, because Rome wants the volume of silk moving rail to rail more than it wants an argument over the expense. It stands under Roman garrison and Roman law. Factors of the Han working in it are protected in it as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory under the standing terms, and the tariffs of the standing agreement are unchanged by this letter.

4. Rome will receive a mechanical clock and escapement of Han making with thanks, for comparison against Roman work.

5. On the making of paper, Rome declines with thanks. Rome has made paper from linen and flax fibre for five hundred years and has printed from cast type for four hundred, and has nothing to gain in it.

6. Rome would value above anything else in this letter any account of how the sphere of the earth was measured and to what figure. Against it Rome offers its own observations of the heavens, which are that the phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter have been confirmed by Roman glasses and that among the men who look through the glass the old order of the heavens is no longer defended, together with the printed argument as it stands. Rome offers with them two things of its own making which it judges the Han would arrive at without Rome in any case: the method of lighting streets and workshops by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains, and the method of carrying written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air. Rome does not offer the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea, and says so in this letter rather than leaving it to be noticed. Rome will not pretend that what it does offer is the equal of what it asks for.

7. Rome was asked four times what it had found to the west and gave four unsatisfactory answers. Rome answers now, and answers narrowly. There is land in the western ocean. It is not a single island but a mainland, whose coast Roman ships have followed for more than a thousand miles without finding an end to it, with an island chain lying off it. It is inhabited. Rome has ships, soldiers, and colonists there and intends to keep them there.

8. Rome gives nothing further. No latitude, no departure point, no passage time, no wind, no soundings, no chart, no survey, and no account of what the land holds or what its peoples possess. Rome states the reason rather than concealing that there is one. In 300 AD Rome handed the Han a full survey of Roman roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines in exchange for a sketch without scale, understood the imbalance as soon as it was made, and has governed every exchange since by the rule it drew from it. A route across an ocean is a survey of exactly that kind, and the sailing directions are the whole of its value. Rome judges an open refusal better than a fifth evasion, and expects the Han to read this article as Rome would read the same article from them.

Rome asks the Han to say whether the yard as described is acceptable, and Rome will begin the works on receipt.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Alexandria network, continued, not new.** Standing since the fourth century, with one branch silent from the previous turn and quarantined. No agent replaced into the silent branch. Tasked as ordered in 525 and unchanged: the size and heading of the shipbuilding in the home yards, and any sign of provisioning for a westward voyage. Cover is the ordinary commercial staff of the formal mission and the merchant houses. Run by the office of the mission at Alexandria under the imperial secretariat.

**2. Dnieper frontier watch, continued, not new.** Tasked as in 525 on the carrying capacity of the eastern rail to the river and the frequency of its arrivals, on the reasoning that a supply line to a frontier is a supply line to an army. Run by the frontier legate's staff. No change.

**3. New operation, the Berber gold traders of the Saharan routes.** Target is the movement of African gold northward. What Rome wants to learn is whether the cargo of the loaded ships counted moving north along the Atlantic coast is gold, and where the interior caravan routes now run and whether their volume has changed in the last two generations. Cover is ordinary gold purchasing, which is what Rome has done through these traders for centuries. Run by the office of the *praefectus* of the African provinces on the treasury's account. The commercial relationship is centuries old; its use for intelligence begins in 548 and the court should expect nothing back for a decade.

No operation against the Maurya. No operation against the Han.

## RESEARCH

**Powder supply.** About 640 men, being the four state mills, the collection contracts, and the deliberate beds. This is plant and supply, not design. Expected: output rising from the 12 to 15 tons of 549 toward 35 to 45 tons a year by 570 as the beds laid from 538 reach full yield and further beds are laid, with the court warned that the bed yield is the least reliable figure in the programme and that a shortfall in it caps everything below.

**Pieces.** About 250 men at the two proving grounds and the drawing offices, with foundry work charged to the naval and frontier accounts. Expected: pieces in service from 180 toward 320 or 350 by 570. Pieces are not linear in powder and should not be projected as though they were. The binding figure is the allowance per piece, presently 40 rounds a year for practice, plus proving, plus the reserve the *curatores* want built up. At 40 rounds, 350 pieces take about 9 tons a year in practice and about 4 in proving, and the remainder of a 35 to 45 ton output goes to stock, which would bring the reserve from the present 22 to 30 tons toward 200 by 570 and would for the first time make a campaign stock possible. Founding capacity is not a constraint at any point in this projection; the ordnance shops could deliver 500 pieces a year on existing plant.

**The hand weapon.** About 60 men, and the split against the two entries above was defended to the Senate in 547 and is argued in the body of this record, together with the objection that it may be the pressure-vessel error a second time. Expected: nothing certain. The three variables of tube, grain, and ignition can each be varied but their effects cannot be attributed, and there is no instrument for measuring what happens inside the tube at the instant of firing. If a serviceable weapon comes it is expected between 570 and 600. A rival's soldiers already carry one, so failure is Roman.

**Barrel metal.** About 120 men, jointly with the naval boiler programme. Object is why boiler proportions overestimate what a piece needs to hold, and how thin a tube may safely be made. Expected within twenty-five years: better proportions by trial, no explanation.

**Screw propulsion and iron hulls.** Continued at previous funding, about 400 men. Expected: the screw fleet-reliable and standard on new construction between 560 and 580. The first all-iron hull was laid down in 548 and is expected in service between 556 and 560, twenty-six to thirty years later than the projection made around 500. The cause of the slip is heavy iron sections, which the mills roll badly and the forges make expensively, and not the plate.

**Western coal and the northern coast.** About 200 men in prospecting parties and survey. Object is the extent of the coal basin above the fourth river and any second basin, and a coaling station at tidewater. Expected: station in service by 560.

**Maize and the western staples.** Agricultural *curatores*, seed distribution and printed method. Expected: northern strains in two further generations, which is 600 at the earliest. No quantity of engineers shortens it.

**The speed at Alexandria.** Eleven men, continued, retitled from "the force in metal" because that name stated a Maurya proposition rather than a Roman question. Object is why Rome's pneumatic tubes do not achieve the speed observed at Alexandria in the fifth century. Expected: nothing.

## TERRITORY

**Held.** Unchanged in the old world: Ireland and Britain to the Dnieper, the north of Scotland and the accessible Scandinavian coasts to the old North African provinces. In the west: Hispaniola and Cuba entire with garrison forts and veteran allotments, the lesser islands of the chain taken or submitted, the fortified station on the Yucatan coast, and the river stations on the northern seaboard including the coal river with its fourteen miles of tramway to tidewater. Fourteen powder pieces in western batteries.

**Surveyed and not held.** The Yucatan interior to about sixty Roman miles, by probe and balloon. The mainland coast north and south of the Yucatan station for more than a thousand miles. Florida and the northern seaboard, charted, poor in gold, thinly peopled. The great bay and its rivers. The African Atlantic coast to the southern corner, by sighting from one to two miles offshore in the previous turn, not revisited and not to be revisited. The Faroes, Iceland, and the Atlantic islands.
