## 525 to 550 AD: Powder, the Yucatan Centres, and the Fleet Built for the Western Run

### The Silence from Pataliputra

The single message of the previous turn went east to the Maurya court, offering the complete working method of the marine steam engine, boiler through transmission, seals and their failure points, fuel figures, and Roman engineers to build the first beside Indian engineers, the whole delivered in one exchange against the whole of a maker's-standard account of the Chinese hand-firearm delivered in return. The instalment scheme was discarded before the message went, on the ground that instalments cannot be worked at a distance where one message crosses in a generation. Twenty-five years passed. Nothing was received. No reply came. No Roman engineer went to any Indian yard. The offer of 525 stands unaltered.

Four readings of the silence were argued and all four remain in the file.

The first is that the message never arrived. The Roman record for the fifth century carries persistent banditry on the Mesopotamian leg of the Euphrates route, reported by Roman merchants and denied by the Maurya at the time, and a single sealed packet on a route that loses cargo is a packet that can be lost. Against it stands twenty-five years of Roman merchant traffic moving both ways with no return message of any kind on any of it, which one theft does not explain.

The second is that the message arrived and the account is being assembled. A maker's-standard account means drawings with dimensions, the metal and its working, the charge and its proportions, and the failures. Rome knows what that costs, the standardised-parts catalogue of its own military steam programme having taken from about 390 to 460 to reach its present form. A court gathering such an account from its own engineers and from survivors of an engagement it lost might take a decade. It would not take twenty-five years, and a court taking a decade could have said so in one line inside the first two.

The third is that the offer was declined and the decline not stated. The fourth is that the interval was itself the object, an open Roman offer of the hull engine standing unanswered committing Rome to nothing it can withdraw from and leaving Rome waiting. The court's disposition toward the Maurya, recorded since about 490 as a commercial partner of value and a strategic partner of limited reliability, weighs the third and fourth above the first and second. That disposition rests on the Syr Darya cannon held back while Rome was pressed four times for what it knew of Chinese firearms, on route reports denying robberies Roman merchants were suffering, on the tariff raised from twelve to twenty-four per cent without consultation, and on a claim of independently discovering a force running in metal that arrived soon after contact at Alexandria.

The operational consequence was settled in 528. The powder programme was placed on the footing of receiving nothing, on the reasoning that a programme which had run two hundred and twenty-five years without a weapon could not stand a further twenty-five waiting on a packet whose arrival could not be dated. Everything below follows from that decision.

### The Working Mixture and the Salt

The search for a stable ratio, reckoned in 525 to stand about six years off, closed in 531. Three materials go into it. Charcoal and the yellow stone of the Sicilian and Campanian workings were long familiar and cost nothing to obtain. The third had to be found, and nothing in Roman practice pointed at it.

After the reorientation of about 485 the programme's standing instruction was to test any substance reported to make a fire burn fiercer, and the instrument used to gather them was print. A circular went out through the provincial offices in 487 and was reprinted twice, asking for reports of such substances from anyone who worked with fire. Several hundred came back over the following decades and almost all were worthless, being resins, pitches, oils, and the ordinary mineral salts. One from a tanning district in the 490s described a white bloom that gathers on the walls of old cattle byres and reported that a handful thrown on a charcoal bed made the bed flare. That report is why anybody scraped a byre wall. The collector was paid the standard bounty and is not recorded further.

Against the bloom stood the obvious candidate, the *nitrum* of ordinary commerce, the mineral salt bought in bulk through Alexandria for glass and for washing, and the programme spent eleven years on it. Batches made with trade *nitrum* burned and did not drive. Batches made from wall bloom and from the earth under old byres drove hard. The two are alike enough in appearance to be confused in a store, and the men who handle both report that they do not taste alike, so the programme can tell them apart. What it cannot do is say why one drives and the other does not, or predict from anything observable which of two unfamiliar salts will behave which way. The working rule is by source rather than by property: the salt is what comes off ground or walls that have stood under animals, and nothing bought as *nitrum* is substituted for it at any price.

Getting from a scraping to a usable salt took five years. The scrapings arrive as earth and rubbish and are about a fortieth part salt by weight. They are steeped in water in vats, the liquor drawn off and strained, boiled down in iron pans, and left to cool, and the crystals that come out are washed and boiled a second time. The first salts to form on cooling were found by trial in 528 to be useless and are thrown away, which cost two years of poor batches before anyone separated what formed early from what formed late. Wood ash entered at the straining step by accident, a contractor in the British district having strained his liquor through a bed of wood ash to clear it of dirt, after which the salt drove measurably harder than his previous deliveries. Ash in the leach was adopted on that trial alone. Ash was then also put into the deliberate beds, on the reasoning that a thing which improves the salt in the vat may improve it in the wall, and no trial supports that step; beds laid with ash and beds laid without have not been compared.

### The Powder Establishment and What the Arithmetic Armed

The supply chain set the size of the armament, so it was built as a standing industry rather than as a programme. At 550 there are fourteen state powder mills, three on the Rhine at Colonia Agrippina, Mogontiacum, and Argentoratum, three in the British coal district, two in Dacia, two in Campania, two on the Danube, one in Baetica, and one in Noricum, sited for water power to drive the stamps, charcoal within a short haul, and rail or river to a frontier. The mills and their attached works employ 1,150 men, which is a small establishment for fourteen works and is set by the tonnage rather than by the buildings, 214 tons across fourteen mills being fifteen tons apiece in a year.

Collection is let by contract through the provincial procurators and pays by delivered weight. Finished powder is three parts in four the salt, so 214 tons wants 160 tons of salt, and scrapings run about a fortieth part salt, so the contractors must deliver some 6,400 tons of earth and rubbish a year. That is carried by 2,600 contractors and labourers at about two and a half tons a man, the work being intermittent and fitted around other trades rather than a full year's labour. Collection began with the stable floors of the army's own remount depots, which the army owns and can order scraped on a schedule, and extends to any byre, cellar, or cave earth a contractor can reach. There are 4,200 deliberate beds. Beds laid in 538 came into first yield in 543 and are not yet at full yield.

Output in 549 was 214 tons of finished powder. Output before 543 was a small fraction of that. Cumulative production from 531 to 550 was 1,640 tons, of which 760 was consumed by proving, by the destruction trials of 540, by the grain trials, by the hand-weapon trials, and by the practice allowance, leaving 880 tons in magazine at 550.

The service charge is two Roman pounds, so a ton of finished powder yields 1,520 charges, and 214 tons is 325,000 charges a year while the magazine holds 1,337,000. The practice allowance fixed by the *curatores* is forty rounds a piece a year. There are 1,544 pieces in service, so practice takes 41 tons a year and proving 22, and the remaining 151 tons goes to magazine. Put the whole armament into continuous action at eighty rounds a piece a day and it burns 81 tons a day, so the magazine carries the armament for eleven days and the annual output replaces less than three.

That figure governs. Powder artillery now goes with the field forces, 212 pieces being on carriages with the frontier armies rather than emplaced, and the eleven-day magazine is why no Roman commander has been authorised to plan a campaign around it. What powder does not do is put a weapon in an infantryman's hands, and the reason there is not supply. Founding capacity was never the limit either: the ordnance shops of the Rhine and British districts are the same shops that bore engine cylinders and roll hull plate, and they can deliver seven hundred pieces a year on existing plant, which is more than the powder will serve.

The 1,544 pieces throw a solid iron shot of five Roman pounds in the standard pattern and six in the heavier siege pattern, and are disposed as 980 at European frontier and river positions, 212 with the field forces in Europe, 172 mounted afloat at two to a gun-armed hull, 96 in the western masonry batteries, and 84 with the Yucatan columns, being 24 to each of the three columns and 12 at the coastal depot. They replaced the fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers at 118 of the 140 launcher positions, and the 22 launchers remaining stand where a prepared defence is the only use contemplated.

### The Branch That Turned

The premise that broke in 485 was that the Han weapon must store pressure, held from about 300 for a hundred and eighty years, and it was a correct inference from a false premise rather than carelessness. Rome had mastered pressure, Rome's own launchers accelerated a projectile with expanding gas, and the flying sphere reported in the incident of the 290s looked like a launch from a better pressure engine than Rome had. What broke it was one fact from the Chinese warning of the 480s: a single soldier carries the weapon and fires it on the instant. Rome knows from two centuries with boilers that stored pressure requires a heavy vessel and time to raise it, and that its own launchers must be pre-heated. A weapon a man carries and fires at once has nowhere to keep pressure and no moment in which to raise it, yet plainly looses great force with flash and smoke at its mouth. Therefore the force is made at the instant of ignition by something that burns and expands violently. That step is settled and 531 confirmed it, because a mixture Rome lit did exactly that.

The step that followed was not drawn until 532. If the force is made in the instant, the vessel is required to hold only for the instant. Rome's boilers are heavy because they hold for hours against a pressure continuously renewed, and Roman practice had never had cause to distinguish the weight a vessel needs from the duration it must last. Therefore the weight is set by duration as much as by force, and a vessel that holds for an instant can be far lighter than boiler practice would size it.

That was not acted on for eight years. The first weaponised pieces were sized by boiler proportions, wall thickness carried in ratio to the bore as the naval boiler programme had established it, and came out three to four times heavier than the pieces now in service. The correction came in 540, when the programme fired deliberately thinned tubes to destruction to find where the proportion actually failed, and found that tubes at a third of the boiler wall survived charges the boiler-proportioned pieces were built for. Rome cannot say why boiler proportion overestimates the requirement so far. One line of argument in the programme holds that a pressure applied in an instant is borne differently by the metal than a pressure sustained, and that metal has some capacity to take a blow it does not have to take a load, and that line is the majority view among the programme's engineers. It stands in the file as the programme's explanation and not as a finding, nothing having been measured that distinguishes it from any other explanation.

Two further results stand as reliable in their core and unexplained. Lengthening the tube increased the force delivered up to a ratio of about eighteen to twenty times the bore and gave nothing measurable beyond it, and there is no account of why a limit should exist. Working the finished powder to a coarser and more even grain rather than a fine dust increased the force and reduced the number of pieces lost in firing, found in 536 when a badly sieved batch outperformed a good one and standardised without being understood.

The hand weapon followed from the light vessel by argument and has not followed in practice. Trials of a tube a man could carry and fire ran from 546. At a hundred paces the results were worse than a crossbow in accuracy, the loading slower, and the ignition unreliable in wet weather. Whether the fault lies in the tube, in the grain, or in the ignition is unresolved. The programme can vary one thing against a fixed rest, having done it in 536 with two grain batches against one piece and in 540 with thinned tubes at fixed charge and bore, and both gave clean answers. The difficulty is that these three do not stay separate. A coarser grain alters the force and also how readily the charge takes fire, so a failure to fire and a failure to drive cannot be assigned to different causes; a narrower tube alters the force and also the fouling that spoils the next shot; a change of ignition alters where in the charge the burning starts, which alters the force again. The programme observes three effects from one change and cannot attribute them, and it has no means of measuring pressure inside a tube at the instant of firing, only of seeing whether the tube burst and how far the ball went. Soldiers of another power plainly carry such weapons and fire them, so the thing can be done and the fault is Roman.

### The Fleet

The steam fleet stood at twenty to thirty operational hulls at 500 and stands at seventy-eight at 550, of which thirty-one are iron-plated and gun-armed and forty-seven are wooden, twelve of the wooden being purpose-built gun vessels framed for recoil from the keel up. The building followed the reassessment of the previous turn, which established that Egyptian hulls are beyond Roman counting, and the *magister classis* was given a standing authority in 531 to lay down six warship hulls a year and took it up in every year but two.

Gun mounting was first tried in 542 and 543 by retrofitting existing wooden hulls framed for a ram and a ballista, and the trials worked their frames loose. That is a finding about retrofits. Rome's shipwrights can frame a wooden vessel for the recoil of a five-pound shot, and from 544 twelve were built that way for the shallow-water work where a plated hull draws too much. New construction is otherwise plated, iron plate having been standard since the 480s against the shallow-draft swarming attack described to Rome in about 420.

The all-iron hull slipped past the projection made around 500, which was twenty-five to thirty years from then. Plate was never the difficulty, the rolling mills having produced consistent sheet since the 480s. Iron framing in heavy sections is, because the mills roll flat plate well and heavy angle and bar badly, so sections have been forged at high cost and inconsistent quality. Behind that stood yard capacity, taken up by plating existing hulls, by the western transports, and by the screw programme, which the *magister classis* has held at higher priority since Egypt's quiet vessels were observed in the fifth century. Four all-iron hulls were laid down from 548 and the first is expected in service between 556 and 560.

Nine warships are in western waters, rigged for sail with engines banked, since no steam hull crosses three thousand nautical miles on a bunker good for six or seven hundred.

### The Western Transports and the Migration They Carried

The commitment of the previous turn was made on three facts, that the land was real and rich, that the veterans owed land had somewhere to go, and that Egypt could not be assumed idle. In its first decade it was carried on hulls taken off the Iberian and African grain and oil trades, which had to be re-let at worse rates, and the *curatores* of the treasury reported in 533 that the largest strategic undertaking in four centuries was being run on borrowed tonnage. From 534 the yards were ordered to build for it. One hundred and sixty-four ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons were laid down in the Atlantic yards of Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, and the Severn and Clyde, at 1.4 million HS a hull for 230 million across the period, charged to the fleet account. The tonnage taken from the grain trades was returned by 541 and exceeded thereafter.

Hulls dedicated to the run stood at 130 through 533 and rose to 268 by 550 as the new transports came off the ways, which is 4,354 hull-years across the period and an average of 174 hulls on station. A hull makes one and a half round voyages a year, the outward run from the Canaries taking six weeks on the trades and the return running the Gulf Stream north to the westerlies, about four months at sea in all. That gives 6,530 voyages. Half carried stores, troops, timber, plant, and returning cargo and no people at all. The other 3,265 averaged 352 emigrants, a six-hundred-ton transport carrying that number with their gear and provisions without difficulty, and landed 1,151,000 people, or 46,000 a year.

The allotment registers and the tax rolls of the western provinces carried 1,742,000 colonists and their children at 550, the balance above this turn's landings being the cohort landed in the previous turn together with children born in the west, less ordinary mortality. The crossing and the island fevers were assessed in the previous turn as not severe and nothing since has changed that.

The demand this serves is worth stating in figures, because it is the reason the commitment was made. The military establishment is 640,000 men on twenty-year terms, which discharges about 32,000 men a year, each owed land and most with families, so the settlement demand runs near 100,000 persons a year. The west absorbed 46,000 a year across this turn, forty-six per cent of it, against something under four per cent in the previous turn. The remainder still goes onto marginal ground in the north and east.

### The Conquest of the Yucatan Centres

The station on the Yucatan coast was established in the previous turn to measure the interior before anything was committed to it, on the stated rule that a fractured landscape of city-states would be taken one at a time and a larger or unified power would be known about before Rome moved. The measuring was finished in 540 and the rule was applied from 541.

Interpreters were built rather than waited for. The island administration had run Arawak interpreters since the 510s and applied the same method here: from 528 the coastal station took Maya-speaking traders into paid service, put Roman boys of the garrison families with them, and printed a word list that went through four revised editions. The merchants of the lagoon town behind the station were the readiest source, several of them already speaking an island tongue from their own canoe trade. Forty competent interpreters existed by 536 and a hundred and ninety by 550.

Reconnaissance ran from 528 to 540. Observation balloons were flown from cleared ground and from vessels off both coasts, mounted columns went inland on cut tracks, and interpreters bought information in the markets. Three hundred and forty Roman miles were surveyed inland across the peninsula and into the hill country beyond it, against the sixty of the previous turn. Forty-four independent centres were counted, no authority was found standing over them, several were found at war with one another, and none fielded a metal weapon.

The sequence was as follows.

The lagoon town went first, in 541, and it went by sea. Six thousand troops were landed from the coastal station in the ships of the western squadron, with two of the shallow-draft wooden gun vessels working inside the lagoon, and the town was taken in nine days for 180 Roman dead. It was not sacked and it was not garrisoned as a conquest. Its merchant houses were left standing under a treaty as Rome's first western client, its trade rights confirmed, and its obligation fixed at pilotage, porterage, market information, and interpreters. Rome wanted the entrepôt working and wanted the roads inland described by men who used them, and a burned town describes nothing.

From the lagoon the columns went inland. Forty thousand further troops came out across 541 and 542, drawn from the Danube and African provinces and not from the island garrisons, so the whole commitment was 46,000, which is seven per cent of the establishment. Three columns worked in sequence rather than together, each of 13,000 with a train of light carriage pieces, with 1,000 at the coastal depot, because a fractured country taken one centre at a time does not need three simultaneous armies and does need roads behind each.

Izamal and the northwestern plain centres fell across 542 and 543. Four were stormed. Rome's method was the ordinary one and needed no innovation: the columns approached on cut road, the pieces reduced a gate or a wall section at two hundred paces, and the shield wall went in. Stone tools and cotton armour against steel, mail, and shot is not a contest, and the four assaults together cost 1,240 Roman dead against losses on the other side that the *legatus* returned as beyond counting and did not attempt to count.

After the fourth storming the pattern changed. Fourteen centres sent to the columns and submitted without fighting, on terms Rome had prepared in advance and printed: tribute assessed in goods and labour, a Roman post inside the wall, no restriction on the ruling houses, and no interference with the temples. Rome does not care what is worshipped in a tributary and said so, which cost Rome nothing and shortened the campaign by years.

Coba on the eastern plain was the hardest and took most of 546, being the largest counted centre, sited among lakes, and connected to its dependencies by raised stone causeways of its own building. The causeways were the reason it fell. They carried Roman columns and Roman guns as readily as they had carried its own traffic, and once two were held the centre could be approached from three directions at once. It cost 810 Roman dead.

Nineteen centres were taken by assault in all, fourteen are held as tributaries, one stands as a client, and eleven counted centres in the hill country have not been reached. Two hundred and sixty Roman miles of military road were cut, four inland forts and eleven fortified posts raised, and the garrison stands at 22,000. Roman dead across the nine years were 3,100 to the fighting and 5,800 to fever, the fever being the heavier charge by a wide margin, as it was in the Delta before 90 BC.

The tribute is gold, cacao, cotton, dye, salt from the northern coast pans, and labour, and it yielded 34 million HS in 549. It is already falling. The tribute rolls of the districts taken first fell by about a third within six years of being counted, in the same manner as the islands, and the same three explanations are argued in the printed journals with the same want of any theory behind them.

### The Northern Seaboard, Worked Up the Rivers

The northern coast was reassessed upward in the previous turn for fuel, and this turn made it the second centre of the western position. It is now settled ground and not a visited one, and it is being worked the way Rome works a river, which is to hold the mouth, survey to the head of navigation, put a station at the head, and lay allotments behind it.

The coal above the fourth navigable opening south of the great bay was proved along nine miles of face and burned under a standing pump engine at within a tenth of the poorer British grades, which serves a bunker and an engine and is not good coal. A tramway of fourteen miles ran from the pit to the head of navigation from 549, laid in local timber faced with iron strip because rolled rail shipped three thousand nautical miles costs more per mile than the tramway earns. A coaling station at tidewater holds 4,000 tons, which is what makes a steam squadron in western waters something other than nine poor sailing ships once its bunkers are out.

A shipyard was laid out at the river mouth in 546 on the ship's carpenter's note from the second voyage, entered into the formal report of the previous turn, that the oak and pine of the northern coast are superior shipbuilding timber. It launched two six-hundred-ton transports in 549 and is building four ways. Naval stores went with it, pitch and tar from the pine and spars cut and floated down.

Four river districts are settled, each on the same pattern: the great bay's western rivers taken as the first, second, and third openings and the coal river as the fourth. Survey parties worked each to the head of navigation and then on foot beyond it, forty to seventy Roman miles inland to a line of falls where the rivers cease to carry a boat, and beyond the falls to a long forested mountain wall running north and south, which the surveys reached at between a hundred and forty and two hundred miles inland and have crossed at three passes without following the ground beyond them. Coal indications were reported at two further points along the fall line and one is being proved.

Settlement is by allotment rather than by conquest, the country being thinly peopled as the second voyage reported. Veteran allotments were laid out from 544 and carried 214,000 colonists at 550 across the four districts, with grain, timber, pitch, and the pit as their trades. Fighting with the river peoples has been local and intermittent, has cost 640 Roman dead across the period, and has been settled district by district by treaties of the kind used with the lagoon town rather than by campaign, there being no centre to storm.

### The Islander Mortality

The tribute roll of Hispaniola stood at 148,000 in 550 against a first count by district heads of three to five hundred thousand, and the true population is estimated between 120,000 and 200,000. Cuba's roll stood at 206,000 with the population estimated between 180,000 and 280,000, and the lesser islands together at 61,000 on the roll. The first counts are unreliable, so the ratio carries more than the absolutes, and the ratio is a fall of half or worse in twenty-five years.

The cause is unresolved. Rome's physicians argue three explanations in the printed medical journals: the labour in the gold creeks, which does kill and cannot account for deaths among those not set to it; the change of diet under Roman requisition; and the air of newly worked ground, on the analogy of the Delta marsh fevers. A minority holds that the sickness came off the ships with the colonists, on the observation that villages nearest the landings emptied first, and from 544 new colonists have been landed at a distance from tributary villages on that argument. No Roman physician has any theory of what is being carried or how.

The economic effect is what the accounts register. Two populations move in opposite directions, one added to by ship every season and by birth every year, the other falling, and the same fall has now begun in the Yucatan districts. Rome planned the west as an extraction economy worked by subject labour where there is no coal, and the labour is going. What replaces it is colonist labour, which must be paid, and machinery, which must be fuelled, and the fuel is on the northern coast and not on the gold islands. The gold creeks of Hispaniola are also thinning at the first workings. This is why the northern coast now carries the weight of the western programme in the *curatores*' papers while the islands and the Yucatan carry the revenue.

### The Western Accounts

The fiscal instrument was argued in 533 and settled in 535. The *curatores* of the treasury extended the existing luxury import duty to western cargo at the ports of landing, chiefly Gades and Olisipo with Ostia for re-export, at twelve per cent. The land tax was not raised, being the largest line in the accounts and falling on the veteran colonies that are the recruiting base of the northern army. Debasement was proposed by a minority and rejected on the standing objection that the mint is the instrument of last resort in a war and is worth less each time it is used. The Imperial Commercial and Industrial Council opposed the duty on raw cotton and hides on the ground that the Rhine and British mills consume the cotton and that a duty on a raw material taxes Roman manufacture rather than western luxury, and that objection was accepted in the manner of the standardisation compromise of 480: twelve per cent on cacao, tobacco, dyestuffs, and worked gold, four per cent on raw cotton and hides. The duty yielded 96 million in 549. Advances from the shipping companies against the following season's landings carried the building programme's early years, which extends a practice Rome has used since it took advances from tax-farmers and large suppliers and is not borrowing in a form the treasury will admit to, there being no bond market to borrow in.

The western charge in 549 was 288 million against western revenue of 240 million, being 110 million of island gold, 96 million of duty, and 34 million of Yucatan tribute. The fiscal conservatives moved in 536 to cap the charge and were beaten. The office that gained standing across both defeats is the *praefectus* of the western fleets, whose command now disposes of more tonnage than any other single naval command in the empire.

### Egypt

The southern survey of the previous turn produced the largest single military reassessment in the record, and two items stand unresolved in the file: the shipbuilding surge in the Egyptian home yards, and a northbound coastal column of large loaded ships counted past two hundred before the counting stopped. Nothing Roman observation produced in twenty-five years bore on either, and both remain unresolved. The pieces fit a race to the Americas and they fit equally an Atlantic gold artery running metal north with its return leg offshore, and Roman ships were never seen going south.

What was done under that uncertainty shows in the accounts. The warship building authority of 531 is the largest single response. Masonry batteries were laid on Hispaniola, Cuba, the Yucatan coast, and the northern river mouths from 546 and hold ninety-six pieces, laid against ships and not against islanders. A standing watch was established on the Canaries and on Madeira with balloon and telescope, counting hulls passing north and west, the crossing latitude being the one place where a Roman observer sits astride whatever is happening. The Alexandria network was kept standing with its silent branch quarantined and untouched.

### The Junction at the River

The matter of joining the rail lines at the Dnieper was answered in this turn on the terms the Senate settled in 525, and the letter is below. It gives a transshipment yard on the Roman bank rather than a joining of the lines, because Rome's single gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and will not be relaid, and because a rail running from one empire onto another's ground is a road for an army. Rome builds the sheds, cranes, and quays at its own cost, wanting the volume of direct rail-to-rail silk more than the argument about who pays.

The letter answers the question Rome was asked four times in the previous century, and answers it narrowly: a mainland exists in the western ocean, its coast has been followed for more than a thousand miles, it is inhabited, and Rome holds ships, soldiers, and colonists on it. It gives no latitude, no departure point, no passage time, no wind, no soundings, no chart, and no account of what the land holds or what its peoples possess. A route across an ocean is a survey of the same kind as the map Rome handed over at the Dnieper in 300 AD, which was a complete Roman military survey of roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines exchanged for a sketch without scale, and the sailing directions are the whole of its value.

The letter also withholds the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea, which is the mechanical clock keeping reference time worked with stellar sights through the glass, and says so plainly rather than leaving it to be noticed. The argument for offering it was Rome's own criterion for disclosure to the Han since the agreements of the late fourth century, that Rome shares what the Han would develop without Rome in any case, on which test Rome has already shared its compass calibration, its type-casting, and its double-wall glass furnace. A court that has measured the sphere of the earth and makes escapements holds both halves of the method and wants only to be told they go together. The *magister classis* and the *curatores* of the naval programme carried the refusal, and the Senate accepted it on two grounds. The first is a difference in kind: a chart says where Rome has been, while a method of fixing position lets a rival cross any ocean and return, repeatedly, without ever being shown a Roman route, which makes refusing charts while handing over the method incoherent. The second is Rome's own record of what the method did. The crossing of the 490s succeeded and came home because the ship carried a compass, a glass, and a good clock and wrote its position down every night for four months. Before that instrument existed Rome worked south down the African coast laying coal depots season by season and lost five ships doing it. The whole of Rome's presence in the west rests on that one technique. The natural-philosophy patrons argued for disclosure and lost, and said so in print.

### The Rails, the Channel, and the Ordinary Charge

The frontier charge held at sixty-six per cent of revenue, up from sixty-three at 500, the rise being the fourteen powder mills, the warship building authority, the western transports, and the Yucatan campaign and garrison. The rest bought the following.

Three thousand four hundred miles of new line were laid and 1,900 doubled, at 1.3 million HS a mile on easy ground. The Dacia to Dnieper corridor was doubled throughout and a third track laid on its western half. The second Alpine crossing, costed at 2.9 million a mile, was begun in 542 and opened in 549 at 214 million.

Six purpose-built steam ferries of eight hundred tons, each taking eighteen loaded cars, were put on the Gesoriacum to Dubris crossing of thirty Roman miles between 537 and 544, with rail-height quays at both ends. A legion with its stores crosses in a day and a half against eight to twelve days before. The ferries reinforce a loyal Britain and do nothing against a Britain already gone, a Britain in revolt holding the far quay, so Britain remains the permanent breakaway risk and its fiscal autonomy under the settlement of 362 remains the instrument that manages it.

Maize is established across the African provinces, Sicily, Baetica, and southern Italy at yields the agricultural *curatores* return as half again to twice wheat where the season is long enough, sown on 940,000 iugera in 549. Northern adaptation is not achieved and is expected to take two further generations of selection. The staples are not traded to any peer. The pneumatic tube network was extended to eleven more administrative centres. The chimney and setback legislation of about 500 was enforced and the relocation fund spent, and the printed gazettes were correct that the rules moved the smoke without reducing the coal burned.

### The Labour Settlement of 540

The fourth-century labour law was framed on productivity and had grown inadequate against a workforce that reads, organises by pamphlet, and has had a century of Christian argument about human dignity circulating in it. The settlement of 540 raised the injury compensation schedule, fixed a shorter maximum shift in the deep workings, extended collegium recognition to the fourteen powder mills and the rail yards, and required mine operators to certify shaft timbering to a printed standard. It was argued to the Senate and passed on output and on the risk of a disaffected industrial workforce in a region that can be separated, which is how the risings of 338 to 358 are recorded and how this law is recorded. The Council and the British and Rhine mine operators opposed the certification requirement and lost. The provincial governors lost discretion, a recognised collegium bringing a dispute to a court rather than to the governor, and several said so in print.

### The Establishment Dispute

The powder establishment at 550 is 11,400 men in the mills, 26,000 contractors and labourers in collection, 1,900 men at four proving grounds and the drawing offices, and 420 men on the hand weapon. The design comparison is 1,900 against 420, and the Senate was asked to defend it in 547.

Three arguments were given. The hand weapon cannot absorb more men while its three variables cannot be separated and there is no instrument for measuring what happens inside the tube, so added men produce added inconsistency. The emplaced and carriage pieces defend the Dnieper line, the river crossings, and the fortified positions, which is the first item in the court's order of concerns and has been in every century on record. And the *praefecti* of the frontier districts asked for pieces and did not ask for tubes, and the Senate funds what its officers ask for.

The standing objection against all three is in print and is not weak. Rome spent from about 300 to 485 on pressure vessels because pressure was what Rome had mastered, and it is now spending four to one on the emplaced piece because the emplaced piece is what Rome has mastered. If the two errors have the same shape, the second will take a century to become visible in the same way the first did.

### The Alexandria Speed Enquiry

Eleven men have worked since 534 on why Rome's pneumatic message tubes do not achieve the speed Roman agents reported for the Egyptian offices at Alexandria across the fifth century. The enquiry was not founded on the Maurya claim about a force running in metal, which Rome declined in the previous century. It was founded because Rome built the pneumatic system as the explanation it could build for itself and the explanation does not account for the observed speed. Sixteen years have produced no result. The enquiry cannot specify what it is looking for, and it continues at eleven men because the sum is small and the gap is real.

The free margin fell from 280 million to 135 million at the highest revenue in Roman history. The warship authority, the western transports, the fourteen powder mills, and the Yucatan campaign are where it went. Reserves stand at about three months of revenue.

Industry is 29.5 per cent of revenue and land tax 29.6, the two lines crossing within the next generation on present trend. Technical workforce 610,000, or 0.50 per cent of the old-territory population. Literacy 76 per cent empire-wide, against 67 at 500 and 48 at 400: the industrial cities of the Rhine valley, Britain, and Rome itself return 93 per cent of working-age adults, the administrative cities 88, the settled rural provinces 70, and the frontier countryside 55, which is where the remaining gain lies. No state school was built. The rise came from the army teaching its recruits, from fee-charging town schools made cheap by printed primers, from the powder mills and rail yards adding trades that will not hire an illiterate, and from the Church after 450.

Technology: powder in 1,460 pieces, of which 212 on carriages with the field forces and 172 afloat; no serviceable hand weapon; seventy-eight steam warships, thirty-one iron-plated, four all-iron hulls laid down; compound steam to 400 horsepower; coal gas, rolling mills, balloons, reliable longitude, pneumatic tubes, western coal at tidewater and a western shipyard. No electricity, no chemistry, no theory of any of it.

## MESSAGES OUT

**To the court of the Han, delivered at the Dnieper crossing by the frontier legate.**

From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans to the Emperor of the Han and his ministers.

Rome answers late and states so plainly. The matter of the lines at the river was put to Rome in the last generation and Rome's answer was settled then and is only now sent. The fault is Rome's.

1. Rome accepts the joining of the traffic at the river under the standing agreement, which continues of itself unless one party moves to change it, and Rome does not move to change it.

2. Rome does not accept the joining of the rails. Rome's track is of one width from the western sea to the Dnieper, some three thousand miles of it, and Rome will not relay any part of it. Rome proposes a transshipment yard instead: cars from the east come to the eastern bank and stop, Roman cars stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods cross where wheels do not. The reason is the same on both sides. A rail that runs from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army, and neither court would lay the other's track inside its own territory.

3. The yard stands on the Roman bank, built by Rome at Rome's cost, sheds, cranes, and quays, under Roman garrison and Roman law. Factors of the Han working in it are protected as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory under the standing terms. The tariffs of the standing agreement are unchanged by this letter. Rome will begin the works on receipt of word that the yard as described is acceptable.

4. Rome holds the measurement of the sphere of the earth given to it in the last generation and has set it against its own work. Rome returns for it what it has: the phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter, confirmed by Roman glasses, and the printed argument as it now stands among Roman natural philosophers, which is that the old order of the heavens is no longer defended by the men who look through the glass. The observations and the argument are sent with this letter.

5. Rome sends with them two methods of its own making: the lighting of streets and workshops by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains, and the carrying of written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air. Rome will not pretend these are the equal of what it has received.

6. Rome will receive a mechanical clock and escapement of Han making with thanks, for comparison against Roman work.

7. On the making of paper Rome declines with thanks, having made paper from linen and flax fibre for five hundred years and printed from cast type for four hundred.

8. Rome was asked four times what it had found to the west and gave four unsatisfactory answers. Rome answers now, and answers narrowly. There is land in the western ocean. It is a mainland, not an island, whose coast Roman ships have followed more than a thousand miles without finding an end to it, with an island chain lying off it. It is inhabited. Rome has ships, soldiers, and colonists there and intends to keep them there.

9. Rome gives nothing further on it. No latitude, no departure point, no passage time, no wind, no soundings, no chart, no survey, and no account of what the land holds or what its peoples possess. Rome also withholds the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea, and states that it is withholding it rather than leaving it to be noticed. The reason for both is one reason. In 300 AD Rome handed the Han a full survey of Roman roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines in exchange for a sketch without scale, and has governed every exchange since by the rule it drew from that. A route across an ocean is a survey of exactly that kind and the sailing directions are the whole of its value; a method of fixing position at sea is more than a survey, because it opens every ocean and not one route. Rome expects the Han to read these two articles as Rome would read the same articles from them.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Alexandria network, continued.** Standing since the fourth century, one branch silent since the previous turn and quarantined, no agent walked into it. Tasked as in 525: the size and heading of the shipbuilding in the home yards, and any sign of provisioning for a westward voyage. Cover is the ordinary commercial staff of the formal mission and the merchant houses. Run by the office of the mission at Alexandria under the imperial secretariat.

**2. Dnieper frontier watch, continued.** Tasked on the carrying capacity of the eastern rail to the river and the frequency of its arrivals, a supply line to a frontier being a supply line to an army. Run by the frontier legate's staff.

**3. The Berber gold traders of the Saharan routes, new from 548.** Target is the movement of African gold northward. Rome wants to learn whether the cargo of the loaded ships counted moving north along the Atlantic coast is gold, and where the interior caravan routes now run and whether their volume has changed in two generations. Cover is ordinary gold purchasing, which is what Rome has done through these traders for centuries. Run by the office of the *praefectus* of the African provinces on the treasury's account. The commercial relationship is centuries old and its use for intelligence begins now, so nothing is looked for inside a decade.

## RESEARCH

**Powder supply.** 11,400 men in fourteen mills, 26,000 contractors in collection, 4,200 deliberate beds. Object is tonnage. Output rises from 214 tons in 549 toward 600 by 570 as the beds laid from 538 reach full yield and 6,000 more are laid. Bed yield is the governing figure and a shortfall in it caps everything below.

**Pieces.** 1,900 men at four proving grounds and the drawing offices, foundry work charged to the naval and frontier accounts. Pieces in service from 1,460 toward 3,200 by 570. Pieces are not linear in powder: at the standing allowance of forty practice rounds a year, 3,200 pieces take 84 tons a year in practice and 40 in proving, and the remainder of a 600-ton output goes to magazine, which would carry the armament for a season rather than eleven days and would let a campaign be planned around it for the first time. Founding capacity is 700 pieces a year and is not the constraint.

**The hand weapon.** 420 men. The three variables of tube, grain, and ignition can each be varied but their effects cannot be attributed, and there is no instrument for measuring what happens inside the tube at the instant of firing. Building such an instrument is the establishment's first object. A serviceable weapon is expected between 570 and 600.

**Barrel metal.** 340 men, jointly with the naval boiler programme. Object is why boiler proportions overestimate what a piece must hold and how thin a tube may safely be made. Expected within twenty-five years: better proportions by trial.

**Screw propulsion and iron hulls.** 900 men. The screw fleet-reliable and standard on new construction between 560 and 580. Heavy rolled iron sections for framing are the gate on the all-iron hull and are the mills' first object; the first of the four hulls laid down from 548 is expected in service 556 to 560.

**Western coal, timber, and the northern yard.** 1,100 men in prospecting parties, survey, the pit, the tramway, and the yard at the river mouth. Object is the extent of the coal basin above the fourth river, the two further indications on the fall line, and four building ways in operation. The yard launched two transports in 549 and is expected to launch eight a year by 565.

**Maize and the western staples.** Agricultural *curatores*, seed distribution and printed method. Northern strains in two further generations, 600 at the earliest. No quantity of engineers shortens it.

**The speed at Alexandria.** Eleven men. Object is why Rome's pneumatic tubes do not achieve the speed observed at Alexandria in the fifth century.

## TERRITORY

**Held.** Unchanged in the old world: Ireland and Britain to the Dnieper, the north of Scotland and the accessible Scandinavian coasts to the old North African provinces.

In the Caribbean: Hispaniola and Cuba entire, with garrison forts, veteran allotments, gold workings, and masonry batteries; the lesser islands of the chain taken or submitted.

In Central America: the northern Yucatan peninsula, being nineteen centres taken by assault and fourteen held as tributaries, with four inland forts, eleven fortified posts, 260 Roman miles of military road, and a garrison of 22,000; the fortified coastal station; the lagoon town held as a client under treaty rather than as conquered ground.

In North America: four settled river districts behind the great bay, being the first, second, and third openings and the coal river as the fourth, each held from the mouth to the head of navigation with allotments behind; the coal pit with nine miles of proved face; fourteen miles of tramway to tidewater; a coaling station of 4,000 tons; a shipyard of four ways with naval stores; 214,000 colonists on the rolls.

**Surveyed and not held.** The Yucatan hill country, eleven counted centres, surveyed to 340 Roman miles inland. The mainland coast north and south of the Yucatan station beyond a thousand miles. In North America, the ground above the falls on all four rivers, the fall line to two further coal indications, and the forested mountain wall reached at 140 to 200 miles inland and crossed at three passes with the ground beyond them unfollowed. Florida and the seaboard beyond the allotted districts. The African Atlantic coast to the southern corner, by sighting from one to two miles offshore in the previous turn. The Faroes, Iceland, and the Atlantic islands, with a standing watch on the Canaries and Madeira.
