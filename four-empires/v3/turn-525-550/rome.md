## 525 to 550 AD: Powder, the Yucatan Centres, and the Fleet Built for the Western Run

### The Volume from Pataliputra, and Four Readings That Were All Wrong

Rome's single message of the previous turn went east in 525, offering the complete working method of the marine steam engine against a maker's-standard account of the Chinese hand-firearm, both delivered whole, the instalment scheme having been discarded before sending.

The answer was sent from Pataliputra in 530, came by the Mesopotamian road to the Syrian frontier, and was in Roman hands in 531. With it came one printed and sealed volume, being the complete maker's account, delivered entire and with nothing asked in return, the Maurya court stating that Rome had set out its own half in full before receiving anything and had required one delivery and nothing less.

Between 526 and 530 the court argued four readings of the silence and weighted them. The third and fourth, that the offer had been declined without the decline being stated and that the interval was itself the object, were held above the others on the strength of the court's standing disposition toward the Maurya. The second, that the account was being assembled, was rated the weakest of the four. The second was correct. The court was wrong, and wrong in the specific way its own disposition made likely, having reasoned from what it thought of the Maurya rather than from what it knew of the work.

The reason given for the delay is one Rome did not consider at all. At the time of the offer the Maurya held the men who survived the weapon at Tidore. Those men could describe being shot at and could not describe how the thing is built so that another maker may copy it, and the Maurya court states plainly that the difference between a witness's account and a maker's account was not understood at Pataliputra until Rome's terms arrived. An example was then sought, which took the better part of two decades, reached their engineers in 524, was measured and drawn across two years, and had barrels cast to its dimensions and fired through two years more before the account was written in 528 and sealed in 529.

That disposes of the *curatores*' answer of 543 as well, and it should be recorded as the worse error of the two. Asked how long Rome would need to write a maker's account of a weapon it held in its hand, they answered a year or two, and the answer was sound on its own terms. The unexamined word was *held*. Rome assumed possession of an example and reasoned only about the writing, when the whole of the delay lay in getting the example. An office that had itself spent a hundred and eighty years failing to obtain a specimen of the same weapon had every reason to see that and did not.

What Rome did in the interval stands, and stands better than the reasoning behind it. The powder programme was put on the footing of receiving nothing in 528, two years before the volume was sent and three before it arrived, on the ground that a programme two hundred and twenty-five years old could not be paused on a packet whose arrival could not be dated. That decision was correct and would have been correct had nothing ever come. Rome's own mixture closed in 531 in the same year the volume landed, so the account arrived to a court that could already make powder, which is the only reason it could be used at once.

### The Working Mixture and the Salt

The search for a stable ratio, reckoned in 525 to stand about six years off, closed in 531. Three materials go into it. Charcoal and the yellow stone of the Sicilian and Campanian workings were long familiar and cost nothing to obtain. The third had to be found, and nothing in Roman practice pointed at it.

After the reorientation of about 485 the programme's standing instruction was to test any substance reported to make a fire burn fiercer, and the instrument used to gather them was print. A circular went out through the provincial offices in 487 and was reprinted twice, asking for reports of such substances from anyone who worked with fire. Several hundred came back over the following decades and almost all were worthless, being resins, pitches, oils, and the ordinary mineral salts. One from a tanning district in the 490s described a white bloom that gathers on the walls of old cattle byres and reported that a handful thrown on a charcoal bed made the bed flare. That report is why anybody scraped a byre wall. The collector was paid the standard bounty and is not recorded further.

Against the bloom stood the obvious candidate, the *nitrum* of ordinary commerce, the mineral salt bought in bulk through Alexandria for glass and for washing, and the programme spent eleven years on it. Batches made with trade *nitrum* burned and did not drive. Batches made from wall bloom and from the earth under old byres drove hard. The two are alike enough in appearance to be confused in a store, and the men who handle both report that they do not taste alike, so the programme can tell them apart. What it cannot do is say why one drives and the other does not, or predict from anything observable which of two unfamiliar salts will behave which way. The working rule is by source rather than by property: the salt is what comes off ground or walls that have stood under animals, and nothing bought as *nitrum* is substituted for it at any price.

Getting from a scraping to a usable salt took five years. The scrapings arrive as earth and rubbish and are about a fortieth part salt by weight. They are steeped in water in vats, the liquor drawn off and strained, boiled down in iron pans, and left to cool, and the crystals that come out are washed and boiled a second time. The first salts to form on cooling were found by trial in 528 to be useless and are thrown away, which cost two years of poor batches before anyone separated what formed early from what formed late. Wood ash entered at the straining step by accident, a contractor in the British district having strained his liquor through a bed of wood ash to clear it of dirt, after which the salt drove measurably harder than his previous deliveries. Ash in the leach was adopted on that trial alone. Ash was then also put into the deliberate beds, on the reasoning that a thing which improves the salt in the vat may improve it in the wall, and no trial supports that step; beds laid with ash and beds laid without have not been compared.

### The Powder Establishment and What the Arithmetic Armed

The supply chain set the size of the armament, so it was built as a standing industry rather than as a programme. At 550 there are fourteen state powder mills, three on the Rhine at Colonia Agrippina, Mogontiacum, and Argentoratum, three in the British coal district, two in Dacia, two in Campania, two on the Danube, one in Baetica, and one in Noricum, sited for water power to drive the stamps, charcoal within a short haul, and rail or river to a frontier. The mills and their attached works employ 1,150 men, which is a small establishment for fourteen works and is set by the tonnage rather than by the buildings, 214 tons across fourteen mills being fifteen tons apiece in a year.

Collection is let by contract through the provincial procurators and pays by delivered weight. Finished powder is three parts in four the salt, so 214 tons wants 160 tons of salt, and scrapings run about a fortieth part salt, so the contractors must deliver some 6,400 tons of earth and rubbish a year. That is carried by 2,600 contractors and labourers at about two and a half tons a man, the work being intermittent and fitted around other trades rather than a full year's labour. Collection began with the stable floors of the army's own remount depots, which the army owns and can order scraped on a schedule, and extends to any byre, cellar, or cave earth a contractor can reach. There are 4,200 deliberate beds. Beds laid in 538 came into first yield in 543 and are not yet at full yield.

Output in 549 was 214 tons of finished powder. Output before 543 was a small fraction of that. Cumulative production from 531 to 550 was 1,640 tons, of which 870 was consumed by proving, by the destruction trials of 540, by the grain trials, by the hand-weapon work from 533 onward, by the practice allowance of both armaments, and by the Yucatan campaign, which fired some 22,000 rounds across nineteen stormings and the demonstrations that brought fourteen further centres in without a fight, being 14 tons and the first powder Rome spent in anger. That leaves 770 tons in magazine at 550.

The piece charge is two Roman pounds, so a ton yields 1,520 piece charges, and the hand charge is a thirtieth of a Roman pound, so a ton yields some 91,000 of those. Output of 214 tons is 325,000 piece charges a year, and the magazine holds 1,170,000. The practice allowance fixed by the *curatores* is forty rounds a year for both armaments. There are 1,544 pieces in service, taking 41 tons, and 58,000 stand of hand arms, taking 25, and proving takes 22, so 88 tons goes to the year's firing and 126 to magazine. Put the whole gun armament into continuous action at eighty rounds a piece a day and it burns 81 tons a day, so the magazine carries the guns nine days and the annual output replaces under three.

The fall from eleven days to nine is the price of the hand arms and was contested. The *praefecti* of the frontier districts held that a magazine thinned to feed infantry weakens the one arm that has kept the Dnieper line, and they are arithmetically correct. The Senate overrode them in 545 on the ground that a nine-day magazine behind a line that can shoot back is worth more than an eleven-day magazine behind a shield wall that cannot close, and that the hand arms take a quarter of what the guns take for a far larger change in what a Roman line can do. The *praefecti* did not withdraw the objection and it stands in the file.

That figure governs. Powder artillery now goes with the field forces, 212 pieces being on carriages with the frontier armies rather than emplaced, and the eleven-day magazine is why no Roman commander has been authorised to plan a campaign around it. What powder does not do is put a weapon in an infantryman's hands, and the reason there is not supply. Founding capacity was never the limit either: the ordnance shops of the Rhine and British districts are the same shops that bore engine cylinders and roll hull plate, and they can deliver seven hundred pieces a year on existing plant, which is more than the powder will serve.

The 1,544 pieces throw a solid iron shot of five Roman pounds in the standard pattern and six in the heavier siege pattern, and are disposed as 980 at European frontier and river positions, 212 with the field forces in Europe, 172 mounted afloat at two to a gun-armed hull, 96 in the western masonry batteries, and 84 with the Yucatan columns, being 24 to each of the three columns and 12 at the coastal depot. They replaced the fixed and cart-mounted steam launchers at 118 of the 140 launcher positions, and the 22 launchers remaining stand where a prepared defence is the only use contemplated.

### The Branch That Turned

The premise that broke in 485 was that the Han weapon must store pressure, held from about 300 for a hundred and eighty years, and it was a correct inference from a false premise rather than carelessness. Rome had mastered pressure, Rome's own launchers accelerated a projectile with expanding gas, and the flying sphere reported in the incident of the 290s looked like a launch from a better pressure engine than Rome had. What broke it was one fact from the Chinese warning of the 480s: a single soldier carries the weapon and fires it on the instant. Rome knows from two centuries with boilers that stored pressure requires a heavy vessel and time to raise it, and that its own launchers must be pre-heated. A weapon a man carries and fires at once has nowhere to keep pressure and no moment in which to raise it, yet plainly looses great force with flash and smoke at its mouth. Therefore the force is made at the instant of ignition by something that burns and expands violently. That step is settled and 531 confirmed it, because a mixture Rome lit did exactly that.

The step that followed was not drawn until 532. If the force is made in the instant, the vessel is required to hold only for the instant. Rome's boilers are heavy because they hold for hours against a pressure continuously renewed, and Roman practice had never had cause to distinguish the weight a vessel needs from the duration it must last. Therefore the weight is set by duration as much as by force, and a vessel that holds for an instant can be far lighter than boiler practice would size it.

That was not acted on for eight years. The first weaponised pieces were sized by boiler proportions, wall thickness carried in ratio to the bore as the naval boiler programme had established it, and came out three to four times heavier than the pieces now in service. The correction came in 540, when the programme fired deliberately thinned tubes to destruction to find where the proportion actually failed, and found that tubes at a third of the boiler wall survived charges the boiler-proportioned pieces were built for. Rome cannot say why boiler proportion overestimates the requirement so far. One line of argument in the programme holds that a pressure applied in an instant is borne differently by the metal than a pressure sustained, and that metal has some capacity to take a blow it does not have to take a load, and that line is the majority view among the programme's engineers. It stands in the file as the programme's explanation and not as a finding, nothing having been measured that distinguishes it from any other explanation.

Two further results stand as reliable in their core and unexplained. Lengthening the tube increased the force delivered up to a ratio of about eighteen to twenty times the bore and gave nothing measurable beyond it, and there is no account of why a limit should exist. Working the finished powder to a coarser and more even grain rather than a fine dust increased the force and reduced the number of pieces lost in firing, found in 536 when a badly sieved batch outperformed a good one and standardised without being understood.

The hand weapon followed from the light vessel by argument, and in practice it followed from the volume of 531. Rome had reasoned that a vessel holding for an instant could be made light enough for a man to carry, which was correct and was not enough, because the three things that govern such a weapon do not stay separate under trial: a coarser grain alters the force and also how readily the charge takes fire, a narrower tube alters the force and also the fouling that spoils the next shot, and a change of ignition alters where in the charge the burning starts and so alters the force again. Rome can vary one thing against a fixed rest and did so cleanly in 536 and 540, but it observes three effects from one change and has no means of measuring pressure inside a tube at the instant of firing. That is a problem a programme can work at for a generation without closing, and Rome was working at it when the volume arrived.

The volume closed it by supplying the answer rather than the method: barrel, lock and stock drawn and measured, the metals and their treatment, the means of ignition, the loading sequence, and the engineers' reasoning entire. Rome did not have to find the combination. It had to build to a drawing, which is the thing Rome does better than anything else it does.

The work still took nineteen years and the record should say why, since a court that is handed a drawing and then takes nineteen years has learned something about the difference between an account and a manufacture. The volume was translated and set out by 533. The first Roman barrels to the drawing fired in 535. Every figure of range and rate had to be got again, because the volume states plainly that its figures were taken with Maurya powder from Maurya saltpetre at Maurya proportions and that those are the two figures a different powder most moves; Rome's own powder is made from wall salt to a ratio settled in 531 and behaves differently, and re-deriving the tables took to 539. The lock was the long part. Roman locks made to the drawing failed wet, which the volume does not address because the account is of a weapon and not of a climate, and a serviceable lock for the Rhine and the Dnieper in winter was not fixed until 541. The standard pattern was settled in 543 and the pattern shops began delivering in 544.

At 550 there are 58,000 stand in service, issued to twenty-four cohorts on the Dnieper, eighteen on the Rhine, and the frontier reserve, with the shops delivering 12,000 a year and able to double that on existing plant. Two things are not known and are entered as unknowns because the volume itself enters them. The powder difference is unmeasured, Rome having never had a grain of China's to weigh. And the example the account was taken from came out of material an arsenal had set aside as unserviceable, so whether it is a current pattern with a fault or an older pattern discarded for age is not settled by anything in the volume; if Rome ever meets a weapon of this class that differs from the drawings, the difference may lie there and not in any error of the measuring.

### The Steam Fleet

The whole navy at 550 is 89 steam warships in service with 16 more building, 380 ocean-going sailing warships, 640 ocean-going sailing transports of which 268 are on the western run, and something near 900 river and supply steam vessels carried in the accounts as infrastructure rather than as ships. The sailing battle fleet holds the Atlantic, the North Sea, and the Mediterranean stations and is more than four times the steam fleet. The rest of this section counts steam warships, which are the newest and smallest part of the navy and the part the arms race is fought in.

The steam fleet stood at twenty to thirty operational hulls at 500 and at thirty-one in 525, being ten iron-plated and twenty-one wooden paddle vessels of the 490s and 500s. The building followed the reassessment of the previous turn, which established that Egyptian hulls are beyond Roman counting, and the *magister classis* was given a standing authority in 531 to lay down six warship hulls a year, which he took up in eighteen of the twenty years to 550 for 108 hulls laid down.

Of those 108, ninety-two were completed and commissioned by 550 and sixteen are still on the ways or fitting out, being the four all-iron hulls of 548 that are not expected in service before 556 and the twelve of the 549 and 550 tranches.

Against that stands an attrition line, which the launcher positions have and the hulls have not had. Thirty-four hulls were struck across the period. Twenty-one are the whole of the wooden paddle fleet of 525, sixteen worn out past economical repair and five hulked as coal lighters at the western stations, so no un-plated hull of that generation is left afloat. The other thirteen are new construction, seven lost at sea to weather, boiler failure, and a grounding on the Iberian coast, and six condemned for frame and boiler defects inside their first decade.

The steam fleet in service at 550 is therefore 31 plus 92 less 34, which is 89 hulls, with 16 building for a programme of 105. The 89 divide as seventy-nine iron-plated and ten purpose-built wooden gun vessels. Eighty-six of them carry pieces at two apiece for the 172 afloat, the other three being newest and still working up.

Gun mounting was first tried in 542 and 543 by retrofitting existing wooden hulls framed for a ram and a ballista, and the trials worked their frames loose, which bears on retrofitting and not on what a hull can be built to do. Rome's shipwrights can frame a wooden vessel for the recoil of a five-pound shot, and from 544 twelve were built that way for the shallow-water work where a plated hull draws too much, of which ten are in service. New construction is otherwise plated, against the shallow-draft swarming attack described to Rome in about 420.

The all-iron hull slipped past the projection made around 500, which was twenty-five to thirty years from then. Plate was never the difficulty, the rolling mills having produced consistent sheet since the 480s. Iron framing in heavy sections is, because the mills roll flat plate well and heavy angle and bar badly, so sections have been forged at high cost and inconsistent quality. Behind that stood yard capacity, taken up by plating existing hulls, by the western transports, and by the screw programme, which the *magister classis* has held at higher priority since Egypt's quiet vessels were observed in the fifth century. Four all-iron hulls were laid down from 548 and the first is expected in service between 556 and 560.

Sixteen steam warships are in western waters, rigged for sail with engines banked, since no steam hull crosses three thousand nautical miles on a bunker good for six or seven hundred. They are stationed there alongside forty-one sailing warships, which need no coal and are what a Roman commander in the west would actually fight with today.

### The Western Transports and the Migration They Carried

The commitment of the previous turn was made on three facts, that the land was real and rich, that the veterans owed land had somewhere to go, and that Egypt could not be assumed idle. In its first decade it was carried on hulls taken off the Iberian and African grain and oil trades, which had to be re-let at worse rates, and the *curatores* of the treasury reported in 533 that the largest strategic undertaking in four centuries was being run on borrowed tonnage. From 534 the yards were ordered to build for it. One hundred and sixty-four ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons were laid down in the Atlantic yards of Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala, and the Severn and Clyde, at 1.4 million HS a hull for 230 million across the period, charged to the fleet account. Against 130 hulls at the start and 164 built, twenty-six were struck across the period, nineteen worn out on a run that works a hull harder than any trade in the Roman world and seven lost at sea, leaving 268. The tonnage taken from the grain trades was returned by 541 and exceeded thereafter.

Hulls dedicated to the run stood at 130 through 533 and rose to 268 by 550 as the new transports came off the ways, which is 4,354 hull-years across the period and an average of 174 hulls on station. A hull makes one and a half round voyages a year, the outward run from the Canaries taking six weeks on the trades and the return running the Gulf Stream north to the westerlies, about four months at sea in all. That gives 6,530 voyages. Half carried stores, troops, timber, plant, and returning cargo and no people at all. The other 3,265 averaged 352 emigrants, a six-hundred-ton transport carrying that number with their gear and provisions without difficulty, and landed 1,151,000 people, or 46,000 a year.

The allotment registers and the tax rolls of the western provinces carried 2,066,000 colonists and their children at 550. That figure is three things and not one. The cohort landed in the previous turn, some 480,000, stands at about 696,000 with twenty-five years of increase on it at one and a half in the hundred a year, a settler population being young and married early. This turn's 1,151,000 landings sit on top of that. And 219,000 children born in the west to this turn's arrivals, at the same rate on an average residence near twelve years. The crossing and the island fevers were assessed in the previous turn as not severe and nothing since has changed that.

The military establishment is 640,000 men on twenty-year terms, which discharges about 32,000 men a year, each owed land and most with families, so the settlement demand runs near 100,000 persons a year. The west absorbed 46,000 a year across this turn, which is forty-six per cent of it. The remainder still goes onto marginal ground in the north and east.

They are distributed as 982,000 on Hispaniola, Cuba, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and the northern outliers, 522,000 on the northern seaboard in 134,000 allotments, and 562,000 on the Yucatan plain in 144,000 allotments. The rolls count persons; an allotment is a family holding and runs near four persons, and the two figures are not to be read against one another.

The landing split is the thing to check the rest against. Of the 1,151,000 who came ashore from Europe this turn, 601,000 landed on the islands, mostly in the earlier years when there was nowhere else to put them, 300,000 on the Yucatan coast across 545 to 550, and 250,000 on the northern seaboard from 532. Births in the west across the turn were 135,000 on the islands, 62,000 on the seaboard, and 22,000 on the Yucatan, which is 219,000 and the same one and a half in the hundred everywhere. The Yucatan's share is small because its cohort is the newest in the west, none of it having been in the country before 545.

The mainland cohorts are not six years of transatlantic landings, and would be impossible if they were. Three things fill them. The seaboard districts were opened in 532 and not with the coal, the previous turn having already marked that coast for survey, so they have had eighteen years of arrivals rather than six. Landings were routed straight to the mainland once there was mainland to route them to, so of the Yucatan's 562,000 some 300,000 came ashore there directly across 545 to 550, which is most of what the run was landing in those years.

And 450,000 colonists moved out from the islands to the mainland, 210,000 to the northern rivers from 534 and 240,000 to the Yucatan plain from 545. This is the line the western papers had been leaving out. It is not an ocean crossing and it needs no hull from Gades. The *legatus* of the island province ran it on his own account with coastal transports of three hundred tons and the supply vessels of the western squadron, carrying two hundred and fifty at a passage of three to eight days. The two streams do not run at one rate. The northern movement is 210,000 across sixteen years, about 13,000 a year, and the Yucatan movement is 240,000 across six, about 40,000 a year, so the years 545 to 550 carry both at once and run near 53,000. The establishment was thirty-four vessels when the northern movement began and sixty-two by 546, and sixty-two hulls at four round trips and two hundred and fifty a passage carry 62,000 a year, which covers the peak with something to spare. Passage was found at public cost at 40 HS a head, 18 million across the sixteen years, against a mainland allotment of equal or better grade and remission of the land tax on it for five years.

They went for reasons the rest of this record sets out. The island creeks are thinning at the first workings, the subject labour the island allotments were planned around has fallen by two thirds, and the Yucatan plain is worked maize ground that needs no clearing and carries a tributary population still working it.

The island colonist roll nonetheless rose across the turn and did not fall. It stands at 982,000, being the 696,000 of the previous cohort with its increase, plus the 601,000 landed there this turn and 135,000 born to them, less the 450,000 who left for the mainland. What has fallen on the islands is the subject roll and not the colonist roll, and the two should not be read as one movement.

The island settlements feed themselves on cassava and maize since 542 and no longer draw shipped grain. The Yucatan plain is the only western district that ships a surplus rather than eating one.

### The Conquest of the Yucatan Centres

The station on the Yucatan coast was established in the previous turn to measure the interior before anything was committed to it, on the stated rule that a fractured landscape of city-states would be taken one at a time and a larger or unified power would be known about before Rome moved. The measuring was finished in 540 and the rule was applied from 541.

Interpreters were built rather than waited for. The island administration had run Arawak interpreters since the 510s and applied the same method here: from 528 the coastal station took Maya-speaking traders into paid service, put Roman boys of the garrison families with them, and printed a word list that went through four revised editions. The merchants of the lagoon town behind the station were the readiest source, several of them already speaking an island tongue from their own canoe trade. Forty competent interpreters existed by 536 and a hundred and ninety by 550.

Reconnaissance ran from 528 to 540. Observation balloons were flown from cleared ground and from vessels off both coasts, mounted columns went inland on cut tracks, and interpreters bought information in the markets. Three hundred and forty Roman miles were surveyed inland across the peninsula and into the hill country beyond it, against the sixty of the previous turn. Forty-four independent inland centres were counted, not reckoning the lagoon town on the coast, no authority was found standing over them, several were found at war with one another, and none fielded a metal weapon.

The sequence was as follows.

The lagoon town went first, in 541, and it went by sea. Six thousand troops were landed from the coastal station in the ships of the western squadron, which carried no pieces, no gun was afloat anywhere in the fleet before the mountings of 544, and the ships gave what support they gave with their ballistae. Six of the light carriage pieces corrected to the new proportions in 540 were landed on the beach and worked from firm ground against the landward wall. The town was taken in nine days for 180 Roman dead. It was not sacked and it was not garrisoned as a conquest. Its merchant houses were left standing under a treaty as Rome's first western client, its trade rights confirmed, and its obligation fixed at pilotage, porterage, market information, and interpreters. Rome wanted the entrepôt working and wanted the roads inland described by men who used them, and a burned town describes nothing.

From the lagoon the columns went inland. Forty thousand further troops came out across 541 and 542, drawn from the Danube and African provinces and not from the island garrisons, so the whole commitment was 46,000, which is seven per cent of the establishment. Three columns worked in sequence rather than together, each of 13,000 with a train of light carriage pieces, with 1,000 at the coastal depot, because a fractured country taken one centre at a time does not need three simultaneous armies and does need roads behind each.

Izamal and the northwestern plain centres fell across 542 and 543. Four were stormed. Rome's method was the ordinary one and needed no innovation: the columns approached on cut road, the pieces reduced a gate or a wall section at two hundred paces, and the shield wall went in. Stone tools and cotton armour against steel, mail, and shot is not a contest, and the four assaults together cost 1,240 Roman dead against losses on the other side that the *legatus* returned as beyond counting and did not attempt to count.

After the fourth storming a second pattern appeared alongside the first and did not replace it. Fourteen centres sent to the columns across the following years and submitted without fighting, having seen what a stormed centre looks like afterward, on terms Rome had prepared in advance and printed: tribute assessed in goods and labour, a Roman post inside the wall, no restriction on the ruling houses, and no interference with what is worshipped. Rome does not care what is worshipped in a tributary and said so, which cost Rome nothing and left the treasuries untouched by the concession. The submitted centres were assessed for metal and worked ornament in the first year and paid it, the difference from the stormed centres being that they handed it over against a receipt and kept their roofs.

Fourteen others did not send, and were stormed one at a time across 544 to 548 as the roads reached them, four in the western plain, six along the northern salt coast, and four in the broken ground toward the hills. None held longer than three weeks once the pieces were up, and none is worth a separate account; together they cost 870 Roman dead, and they are the fourteen that make the nineteen with the first four and with Coba.

Coba on the eastern plain was the hardest and took most of 546, being the largest counted centre, sited among lakes, and connected to its dependencies by raised stone causeways of its own building. The causeways were the reason it fell. They carried Roman columns and Roman guns as readily as they had carried its own traffic, and once two were held the centre could be approached from three directions at once. It cost 810 Roman dead.

Nineteen centres were taken by assault in all, fourteen are held as tributaries, one stands as a client, and eleven counted centres in the hill country have not been reached. Two hundred and sixty Roman miles of military road were cut, four inland forts and eleven fortified posts raised, and the garrison stands at 22,000. Roman dead across the nine years were 3,100 to the fighting, being 180 at the lagoon town, 1,240 in the first four stormings, 870 in the fourteen that followed, and 810 at Coba, and 5,800 to fever, the fever being the heavier charge by a wide margin, as it was in the Delta before 90 BC.

The columns halted in 549 and the halt is a supply halt. Three things bind and each was stated in the *legatus*' returns of 548 and 549. The hill country begins about a hundred and eighty Roman miles beyond the present road head and cannot be supplied from the coastal depot without that road, and the road cannot be cut, because the labour that cut the first two hundred and sixty miles is dying faster than seizure replaces it and the districts nearest the works are the emptiest. Fever is taking the columns at about 640 a year against replacements arriving at 400, so the force in the field shrinks in every season it stands still. And the request of 548 for 20,000 further troops was refused, the uncommitted field reserve being held against the Dnieper and the Rhine under the standing rule that Rome does not thin two frontiers to press a third, and the Yucatan being the least of the three by a long way.

The eleven in the hills are counted, their approaches are surveyed, and the coast beyond runs more than a thousand miles with nothing on it Rome has yet had to reckon with. The programme entered for the next turn resumes the columns in 552, when the road is expected to reach the hill line, and works the eleven centres across 552 to 560 on the same pattern. Whether the labour exists to cut that road is the open question, and it is the same question as everything else in these districts.

The centres were stripped, and the strip is the larger number. Nineteen stormed centres and fourteen assessed ones returned 210 million HS across 541 to 549, being 46 million in worked gold and silver ornament and temple plate, 28 million in jade, worked shell, and feather work, 61 million in stored cacao and cotton mantles, both of which pass as money in the country and were taken as money, 12 million in salt from the coastal pans, and 63 million entered against people. The metal was the disappointment of the campaign. These centres trade gold in rather than dig it, so the plate of nineteen stormed cities came to less than one season of the Hispaniola creeks in their good years, and the *legatus* said so in his first return and was not believed until the third.

Two hundred and forty thousand people were taken. Eighty-eight thousand were shipped to the islands, where they were the answer the *curatores* had for a subject labour force that had halved and the only one they had; of those, the *legatus* returns one in six dead on the passage and one in four of the survivors dead within two years of landing, so 56,000 of the 88,000 were alive in 550. Twelve thousand went to the old provinces, chiefly to the mine districts and to households, which is a small number because a slave carried three thousand nautical miles costs more than a wage in a country where wage labour has been the norm in production for two centuries. The remaining hundred and forty thousand were set to labour where they stood, on the roads, the forts, the coastal works, and the colonist allotments, and they are dying at the rate of the districts they came from and faster where the work is heaviest.

Against a campaign cost of 168 million in nine years of pay, transport, roads, and forts, the strip returned 210 million and paid for the conquest with 42 million over. The plunder line, empty in the imperial accounts since expansion in Europe closed around 300, was opened again for those nine years and closes with them.

The tribute is what the districts yield after that, and the base under it is going faster than anything in the Roman accounts has gone before.

The first assessment counted 1,200,000 across the taken and tributary districts, taken district by district as each fell between 541 and 546. That figure is not a count of what was there before Rome came. Officers entering the plain in 542 and 543 recorded settlements standing empty or half empty with the maize still in the ground and nobody harvesting, ahead of any column, and the same was reported at centres that submitted before a Roman was seen inside them. What the pre-contact number was, Rome cannot establish, and the *legatus* who tried put it between 1,700,000 and 2,200,000 from house counts in abandoned quarters, which is a guess made from empty buildings.

From the 1,200,000 assessed, the rolls at 550 carry 584,000. The fall of 616,000 in under a decade breaks down as roughly 380,000 to sickness, 46,000 killed in the storming and in the weeks after it, 100,000 struck off as shipped away, and 90,000 gone into the hill country beyond Roman reach. The sickness is far the largest and it does not spread its losses evenly. It takes a district hardest in the first two or three years after Rome reaches it, and the districts entered in 542 lost near half their assessed heads by 546 while the districts entered in 546 have so far lost a fifth.

Tribute followed the base down. It was assessed at 34 million HS in 545 with most of the plain on the rolls and yielded 19 million in 549, which is 33 HS a head on 584,000 and should not be projected forward flat. What the *curatores* have in front of them is a revenue that halves as its population halves, against a garrison and a road system whose cost does not.

Rome's answer to a workforce that is disappearing has been to take more people, which is the ordinary answer and is making it worse. The eighty-eight thousand shipped to the islands were taken because island labour had halved; they died on the passage and again on arrival at rates the *legatus* returns as one in six and one in four; the districts they came from lost their harvest labour and fell further behind their assessment; and the assessment was then met by seizing more. That circle has run three times since 543 and nothing in the western administration is set up to break it. The tribute rolls of the districts taken first fell by about a third within six years of being counted, in the same manner as the islands, and the same three explanations are argued in the printed journals with the same want of any theory behind them.

Colonisation followed the columns. Veteran allotments were laid from 545 on the cleared plain behind the taken centres and on the ground the tributary houses gave up under assessment, and carried 562,000 colonists at 550 in 144,000 allotments, being 300,000 landed from Europe direct, 240,000 come over from the islands under the transfer of 545, and 22,000 born in the west, the cohort being too new to have produced more. They are the best fed of the western settlements. The plain is worked maize ground already and needs no clearing, tributary labour works it under the old arrangements with a Roman assessment over the top, and the district is the only one in the west that ships a grain surplus rather than eating one.

### The Northern Seaboard, Worked Up the Rivers

The northern coast was reassessed upward in the previous turn for fuel, and this turn made it the second centre of the western position. It is now settled ground and not a visited one, and it is being worked the way Rome works a river, which is to hold the mouth, survey to the head of navigation, put a station at the head, and lay allotments behind it.

The coal above the fourth navigable opening south of the great bay was proved along nine miles of face and burned under a standing pump engine at within a tenth of the poorer British grades, which serves a bunker and an engine and is not good coal. A tramway of fourteen miles ran from the pit to the head of navigation from 549, laid in local timber faced with iron strip because rolled rail shipped three thousand nautical miles costs more per mile than the tramway earns. A coaling station at tidewater holds 4,000 tons, which is what makes a steam squadron in western waters something other than sixteen poor sailing ships once its bunkers are out.

A shipyard was laid out at the river mouth in 546 on the ship's carpenter's note from the second voyage, entered into the formal report of the previous turn, that the oak and pine of the northern coast are superior shipbuilding timber. It launched two six-hundred-ton transports in 549 and is building four ways. Naval stores went with it, pitch and tar from the pine and spars cut and floated down.

Four river districts are settled, each on the same pattern: the great bay's western rivers taken as the first, second, and third openings and the coal river as the fourth. Survey parties worked each to the head of navigation and then on foot beyond it, forty to seventy Roman miles inland to a line of falls where the rivers cease to carry a boat, and beyond the falls to a long forested mountain wall running north and south, which the surveys reached at between a hundred and forty and two hundred miles inland and have crossed at three passes without following the ground beyond them. Coal indications were reported at two further points along the fall line and one is being proved.

Settlement is by allotment rather than by conquest, the country being thinly peopled as the second voyage reported. Veteran allotments were laid out from 532, the previous turn having already marked this coast for survey, and carried 522,000 colonists at 550 in 134,000 allotments across the four districts, being 250,000 landed direct, 210,000 moved over from the islands from 534, and 62,000 born in the west, with grain, timber, pitch, and the pit as their trades. Fighting with the river peoples has been local and intermittent, has cost 640 Roman dead across the period, and has been settled district by district by treaties of the kind used with the lagoon town rather than by campaign, there being no centre to storm.

### The Subject Populations Are Emptying

The islands went first and are the further along. The native tribute roll of Hispaniola stood at 96,000 in 550 against a first count by district heads of three to five hundred thousand; Cuba at 134,000, and Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and the northern outliers together at 38,000, for 268,000 native islanders where the first counts had put something between six hundred thousand and a million. To those are added the 56,000 survivors of the 88,000 shipped in from the mainland, giving 324,000 on the island subject rolls in all. The first counts are unreliable, so the proportion carries more than the absolutes, and the proportion is a fall of two thirds or worse in twenty-five years with the steepest part in the first decade after each district was entered rather than spread across the twenty-five.

The mainland is going the same way, faster, and from a larger base: 1,200,000 assessed between 541 and 546, 584,000 on the rolls in 550. Set the two together and the western subject population Rome has counted at one time or another comes to something over two million, against 908,000 carried on all its rolls today.

The causes stack and the *curatores* have stopped trying to separate them. Sickness is much the largest and it arrives ahead of the columns, reaching centres before any Roman entered them. On top of it sit the storming of nineteen cities, the seizure of two hundred and forty thousand people, the deaths of a sixth of the shipped on the passage and a quarter of the rest within two years of landing, and the work itself in the gold creeks, the road cuttings, and the coastal works, which kills at a rate Rome recognises from its own deep mines. Each of those is a mortality on its own and they fall on the same people in the same years.

The sickness itself is unresolved. Rome's physicians argue three explanations in the printed medical journals: the labour, which does kill and cannot account for deaths among those never set to it; the change of diet under requisition; and the air of newly worked ground, on the analogy of the Delta marsh fevers. The labour and diet explanations fit the mainland worse than they fit the islands, because Yucatan tributaries work their own maize ground under their own houses much as before and eat what they grew before, and they are dying anyway. The minority holding that the sickness travels with people rather than with conditions has been gaining since the reports of empty settlements ahead of the columns, and from 544 new colonists have been landed at a distance from tributary villages on that argument. Nothing further follows from it, because nobody can say what travels or how, and the distance rule has not visibly worked.

Two consequences run through the western accounts. The tribute falls with the rolls, from 34 million assessed in 545 to 19 million yielded in 549, and it should not be projected forward flat. And ground comes free faster than colonists can be put on it, which is how 144,000 allotments were laid on worked Yucatan maize land inside five years without turning a tributary house off its own fields.

The economic effect is what the accounts register. Two populations move in opposite directions, one added to by ship every season and by birth every year, the other falling on both the islands and the mainland. Rome planned the west as an extraction economy worked by subject labour where there is no coal, and the labour is going. What replaces it is colonist labour, which must be paid, and machinery, which must be fuelled, and the fuel is on the northern coast and not on the gold islands. The gold creeks of Hispaniola are also thinning at the first workings. This is why the northern coast now carries the weight of the western programme in the *curatores*' papers while the islands and the Yucatan carry the revenue.

### The Western Accounts

The fiscal instrument was argued in 533 and settled in 535. The *curatores* of the treasury extended the existing luxury import duty to western cargo at the ports of landing, chiefly Gades and Olisipo with Ostia for re-export, at twelve per cent. The land tax was not raised, being the largest line in the accounts and falling on the veteran colonies that are the recruiting base of the northern army. Debasement was proposed by a minority and rejected on the standing objection that the mint is the instrument of last resort in a war and is worth less each time it is used. The Imperial Commercial and Industrial Council opposed the duty on raw cotton and hides on the ground that the Rhine and British mills consume the cotton and that a duty on a raw material taxes Roman manufacture rather than western luxury, and that objection was accepted in the manner of the standardisation compromise of 480: twelve per cent on cacao, tobacco, dyestuffs, and worked gold, four per cent on raw cotton and hides. Dutiable landings were valued at 470 million in 549, being 180 million of cacao, 95 million of dyestuffs in cochineal and logwood, 70 million of tobacco, 30 million of worked gold ornaments, and 95 million of raw cotton and hides, so the duty yielded 49 million. Bullion gold is not in that figure, being landed on the imperial account and entered under mining. Advances from the shipping companies against the following season's landings carried the building programme's early years, which extends a practice Rome has used since it took advances from tax-farmers and large suppliers and is not borrowing in a form the treasury will admit to, there being no bond market to borrow in.

Island gold has been falling for a decade on the same curve as everything else in the west. It yielded 168 million at its peak in 534, when the creeks were fresh and the island subject rolls were near their first count, and 110 million in 549. Both causes are known and neither is reversible: alluvial gold thins at the first workings once the easy ground is turned over, and the labour that turns it has fallen by two thirds. The eighty-eight thousand shipped from the mainland were sent to hold that line and have not held it. On present trend the creeks yield under 70 million by 560.

The western charge in 549 was 288 million against western revenue of 178 million, being 110 million of island gold, 49 million of duty, and 19 million of Yucatan tribute, so the west runs 110 million short. The charge divides as 76 million of garrisons, being 46 in the Yucatan on 22,000 men at frontier rates with a transport charge on every relief, 22 in the islands on 11,000, and 8 on the seaboard posts; 54 million for the western naval station, sixteen steam hulls and forty-one sailing warships with their crews, coal, and upkeep; 62 million of capital works in harbours, batteries, roads, forts, the tramway, the coaling station, and the yard; 34 million of provincial administration and courts; 34 million of colonist establishment in passage, survey, seed and tools, and the transfer passages; 19 million of Yucatan campaign charge still running in 549; and 9 million for the western transports in building and upkeep. The one-time strip of 210 million covered that gap while the columns were moving and does not recur. Gold is thinning at the first workings and tribute is falling with the population it is assessed on, so on present trend the shortfall widens every year from here. The fiscal conservatives moved in 536 to cap the charge and were beaten. The office that gained standing across both defeats is the *praefectus* of the western fleets, whose command now disposes of more tonnage than any other single naval command in the empire.

### Egypt

The southern survey of the previous turn produced the largest single military reassessment in the record, and two items stand unresolved in the file: the shipbuilding surge in the Egyptian home yards, and a northbound coastal column of large loaded ships counted past two hundred before the counting stopped. Nothing Roman observation produced in twenty-five years bore on either, and both remain unresolved. The pieces fit a race to the Americas and they fit equally an Atlantic gold artery running metal north with its return leg offshore, and the great Egyptian hulls were counted going north and were never once seen going back south, which is exactly what a laden artery running one way with its empty return leg held offshore would look like.

What was done under that uncertainty shows in the accounts. The warship building authority of 531 is the largest single response and the largest single new charge in the accounts, at 9 million HS a hull for 972 million across twenty years, being 49 million a year in building and a further 31 million a year to keep 89 hulls commissioned, crewed, and coaled. Masonry batteries were laid on Hispaniola, Cuba, the Yucatan coast, and the northern river mouths from 546 and hold ninety-six pieces, laid against ships and not against islanders. A standing watch was established on the Canaries and on Madeira with balloon and telescope, counting hulls passing north and west, the crossing latitude being the one place where a Roman observer sits astride whatever is happening. The Alexandria network was kept standing with its silent branch quarantined and untouched.

Twenty-five years of that watch produced no sighting, and the null goes on the file because a purpose-built observation that returns nothing has returned something. No Egyptian hull was seen by the Canaries or Madeira stations, by any of the 268 transports on the run, by the fifty-seven warships on the western station, by the coastal patrols of Hispaniola and Cuba, by the Yucatan station, or by the four river districts of the northern seaboard, across twenty-five years.

The net is only as good as the water it covers, so the water is set out. It is the crossing latitude off the Canaries and Madeira; the outward track west on the trades; the head of the island arc and its two watering anchorages; the passages north of Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, and Cuba; the Yucatan channel and the coast of the station; the northern seaboard and the mouths of its four rivers; and the return track running north to the westerlies. Outside it lie the arc below its head, the sea south and west of Cuba beyond the channel, and everything below the latitude of the landfall. That is a wide net and it caught nothing, and it is a net with edges that can be pointed to.

It weighs against the reading that had Egypt racing Rome to the Americas, and it does not close it. A crossing made from a latitude south of the Canaries would pass under the watch entirely, the column Rome counted was seen well south of it, and a fleet standing far offshore on a westing would not be seen from an island station at all. What the null establishes is that no Egyptian force has appeared on any ground Rome holds or sails. On that the court shifted weight toward the gold-artery reading.

That was the wrong way to shift, and the Alexandria network settled it in the same period. Egypt has been putting settlers across the western ocean every sailing season for the whole twenty-five years, and doing it in the open at the Alexandria end because the instrument cannot be concealed. It is the old Madagascar instrument, published to recruit by: land surveyed and registered at the destination, exemption from the land tax for fifteen years, subsidised passage for a family with its stock and tools, and harbour works, roads and cisterns built before the settlers arrive. The network had the terms from the recruiters and from the registry as matter of public record, watched families with livestock and tools go aboard season after season, and watched those transports sail west out of Alexandria and not into the canal, gone for long periods with a proportion never returning. Transport hull building at Alexandria and the Delta rose from a handful of hulls a year in the 520s to twelve or eighteen a year by the 540s, broad, deep and cargo-carrying rather than a warship's build, and of the hulls the network could put a hand on at the commercial quays the great majority were transports and river craft. The building is pointed at carriage.

Where they are going is reported rather than firm, and is entered as sailors' talk gathered over years and consistent between tellers: a crossing of the western ocean; a temperate country of level treeless grass on black soil, with two harbours facing one another across a brown estuary a hundred miles wide, where wheat and cattle take at once and horses have gone wild; and separately a hot coast of reefs, mangrove and rain forest cut by river mouths. Towns are named in the talk. None of it is a document and the network obtained nothing of the route, the courses, the distances, the landfall or the depot chain, Egypt's sailing directions having been under guard for four centuries.

The correction is worth stating as a correction. The court held the question unresolved and then leaned it the wrong way, and it leaned it on a null that was honestly bounded and correctly described. The net was real, its edges were stated, and the answer lay outside them, in water below the latitude of the landfall that Rome had written down as uncovered. The discipline was sound and the conclusion drawn from it was not, and the lesson the court takes is narrower than it might have taken: a bounded null is worth what its bounds are worth, and Rome had already written the bound that should have stopped it.

Two figures move with this. Rome's five-year head start of the previous turn is no head start at all, and was probably never one. And the northbound column of two hundred hulls, which Rome logged unresolved, is no longer needed to explain anything, since a settlement programme sailing west out of the Delta accounts for Egyptian hulls in the Atlantic without a gold artery, without an armada, and without either reading Rome constructed. That column stays unresolved. It is simply no longer the question.

The Alexandria network also returned what it could not do, and the limits are as useful as the findings. It cannot aggregate an empire-wide shipbuilding rate, Egyptian hulls being built at yards on the East African coast beside the hardwood, on the Red Sea, and on the great rivers of the interior, so every count it renders is a share of an unknown whole and must not be read as a national figure. It has been shut out of the naval yard since 365 and can count hulls in the roads on a given day and nothing more, so Egyptian warship strength, build rate, refit cycle and disposition stand at nil. It sees nothing of Egypt's industry, treasury or interior works, and nothing of settler embarkation from any harbour but Alexandria and the Delta. What it did see of the warships is that the major steam hulls in the roads carry metal plating on their sides and the newest carry it as built rather than as patched, and that a growing number carry metal frames and wires at the masthead of a kind no rigging requires, which the network reported as an object and correctly declined to guess at.

### Egypt's Note on the African Coast

A note came through the formal channel within the period, undated in Rome's register. It states that the Atlantic coast of Africa has lately come under Egyptian administration, asks that Rome give notice of the passage of its vessels along that coast before such passages are made, requires vessels with no commercial business there to keep to open water and stand clear of Egyptian harbours, works and signal stations, and states that what Egypt has arranged with other powers in the matter is not Rome's business and that Egypt will not discuss it, nor the extent of its holdings, nor its routes, nor the disposition of its fleet.

Rome treats this as a court's statement and not as a verified fact, which is the standing rule for anything out of Alexandria. Two things follow.

The first is that it concedes in writing what Rome's three ships established by eye in the previous turn and Egypt had spent a century denying. The coast is Egyptian. That is the first Egyptian acknowledgement of it in six centuries of contact, and it retires the account Egypt gave in about 420 of the five Roman ships lost there, which attributed them to maritime peoples with fast shallow boats and the Canary Current. Rome had already reduced that account to a fiction on its own evidence. Egypt has now written the correction itself without calling it one.

The second is the word *lately*. Roman eyes counted planned stone cities running three thousand miles, a coastal signal net in operation, harbour works, foundries at work, and subject populations beyond counting, all of which the survey judged to imply decades at the least. A coast does not come lately under an administration that has already built it. Rome enters *lately* as the least reliable word in the note and does not otherwise dispute the note's substance, since the substance agrees with what Roman ships saw.

On compliance, Rome gives the notice asked and keeps its vessels off that shore, which costs nothing: the probe has been suspended since the losses of the 390s and about 420, no Roman hull has business on that coast, and a request to stay out of water Rome has already resolved to stay out of is a request Rome can grant at no price. What Rome does not do is accept the note as establishing anything about the extent of Egyptian holdings, since the note declines to state that extent, and a claim to a coast is not a survey of it.

### The Junction at the River

The matter of joining the rail lines at the Dnieper was answered in this turn on the terms the Senate settled in 525, and the letter is below. It went twenty-five years late and it went stale, and the record should carry both.

The Han built a transfer yard on their own bank between 526 and 531 and did not wait for a Roman answer to do it. Rome's own frontier watch stood opposite and saw it go up, entered it under construction, and has counted its covered stores rising from one in 527 to three in 544, its cranes from two to four, its standing labour from forty men to a hundred and thirty on the heaviest days of 549, and its lighters crossing to the Roman bank from 244 in 527 to 610 in 549. So a letter proposing a transshipment yard was carried in the Roman secretariat for a generation while a Roman officer on the far bank paced and weighed the yard that already existed, and no one joined the two files. The terms sent are still the right terms and the arrangement is the one already working, which is why sending them does no harm. The failure is that Rome was informed by its own watch and did not read itself.

That is the second such failure in this turn and they are the same failure. An article of the same letter accepted a clock already in Roman hands, and the eastern trade office in 549 tasked an operation to discover whether a packet had reached Pataliputra which had been answered in Rome's own hands since 531. Three offices, one habit. It gives a transshipment yard on the Roman bank rather than a joining of the lines, because Rome's single gauge runs unbroken from the Irish sea to the Dnieper and will not be relaid, and because a rail running from one empire onto another's ground is a road for an army. Rome builds the sheds, cranes, and quays at its own cost, wanting the volume of direct rail-to-rail silk more than the argument about who pays. The terms went as the Senate settled them in 525 and were not worked over again before sealing, which is why one article of the letter accepts a clock that has been in Roman hands since it arrived with the last delivery. That article is corrected in the letter itself rather than quietly redrafted, a packet held twenty-five years being a packet whose contents have aged, and the *curatores* are directed to read a standing draft through before it is sent rather than after.

On the west the letter reports a change of position rather than a discovery. Rome's disclosure went at about 500, promptly on the arrival of the western voyage's report in the closing years of that century, and was received: the islands lying some six weeks west of the Canaries, the wind and the current that carry a ship out and will not bring it back, the mainland beyond them, and something of the peoples on it. It went before the generation in which Rome's single message went to Pataliputra, which is why the two do not collide. What the western secretariat carried as prepared and not sent through the last generation was the Dnieper junction reply, which is the document this letter finally sends. Nothing here withdraws a word of the western disclosure. What the letter adds is that Rome now holds the islands, holds a stretch of the mainland coast, has taken the inland centres behind it, and keeps soldiers, colonists, and an administration there, which the Han are told plainly so that they hear it from Rome first.

What the letter refuses is what was never sent. No soundings, no charts, no surveys, no fixed position of any coast, harbour, station, or garrison in the west, and nothing of the strength or defensive state of any Roman holding there. It does not refuse an account of what the land yields, and could not: Rome has been carrying western cacao, cotton, dyestuffs, resins, and fruits to the Dnieper on the standing terms for a generation, so the goods are in Han hands already and a manifest conceals nothing. The distinction Rome is working to is that a road is one thing and a survey of what lies at the end of it is another. The map handed over at the first embassy was the second kind, a complete Roman military survey of roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines given for a sketch without scale, and the rule Rome drew from it governs this letter as it governed the last one.

The letter also withholds the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea, which within this record is the mechanical clock keeping reference time worked with stellar sights through the glass. The letter names the withholding and does not describe the thing withheld, and the difference is the whole of the matter. Rome's own assessment is that a court which has measured the sphere and makes escapements holds both halves of the method and wants only to be told they go together, so a sentence setting out what Rome combines would hand over the combination while appearing to refuse it. Plainness about the fact of a refusal costs Rome nothing. Plainness about its content would be the disclosure. The argument for offering it was Rome's own criterion for disclosure to the Han since the agreements of the late fourth century, that Rome shares what the Han would develop without Rome in any case, on which test Rome has already shared its compass calibration, its type-casting, and its double-wall glass furnace. On that test the disclosure looked cheap, for the reason already given, the Han wanting only the assembly. The *magister classis* and the *curatores* of the naval programme carried the refusal, and the Senate accepted it on two grounds. The first is a difference in kind: a chart says where Rome has been, while a method of fixing position lets a rival cross any ocean and return, repeatedly, without ever being shown a Roman route, which makes refusing charts while handing over the method incoherent. The second is Rome's own record of what the method did. The crossing of the 490s succeeded and came home because the ship carried a compass, a glass, and a good clock and wrote its position down every night for four months. Before that instrument existed Rome worked south down the African coast laying coal depots season by season and lost five ships doing it. The whole of Rome's presence in the west rests on that one technique. The natural-philosophy patrons argued for disclosure and lost, and said so in print.

### The Han Report of a Far Coast, and Two Clocks

A letter came from the Han court in 549 under the standing agreement. On the yard it states the arrangement Rome's watch has been counting for eighteen years, that it opens from the thaw to the first hard frost, that it takes silk, lacquerware and worked metals outbound and wine, oil, glass, tin, coin and horses inbound together with Rome's western goods, for which a dry store was raised in 544, at the tariffs of the standing agreement, which that court does not propose to alter, and that consignments are arranged at the yard season by season with no timetable and no tonnage published. Rome's own weighing on its own bank has that trade at 2,240 tonnes received and 2,760 despatched in 549 with 220 horses, against 940 and 1,180 with 96 head in 527.

The matter of consequence is elsewhere in the letter. The Han state that their ships crossed the eastern ocean and reached the far land, that its coast has since been worked and settled, that the coast is now a frontier of their empire with posts established and the shore marked between them, and that the country behind is entered in their registers under a commandery. They state it as a report under the transparency Rome itself asked for at the settling of the agreements, they propose nothing new, and they ask for the extension of the Dnieper article, which is word beforehand rather than after. They undertake the same toward Rome. They withhold the latitude of the crossing, the winds and currents, and the charts, and they say so plainly rather than let it be found out, noting that Rome kept its own reckoning on the same footing.

Rome weighs this as a court's statement and not as a verified fact, and has no means of testing any part of it. Taken at its word it is the largest change in Rome's picture of the western ocean since the crossing itself, because it puts a third power on the far land, approaching from the ocean on its other side, with Egypt settling its temperate south and Rome holding its Caribbean and its Atlantic rivers. Three powers are now on one land mass by three different roads, and none of them reached it by the same water.

The court adopts the following position, for the answer that goes next. Rome accepts the extension of the notification article, since Rome asked for that transparency and cannot refuse it when it is offered back. Rome names its own western holdings by name and not by position, as it named them in this turn's letter, and asks the same. Rome makes no claim to ground it does not hold, having learned in this turn what a territory clause costs when it outruns the garrison line. And Rome notes that the Han withheld exactly what Rome withheld, in the same terms, which is either a court applying Rome's own rule back to Rome or a court that has read Rome accurately, and either way is a thing to know.

The letter came with two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, with drawings of the escapement and the table relating the length of the pendulum to the beat, conditional on nothing. The naval offices report the beat far more even than the weight-and-escapement work Rome has, and the *curatores* have set them as the reference standard at the observatories and against the works that keep legal and factory time. They will not go to sea. A pendulum needs a still floor and a ship has none, so the marine timekeeper Rome refused to describe in this same exchange is untouched by the gift and remains the instrument the western run is navigated on. The natural-philosophy patrons, who argued for offering the position-fixing method and lost, have put in print that Rome refused to send a method and was sent a better clock for nothing, and that the criterion the *magister classis* beat them with has now been tested once and did not predict this. The *magister classis* has not answered them.

### The Rails, the Channel, and the Ordinary Charge

The frontier charge held at sixty-six per cent of revenue, up from sixty-three at 500, the rise being the fourteen powder mills, the warship building authority, the western transports, and the Yucatan campaign and garrison. The rest bought the following.

Three thousand four hundred miles of new line were laid and 1,900 doubled, at 1.3 million HS a mile on easy ground. The Dacia to Dnieper corridor was doubled throughout and a third track laid on its western half. The second Alpine crossing, costed at 2.9 million a mile, was begun in 542 and opened in 549 at 214 million.

Six purpose-built steam ferries of eight hundred tons, each taking eighteen loaded cars, were put on the Gesoriacum to Dubris crossing of thirty Roman miles between 537 and 544, with rail-height quays at both ends. A legion with its stores crosses in a day and a half against eight to twelve days before. The ferries reinforce a loyal Britain and do nothing against a Britain already gone, a Britain in revolt holding the far quay, so Britain remains the permanent breakaway risk and its fiscal autonomy under the settlement of 362 remains the instrument that manages it.

Maize is established across the African provinces, Sicily, Baetica, and southern Italy at yields the agricultural *curatores* return as half again to twice wheat where the season is long enough, sown on 940,000 iugera in 549. Northern adaptation is not achieved and is expected to take two further generations of selection. The staples are not traded to any peer. The pneumatic tube network was extended to eleven more administrative centres. The chimney and setback legislation of about 500 was enforced and the relocation fund spent, and the printed gazettes were correct that the rules moved the smoke without reducing the coal burned.

### The Labour Settlement of 540

The fourth-century labour law was framed on productivity and had grown inadequate against a workforce that reads, organises by pamphlet, and has had a century of Christian argument about human dignity circulating in it. The settlement of 540 raised the injury compensation schedule, fixed a shorter maximum shift in the deep workings, extended collegium recognition to the fourteen powder mills and the rail yards, and required mine operators to certify shaft timbering to a printed standard. It was argued to the Senate and passed on output and on the risk of a disaffected industrial workforce in a region that can be separated, which is how the risings of 338 to 358 are recorded and how this law is recorded. The Council and the British and Rhine mine operators opposed the certification requirement and lost. The provincial governors lost discretion, a recognised collegium bringing a dispute to a court rather than to the governor, and several said so in print.

### The Establishment Dispute, and Who Was Right

The powder establishment at 550 is 1,150 men in the mills, 2,600 contractors and labourers in collection, 640 men at four proving grounds and the drawing offices, and 2,400 on the hand arms across design, the pattern shops and their proving.

That last figure was 180 as late as 532 and the reordering that produced it should be recorded honestly, because the court did not reason its way to it. Through the 520s the establishment ran better than three to one toward the emplaced piece, and three arguments held it there: that the hand weapon could not absorb men while its three variables could not be separated, which was true; that the emplaced pieces defend the Dnieper line and the river crossings, which is the first item in the court's order of concerns and has been in every century on record; and that the *praefecti* of the frontier districts asked for pieces and did not ask for tubes, and the Senate funds what its officers ask for.

Against those a minority published, and published in print where anyone could read it, that Rome had spent from about 300 to 485 on pressure vessels because pressure was what Rome had mastered and was now spending three to one on the emplaced piece because the emplaced piece was what Rome had mastered, and that if the two errors had the same shape the second would take a century to become visible in the same way the first had. They were right. They did not win the argument, and the establishment did not move until a foreign court sent Rome a drawing in 531, after which it moved within two years and nobody defended the old ratio. What corrected Rome was a gift, not an argument, and the men who made the argument were correct for eleven years while being funded at a tenth of what their case deserved. Four of them were named to the pattern establishment in 534 and one is its senior engineer.

The court records this because the pattern is now twice attested. In both cases Rome persisted in the direction of its own mastery, in both cases the objection was available in print at the time, and in both cases the correction came from outside. Nothing in the present establishment guarantees the third instance will be caught either.

### The Enquiry into the Speed at Alexandria, Reopened on Wire

Eleven men have worked since 534 on why Rome's pneumatic message tubes do not achieve the speed Roman agents reported for the Egyptian offices at Alexandria across the fifth century. The enquiry was not founded on the Maurya claim about a force running in metal, which Rome declined in the previous century on the ground that a partner who had misread steam was not a reliable interpreter of an invisible thing. Sixteen years produced no result, because the enquiry could not specify what it was looking for.

It can specify it now, and the reason is that three separate Roman observations have come in and they are all of wire.

The first is old and was misread. Roman agents across the fifth century noted strips of copper, bronze and gold running along the walls of Egypt's official buildings and between structures and reported them as ornament, a wealthy state's decorative banding. The second is from the Alexandria network in this period: a growing number of major Egyptian steam hulls carry metal frames and wires at the masthead of a kind no rigging requires, which the network reported as an object and correctly declined to guess at. The third is from the Berber gold traders in 548 and 549: poles carrying wire run along the corridor stages of the Saharan routes, guarded, with small manned houses at intervals, which the traders describe as an object and cannot say the use of. Banding on a wall in a city might be ornament. Poles carrying wire across a desert, guarded and manned at intervals, are not ornament, and nothing on a masthead is.

To that Rome adds what the Maurya court sent for nothing in the volume of 530. Egypt gave the Maurya guidance in 495 on the force that runs in metals joined in salt water, counselling that the road to it lies through the study of plants. The Maurya set their botanists to it for thirty-two years and closed the programme without the understanding it was pointed at. What it returned instead was the behaviour of the force in salted plant matter, an effect of metal plating, and a twitching of dead tissue under live current, which their engineers took as a second confirmation of the force arrived at against the design of the programme. The Maurya read the counsel as given to spend their effort where it would not arrive, mark that reading as theirs and possibly wrong, and state that the result is not in question.

Rome treats the reading as the Maurya's and the result as fact, which is the correct division. Two things follow whatever the intent was. The force exists, because a court that spent thirty-two years and every botanist it had came out the far side with three independent effects of it. And the road through plants does not lead to it, which is a route Rome now does not have to walk.

The enquiry is accordingly reopened, renamed for what it is looking for, and funded at 260 men. Its object is the force that runs in metals joined in salt water, and its first three objects are the three the Maurya arrived at by accident, since a thing already produced twice by a peer is a thing Rome can try to produce: metals of two kinds joined in salt water, the effect of metal plating, and the twitching of dead tissue. Rome expects nothing quickly and says so. Rome has no chemistry, no account of what the force is, and no theory that would tell it what to build. What it now has is the knowledge that there is something to find, three sightings of the wire that carries it, one blind road marked as blind, and three effects reported by a court with no reason to invent them.

Rome does not ask Egypt. The one court that has offered guidance on this to anyone offered it to a peer, and that peer spent a reign following it into nothing.

**1. From the throne at Pataliputra. Sent 530; carried by the Mesopotamian road to the Syrian frontier; in Roman hands 531. One printed and sealed volume enclosed.**

Answers Rome's offer of 525 and delivers the complete maker's account of the Chinese hand-firearm, entire, with nothing asked in return. ### Technical assessments

**The Maurya volume.** Translated and set out by 533. Judged complete and unpadded on its own terms, and its three stated warnings judged sound and material rather than hedging. Its range and rate tables are not usable in Rome as written, the powder being different and unmeasurable against Roman powder, and were re-derived on Roman powder by 539. Its drawings of barrel, lock and stock were built to directly. The lock as drawn failed wet in northern service and was redesigned by 541, which the volume cannot be faulted for, an account of a weapon not being an account of a climate. Whether the example represents current Chinese work is not established by anything in the volume and is entered as an open unknown.

**The Han pendulum clocks.** Beat judged far more even than Roman weight-and-escapement work. Set as the reference standard at the observatories and against the works keeping legal and factory time. They will not serve at sea, a pendulum requiring a still floor, so the marine timekeeper is untouched and the western run continues to be navigated on the clock, the glass and the compass as before.

## MESSAGES OUT

**To the court of the Han, delivered at the Dnieper crossing by the frontier legate.**

From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans to the Emperor of the Han and his ministers.

Rome answers late and states so plainly. The matter of the lines at the river was put to Rome in the last generation and Rome's answer was settled then and is only now sent. The fault is Rome's.

1. Rome accepts the joining of the traffic at the river under the standing agreement, which continues of itself unless one party moves to change it, and Rome does not move to change it.

2. Rome does not accept the joining of the rails. Rome's track is of one width from the western sea to the Dnieper, some three thousand miles of it, and Rome will not relay any part of it. Rome proposes a transshipment yard instead: cars from the east come to the eastern bank and stop, Roman cars stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods cross where wheels do not. The reason is the same on both sides. A rail that runs from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army, and neither court would lay the other's track inside its own territory.

3. The yard stands on the Roman bank, built by Rome at Rome's cost, sheds, cranes, and quays, under Roman garrison and Roman law. Factors of the Han working in it are protected as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory under the standing terms. The tariffs of the standing agreement are unchanged by this letter. Rome will begin the works on receipt of word that the yard as described is acceptable.

4. Rome holds the measurement of the sphere of the earth given to it in the last generation and has set it against its own work. Rome returns for it what it has: the phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter, confirmed by Roman glasses, and the printed argument as it now stands among Roman natural philosophers, which is that the old order of the heavens is no longer defended by the men who look through the glass. The observations and the argument are sent with this letter.

5. Rome sends with them two methods of its own making: the lighting of streets and workshops by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains, and the carrying of written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air. Rome will not pretend these are the equal of what it has received.

6. The mechanical clock and escapement of Han making came with the last delivery and has been in Roman hands since, set against Roman work in the naval offices, and Rome gives thanks for it now rather than earlier. This article stood in the draft of 525 as an acceptance of a thing not yet received and was not read again before the packet was sealed. Rome corrects it here rather than mending it quietly, the error being of a piece with answering twenty-five years late.

7. On the making of paper Rome declines with thanks, having made paper from linen and flax fibre for five hundred years and printed from cast type for four hundred.

8. On the west, Rome adds to what it has already sent rather than repeating it. Rome was asked four times in the last century and gave four unsatisfactory answers before setting the matter out properly in the letter Rome sent at about the turn of the century, on the return of the ship that made the crossing: the islands lying some six weeks west of the Canaries, the wind and the current that carry a ship out and will not bring it back, the mainland beyond them, and what Rome then knew of the peoples there. Rome does not withdraw a word of that and would send it again.

What has changed since is possession and not geography. Rome holds the islands. Rome holds a stretch of the mainland coast and has taken the inland centres standing behind it. Rome has soldiers, colonists, and an administration there and intends to keep them there. Rome sets this down so that the Han have it from Rome rather than from a merchant.

9. Beyond that Rome sends nothing further of the kind, and names what it is holding back rather than leaving it to be noticed. Rome does not send soundings, charts, or surveys of any western coast or harbour; it does not send the fixed position of any Roman station, garrison, or holding in the west; and it sends nothing of their strength or their defensive state. Rome makes no secret of what the country yields, the cacao, cotton, dyestuffs, resins, and fruits of it having gone to the Dnieper in Roman hulls on the standing terms for a generation, and the Han may judge the land by what arrives.

Rome also withholds the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea. Rome names that it is withheld and does not describe it, and says why it does not describe it: a sentence setting out how the thing is assembled would be the disclosure itself rather than a refusal of one.

One reason covers all of it, and it is old. At the first embassy Rome handed the Han a full survey of its own roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines in exchange for a sketch without scale, understood the imbalance as soon as it was made, and has governed every exchange since by the rule it drew from that. What Rome sent at the turn of the century it sent under that rule and not against it. A road across an ocean is one thing and a survey of what stands at the end of the road is another, and the second is what Rome keeps. The method of fixing position is more again than a survey, because it opens every ocean rather than one route. Rome expects the Han to read these two articles as Rome would read the same articles arriving from them.

## SPY TASKINGS

**1. Alexandria network, continued.** Standing since the fourth century, one branch silent since the previous turn and quarantined, no agent walked into it. Tasked as in 525: the size and heading of the shipbuilding in the home yards, and any sign of provisioning for a westward voyage. Cover is the ordinary commercial staff of the formal mission and the merchant houses. Run by the office of the mission at Alexandria under the imperial secretariat.

**2. Dnieper frontier watch, continued.** Tasked on the carrying capacity of the eastern rail to the river and the frequency of its arrivals, a supply line to a frontier being a supply line to an army. Run by the frontier legate's staff.

**3. The Maurya, opened 549, mis-tasked and to be re-tasked.** This operation was opened and tasked to discover whether the message of 525 had been received at Pataliputra. The answer had been in Roman hands since 531, when the reply and its volume arrived, and the eastern trade office did not know its own file. That is the office's failure and not the operation's, and it is the third instance of the same failure this turn. Of the two remaining taskings, the yards below Pataliputra and the Syr Darya arsenals both lie three to four thousand kilometres beyond where the factors stand, on roads these factors do not work, and the treaty cover forbids travel, a resident factor who leaves the waystations ceasing to be one. No number of years fixes that. The operation is therefore re-tasked to what it can actually see from the Euphrates: the state and traffic of the Rome road itself, the spread of printed administration through the trading cities, tariff and toll changes at the waystations, and any further state traffic crossing westward. Cover and establishment unchanged. Anything wanted from the Indian interior needs a different instrument and this is not it.

**4. The Berber gold traders of the Saharan routes, opened 548, half re-tasked.** Two seasons have run and the operation has answered what it stands in the right place for and stated plainly what it cannot reach. The question of whether the ships moving north along the Atlantic coast carry gold is struck from this tasking: a caravan trader at a Saharan terminus is not present when a hull is loaded on the ocean shore, has no commercial reason to be, and would be conspicuous if he went, and the question needs eyes on a wharf where this operation has eyes on a route. It is not re-tasked elsewhere this turn, Rome having no instrument on that shore. What stands is the interior: the routes and their stages, the Egyptian posts, tolls and standing buyer at the southern termini, the gold price and the weights moved, and above all the guarded poles carrying wire along the corridor stages, which are now the third Roman sighting of wire and are referred to the reopened enquiry. Volume across two generations is not answerable before the 560s and the horizon stands. Cover is ordinary gold purchasing, which is what Rome has done through these traders for centuries. Run by the office of the *praefectus* of the African provinces on the treasury's account. The commercial relationship is centuries old and its use for intelligence begins now, so nothing is looked for inside a decade.

## RESEARCH

**Powder supply.** 1,150 men in fourteen mills, 2,600 contractors and labourers in collection moving 6,400 tons of scrapings a year, 4,200 deliberate beds. Object is tonnage. Output rises from 214 tons in 549 toward 600 by 570 as the beds laid from 538 reach full yield and 6,000 more are laid. Bed yield is the governing figure and a shortfall in it caps everything below.

**Pieces.** 640 men at four proving grounds and the drawing offices, foundry work charged to the naval and frontier accounts. Pieces in service from 1,544 toward 3,200 by 570. Pieces are not linear in powder, and the hand arms now draw on the same output. At the standing allowance of forty rounds a year, 3,200 pieces take 84 tons in practice and 46 in proving, and 250,000 stand of hand arms take 110, so 240 tons of a 600-ton output goes to the year's firing and 360 to magazine. The magazine is targeted at 5,200 tons by 570, which carries 3,200 pieces in continuous action at eighty rounds a day for four to five weeks against nine days now. Founding capacity is 700 pieces a year and is not the constraint.

**Hand arms.** 2,400 men across design, the pattern shops and their proving. The weapon exists and is in service; the work now is manufacture, the wet-weather lock, and re-deriving what the Maurya volume could not give on Roman powder. Stand in service from 58,000 toward 250,000 by 570, the shops able to double delivery on existing plant. Two open unknowns are carried and neither is closable in Rome: the difference Roman powder makes against Chinese, Rome having never had a grain of the latter to weigh, and whether the example the account was taken from is current Chinese work or an older pattern set aside.

**Barrel metal.** 340 men, jointly with the naval boiler programme. Object is why boiler proportions overestimate what a piece must hold and how thin a tube may safely be made. Expected within twenty-five years: better proportions by trial.

**Screw propulsion and iron hulls.** 900 men. The screw fleet-reliable and standard on new construction between 560 and 580. Heavy rolled iron sections for framing are the gate on the all-iron hull and are the mills' first object; the first of the four hulls laid down from 548 is expected in service 556 to 560.

**Western coal, timber, and the northern yard.** 1,100 men in prospecting parties, survey, the pit, the tramway, and the yard at the river mouth. Object is the extent of the coal basin above the fourth river, the two further indications on the fall line, and four building ways in operation. The yard launched two transports in 549 and is expected to launch eight a year by 565.

**Maize and the western staples.** Agricultural *curatores*, seed distribution and printed method. Northern strains in two further generations, 600 at the earliest. No quantity of engineers shortens it.

**The force that runs in metals joined in salt water.** 260 men, reopened and renamed in 550 from the eleven-man enquiry into the speed at Alexandria, which produced nothing in sixteen years because it could not state its question. It can now. Basis is three Roman sightings of wire, the fifth-century banding at Alexandria formerly read as ornament, the masthead frames and wires on Egyptian steam hulls, and the guarded poles carrying wire along the Saharan corridor stages; and the three effects a peer court reported arriving at against the design of its own programme. First objects are those three effects: metals of two kinds joined in salt water, the effect of metal plating, and the twitching of dead tissue. The road through the study of plants is entered as tried and blind and will not be walked again. Rome expects nothing quickly, has no chemistry and no theory that would say what to build, and does not intend to ask Egypt.

## TERRITORY

**Held.** Unchanged in the old world: Ireland and Britain to the Dnieper, the north of Scotland and the accessible Scandinavian coasts to the old North African provinces.

In the Caribbean: Hispaniola and Cuba entire, with garrison forts, veteran allotments, gold workings, and masonry batteries; Puerto Rico, Jamaica, and the northern outliers of the chain, held on the same footing with garrison posts and allotments. The island garrison of 11,000 and the 22 million charged against it cover these and nothing further.

In Central America: the northern Yucatan peninsula, being nineteen centres taken by assault and fourteen held as tributaries, with four inland forts, eleven fortified posts, 260 Roman miles of military road, and a garrison of 22,000; the fortified coastal station; the lagoon town held as a client under treaty rather than as conquered ground.

In North America: four settled river districts behind the great bay, being the first, second, and third openings and the coal river as the fourth, each held from the mouth to the head of navigation with allotments behind; the coal pit with nine miles of proved face; fourteen miles of tramway to tidewater; a coaling station of 4,000 tons; a shipyard of four ways with naval stores; 522,000 colonists on the rolls in 134,000 allotments.

**Surveyed and not held.** The arc of small islands running south and east below the outliers, charted, not held, and touched at only at its head. The bound is worth stating exactly, because the western null rests on where Roman hulls have been. The inbound run comes west on the trades and makes its landfall at the head of the arc, where two anchorages are used and no others, lying about forty Roman miles apart and both within a day's sail of the track; roughly two voyages in three water at one of them and the rest carry enough to run straight through to Puerto Rico. From those two anchorages the track turns west-north-west for Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, and Cuba, and the arc continues south and east below them for some four hundred and fifty Roman miles into water no Roman transport has any business in and none has entered. No Roman station, depot, or garrison stands anywhere down it, no campaign or submission has been recorded on any of them, none is entered on any tribute roll, and none has yielded gold. Rome has passed across the head of that chain for a generation without troubling to take even the two islands it drinks at. The reason is in the rest of these accounts rather than in any decision: the western effort has gone where ground pays a garrison, which has meant gold on the large islands, worked maize land and a tributary population on the Yucatan plain, and coal, timber, and grain on the northern rivers. The arc has none of the four. It is a watering stop on the way to places that do.

The Yucatan hill country, eleven counted centres, surveyed to 340 Roman miles inland. The mainland coast north and south of the Yucatan station beyond a thousand miles. In North America, the ground above the falls on all four rivers, the fall line to two further coal indications, and the forested mountain wall reached at 140 to 200 miles inland and crossed at three passes with the ground beyond them unfollowed. Florida and the seaboard beyond the allotted districts. The African Atlantic coast to the southern corner, by sighting from one to two miles offshore in the previous turn. The Faroes, Iceland, and the Atlantic islands, with a standing watch on the Canaries and Madeira.
