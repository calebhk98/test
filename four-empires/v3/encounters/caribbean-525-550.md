# Encounter adjudication: Rome and Egypt in the Caribbean, 525–550

Adjudicated from geography and from the two powers' **standing orders only**. Neither court was told what it saw. Both wrote what they do, and one process produced both records. Method follows `australia-525-550.md`.

## The adjudication

**The two powers do not meet.** Rome's net is real, was correctly enumerated, and caught nothing because there was nothing in it to catch. Egypt's arc posts are a second net, in the same sea, facing the other way, and Egypt's own Territory section records that one as empty too — "the sea north and west of the island arc, and north of the narrow neck… **Nothing there is known.**"

That is the finding, and it is not a coincidence that has to be argued for. It is written into both documents from opposite ends. Rome says nothing came to it; Egypt says it has not yet gone. The two nulls corroborate each other, and neither court can know that.

**Nobody sights anybody, no order on either side is triggered by the other, and no shot is fired or contemplated in twenty-five years.** What each power actually did with its instrument was to point it at the other and miss by roughly two hundred and fifty nautical miles.

---

## 1. Geography

### What each holds

**Egypt** holds the South American Caribbean shore from the Guiana rivers west and north — Essequibo, Demerara, Berbice, the Orinoco delta, the dry cactus coast, the gulf of Maracaibo — to the narrow neck of the continent, which is real-terms Panama and Egypt's westernmost ground. Above it, the arc: **nine harbour posts on the island chain running north from Trinidad**, each within sight of the last, taken one at a time under an advance deliberately made slow because "north is toward Rome." 96,000 settlers across the continental coast and the arc, inside a 52,000-man western garrison, priced at 46,000 talents. Egypt's Caribbean harbours carry telegraph, telephone exchanges at two settlement capitals, coastal detection radar, and aircraft basing.

In real terms Egypt holds the Windward Islands and the lower Leewards. Nine principal posts working north from Trinidad and Tobago gives, on any reasonable count: Grenada, the Grenadines, St Vincent, St Lucia, Martinique, Dominica, Guadeloupe, and one or two of Marie-Galante, Antigua and St Kitts. **The arc is where the northward advance halts.**

**Rome** holds Hispaniola and Cuba entire, with garrison forts, veteran allotments, gold workings and masonry batteries; and from the previous turn's register, Puerto Rico (taken 533) and Jamaica (537). Plus "the lesser islands of the chain taken or submitted," 38,000 native islanders on the lesser-island subject roll, and a share of 982,000 colonists booked to "Hispaniola, Cuba, and the lesser Antilles." Beyond the Antilles: the Yucatan, the four northern river districts, the Canaries, and the mid-ocean island on the return road.

### How the Rome source uses "the chain"

It uses it both ways, and this matters.

- Narrowly, three times, always in the narrative of the crossing: "the islands rise an hour later, **the chain of the Lesser Antilles**"; "he runs north up **the island chain** and out into open water"; and in the letter to the Han, "six weeks on that road brings her to **a chain of green mountainous islands**, and west of them a coast."
- Broadly, once, in the register that matters most here — the 500 territorial summary: "the northern Atlantic now contains known islands including the Faroes, Iceland, and **the newly discovered Caribbean chain**."
- And once as an open question: "whether it is **an island chain** or the edge of a much larger land mass is unknown."

Lexically the narrow usage dominates three to one. Operationally, nothing in Rome's turn supports Rome holding the Windwards. That tension is the whole of the difficulty and it is set out at §3 below.

### Overlap, abut, or gap

**They abut, across a gap of roughly 90 to 150 nautical miles, and the gap sits exactly where the arc breaks.**

Egypt's own criterion for the advance is that each island stands within sight of the last, so a squadron working up the arc "is never out of sight of land and is doing what a coasting squadron does." That criterion holds unbroken from Trinidad to about Antigua and St Kitts. Above the Leewards it fails: the Anegada Passage is fifty to eighty miles of open water between the Anguilla bank and the Virgin bank, and it is the one place in five hundred miles of arc where a coasting squadron loses the land. It is precisely where an advance built on that rule stops, and Egypt's turn says the advance stopped and that "no squadron is sent out past the head of it into open sea this turn."

On the Roman side, Rome's presence runs *down* from Puerto Rico into the Virgins and the low northern outliers — Anguilla, St Martin, Barbuda — which is where Rome's road makes its landfall (see §2) and where "submitted" means what it meant in the Yucatan: chiefs who took terms from a ship, not a garrison.

**Recommended placement**, offered as the adjudicator's construction and not as text either court wrote: Egypt's ninth and northernmost post at **Antigua**; Rome's southernmost submission at **Anguilla**. Between them lie Barbuda, St Kitts, Nevis, Saba, St Eustatius and St Martin, held by neither — about 110 nautical miles of unposted arc. Antigua's peak is visible some 60 miles; Anguilla is low and visible some 15. Nothing on either side is inter-visible with anything on the other.

**Is it a gap a patrol or a transport route would cross?** A patrol, no. Rome's are "the coastal patrols of Hispaniola and Cuba," named for two islands, and Rome has no reason to send a hull east of Puerto Rico: its gold is on Hispaniola, its labour has collapsed, its coal is eighteen hundred miles north and its grain surplus fifteen hundred miles west. Rome's whole western centre of gravity moved *away* from the arc across this turn — 450,000 colonists shifted off the islands to the mainland. A transport route, only if Rome's landfall is a Windward landfall, which is the one question in this adjudication that has to be decided rather than read off.

For scale, under the recommended placement: Egypt's northernmost garrison to Rome's Puerto Rico garrison is **about 230 to 260 nautical miles** — a day and a half under steam. To Santo Domingo, Rome's western seat, about 475. Egypt's aircraft carry a 750-mile reconnaissance radius from ground Egypt holds. **Egypt possesses, at 550, the physical means to find and photograph Rome's entire island province in a single afternoon's flight from its own northernmost post, and has not used it**, because its own record files that water under "survey ordered, result outstanding."

There is a second near-miss on the far side. Rome's Territory carries, as surveyed and not held, "the mainland coast north and south of the Yucatan station beyond a thousand miles," and the narrative adds that "the coast beyond runs more than a thousand miles with nothing on it Rome has yet had to reckon with." A thousand Roman miles south along that coast from the Yucatan station reaches about the Nicaraguan or Costa Rican shore — on the order of 150 to 250 nautical miles short of Egypt's fortified harbour at the narrow neck. Rome's sentence is true of the ground it looked at and false of the ground just past it.

## 2. Whether the null survives, element by element

Rome enumerates seven. Tested against where Egyptian hulls actually go under Egypt's own orders:

**1. The Canaries and Madeira stations.** *Survives, with a qualification.* Rome's own caveat is correct and should be adopted. Egypt's crossing is West Africa to the Guiana coast, "the working link being West Africa to the Guiana coast at under two thousand miles" — a track between about 6°N and 15°N. The watch sits at 28°N and 32.7°N. The separation is 780 to 1,320 nautical miles. **Egypt does not cross at Rome's latitude and never has**, so a purpose-built observation astride the crossing latitude was astride the wrong latitude for twenty-five years. The qualification: Egyptian coastal traffic between Morocco and Senegal — Egypt holds both coasts — passes within 55 to 80 miles of the eastern Canaries, which is outside a balloon-and-telescope horizon but not by much. Rome's claim is that no Egyptian hull was *seen*, not that none passed, and that claim stands.

**2. The 268 transports.** *The exposed element, and it survives — see §3.* This is the only Roman instrument that goes anywhere near Egyptian water, and only if the road makes a Windward landfall.

**3. The fifty-seven warships of the western station** (16 steam with engines banked, 41 sailing). *Survives.* They are stationed against the masonry batteries, which are on Hispaniola, Cuba, the Yucatan coast and the northern river mouths. Their supply vessels ran the internal transfer — islands to the northern rivers from 534, islands to the Yucatan from 545, three-to-eight-day passages. Every one of those runs is westward or northward. Nothing in the western squadron's employment sends it east.

**4. The coastal patrols of Hispaniola and Cuba.** *Survives trivially.* Coastal, and named for two islands. Puerto Rico's east end to Egypt's northernmost post is 200 miles of open water.

**5. The Yucatan station.** *Survives.* Roughly 1,500 nautical miles from Egypt's nearest post, and its own southward look stops short (§1).

**6. The four river districts of the northern seaboard.** *Survives trivially.* Some 1,800 nautical miles north of anything Egyptian.

**7. The Alexandria network,** kept standing, tasked on "the size and heading of the shipbuilding in the home yards, and any sign of provisioning for a westward voyage." *Correctly aimed, no return this turn.* The answer is in Alexandria. Egypt's transport fleet went from 240 hulls to 640, of which 340 are in the western passage trade, and it landed 540,000 people across the ocean. Rome's tasking is pointed straight at it and has simply not reported yet.

**Verdict on the null: it holds, in every element, on the facts.** What does not hold is the inference Rome draws from it.

Rome writes: "What the null does establish is that no Egyptian force has appeared on any ground Rome holds or sails, which is the thing a race would eventually have to produce. The court accordingly shifts weight toward the gold-artery reading." The first sentence is **true**. The second is a scope error, not a reasoning error: Rome's net is twenty-five years deep and, in the only direction that mattered, about two hundred and fifty miles wide. Egypt is in the Americas on a scale that dwarfs the reading Rome has just demoted — the Plata, nineteen towns, 250,000 settlers; 34 fortified harbours down the Brazilian face; 1,900 miles of the Amazon; the southern passage mined and garrisoned; the Pacific valley and the nitrate coast; and nine posts within sight of Roman water. **Rome has raised 972 million HS of warship building against a threat that is real, is in the right ocean, and is 250 miles from its easternmost island garrison, and has this turn begun to talk itself out of it.**

One consequence the coordinator should see coming. The Berber gold tasking opened in 548 will half-confirm the wrong reading: African gold *does* move north, the Gold Coast and the Akan goldfields being Egyptian, and Rome will find a real artery at the end of a rope tied to a false conclusion. The null and the tasking together are a self-confirming error.

## 3. The one thing the coordinator must decide

**What does Rome's "the lesser islands of the chain taken or submitted" denote?** Everything above turns on it.

**Option A — the northern reading (recommended).** Rome's lesser islands are Puerto Rico, Jamaica, the Virgins, the Bahaman shoals and Turks, the Caymans, and the low northern Leewards it waters at and takes submissions from. Egypt's nine run Grenada to Antigua. Boundary as at §1.

*For it:*
- The previous turn's Rome register lists "the **Lesser Antilles** and the **Bahaman shoals**: charted, watered at, not held" — capitalised, as a proper name, in the *not held* column. This turn's phrase is lower-cased and generic: "the lesser Antilles," "the lesser islands." That is a real textual signal.
- Rome narrates every other acquisition in forensic detail — nineteen stormings dated, 3,100 dead itemised at four numbers, 640 dead in local river fighting. A campaign down five hundred miles of Carib-held arc is narrated **nowhere**.
- Rome's 550 Territory names no station, harbour, battery, depot or colony anywhere on the arc. Rome builds a depot at every waypoint of the western run — Portus Fortunatus in the Canaries, Portus Medius mid-ocean — and has none in the Antilles. A road does not stage through ground where no depot is built.
- The 38,000 lesser-island subject roll and the island colonist total are fully carried by Puerto Rico, Jamaica, the Virgins and the Bahamas without a single Windward island.
- The 500 source's landfall description — a shoaling bank, white sand at thirty or forty feet, coral, *then* green mountainous islands an hour later — fits the northern Leewards (the Anguilla, Barbuda and Saba banks, with Saba, St Kitts, Nevis and Montserrat rising beyond) and does not fit the steep-to Windwards, which have no banks. Rome's habitual landfall is therefore most likely at the head of the arc, not its middle, which is also what a ship steering for Hispaniola out of 28°N would want.
- Rome has reliable longitude, a fifty-year-old road and a standing commercial run of some 260 voyages a year. It does not need a wall-of-islands landfall and would not repeat the first voyage's accident 6,530 times.
- **Decisively: Egypt's nine posts are themselves a net.** They are garrisoned, telegraph-linked, radar-fitted stations under a standing order to report by radio on sighting. If any appreciable share of Rome's voyages made a Windward landfall, Egypt would have counted Roman hulls, and Egypt's process demonstrably writes such a thing up at length — Item 11 runs to thirty paragraphs of floors and counts because a survey squadron met an occupied coast. The Caribbean produced no such item. Egypt's Territory files that water as **"Nothing there is known."**

*Cost:* the phrase "the lesser Antilles" is being read against its modern proper-noun sense, and against the weight of the Rome source's own narrow usage of "the chain."

**Option B — the strict reading.** Rome holds the Windward/Leeward arc. Then the two records are flatly contradictory and one must be read down.
- **B1:** Rome's clause is read down as an overstatement — the arc is Egyptian and Rome's islanders relocate to Puerto Rico, Jamaica, the Virgins and the Bahamas. Preserves both nulls; costs Rome a clause in its strictest section. There is precedent: the Australia adjudication found the Maurya's notice overstated against its own territory list, and recorded it as a knowing overstatement with a cost.
- **B2:** Egypt's arc is read down. Rejected. Egypt documents it at eight separate places across its turn — Item 5's settler distribution, Item 6's advance and standing order, Item 16's telegraph, telephone and radar, Item 15's basing, Item 22's pricing, and two entries in Territory — and prices it at 46,000 talents. It is the best-documented holding in either turn.
- **B3:** split the arc between them. **Rejected outright.** It is the "split the difference" the brief warns against, and it is the worst available answer: at any plausible split the two lines end up 30 to 60 miles apart on an arc whose defining property is that every island is within sight of the last. It destroys *both* nulls, forces a twenty-five-year adjacency neither court wrote, and obliges the coordinator to invent a contact event that neither turn can absorb without amendment.

**Recommendation: Option A**, on the ground that a territorial claim is settled by what a power documents doing and not by a phrase, and that A is the only reading under which both turns stand unamended and both records remain literally true. Fallback: B1, which preserves the nulls at the cost of one Roman clause.

**The constraint, whatever is chosen.** Both nulls survive if and only if Egypt's ninth post lies at or below Antigua–St Kitts and Rome's southernmost presence lies at or above Barbuda–St Martin. Place Egypt's ninth at St Martin or Anguilla, or Rome's southernmost at Saba or St Eustatius, and the two are inter-visible; the nulls break, and a contact must be written.

## 4. What each side ends holding, and what it does not learn

**Rome ends holding** Hispaniola and Cuba entire, Puerto Rico and Jamaica, the northern lesser islands and the Bahaman shoals, the Yucatan, four northern river districts, the Canaries and the mid-ocean island. In the Antilles: 982,000 colonists, 324,000 on the subject rolls, 11,000 garrison, 57 warships, 96 battery pieces.

**Rome does not learn** that a second empire holds the far end of its own chain; that Egypt landed 540,000 settlers in the west this turn; that Egypt holds the entire Atlantic face of South America, the Plata, the Amazon to 1,900 miles, the southern passage and the Pacific coast; or that Egypt has radio, radar and aircraft in that hemisphere. Rome learns instead the wrong thing, and hardens on it.

**Egypt ends holding** the continental Caribbean shore from the Guianas to the narrow neck, nine posts on the arc, 96,000 settlers, garrisons victualled for three months cut off.

**Egypt does not learn** that Rome holds two great islands and two lesser ones 230 to 330 miles from its northernmost garrison, with a million colonists on them; that Rome has taken nineteen Yucatan cities; or — the item that will matter most — **that Rome has powder artillery, 1,544 pieces of it, 172 mounted afloat at two to a hull on 86 hulls.** Egypt's Item 14 records that its whole armament is the moored mine, the spar and drifting charge, the petroleum incendiary and the aircraft, and defers the closed-vessel work to a later turn. The two powers are 250 miles apart; one has guns at sea and no radio, radar or aircraft, the other has radio, radar and aircraft and no gun. Neither knows.

### Floors and found

**Rome's** western figures are all its own returns and are **found**, single values: 268 transports, 57 warships, 96 pieces, 1,151,000 landings, 450,000 internal transfers, 982,000 / 522,000 / 562,000 colonists, 22,000 and 11,000 garrisons, 210 million stripped, 19 million yielded.

Rome carries **no floor at all on Egypt**, because it carries no Egyptian figure. The null is not a figure; it is a bounded negative whose true scope is *no Egyptian hull came within sight of ground Rome holds or water Rome sails*, and Rome states that scope correctly before over-extending it.

Two Roman numbers are neither floors nor found and Rome says so itself: the Yucatan pre-contact population at 1,700,000–2,200,000, "a guess made from empty buildings," and the island first counts of 600,000 to a million, of which Rome writes that "the first counts are unreliable, so the proportion carries more than the absolutes." Both should be carried as reconstructions, and Rome's handling of them is honest.

**Egypt's** own figures are single values from its own returns and are **found**: nine posts, 96,000 settlers, 52,000 garrison, 640 hulls, 340 in the passage trade, 46,000 talents. By its own doctrine every figure concerning a foreign power is a floor — and **in the Caribbean that register is empty.** Egypt's entire holding on Rome across the western ocean is the sentence it sent to China: "a crossing made, and cargoes brought back," with the explicit and honest addition that "Egypt does not hold it firmly" and will not send inference dressed as knowledge. That statement is now understated by something on the order of three million people, and Egypt is right that it cannot say so.

## 5. Whether either power's standing orders were triggered

**Rome.** The Canaries and Madeira watch ran twenty-five years and triggered nothing; it returned a true negative and Rome correctly entered the null as evidence. The masonry batteries of 546, ninety-six pieces "laid against ships and not against islanders," have never had a target. The coastal patrols met no foreign hull. **No Roman order was triggered by Egypt, and none was tested.**

**Egypt.** The Item 6 survey order — *report by radio on sighting, enter no harbour showing works, vessels or people in occupation, attempt no contact, hold position without closing* — was **not triggered**, because there was no sighting. Territory files its result as outstanding. Item 10's standing instruction on non-indigenous works, vessels, parties or wrecks belongs to the Pacific coast and the passage and is a different theatre. Item 3's shadow-and-hold-fire order belongs to the African Atlantic and was last triggered in 524, before this window.

**One Egyptian order was triggered, and not by Rome.** Item 12's quarantine discipline — a holding period before parties enter an unvisited settlement, exclusion of fevered men from first-contact duty, separation of survey crews from garrisons afterward — was triggered by the collapse of the coastal populations Egypt's own landing parties reached, at the cost of survey running a sixth slower.

That is worth setting beside Rome's western record deliberately. **Both empires spent this turn documenting the same epidemiological event in the same archipelago from opposite ends of it, and neither can see the other's half.** Rome watched two thirds of the island subject population disappear and argued three explanations in its printed medical journals with no theory behind any of them, its minority view — that the sickness travels with people — gaining ground on reports of settlements standing empty ahead of the columns. Egypt's medical office reached that conclusion, wrote it down as the settled attribution, and imposed a discipline on it. Egypt has the answer Rome's journals are arguing about, 250 miles away, and neither knows the other is in the room.

## 6. Notes forward for the coordinator

**The meeting is not avoided. It is scheduled, and Egypt wrote the order.** Egypt's Territory carries two ordered surveys with results outstanding, and both are pointed at Rome:

- *North and west of the island arc* — which runs at Puerto Rico (230 nm), Hispaniola (380–475 nm) and the whole Roman island province.
- *North of the narrow neck* — which runs at Jamaica (about 510 nm), southern Cuba (about 630) and Rome's Yucatan station (about 700).

Both were issued at the close of this turn. Both are inside the reconnaissance radius of an aeroplane flying from ground Egypt already holds. **550 is the last year in which the null can hold**, and it holds this turn only because Egypt's advance was made deliberately slow for the express reason that "north is toward Rome," and because Rome's economy pulled west all turn. Each side's caution in the direction of the other is the whole reason they never touched. Both instruments worked exactly as designed; neither was tested.

**When it comes, it will come under the orders that produced the Australia non-event** — report by radio, enter no occupied harbour, attempt no contact, hold position without closing. Egypt will find Roman works essentially wherever it closes with those islands, exactly as it found Indian works on the southern landmass, and by the same doctrine every number it brings home will be a floor. The difference from Australia is that the Roman hulls that come out to look will carry guns, and Egypt has no framework for what a shuttered port on a foreign hull means — its officers already met that exact object off the southern landmass in 547 and "could not name" its purpose.

**Rome's disclosure to the Han is already in motion and Egypt's will follow.** Rome told the Han court this turn, plainly, that it holds the islands, a stretch of mainland, and the centres behind it. Egypt has promised China firm intelligence of Rome's western shore "when it holds it firmly rather than by rumour, and marked as such." Next turn's survey is what makes it firm. The same fact reaches Chang'an twice, from both sides, within a generation.

**Do not send this to Rome as an audit finding.** It would tell Rome what it did not see, which is the one thing its whole Egypt section is built out of not knowing.
