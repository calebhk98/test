# Packet for the court of the Han

This is everything delivered to the Han court for the period 525–550 AD: correspondence addressed to this court, this court's own intelligence returns, and a note of what was physically handed over in an encounter to which this court was a party.

---

## Documents received

### 1. From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans. Delivered at the Dnieper crossing by the frontier legate. The sending court's record does not fix the year; it falls within 525–550.

> From the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans to the Emperor of the Han and his ministers.
>
> Rome answers late and states so plainly. The matter of the lines at the river was put to Rome in the last generation and Rome's answer was settled then and is only now sent. The fault is Rome's.
>
> 1. Rome accepts the joining of the traffic at the river under the standing agreement, which continues of itself unless one party moves to change it, and Rome does not move to change it.
>
> 2. Rome does not accept the joining of the rails. Rome's track is of one width from the western sea to the Dnieper, some three thousand miles of it, and Rome will not relay any part of it. Rome proposes a transshipment yard instead: cars from the east come to the eastern bank and stop, Roman cars stand on the western bank and take the load, and goods cross where wheels do not. The reason is the same on both sides. A rail that runs from one empire onto the ground of another is a road for an army, and neither court would lay the other's track inside its own territory.
>
> 3. The yard stands on the Roman bank, built by Rome at Rome's cost, sheds, cranes, and quays, under Roman garrison and Roman law. Factors of the Han working in it are protected as Roman merchants are protected in Han territory under the standing terms. The tariffs of the standing agreement are unchanged by this letter. Rome will begin the works on receipt of word that the yard as described is acceptable.
>
> 4. Rome holds the measurement of the sphere of the earth given to it in the last generation and has set it against its own work. Rome returns for it what it has: the phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter, confirmed by Roman glasses, and the printed argument as it now stands among Roman natural philosophers, which is that the old order of the heavens is no longer defended by the men who look through the glass. The observations and the argument are sent with this letter.
>
> 5. Rome sends with them two methods of its own making: the lighting of streets and workshops by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains, and the carrying of written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air. Rome will not pretend these are the equal of what it has received.
>
> 6. The mechanical clock and escapement of Han making came with the last delivery and has been in Roman hands since, set against Roman work in the naval offices, and Rome gives thanks for it now rather than earlier. This article stood in the draft of 525 as an acceptance of a thing not yet received and was not read again before the packet was sealed. Rome corrects it here rather than mending it quietly, the error being of a piece with answering twenty-five years late.
>
> 7. On the making of paper Rome declines with thanks, having made paper from linen and flax fibre for five hundred years and printed from cast type for four hundred.
>
> 8. On the west, Rome adds to what it has already sent rather than repeating it. Rome was asked four times in the last century and gave four unsatisfactory answers before setting the matter out properly in the letter Rome sent at about the turn of the century, on the return of the ship that made the crossing: the islands lying some six weeks west of the Canaries, the wind and the current that carry a ship out and will not bring it back, the mainland beyond them, and what Rome then knew of the peoples there. Rome does not withdraw a word of that and would send it again.
>
> What has changed since is possession and not geography. Rome holds the islands. Rome holds a stretch of the mainland coast and has taken the inland centres standing behind it. Rome has soldiers, colonists, and an administration there and intends to keep them there. Rome sets this down so that the Han have it from Rome rather than from a merchant.
>
> 9. Beyond that Rome sends nothing further of the kind, and names what it is holding back rather than leaving it to be noticed. Rome does not send soundings, charts, or surveys of any western coast or harbour; it does not send the fixed position of any Roman station, garrison, or holding in the west; and it sends nothing of their strength or their defensive state. Rome makes no secret of what the country yields, the cacao, cotton, dyestuffs, resins, and fruits of it having gone to the Dnieper in Roman hulls on the standing terms for a generation, and the Han may judge the land by what arrives.
>
> Rome also withholds the method by which a Roman ship fixes its place at sea. Rome names that it is withheld and does not describe it, and says why it does not describe it: a sentence setting out how the thing is assembled would be the disclosure itself rather than a refusal of one.
>
> One reason covers all of it, and it is old. At the first embassy Rome handed the Han a full survey of its own roads, cities, frontier posts, and supply lines in exchange for a sketch without scale, understood the imbalance as soon as it was made, and has governed every exchange since by the rule it drew from that. What Rome sent at the turn of the century it sent under that rule and not against it. A road across an ocean is one thing and a survey of what stands at the end of the road is another, and the second is what Rome keeps. The method of fixing position is more again than a survey, because it opens every ocean rather than one route. Rome expects the Han to read these two articles as Rome would read the same articles arriving from them.

*Delivered with this letter, as it states: the observations of the phases of Venus and the moving lights near Jupiter with the printed argument; the method of lighting by gas drawn off coal, with the retorts and the mains; the method of carrying written messages between the offices of a city in sealed tubes on driven air.*

### 2. From the council at Alexandria. Sent under the consultation clause. The sending court's record does not fix the year; it falls within 525–550.

> From the council at Alexandria to the court of the Emperor.
>
> Egypt writes under the consultation clause, and on three matters.
>
> First. Egypt has this year used the formal channel to Rome, which is old, on a subject new to it: the Atlantic coast of Africa, which Egypt administers, and the passage of Roman vessels along it. Egypt asked for notice of such passages and asked that vessels with no business there stand clear of its works. Egypt sought nothing else from Rome, was offered nothing, and made no undertaking to Rome touching any other power. China is told of it because Egypt undertook to tell.
>
> Egypt sets down the two things in that letter which are not true, since Rome may repeat either and a thing China hears first from Rome would read as concealment.
>
> The letter tells Rome that the coast has lately come under Egyptian administration. It has not. Egypt has held it for generations, and China is told the true thing.
>
> The letter also tells Rome that what Egypt has arranged with other powers in the matter of that coast is not Rome's business. There is no such arrangement. No power had any hand in how that coast came under Egypt, China no more than any other, and the sentence was put in to give Rome a direction to spend effort on that leads away from Alexandria.
>
> Egypt would rather China had both of these from Egypt.
>
> Second. Egypt promised firm intelligence of Rome's western shore when it held it firmly rather than by rumour, and marked as such. Egypt does not hold it firmly. What Egypt has of Rome across the western ocean remains what it was: a crossing made, and cargoes brought back. Anything further Egypt might now add would be inference, and Egypt undertook not to send inference dressed as knowledge. When it is firm it will come, so marked.
>
> Third. Egypt has kept its full germ and tropical-disease body open to China's scholars, and one thing in it is new and is the reason this letter does not wait. A bark breaks the intermittent fevers. Egypt's physicians have tried it over these years across eleven hundred cases of the fevers as they run in hot country, under the germ practice, and it holds. Preparation and dosage go to China's twenty with this letter and without price. Egypt understands what country lies below China's southern frontier and what has kept China out of it for four hundred years.
>
> Egypt sends the medicine and not the plant, and will not say where it grows. On that Egypt keeps its own practice, as China keeps its own on the making of silk, and Egypt would rather state the reservation than have it found.
>
> Egypt also gives notice, as a courtesy and not as a request, that it sent a survey squadron to the southern landmass in these years, whose approach lies near water under China's protection. It has been withdrawn.
>
> Egypt sets down the orders it carried, since orders of this kind are worth nothing to either party if the other does not know them. It was to make no landing where there were works, vessels or people; to attempt no contact; to signal and hold position without closing on anything it did not expect; and to fire only if fired upon. Those orders were kept. It made one landing, to repair a hull damaged on a reef, on an empty stretch of shore where there were none of those things, and it claimed no ground.
>
> Alexandria.

*Delivered with this letter, as it states: the preparation and dosage of the fever bark, to the twenty scholars in residence.*

## Intelligence returns

Marks throughout: **firm** — the agent saw, handled, counted or heard the thing himself. **Reported** — someone told the agent. **Inferred in the field** — the agent's own supposition, flagged wherever it appears.

---

### Operation 1. The Maurya Realm — western commanderies, continued

**Standing.** Hired Sogdian factors of the Samarkand and Gangetic-road houses, paid by the season and not resident, renewed each season under an arrangement of the last fifty years. Cover is merchant: commercial districts, dockyards, the caravan trades. Self-imposed limit: no approach to military facilities, arsenals or garrisons. The limit was held throughout and is the reason the network is intact; where it bit, it is marked.

**Tasked.** (i) Cannon mounted on hulls and fired at sea, as against hulls merely reinforced and carrying nothing. (ii) Volume of powder produced, judged by saltpetre price and availability in civilian markets and by the size of the works. (iii) Depot, road and magazine work behind the cannon positions on the south bank of the Syr Darya. (iv) Raised in priority this period: whether anything of the electrical force exists as a working apparatus rather than a scholarly claim — what it is made of, what turns it, and whether any of it has been put to a use.

#### I. Guns upon ships — channel: the factor of the eastern river road, at the delta yards

**534, at the fitting quay.** *Firm.* Hulls on the slips and at the quay with square ports cut low in the sides, closed with shutters, four to a side on the one side he could see whole.

**534, on the same quay.** *Reported.* A shipwright's man, unpressed, told him the heavy iron going into those hulls was to stiffen them, that a ship of that build works itself loose in a sea, and that the iron was there for weight and strength and was not a thing fired from the deck.

**542, two mornings in the roads, waiting on a tide.** *Firm.* Firing from a hull standing off, spaced slow; eleven or twelve reports counted the first morning. On the second morning he saw the smoke from her side and saw a piece run out at a port and run back in. He states that this is the same class of hull he had been told carried the iron for stiffening only, and he does not undertake to reconcile the two accounts. He was put off the outer quay by a yard officer that day while barrels were being lighted out to that hull under escort, and went without argument.

**548, on the quay.** *Firm.* An iron piece the length of a man craned down onto a timber carriage set on small solid wheels and run inboard on planks. Three such carriages lying finished on the quay awaiting hulls.

**548, at the river mouth.** *Reported, second-hand, the man drunk at the time.* A ship-master of the southern coast trade said the yards of the far southern coast are busier than the eastern yards and have been for years, and that the ports are cut there too. The factor has not been on that coast.

*Inferred in the field, flagged:* that the guns are not many to a ship, three or four to a side by the ports he counted, and that a yard holding three spare carriages is building for more hulls than it has ready.

**Nil.** The number of the realm's armed hulls, their stations, their standing orders, and anything of any fleet as a fleet. One yard shows one yard.

**Adjudication.** The task is answered and answered correctly. Guns are mounted and are fired at sea. "Reinforced and carrying nothing" is wrong.

#### II. The white salt, its price, and the works — channel: the factor of the Gangetic road

**531, 537 and 545, two markets each time, dry season and after the rains.** *Firm.* He priced the white salt at the great market of the capital's river town and at a market four days up the road. At every visit the price stood within a little of the same, and the same as he remembers it from earlier years. It was there in quantity every day he looked for it, sold in the open, in baskets, by villagers who scrape it off the ground where it comes up white on the fields after the dry weather. The buyers he saw were dyers, a man curing meat, and apothecaries taking small lots.

**537, in that market.** *Reported.* A broker told him there is no forward dealing in the stuff and no house that holds it against a rise, because a man who wants it walks out beyond the town and takes it up himself, and no one has ever grown rich on it.

**537 and 545, on the same road he has ridden for years.** *Firm.* Three walled compounds new to him between one town and the next, set back off the road, with brick kilns burning beside two of them and gangs at work on the walls of the third. He did not turn off the road toward any. Returning, the third stood finished and walled with its gate shut.

**545, at the waystation on that stretch.** *Reported.* Carters said their season's hire had been hauling to such places: charcoal chiefly and in great quantity, sulphur brought up from the south in sacks, timber and cooperage. One had made the same short run six times between the rains and had never once been inside the gate. Another said there were more such places away south on the plateau roads, well off his own run, and had it from his brother.

*Inferred in the field, flagged:* that where a thing lies cheap and unmoving and no house troubles to hold it, there is no great buyer in the country for it; and that he cannot make that agree with the carts and the compounds, and reports both as he found them.

**Nil.** What is made inside any of those walls; the output of any works; any figure of powder whatever.

**Adjudication.** The price half of the task returns something true about the price and nothing usable about the quantity. The size-of-works half succeeds at low precision and is the half that carries the task. The factor's own inability to reconcile the flat price with the carts and the compounds is the most valuable line in his return and should be read as the finding rather than as his confusion.

#### III. The northern road and what stands behind it — channel: the senior factor of the Samarkand house, with a second man on the same road

**536, from the second man, received late.** *Firm.* The mountain crossing no faster than of old, the upper road broken and under work. He says nothing of the grain stores.

**540, from the senior factor.** *Firm.* The mountain road cut wider than he has known it and metalled in the bad stretches, with two new relay houses of fresh stone where there were none, each with water and a walled yard for beasts. His caravan made the crossing in noticeably fewer days than his best previous crossing and lost no animal. Work gangs still on the upper stretch as he passed.

The two returns disagree. Both are set down. They were written four years apart on a road that was under rebuilding between them.

**543, below the passes.** *Firm.* Three towns new on the road, which he gives as his ears took them: Ashvasthala, Girivraja, Uttarakota. Each walled, each with a walled market within it. He traded in two of the three, selling hides and buying nothing worth the carriage. The markets are new-built, the stalls are let by an officer of the town, and a name and a house must be given to take a stall. He gave his and was not questioned further.

**543, behind the passes.** *Firm.* Four great grain stores on his own road, long low buildings with raised floors, two plainly new and two enlarged, with grain going in by cart at a season when he would expect it to be going out.

**543, at one store.** *Reported.* A clerk, taken with drink and glad of company, said the order now is that the store shall be full through the winter and stand full into the next season, where in his father's time it was empty by spring and refilled from below. He gave no measures and was not pressed for any.

**546, at a waystation two days short of the river.** *Firm.* Sitting three days on a lame beast, he counted the carts passing east in daylight: thirty-one, twenty-six, forty-four. The road is fuller than he has ever ridden it, the traffic nearly all one way and nearly all state hire, the beasts branded and the carters paid by an officer at the stage.

**546, at the same stage — the limit line.** *Firm.* Where a made track leaves the road toward the river he turned up it a short way looking for water and was turned back by a mounted man who was civil and did not ask his name. He did not try again.

**546, at that stage.** *Reported.* A carter said the loads off that track go to stone houses standing back behind the bank, that there are more of them now than when he began the run, and that the men who work them are not soldiers of the line but a service of their own that lodges apart. Asked how many, he said he had never counted and would not.

**543, market talk in two of the three new towns.** *Reported, low value.* That the pieces standing on the bank have been added to — said with satisfaction, as men speak of a thing that makes their own town safe. No number the factor would repeat.

*Inferred in the field, flagged:* that the whole of this road is being made ready to carry and feed something larger than it carries now. He notes that the new markets are good for his house and says so plainly, and asks a higher seasonal rate on account of the market officers taking names.

**Nil.** The number of pieces on the bank. The number, contents or capacity of the magazines behind it. Anything inside any arsenal, garrison or work of the frontier establishment. All of it lies past this service's own limit line, and the line held.

**Adjudication.** This is the best-placed of the four tasks and it performed accordingly. Road, relay stations, forward granaries and the new towns are all open commerce on ground that is the factors' own country, and a caravan man's truest instrument — how many days the crossing takes and how many carts he passes — measures exactly what was asked. The guns and the magazines return second-hand, directionally true, and without a number. Any conversion of "the pieces have been added to" into a quantity will be wrong.

#### IV. The electrical force — channels: the Samarkand house's own copper trade, and the Gangetic-road factor on the northwest road

**534, the house's own books.** *Firm as to the trade, nil as to the purpose.* A state buyer has been taking copper in quantity out of the Central Asian workings for a season or more, and takes it to be drawn and not cast — wanted fine and long, in lengths, at the drawing shops of the plain. The Samarkand house handled part of the metal itself. Nobody in that trade could say at that date what it was for.

**542 and 546, on the northwest road out of the capital.** *Firm.* Beside the road, running with it for many days' travel, a raised causeway of new work, drained, carrying a line of glazed earthen pipe laid end to end with the joints wrapped in a black baked cloth. Gangs laying it in 542; the work standing finished in 546. Four small new houses stand along it at intervals. At one point the causeway forks and a branch runs off toward walled ground. He did not follow the branch.

**546, at one of the small houses.** *Reported — one man, one season, not repeated or corroborated.* A station man, glad of company, showed him the inside: rows of earthen jars in a rack, each with plates of two metals standing in it and a liquor in the jar; the spent jars carried out and fresh ones brought up by cart on a set round. Beside the wire a needle hung under a glass, which jumps when the man at the far end works his, and lies still when he stops. The man said the words are spelled out on it as they are spelled on the towers.

*This answers the second limb of the task in the negative and it should be read as an answer.* **Nothing turns it.** There is no wheel, no beast, no water and no mill at any of the four houses. The apparatus is jars, plates and a liquor, consumed and replaced like any stores.

**547, from carters and station people on the same stretch.** *Reported.* That word from the capital reaches the up-road station inside the hour where a rider takes two days; and that in the heaviest rains the line goes dead for some weeks together and the old tower signals carry the traffic until it dries.

*Inferred in the field, flagged:* that the pipe and the causeway are there to keep the wet off the wire, and that the whole is a message line of the same office that keeps the towers, only faster and far dearer.

**Nil.** Where the branch goes and what stands at its end — it runs toward walled ground and the factor did not follow it, correctly under the cover. The metals of the plates and the liquor in the jars, neither of which he could name. The code. Any drawing or measurement of the reader. The length of the trunk beyond "many days' travel," and its far terminus.

**Adjudication.** The task asked whether the force exists as a working apparatus rather than a scholarly claim, what it is made of, what turns it, and whether it has been put to a use. All four limbs are answered, three of them well. It exists and it works. It is made of drawn copper, a baked cloth covering, glazed pipe and a raised causeway, fed from jars of two metals in a liquor. Nothing turns it. It has been put to a use, and the use is carrying spelled words faster than a rider, with a seasonal failure in the wet the realm has not solved. The negative on "what turns it" is an answer and must not be filed as a gap.

#### Network risk

What changed on the ground these factors work: the three new garrison towns brought market supervisors and toll clerks onto what had been open road, and a stall is now let by an officer against a name and a house. A Sogdian asking after stores off the road is noticed and remembered where he was not before. One factor was turned back from a track toward the river; one was put off a quay. Both were handled civilly and neither was asked his name.

The decisive protection was the self-imposed limit line, which kept the factors off arsenal, garrison and military ground entirely.

**Assessment: network intact, uncompromised, renewable.** Expect a slightly more watched trade in the new frontier markets and one raised seasonal rate.

### Operation 2. Egypt — the scholar exchange, twenty in residence, eyes only

No active collection attempted, by this court's own instruction. The representatives are held irreplaceable and a failed attempt would forfeit the window. What follows is what a scholar in residence under an official exchange simply saw.

#### Observed in the ordinary course

**D1. Egypt kept the arrangement in full.** Twenty scholars a decade sat inside Egyptian institutions and the germ and tropical-disease body was open to them at full extent and on no schedule. Nothing was quietly narrowed. *(Firm.)*

**D2. The fever bark passed whole — the medicine, its preparation and its dosage — together with the case series behind it.** Eleven hundred cases run under the germ practice across three separate tropical regions. The scholars had the method as well as the remedy: how Egypt designs a trial, how it records it, and what it counts as a result. That method is arguably worth more than the bark. *(Firm — shown and handled.)*

**D3. Egypt stated its reservation rather than concealing it.** The scholars were told, and the court was told by letter, that the plant and its country would not be given. *(Firm.)*

**D4. Egypt had put this court's own gifts into service within a few years of receiving them** — the mirror-principle instrument working from 541, pendulum regulation in the Alexandrian clock shops from 538. A visiting scholar sees his own court's contribution in Egyptian hands and working. *(Firm.)*

**D5. Ambient Alexandria, over twenty-five years of residence.** Arc light where flame used to be, including at the Pharos. Wire on poles through the city and out of it. Hand-connected voice instruments in the administrative offices. Paper as the ordinary substrate. Printing everywhere in official use. Coffee houses as fixtures of the commercial districts. Aluminum and plated goods for sale. Tall masts with wires at the harbour, guarded. *(Firm as to all of it, being unconcealable in a city a man lives in. Nil as to how any of it works.)*

**D6. Settler families embarking from Alexandria in numbers, with livestock and tools, every sailing season, year after year.** A resident scholar walking past the harbour cannot fail to see it. *(Firm.)*

**D7. Egypt's letter to this court disclosed the two falsehoods it had put into its note to Rome, disclosed the survey squadron sent toward the southern landmass along with its standing orders, and named the bark reservation — and said nothing whatever of any Egyptian holding across the western ocean.** *(Firm — the document is in this court's hands. The omission is a fact about the document; what to make of it beside D6 is the court's own work.)*

#### Kept away from them

- **Nothing outside the access the arrangement defines** — the germ and tropical-disease body together with the published optics and mathematics — was obtained, and the defined access was not exceeded.
- **The bark's source**, as at D3 — disclosed as withheld rather than hidden, which is a different thing and should be recorded as such.

**Assessment.** The window was not spent and was not risked, which was the instruction. What it yielded is real, large, and entirely lawful: a working medicine for the disease that has held the southern frontier for four centuries, plus the method behind it, plus twenty-five years of a man simply living in the richest city in the world.

## Handed over in person

Nothing. The Han court was not a party to any adjudicated encounter in this period, and no object, document or material was physically handed over to or by it in one.

Items delivered as enclosures to correspondence — Rome's observations and printed argument, the gas-lighting and pneumatic-tube methods, and Egypt's preparation and dosage of the fever bark — are listed under *Documents received* above.
