# Packet for the council at Alexandria

This is everything delivered to Egypt for the period 525–550 AD: correspondence addressed to this court, this court's own intelligence returns, and a note of anything handed to or by this court in person.

---

## Documents received

### 1. From the throne at Pataliputra. Sent in the year reckoned 543, by sea through Egypt's own waters.

> From the throne at Pataliputra to the throne of Egypt.
>
> This notice concerns the great dry southern continent that lies beyond the eastern islands, and the islands to its north and to its south. It asks nothing and offers nothing.
>
> That continent was found, and its northwest coast charted, by a survey expedition of this realm in the years reckoned 415 to 420. A depot with shipwrights was placed upon that northwest coast in about 478 and has been kept there since. Its coasts are settled, administered, and taxed by this realm, are garrisoned along their length, and are governed under this realm's law from seats established upon the continent itself. The great island to that continent's north and the cold island to its south are held by this realm also.
>
> Egypt has known of that continent since this realm told it, in the exchange of about the year 462, when the charts of the eastern seas, the harbour notes, and the continent's location were given, and the account of what the copper lines along Alexandria's walls are for was received. That bargain is raised here not as a complaint but to record that Egypt learned of the ground from this realm, that this realm was upon it first, and that the dates above may be tested against Egypt's own memory of the exchange.
>
> One matter of practice. The harbours of this realm upon that continent and its islands are open to Egypt's ships for trade on the same terms as the ships of any other power, and will grant no base, no garrison right, and no ground. That rule is adopted because it was applied to this realm at Aden and along the whole length of the East African coast for five hundred years, from the first meeting of the two powers at that strait, and because five hundred years of being turned from those harbours showed this court that the rule works and keeps a coast in one hand. No grievance is raised that requires an answer, and none is expected.

### 2. From the court of the Han. Sent 537, under the scholar exchange.

> The knowledge of fevers and of the barks and plants that answer them is in use. Sixty physicians trained on it are posted to the southern provinces and the highland frontier, and a record is kept of which remedy answers which fever and in what quantity. Your practice for closing a port against a sickness coming up a trade road was given in full and has been applied: the quarantine rule for arriving vessels was widened in 534 to cover every ship returning from a long voyage, the northern and eastern squadrons' own hulls included.
>
> Three things are asked. First, that physicians of your own be received in residence for a term of years at the southern stations rather than at the capital only, so that they may see the fevers in the country where the fevers are. Second, your practice for keeping a ship's company in health on a voyage of many months, in respect of water, provisions and the sickness of the gums. Third, a matter of the glasshouse. This court's workmen grind to the standards your instructors set, and the difficulty that remains to them is of size. A small piece comes out sound and a large one cools faster at its face than at its heart, so that it cracks, or holds a strain that shows later, or keeps its bubbles. This court would value your practice for the slow cooling of a large piece: how the chamber is arranged, over how many days the heat is let down, and by what sign a piece is judged to have come through sound.
>
> Offered with them, and not as their price, is the practice of the sealed deposit note, by which coin received at one office is paid against a stamped certificate at another and the coin itself does not travel, with the forms, the seals and the rules by which the offices settle between themselves. It has carried this court's remittances and much of its merchants' trade for three centuries, and it has not been sent to your court before. Two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum go with this letter as a courtesy to your workshops rather than as consideration, the regulation itself having been given to your court in the last generation and this court not proposing to send the same knowledge twice.
>
> This court has nothing of equal weight to set against what it asks. It has looked at what has passed each way and does not pretend the account is level. It asks anyway, on the strength of the exchange itself.

*Delivered with this letter, as it states: the practice of the sealed deposit note with its forms, seals and rules; two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum.*

## Intelligence returns

**Egypt against Rome. Commercial cover through the trade-corridor stations, reporting as a sideline to genuine trade. No electrical apparatus carried. Run by the directorate through recruited-insider and embedded Greek-Egyptian channels in combination, corroborated across two of three before reaching the council.**

Channels used: **(A)** recruited insiders among Roman lading, naval and fleet-account clerks in the Atlantic ports; **(B)** the resident Greek-Egyptian commercial houses at Gades, Olisipo and Ostia, generations old; **(C)** the station in the Roman African provinces, working the gold and caravan trades.

Marks: **firm** — the agent or the watch saw, counted, paced or handled the thing. **Reported** — someone told the agent. **Inferred** — reasoned in the field.

On the second tasking, a note on where the answer lives: it is not a technical secret. It is a fifty-year grievance belonging to the Atlantic ports whose men died, and the evidence that overturned the old account arrived home in three ships with their crews. Grievances of that kind live on quaysides, at underwriters' tables and in printed sheets, and Rome runs a dense commercial press in which the losing side of an argument prints its case.

### Collected

**1. The warship authority. 531 onward. (B), corroborated by (A). Firm.**
From 531 the Roman yards lay down six warship hulls a year under a standing authority, and did so in eighteen of the twenty years. Hulls on the ways were counted directly in the Atlantic and Mediterranean yards across the period. (A) confirms the charge stands on the fleet account and is the largest single new line in it.

**2. The western transports. 534 onward. (B). Firm.**
From 534 the Atlantic yards at Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala and the Severn and Clyde build ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons for the western run and nothing else. Counted on the slips and in the port registers across the period.

**3. The western sailing is a settlement programme. 526–550. (B). Firm at the quay, reported as to scale.**
Emigrant embarkations were counted at the Iberian quays every season of the period. Two round voyages in three years to a hull, outward from the Canaries, homeward on a northern track. The people going out are veterans with families and their gear, not garrisons.

**4. The standing watch on the Canaries and Madeira. From about 532; best returns in the 540s. (B), through the watch's own crews and the men who supply and relieve them. Existence firm; standing order reported; the null reported.**
Watchers set with glasses and a tethered balloon flown from high ground. Their standing order is to count large hulls passing north and west, and captains on the western run are instructed to report any such hull, its heading and its size, on making port. It is not concealed; the clerks speak of it as routine. The watch's own people say that in twenty-five years they have counted none.

**5. Ordnance going west for works against ships. 546–550. (A), handled. Firm.**
Cut stone and heavy pieces shipped in the western transports for harbour works on the far side, entered on the ladings across several seasons. The clerk's own words, asked why so much for a country of people who work no metal, were that they are laid for ships and not for islanders.

**6. The five lost ships. 526–550, thickening after the southern survey's crews came home. (B) and (A), and in print. Reported, and corroborated across both channels.**
The reef-and-current account is dead on the Atlantic runs. What the returned crews describe — a built coast, harbour works, towers throwing mirrored light, towns of cut stone one after another, foundry smoke on the waterfronts, warships lying among cargo hulls, and a single vessel that kept company the whole length of the voyage in relay and never closed — is repeated everywhere, and the conclusion drawn from it is repeated with it: the coast has masters and the masters are ours. (A) hears the same said in the naval offices without argument, as a matter no longer discussed, and adds one thing the quayside does not, that there is real bitterness in it because the account Rome believed for a century is now said to have been given to Rome deliberately and to have been false. (B) gives the hardest measure of how far the belief has set: no shipowner will put a hull down that coast at any charter and the underwriters will not price the risk.

**7. A change in Roman questions in the caravan trade. 548–550. (C). Reported, thin — two returns.**
From 548 the Roman gold-buying houses of the African provinces, an old and ordinary trade, began putting new questions to the Berber caravan factors: where the interior routes now run, what volume they carry, and whether that volume has changed in two generations. Nothing beyond the change of questions was obtained, the practice being two years old.

**8. Collateral, outside the tasking and unmissable. 540–550. (A) and printed Roman law. Firm.**
Rome has a state powder industry. A labour settlement passed in 540 extended collegium recognition to fourteen powder mills, naming them, in published law; the mills stand on the Rhine, in the British coal district, in Dacia, Campania, Noricum, Baetica and on the Danube. From 544 Roman warships in port carry mounted pieces. This was not sought and could not have been avoided by any network standing in Roman ports.

### What was attempted and failed

**The secretariat and the Senate's deliberation.** An approach toward the papers of the western secretariat, and toward men who handle them, returned nothing. This is a placement failure and not a tradecraft failure: the commercial network stands in ports, and the papers do not leave the office. No member of that office was recruitable inside the window.

**The northern military-technical placement.** Not tasked, not approached, not implicated. It could not have answered either question in any case; it stands in the northern industrial provinces and sees yards, ordnance and metal, not court opinion. Recorded so that it is known it was neither used nor risked.

### Nil returns

- **No Roman document, printed or handled, states a conclusion about Egypt's presence in the western ocean.** A nil against a well-framed question.
- **No change in the Roman court's disposition or emphasis on the question was detected.** A change of emphasis inside an open file has no observable signature and produces no order, no building and no printed argument of its own. Nil.
- **No Roman military or naval preparation directed at the African Atlantic coast, in twenty-five years.** Firm as a nil: no depot laid, no coal stocked south of the Moroccan holdings, no charter offered, no probe fitted out, no hull sent. (C) watched for exactly this.
- **No Roman démarche to Egypt on the lost ships or on the coast, in twenty-five years.** This belongs to the formal mission and not to this operation, and is entered here only so the two are not confused in the council's reading.

### Assessment against the second tasking

Yes, hardened, and attached exactly where the tasking asks — not to a hazard of water but to the works, the towers, the garrisons and the power that raised them. This return is firm-edged and corroborated. It is also the return the council will find least comfortable, since it establishes that a century of Egyptian management of Roman belief has been undone and is known to have been management.

**Network status.** Intact, undetected, cover unimpaired. Every item is sourced to a quayside, a customs house, an underwriters' table, a lading book or a printed sheet.

## Handed over in person

**Off the southern landmass, 547 and again in 549.** A sealed instrument was handed across to Egyptian officers at sea and is in this court's hands: struck at a press and sealed, in three tongues, Greek, Latin and the script of India. Nothing else was passed in either direction on either occasion, and nothing was fired.

Nothing else was handed to or by Egypt in person anywhere in the period.
