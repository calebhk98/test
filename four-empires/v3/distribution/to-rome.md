# Packet for the Senate and the Emperor of the Romans

This is everything delivered to Rome for the period 525–550 AD: correspondence addressed to this court, this court's own intelligence returns, and a note of anything handed to or by this court in person.

---

## Documents received

### 1. From the throne at Pataliputra. Sent in the year reckoned 530, by the Mesopotamian road to the Syrian frontier, with one printed and sealed volume.

> From the throne at Pataliputra to the throne of Rome.
>
> In the year reckoned 501 this realm asked Rome for the working method of an engine that moves a hull, and offered in exchange the complete account of the hand-weapon carried by China's soldiers. Rome set out its own half in full before receiving anything, and required one delivery and nothing less. The volume accompanying this letter is that delivery, complete. Nothing is asked in return for it.
>
> The delay was twenty-nine years and the reason does not flatter this realm. At the time of the offer this court held the men who survived that weapon at the island of Tidore. Those men could describe being shot at. They could not describe how the thing is built so that another maker may copy it, which was the substance of what Rome required. A witness's account had been offered where a maker's account was needed, and the difference was not understood at Pataliputra until Rome's terms arrived. An example of the weapon was then sought. That took the better part of two decades and cost more than is set down here. It reached the engineers of this realm in the year reckoned 524 and was still on the bench when the present ruler came to the throne. It was measured and drawn across the two years following. Barrels were then cast to its dimensions and fired through two years more, that proving being the difference between a description of a weapon and an account a maker can build from. Each such barrel was checked against the example itself, part offered up to part, the example being in the room. The account was written in the year reckoned 528 and struck and sealed in 529.
>
> The volume gives, under separate heads: the weight and the length; the manner of carrying and of holding; the construction part by part, with barrel, lock, and stock drawn and measured, and the metals and their treatment so far as the metalworkers of this realm could determine them; the means of ignition; the loading sequence; the assessed killing range and the range at which aim fails; the time between shots for a trained man with his charges made ready; the effect observed on timber, on stone, and on men; and the full reasoning of the engineers.
>
> Three plain warnings about the figures, all set down in the volume beside the figures they touch.
>
> The example arrived with no powder. Every figure of range and of rate of fire was got by firing this realm's own powder in that barrel, and this realm's powder is made from the saltpetre of its own plains to its own proportions. Range and rate are the two figures most moved by the charge. A different powder would change both, and by how much the engineers of this realm cannot say, having never had a grain of China's to weigh against their own.
>
> The example is one piece and could not bear the firing the observed effects required. A small number of proving shots were fired with the example itself, and the effects upon timber, upon stone, and upon men were observed with barrels cast in this realm's shops to the example's own dimensions. Every figure is marked according to which piece produced it. Those copies were made to the Chinese pattern and are not the weapon this realm has since built for its own soldiers, which the volume does not describe.
>
> The two points on which the engineers of this realm remain uncertain, and say so in the volume, are these. The first is the powder, as above. The second is whether the example represents what China now makes. It came out of material an arsenal had set aside as unserviceable, and a piece so set aside may be a current pattern discarded for a fault or an older pattern discarded for being old. Nothing about the piece settles which. If Rome finds elsewhere a weapon of this class that differs from the drawings, the difference may lie there and not in any error of the measuring.
>
> There is no padding in the volume. Where a thing is not known, it states that it is not known.
>
> Two further matters, given for nothing.
>
> First, this realm has since built a weapon of the same class by its own casting work, and has had such weapons in its soldiers' hands since the year reckoned 518. In about 478 this court answered Rome's questions upon that weapon with what the survivors of Tidore could say and what its engineers reasoned of its reach and its loading, which was thin, and it has sent no word since. Rome should have this from this letter rather than from another quarter.
>
> Second, a warning, the two courts having traded what each sees of Egypt for a hundred and fifty years. In the year reckoned 495 Egypt gave this realm guidance on the force that runs in metals joined in salt water, the force by which Egypt binds its administration through copper lines. Egypt's counsel was that the road to it lies through the study of plants, and that this realm should look for the force in its trees and grasses and grain. The counsel was taken gratefully, every botanist of the medical tradition was set to it, and the programme ran the whole twenty-five years of the last reign and was closed in the third year of this one, thirty-two years from the giving of the counsel. It never produced the understanding of the force it was pointed at. It did return other things: the behaviour of the force in salted plant matter, an effect of metal plating, and a twitching of dead tissue under live current which served this realm as a second confirmation of the force from another angle, arrived at against the design of the programme. None of that was the thing sought, and the road Egypt named did not lead to it. This court judges the counsel to have been given in order to spend the effort of this realm where it would not arrive. The purpose is this court's reading and may be wrong. The result is not in question. If Egypt has ever offered Rome guidance of that shape, this is what it was worth here.
>
> Nothing is asked of Rome in this letter.

*Delivered with this letter: one printed and sealed volume, being the maker's account described above.*

### 2. From the court of the Han. Sent 549, under the standing Han Rome Agreement.

> The rail from the interior to the frontier at the Dnieper has been in operation for a generation, and the transfer yard this court built on the eastern bank has stood and worked these eighteen years, with covered store, weighing house, standing labour, and dry store added since your western goods began coming up the road.
>
> The yard is open through the season in which the ground is passable, which in most years runs from the thaw to the first hard frost. It takes silk, lacquerware, worked steel and worked bronze outbound, and wine, oil, glass, tin, coin and horses inbound, together with the goods of your western shore now carried to the Dnieper on the standing terms, the bitter seed, the fine cotton, the fruits, resins and dyes, for which the yard has built separate dry store, at the tariffs of the standing agreement, which this court does not propose to alter. This court publishes no timetable and no tonnage. Consignments are to be arranged at the yard itself, season by season, between the yard's officers and whichever officer your court appoints to it, under the dispute officials the agreement already provides for, and any quantity your merchants wish to move in a season can be answered there.
>
> One matter is reported, on the standing transparency your court asked for when the agreements were settled. Ships of this court crossed the eastern ocean and reached the far land, and its coast has since been worked and settled. That coast is now a frontier of this empire. Posts are established along it and the shore is marked between them, and the country behind is entered in the registers under a commandery of this empire as the northern forests have been entered for four centuries.
>
> On what follows from that, this court proposes nothing new and only the extension of what the two courts already keep. The agreement at the Dnieper rests on a boundary named plainly by both and on each telling the other before it crosses in force, and that arrangement has held for two lifetimes because it was made before either court had cause to quarrel over the ground. The same is offered here. This court names the coast it holds so that it is named before there is anything to dispute, and asks only the courtesy the Dnieper article already provides, which is word beforehand rather than after. It says the same of itself: your court will hear from this one before any ship of this court goes where your own is settled.
>
> It will keep to itself the latitude at which the crossing is made, the winds and currents by which a ship goes out and comes home, and the charts, as your own court kept its reckoning and said so plainly rather than let it be discovered. It is reported because it was told to you as an intention and has become a fact.
>
> Sent as a gift are two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, which keep time more evenly than the weight and escapement clocks previously sent, with drawings of the escapement and the table relating the length of the pendulum to the beat. They are for the use of your scholars and your yards and are conditional on nothing.

*Delivered with this letter, as it states: two clocks regulated by a swinging pendulum, with drawings of the escapement and the table relating the length of the pendulum to the beat.*

### 3. From the council at Alexandria. Sent through the formal channel. The sending court's record does not fix the year; it falls within 525–550.

> From the council at Alexandria to the government at Rome.
>
> The Atlantic coast of Africa has lately come under Egyptian administration.
>
> Rome is asked to give notice of the passage of its vessels along that coast before such passages are made.
>
> Vessels with no commercial business on that coast are to keep to open water and stand clear of Egyptian harbours, works and signal stations.
>
> What Egypt has arranged with other powers in the matter is not Rome's business, and Egypt will not discuss it, nor the extent of its holdings, nor its routes, nor the disposition of its fleet.
>
> Alexandria.

## Intelligence returns

Marks throughout: **firm** — the agent or the watch saw, counted, paced, bought or handled the thing. **Reported** — someone told the agent. **Inferred in the field** — the agent's own supposition, flagged where it appears.

---

### Operation 1. The Alexandria network, continued

Cover: ordinary commercial staff of the formal Roman mission and the merchant houses. Run by the mission office under the imperial secretariat. One branch silent since the previous turn, quarantined.

**Tasking read.** "Size and heading of the shipbuilding in the home yards" is taken as scale and what the building is pointed at. "Any sign of provisioning for a westward voyage" is taken literally.

#### Collected

**A1. The quarantined branch produced nothing and was not touched.** No agent was walked into it across the twenty-five years. No product, no loss, no exposure. Recorded as a deliberate nil. *(Firm — negative, the mission's own file.)*

**A2. Transport hull construction at Alexandria and the Delta yards rose markedly and steadily across the period.** The merchant houses supply cordage, sailcloth, pitch and ships' ironwork to these yards and count what they supply and what they see on the stocks. The count in the 520s was a handful of hulls a year at the commercial yards; by the 540s the network was counting on the order of a dozen to eighteen hulls a year launched or fitting out at Alexandria and the Delta together, of a build that is broad, deep and cargo-carrying rather than a warship's. *(Firm as to the local count. See the caveat at A9 — this is a share of a total the network cannot reach.)*

**A3. The building is pointed at carriage, not at battle.** Of the hulls the network could put a hand on at the commercial quays, the great majority were transports and river craft. *(Firm.)*

**A4. Major steam hulls lying in the Alexandria roads carry metal plating on their sides, and the newest carry it as built rather than as patched.** *(Firm — seen from the water, over years.)*

**A5. Some major hulls carry metal frames and wires at the masthead of a kind no rigging requires.** The number carrying them grew across the period. The network cannot say what they are, and did not guess. *(Firm as to the object. Nil as to function — see the nil below.)*

**A6. Egypt is moving settlers out of Alexandria and the Delta ports continuously, in families, with livestock and tools, on subsidised passage, and has done so every sailing season across the whole twenty-five years.** This is not concealed and is not concealable: the instrument is the old Madagascar one, offered publicly — land grants surveyed and registered at the destination, exemption from the land tax for fifteen years, subsidised passage for a family with its stock and tools, and harbour works, roads and cisterns built in advance of arrival. The terms were had from the recruiters and from the registry, being matter of public record. *(Firm as to the instrument and its terms. Firm as to the fact and continuity of the outflow.)*

**A7. Those transports sail west out of Alexandria and do not pass into the canal.** They are gone for long periods and a proportion do not come back at all. *(Firm — sailing observation in the roads and at the canal mouth.)*

**A8. Harbour talk from returning Egyptian crews, gathered over years and consistent between tellers: a crossing of the western ocean; a temperate country of level treeless grass on black soil with two harbours facing each other across a great brown estuary a hundred miles wide, where wheat and cattle take at once and horses have run wild; and separately a hot coast of reefs, mangrove and rain forest with river mouths.** Towns are named in the talk. *(Reported. Consistent across sources and across years, which is why it is entered, but it is sailors' talk and not a document.)*

**A9. The network could not aggregate an empire-wide shipbuilding rate.** Egyptian hulls are built at yards on the East African coast beside the hardwood, on the Red Sea, and in the African interior on the great rivers, none of which the Alexandria network reaches. Any figure it returns is a share of an unknown whole. This is stated so that a local count is not mistaken for a national one.

**A10. Grain: Egyptian grain leaving Alexandria did not fall across the period despite the city and the country plainly carrying more people.** Grain factors attribute it to provinces that formerly drew Nile grain now feeding themselves. *(Firm as to volumes handled; reported as to the reason.)*

**A11. Aluminum, plated goods and precision instruments continue to be offered for sale in Alexandria at monopoly prices, and the aluminum in quantities larger than the metal has previously been seen in.** *(Firm — purchased.)*

#### Attempted and failed, or nil

- **Warship strength, build rate, refit cycle, fleet disposition.** Nil. The naval yard has been closed to foreign eyes since the relief mission of 365–368 was admitted to the port and refused the secure yard. The network can count hulls in the roads on a given day and nothing more.
- **What the masthead frames at A5 are.** Nil. A capability cannot be read off an aerial by a man on a quay, and the network correctly did not try.
- **The route of the westward voyage — courses, distances, landfall, the depot chain.** Nil. Egypt's sailing directions have been kept under guard for four centuries. Nothing was obtained beyond A8.
- **Any settler movement shipping from harbours other than Alexandria and the Delta.** Nil. The network's counts cover Alexandria and the Delta ports only, as at A9, and it can see nothing of embarkation anywhere else.
- **Anything of Egypt's industry, treasury or interior works.** Nil across the board. Nothing in this operation's product reaches past the port, its quays and its registry.
- **Egypt's diplomatic intent behind the note on the Atlantic coast of Africa.** Not attempted, and correctly so — a commercial staff cannot report a council's reasoning. The mission received the note itself through the formal channel; that is diplomatic receipt, not collection, and is not entered here.

**Assessment of the operation.** Well placed, asking questions answerable from where it stands, and it succeeded on both limbs of its tasking. Not detected.

### Operation 2. The Berber gold traders of the Saharan routes, new from 548

Cover: ordinary gold purchasing, a centuries-old commercial relationship. Run by the office of the *praefectus* of the African provinces. Intelligence use begins 548. Rome states it looks for nothing inside a decade.

**Two seasons of running, 548 and 549, with the 549 product reaching the praefecture in 550.** The stated horizon is correct: almost nothing is available yet, and what is available is the traders' own book.

#### Collected

**B1. The routes these particular traders walk, their stages, their waters and their termini at both ends.** This is their trade and they can describe it exactly. *(Firm.)*

**B2. The southern end of every route they use lies under Egyptian administration, with Egyptian posts, an assessed toll taken once, and a standing Egyptian buyer at the entrepôts.** *(Firm — they pay the toll and sell to the buyer.)*

**B3. Poles carrying wire run along the corridor stages, guarded, with small manned houses at intervals.** The traders describe the object and cannot say what it is for. *(Firm as to the object.)*

**B4. The price at which gold was offered to them at the northern termini in 548 and 549, and the weight their own houses moved in those two seasons.** *(Firm — their own book.)*

**B5. Elders' account that the volume moving on these routes has not greatly changed within memory, and that the trade has run under Egyptian arrangement for longer than any of them has been trading.** *(Reported, and soft. Two seasons cannot test it and it should not be treated as more than a starting hypothesis.)*

#### Attempted and failed, or nil

- **Whether the cargo of the loaded ships counted moving north along the Atlantic coast is gold.** **Nil, and unanswerable from this operation's standing.** A caravan trader in the interior and at the Saharan termini is not present when a hull is loaded on the Atlantic shore, has no commercial reason to be, and would be conspicuous if he went. The question requires eyes on a wharf and this operation has eyes on a route. It should be re-tasked to a maritime operation or dropped.
- **Whether volume has changed in two generations.** Nil beyond B5. A two-season-old operation cannot measure a two-generation trend, and the traders' own books do not run that far. This becomes answerable, if at all, in the 560s or 570s.
- **Egyptian gold output.** Nil. Output at the Akan and Nubian and Zimbabwe workings is a matter of the Egyptian mine directorate's returns and is not visible from a trade route.
- **Anything of Egyptian shipping, the Atlantic coast, the crossing, or the western holdings.** Nil, and outside the operation's reach entirely.

**Assessment of the operation.** Sound cover, genuine access, and a correctly modest horizon stated by its own owner. It is standing in the right place for questions about routes and in the wrong place for questions about ships. It was not detected; nothing was asked in these two seasons that a gold buyer would not ask.

### Operation 3. The Maurya — frontier trade office, opened 549

**Standing.** Roman merchant factors resident at the Euphrates boundary waystations under the road terms of the 320s, trading there openly for two centuries. Run by the frontier trade office, not the secretariat. One year of running before 550 closes.

**Tasked.** (i) Whether the message Rome sent in 525 was received at all. (ii) Whether Indian yards show work on a hull engine. Named targets: the Maurya arsenal establishments on the Syr Darya frontier and the river yards below Pataliputra.

#### The geography of this operation, stated first because it governs everything below

The factors stand on the Euphrates. The Syr Darya arsenals lie some three thousand kilometres east-north-east of them, served by the Bactrian and Sogdian road and not by the Rome road at all. The river yards below Pataliputra lie some four thousand kilometres east, on a different watershed and a different trade. No factor of this operation has been east of the Euphrates in the year it has run, and the cover forbids it: a resident treaty factor who leaves the waystations for Bengal ceases to be a resident treaty factor and becomes a traveller with no standing anywhere.

Both named targets are therefore out of reach, and no quantity of years fixes it. Rome's patience is correctly set and is aimed at the wrong variable.

#### Collected

**1. The state of the road the factors live on.** *Firm, by residence; obtained continuously, entered 549.*
The Mesopotamian stretch of the Rome road carries roughly twice the waystations it carried a generation ago, and permanent mounted patrols work the long runs between them. The building was done in the factors' own time. Every house on that road knows it; it required no enquiry. Not tasked, and entered because it is the physical setting of everything else and because it is the thing Rome asked of the realm long ago and did not get.

**2. Printed Maurya administration in the trading cities.** *Firm — seen in use and a copy handled; 549.*
The named official who hears trade disputes in the Mesopotamian trading city under the treaty now works from a printed and bound book of the tax and law codes rather than a written roll, and a copy of the tariff portion was obtained openly by a factor's clerk for his house's own use. Printed matter of this kind is new on that road within these men's working lives. No factor can say how many such books exist or where they are struck.

**3. The only state traffic on the road in a generation.** *Reported; obtained 549–550 from Maurya road staff and from the factors' own house memory.*
About twenty years ago a Maurya state party crossed westward at these stations under escort, carrying a sealed volume, bound for the Syrian frontier. Nothing of that character has passed in either direction since, eastward or westward. This is recollection, not record; see the failures below for why no record exists.

**4. Ships that keep the wet season.** *Reported, third-hand, thin; 550.*
A carrier come up from the head of the Gulf gave it, from talk at the Gulf ports, that the realm's packet to its far southern possessions now sails the year round where within memory it waited on the fair season, and that some of the realm's hulls carry a warm pipe amidships and hold their way against a wind that would stop a ship under sail alone. No hull was seen by any man of this operation. No yard, no engine, no number, no place of building. It is sailors' talk bought at third hand in a market, and it is entered as such.

#### Attempted and failed

**THE FIRST TASKING RETURNED TWO SEPARATE NILS. THEY ARE DIFFERENT FINDINGS, THEY ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS, AND THEY ARE NOT TO BE READ TOGETHER OR COMBINED.**

##### Nil the first — whether the message of 525 was received at Pataliputra

*Nil, and the nil is structural.*

Whether a foreign court received a packet is a fact held in one chancery register four thousand kilometres from where this operation stands. Nothing in a waystation market touches it. More to the point: **non-receipt has no signature anywhere.** A letter that did not arrive leaves nothing behind it — no absence a merchant can price, no gap in a market, no change in any traffic. There is no consequence of non-receipt to observe, because non-receipt consists precisely in nothing having happened.

**Reason this is a nil:** there is nothing to look for. The question is a chancery fact and the negative case leaves no observable trace of any kind, anywhere.

**This nil says nothing whatever about whether the message arrived.** It says the question cannot be reached from where this operation stands.

##### Nil the second — whether a record of a 525 packet exists at the frontier waystations

*A separate nil, on a separate question, obtained by a separate act.*

Framed through the treaty's dispute officials as an enquiry after a lost consignment of 525, the factors established that **no record of any Roman packet passing east in that year is held at these stations.**

**Reason this is a nil:** the waystation establishment of that stretch stood thinned in 525 and was doubled and re-staffed across the years following, so that neither the stations nor the men of 525 are there to be asked.

**The absence of a frontier record from 525 is evidence about the frontier record and about nothing else.** It is not evidence that a packet did not pass, and it must not be forwarded, summarised or read as "not received."

##### The remaining failures

**Work on a hull engine in the yards below Pataliputra.** *Nil.* No access, no traveller of this operation, no channel. Item 4 above is not an answer to this question and should not be filed as a partial one; it speaks to ships at sea in the Gulf, not to work in a yard.

**The arsenal establishments on the Syr Darya.** *Nil.* Nothing of that frontier reaches the Euphrates waystations by any road these factors work. One factor recorded bazaar talk, unsourced and undated, that the guns on the northern river had been added to. It carries no number, no year and no origin he could name, and it is not treated here as collection.

**Summary of the year.** Four facts, two of them mundane and one of them third-hand, and three nils. That is a correct first-year return for an operation of this shape, and the shape is the problem, not the year.

### Operation 4. The Dnieper frontier watch, continued (Han China)

Not a network inside the target. An observation post of the frontier legate's staff on the far bank of a shared river, rendering a fixed form each season for twenty-five seasons whether or not there was matter to enter.

#### Fit of instrument to tasking

The tasking is unusually well matched to its instrument, and this should be said plainly. Counting arrivals at a railhead across a river is exactly what a frontier staff can do, and it does it with the ordinary equipment of a Roman frontier: the glass, and the tethered balloon that has been standard frontier furniture for a hundred and twenty years and therefore reads to the far bank as nothing new. The terminus and the yard stand on the eastern bank at the water, in plain view. Rome additionally holds a lawful presence inside the target's own facility, since consignments are arranged at the yard itself between the yard's officers and an officer Rome appoints to it, which puts a Roman officer on the ground with a pace and a rule.

The tasking has two halves and they are not equally answerable. **Frequency** is directly answerable and was answered, to a precision the target court does not hold in any single place of its own. **Carrying capacity** is not answerable from this bank at all, and can only be floored.

#### What was collected

**I. Season of the yard opposite. 526–550. Firm, counted.**
The yard works from the thaw to the first hard frost and is shut between. Twenty-five seasons counted: shortest 184 days (531), longest 216 (547), mean 201. *The seasonal pattern was established by the watch years before the target court stated it in correspondence in 549, so Rome holds it independently.*

**II. Arrivals. 526–550. Firm, counted.**
An arrival is one train brought into the yard opposite and there broken up. Rising steadily across the period: 41 in the season of 527, 57 in 534, 74 in 541, 96 in 549. Twenty-five seasons, 1,674 arrivals counted.

Interval in 549: mean one arrival in 2.2 days; longest gap 19 days; four arrivals inside two days on two occasions. Interval in 527: mean one in 4.7 days; longest gap 26 days. The pattern is the same through all twenty-five seasons — clusters of three and four across two or three days, then a stand-off of a week or more. Arrivals cluster upon consignments arranged at the yard.

**III. Composition of trains. Firm, counted; gauge and profile paced by the appointed officer on consignment days.**
Wagons to a train: least 8, most 16, commonest 12; 19,880 wagons counted across the period. Motive power counted at the yard head as draught teams, horses six to eight to a train in the ordinary case and oxen ten to twelve on the heaviest, changed at the yard and taken away eastward light. Laden wagons on arrival in 549: mean 9 of 12. Laden wagons on departure eastward: 12 of 12, and this proportion has held every season it was counted.

Single road to the yard head, with a run-round and four standing sidings inside the yard. **No second road was laid at any point in twenty-five years.** Rail profile, sleeper spacing, flange and gauge are those of the commercial rail method; the wagon is that method's ordinary cargo pattern.

**IV. The yard and its works. Firm, counted.**
Covered stores: 1 at 527, 2 at 536, 3 at 544, 3 at 550 — the third raised in 544 as a dry store, and it is for Rome's own western cargo. Weighing house: 1 throughout. Cranes and sheer-legs at the water: 2 at 527, 4 at 550. Standing labour on working days: 40 to 55 men in the seasons 526 to 530, 70 to 110 in 546 to 550, 130 on the heaviest days of 549. Laden lighters crossing to the Roman bank: 244 in 527, 610 in 549.

The yard itself was built between 526 and 531 — that is, the target built a transfer yard on its own bank while Rome's answer on joining the lines was still unsent, and needed no one's agreement to do it. Firm, watched under construction.

**V. Weighed at Rome's own yard on the Roman bank. Firm, handled and weighed.**
Season of 549, received across the river: silk in bales 610 t, lacquerware 240 t, worked steel 780 t, worked bronze 610 t — total 2,240 t. Despatched eastward the same season: wine 900 t, oil 640 t, tin in pigs 1,010 t, glass 180 t, coin 30 t — 2,760 t weighed, and 220 head of horses counted. For comparison, 527: 940 t received, 1,180 t despatched, 96 head. Twenty-five seasons: 38,400 t received, 46,900 t despatched, 3,140 head.

This is measurement on Rome's own scales, not espionage and not estimate.

**VI. Inference, entered by the curator of the frontier rail district over the hand of its senior engineer. Inferred — the only inference in this return, and its basis is stated.**

*Basis.* The road opposite is laid to the profile, sleeper spacing and gauge of the commercial rail method this office works daily. Rome therefore knows what one such wagon bears on that profile, being 2.6 to 3.0 tonnes of merchandise, and what such a road bears in Roman service with the relays stocked, being not fewer than four laden trains a day each way.

*First.* The counted wagons and the weighed tonnage agree. Ninety-six arrivals at nine laden wagons is some 23 tonnes to the arrival; the scales weighed 2,240 tonnes the same season. The same figure reached twice by two independent means.

*Second, and stated only as a floor.* On the counted interval of one arrival in 2.2 days, and on the profile, a road of that kind with stocked relays will bear not less than 25,000 tonnes in a season of the length counted. What was delivered to the river in 549 was 2,240.

*Third.* No closer figure can be set, and the reason is specific. Two things govern such a road and neither is at the river: the draught stock held at the relays behind, and the spacing of the passing places. Both lie inland and nothing at the yard head measures them.

*Fourth.* Two counts bear on this. The interval is irregular, clustering on consignments and then standing off for a fortnight, where a road worked to its limit runs to an even interval. And trains arrive at nine laden wagons of twelve while departing at twelve of twelve. What is carried is set by what is consigned, not by what the road will bear.

#### What the count establishes and what it does not

**Establishes.** The frequency, exactly. The season, exactly. The delivered tonnage by commodity, exactly, for twenty-five years. That the line to the river is a single road and has not been doubled in a generation. That a road of that profile floors at roughly ten times what is being delivered on it. That in twenty-five seasons **no troop train, no artillery, no munition, no magazine construction and no military store identifiable as such reached that terminus.**

**Does not establish.** The carrying capacity, which has a floor and no ceiling from this bank. The state of the line east of the horizon, which no watcher at the river can see. The forward magazine depth. Whether the line would be turned to military use, and what it could bear if it were.

**A caution on what the tonnage is a measure of.** The tonnage crossing the river is a true number about the Rome trade. It is not a measure of the line, and nothing at the terminus establishes what else the line carries or where else it terminates. Rome's premise, that a supply line to a frontier is a supply line to an army, is sound doctrine; nothing in this return tests it either way.

#### Nil returns

- **Twenty-five seasons, no military traffic of any kind at the terminus.** A nil, purpose-sought, and the most informative single line in the return.
- **No second track, no new engine shed, no isolated or fireproofed building behind the yard, no depot of the kind that stores powder.** Nil across the period, and this is the nil that would have mattered most had it broken.
- **No elicitation was attempted and none succeeded.** The watch asked nothing of anyone. That is recorded as a nil.
