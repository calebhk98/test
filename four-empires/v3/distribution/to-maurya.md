# Packet for the throne at Pataliputra

This is everything delivered to the Maurya realm for the period 525–550 AD: correspondence addressed to this court, this court's own intelligence returns, and a note of what was physically handed over in an encounter to which this court was a party.

---

## Documents received

### 1. From the council at Alexandria. The sending court's record does not fix the year; it falls within 525–550.

> From the council at Alexandria to the throne at Pataliputra.
>
> Egypt has received the notice sent to Alexandria concerning holdings on the southern coast, and the sealed paper handed to Egyptian officers at sea upon that coast. Egypt asserts no claim to ground upon that coast and disputes none of India's.
>
> Egypt sets down one thing unasked, because India may have found it already and it would be read wrongly. In 534 an Egyptian hull struck a reef on the southern coast and was beached to be repaired on an empty stretch of shore, where the party lay eleven days. It left the refuse of a repair, and before sailing it raised a cairn and set in it a squared timber cut with Egyptian characters. That is what Egyptian survey parties do on every coast they chart, to fix a position for the ship that comes after. It marks a place and claims nothing, and Egypt gives notice of it here so that it is not taken for what it is not. If India would have it taken down, Egypt will take it down.
>
> The terms set out in that paper are the terms Egypt keeps at its own ports: harbours open to any power for trade, water and repair on the same terms, and no base, no garrison right and no ground granted upon them. Egypt will hold to them where its ships touch that coast, and looks for the same at Egyptian ports, which India has long had.
>
> Egypt's squadron in those waters carried orders to make no landing where there were works, vessels or people, to attempt no contact, and to fire only if fired upon. Those orders were kept throughout. The squadron has been withdrawn and Egypt keeps no vessel on that coast.
>
> Egypt seeks no ground upon that coast and asks nothing of India there.
>
> Alexandria.

**No other correspondence was addressed to this court in this period.** Nothing was received from Rome and nothing from the court of the Han.

## Intelligence returns

**The East African coast. Ordered 529, running twenty-one years.**

Two resident factories, Gujarati and Omani, twenty-five to fifty years established and never used for the realm's business before. Ship-masters of the Oman gum, hide and grain trade as carriers. From 529 one officer of the realm's service under the Gujarati factor's cover, travelling as a junior man learning the accounts. Cover trade: gum, hides and coastal grain for the Oman market. Communication only by the seasonal ships — a season to Oman, two to three weeks more to the capital.

Marks: **firm** — the agent saw, handled, counted or bought it himself. **Reported** — someone told the agent. **Field inference** — the agent went beyond what he saw, flagged in place.

**Standing on cover.** The cover is as good as cover gets against this target. Egypt has held these harbours and traded through them for six centuries, and Indian sailors work them in such numbers that Egyptian hulls, rig, dress and speech are known on sight to most of them. Foreign merchant presence at Egyptian ports is legally defined and routine. Nothing about a Gujarati factory buying gum and hides in these harbours draws a second look.

**Standing on lag.** Every item below reached the capital roughly a year after it was seen. The last usable collection of the turn is the 549 season, arriving 550.

### Collected

**C1. Answering the tasking directly: at the specific harbours where bulk provisioning was seen about 500 to 505, no second provisioning of that character occurred.** What runs instead is a continuous, unremarkable, year-on-year flow that never spikes. The factors watched for the spike for twenty-one years and it did not come. *(Firm — a nil return against a well-framed question, and the most valuable single line in this operation's product.)*

**C2. One modest crown victualling, in about 527, out of proportion to nothing else that season.** Grain, cordage, cooperage and water casks taken up at short notice on crown account, enough to move the coastal grain price for part of a season and no longer. The factors put it at the order of a few hulls' worth. It did not repeat. *(Firm — their own market book and the price movement in it.)*

**C3. What does run continuously, and is the real answer to "what sailed, in how many hulls, in which season": Egyptian transports loading people, not cargo, and sailing south.** Families with stock and tools, term-contract men out of their term with their households, and garrison drafts. Every sailing season across the whole twenty-one years. The factors counted embarkations at the harbours they sit in and returned running counts by season. *(Firm.)*

**C4. Those hulls sail south toward the Cape, and a large proportion do not return within the season.** Some return after two or three years. Some do not return. Over twenty-one years the factors could establish that this is the normal pattern and not a wave. *(Firm.)*

**C5. Timber, cordage and water orders — specifically asked for and specifically answered.** Timber and cordage buying at these harbours rose across the period and stayed risen; water and cooperage rose with the number of hulls. But the rise is smooth and it tracks the number of hulls in the coastal trade, not any single expedition. *(Firm — the factors buy in the same markets.)*

**C6. Shipbuilding on this coast increased.** New building and more of it at the coastal hardwood yards across the period, of transport build. *(Firm — seen from the water and from the timber market.)*

**C7. Egyptian crews and garrison men on this coast stopped dying of the intermittent fevers at the rate they formerly did, from the later 540s, and a bark preparation is issued to them.** A resident of twenty-one years is exactly the observer who can see this change, and it is the most consequential thing this operation collected. *(Firm as to the change in mortality and the issue of the preparation. The bark's identity, its preparation and its dosage were not obtained here.)*

**C8. Horses moving south along the coast by sea in numbers, from the later years, together with breeding stock coming down from the highland studs.** *(Firm.)*

**C9. Harbour and road works at these older provincial harbours are running behind — moles and wharves begun and standing unfinished.** *(Firm — visible from the anchorage every season.)*

**C10. Local food supply rose and a new root crop has come into the coastal villages, and separately a new highland tuber began coming down to market from about 546.** The factors buy coastal grain and this is squarely their trade. *(Firm.)*

**C11. Tall masts with wires at the Egyptian harbour posts, guarded, with men attending them.** Standing for as long as anyone in the factories remembers. Function unknown and not guessed at. *(Firm as to the object.)*

**C12. Field inference, flagged.** The officer under the factor's cover entered one judgement of his own beyond what he saw: that the southward hulls of C3 and C4 and the shipbuilding of C6 are one programme and not several, on the ground that the counts rise together. **This is the agent's reasoning and not his observation, and it is to be read as such.** He entered no destination and correctly declined to name one.

### Attempted and failed, or nil

- **Where the southward hulls go.** Nil beyond "south, round the Cape, gone." Omani and Gujarati hulls do not work past the Cape, and the officer had no way to follow. Second-hand harbour talk about a land across a western ocean reached the factory but arrived inconsistent between tellers and was not corroborated. *(Reported, weak, entered as unconfirmed.)*
- **Where the fever bark grows, and where it is grown.** **Nil.** A coastal factor can see that the fevers stopped killing Egyptians and cannot see the plant. Nothing of the plant, its country, or where it is cultivated was obtained by any channel of this operation.
- **The 527 victualling's purpose or destination.** Nil. The factors have the fact and the quantity and nothing else.
- **Anything of Egypt's industry, fleet strength or treasury, or anything at all beyond these harbours** — including the function of the masts at C11. Nil throughout.
- **Any change in Egyptian handling of Indian merchants after the exchanges at sea of 543, 547 and 549.** Nil — there was no change at these harbours, and the resident factories were not disturbed.

**Assessment of the operation.** Well placed: twenty-one years of residency against a question that is answerable in a harbour market, and it answered it — including the negative, which it should be credited for. Not detected. The standing risk, unrealised so far, is that the officer's interest in crown victualling quantities is an interest a gum-and-hides buyer has no commercial reason to hold; one direct question about a crown contract's tonnage is what would burn him.

**One caution on reading C1.** The nil is a nil on a spike, not a nil on a programme. It establishes that no second bulk provisioning of the 500–505 kind occurred at those harbours. It does not establish that the traffic now running is ordinary colonial supply, and that reading is not supported by the product.

## Handed over in person

**Off the southern coast, at Walled Station Five in 547 and at the Nauvandha approaches in 549.** The realm's written statement, struck at the capital's press in Greek, Latin and Sanskrit, was handed across to the foreign hulls' officers on both occasions and was taken aboard. Nothing was given in return on either occasion, no name was given, no answer of any kind was made, and nothing was fired.

No other object, document or material was physically handed over to or by the realm in any adjudicated encounter in this period.
