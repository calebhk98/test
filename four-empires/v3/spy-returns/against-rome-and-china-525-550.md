# Adjudication of foreign collection against Rome and against Han China, 525–550

Adjudicated by a neutral pass holding only the two target countries' own files. Nothing in the operations' owners' documents was read.

Consolidates the two operations the coordinator set for this period. Where a single-target return has already been filed for the same operation, the load-bearing figures here are the same figures; the one correction is at Operation 2, head IV, where the draft-ox count of the Pontic pool is 54,000 and not 26,000.

**Marks used throughout.** **Firm** — the agent or the watch saw, counted, paced or handled the thing. **Reported** — someone told the agent. **Inferred** — reasoned in the field, with the reasoner named and the basis stated. Field inference is kept to a minimum and is flagged wherever it appears.

# OPERATION 1 — EGYPT AGAINST ROME

Commercial cover through the trade-corridor stations, reporting as a sideline to genuine trade. No electrical apparatus carried. Run by the directorate through recruited-insider and embedded Greek-Egyptian channels in combination, corroborated across two of three before reaching the council.

Channels used: **(A)** recruited insiders among Roman lading, naval and fleet-account clerks in the Atlantic ports; **(B)** the resident Greek-Egyptian commercial houses at Gades, Olisipo and Ostia, generations old; **(C)** the station in the Roman African provinces, working the gold and caravan trades.

## Where the answers physically live

Both taskings ask what a government holds. It is worth being exact about where each answer sits before saying what came back.

**Question one — has Rome concluded that Egypt is across the western ocean?** The Roman disposition sits in the imperial secretariat's file and in the Senate's deliberation. It is not a conclusion. Rome carries two readings side by side — a race to the Americas, and a long-standing Atlantic gold artery running metal north with its return leg held offshore — and across the period it moves weight from the first toward the second without closing the file. A port network does not reach a secretariat file, and in this case there is no conclusion in it to reach.

But Rome's *conduct under* the uncertainty is almost entirely in ports, and one item is purpose-built: a standing watch on the Canaries and on Madeira with glass and tethered balloon, established for no purpose but this one, whose business is to count large hulls passing north and west. Its existence is the question made physical. It stands on Roman Atlantic islands that the western run stages through, it is supplied and relieved out of Atlantic ports, and a tethered balloon at height is visible from the sea for twenty miles. It cannot be hidden and was not being hidden.

**Question two — have the lost ships hardened into a settled belief that Egypt destroyed them?** This one is not a technical secret. It is a fifty-year grievance belonging to the Atlantic ports whose men died, and the evidence that overturned the old account arrived home in three ships with their crews. Grievances of that kind live on quaysides, at underwriters' tables and in printed sheets, and Rome runs a dense commercial press in which the losing side of an argument prints its case.

## What was collected

**1. The warship authority. 531 onward. (B), corroborated by (A). Firm.**
From 531 the Roman yards lay down six warship hulls a year under a standing authority, and did so in eighteen of the twenty years. Hulls on the ways were counted directly in the Atlantic and Mediterranean yards across the period. (A) confirms the charge stands on the fleet account and is the largest single new line in it.

**2. The western transports. 534 onward. (B). Firm.**
From 534 the Atlantic yards at Gades, Olisipo, Burdigala and the Severn and Clyde build ocean-going sailing transports of six hundred tons for the western run and nothing else. Counted on the slips and in the port registers across the period.

**3. The western sailing is a settlement programme. 526–550. (B). Firm at the quay, reported as to scale.**
Emigrant embarkations were counted at the Iberian quays every season of the period. Two round voyages in three years to a hull, outward from the Canaries, homeward on a northern track. The people going out are veterans with families and their gear, not garrisons.

**4. The standing watch on the Canaries and Madeira. From about 532; best returns in the 540s. (B), through the watch's own crews and the men who supply and relieve them. Existence firm; standing order reported; the null reported.**
Watchers set with glasses and a tethered balloon flown from high ground. Their standing order is to count large hulls passing north and west, and captains on the western run are instructed to report any such hull, its heading and its size, on making port. It is not concealed; the clerks speak of it as routine. The watch's own people say that in twenty-five years they have counted none.

*This is the single item that most nearly answers question one, and it answers it obliquely: it establishes that Rome has not settled on Egypt being confined to the African coast, because a court that held that would not pay for the watch. It does not establish that Rome believes Egypt is across.*

**5. Ordnance going west for works against ships. 546–550. (A), handled. Firm.**
Cut stone and heavy pieces shipped in the western transports for harbour works on the far side, entered on the ladings across several seasons. The clerk's own words, asked why so much for a country of people who work no metal, were that they are laid for ships and not for islanders.

**6. The five lost ships. 526–550, thickening after the southern survey's crews came home. (B) and (A), and in print. Reported, and corroborated across both channels.**
The reef-and-current account is dead on the Atlantic runs. What the returned crews describe — a built coast, harbour works, towers throwing mirrored light, towns of cut stone one after another, foundry smoke on the waterfronts, warships lying among cargo hulls, and a single vessel that kept company the whole length of the voyage in relay and never closed — is repeated everywhere, and the conclusion drawn from it is repeated with it: the coast has masters and the masters are ours. (A) hears the same said in the naval offices without argument, as a matter no longer discussed, and adds one thing the quayside does not, that there is real bitterness in it because the account Rome believed for a century is now said to have been given to Rome deliberately and to have been false. (B) gives the hardest measure of how far the belief has set: no shipowner will put a hull down that coast at any charter and the underwriters will not price the risk.

**7. A change in Roman questions in the caravan trade. 548–550. (C). Reported, thin — two returns.**
From 548 the Roman gold-buying houses of the African provinces, an old and ordinary trade, began putting new questions to the Berber caravan factors: where the interior routes now run, what volume they carry, and whether that volume has changed in two generations. Nothing beyond the change of questions was obtained, the practice being two years old.

**8. Collateral, outside the tasking and unmissable. 540–550. (A) and printed Roman law. Firm.**
Rome has a state powder industry. A labour settlement passed in 540 extended collegium recognition to fourteen powder mills, naming them, in published law; the mills stand on the Rhine, in the British coal district, in Dacia, Campania, Noricum, Baetica and on the Danube. From 544 Roman warships in port carry mounted pieces. This was not sought and could not have been avoided by any network standing in Roman ports.

## What was attempted and failed

**The secretariat and the Senate's deliberation.** An approach toward the papers of the western secretariat, and toward men who handle them, returned nothing. This is a placement failure and not a tradecraft failure: the commercial network stands in ports, and the papers do not leave the office. No member of that office was recruitable inside the window.

**The northern military-technical placement.** Not tasked, not approached, not implicated. It could not have answered either question in any case; it stands in the northern industrial provinces and sees yards, ordnance and metal, not court opinion. Recorded so the coordinator knows it was neither used nor risked.

## Nil returns

- **No Roman document, printed or handled, states a conclusion about Egypt's presence in the western ocean.** A nil against a well-framed question, and an informative one: there is no such document because there is no such conclusion.
- **The court's movement of weight across the period, from the race reading toward the gold-artery reading, was not detected.** A change of emphasis inside an open file has no observable signature and produces no order, no building and no printed argument of its own. Nil.
- **No Roman military or naval preparation directed at the African Atlantic coast, in twenty-five years.** Firm as a nil: no depot laid, no coal stocked south of the Moroccan holdings, no charter offered, no probe fitted out, no hull sent. (C) watched for exactly this.
- **No Roman démarche to Egypt on the lost ships or on the coast, in twenty-five years.** This belongs to the formal mission and not to this operation, and is entered here only so the two are not confused in the council's reading.

## What the operation actually established

On question one: that Rome has entirely ceased to hold Egypt confined to the African coast, that it has not concluded Egypt is across, that it treats the possibility as live enough to pay for a purpose-built watch astride the crossing latitude, and that the watch has counted nothing in twenty-five years. The operation returns Rome's conduct. It does not return Rome's conclusion, because Rome has not reached one.

On question two: yes, hardened, and attached exactly where the tasking asks — not to a hazard of water but to the works, the towers, the garrisons and the power that raised them. This return is firm-edged and corroborated. It is also the return the sending court will find least comfortable, since it establishes that a century of Egyptian management of Roman belief has been undone and is known to have been management.

**Network status.** Intact, undetected, cover unimpaired. Every item is sourced to a quayside, a customs house, an underwriters' table, a lading book or a printed sheet.

# OPERATION 2 — ROME AGAINST HAN CHINA: THE DNIEPER FRONTIER WATCH, CONTINUED

Not a network inside the target. An observation post of the frontier legate's staff on the far bank of a shared river, rendering a fixed form each season for twenty-five seasons whether or not there was matter to enter.

## Fit of instrument to tasking

The tasking is unusually well matched to its instrument, and this should be said plainly. Counting arrivals at a railhead across a river is exactly what a frontier staff can do, and it does it with the ordinary equipment of a Roman frontier: the glass, and the tethered balloon that has been standard frontier furniture for a hundred and twenty years and therefore reads to the far bank as nothing new. The terminus and the yard stand on the eastern bank at the water, in plain view. Rome additionally holds a lawful presence inside the target's own facility, since consignments are arranged at the yard itself between the yard's officers and an officer Rome appoints to it, which puts a Roman officer on the ground with a pace and a rule.

The tasking has two halves and they are not equally answerable. **Frequency** is directly answerable and was answered, to a precision the target court does not hold in any single place of its own. **Carrying capacity** is not answerable from this bank at all, and can only be floored.

## What was collected

**I. Season of the yard opposite. 526–550. Firm, counted.**
The yard works from the thaw to the first hard frost and is shut between. Twenty-five seasons counted: shortest 184 days (531), longest 216 (547), mean 201. *The seasonal pattern was established by the watch years before the target court stated it in correspondence in 549, so Rome holds it independently.*

**II. Arrivals. 526–550. Firm, counted.**
An arrival is one train brought into the yard opposite and there broken up. Rising steadily across the period: 41 in the season of 527, 57 in 534, 74 in 541, 96 in 549. Twenty-five seasons, 1,674 arrivals counted.

Interval in 549: mean one arrival in 2.2 days; longest gap 19 days; four arrivals inside two days on two occasions. Interval in 527: mean one in 4.7 days; longest gap 26 days. The pattern is the same through all twenty-five seasons — clusters of three and four across two or three days, then a stand-off of a week or more. Arrivals cluster upon consignments arranged at the yard.

**III. Composition of trains. Firm, counted; gauge and profile paced by the appointed officer on consignment days.**
Wagons to a train: least 8, most 16, commonest 12; 19,880 wagons counted across the period. Motive power counted at the yard head as draught teams, horses six to eight to a train in the ordinary case and oxen ten to twelve on the heaviest, changed at the yard and taken away eastward light. Laden wagons on arrival in 549: mean 9 of 12. Laden wagons on departure eastward: 12 of 12, and this proportion has held every season it was counted.

Single road to the yard head, with a run-round and four standing sidings inside the yard. **No second road was laid at any point in twenty-five years.** Rail profile, sleeper spacing, flange and gauge are those of the commercial rail method; the wagon is that method's ordinary cargo pattern.

**IV. The yard and its works. Firm, counted.**
Covered stores: 1 at 527, 2 at 536, 3 at 544, 3 at 550 — the third raised in 544 as a dry store, and it is for Rome's own western cargo. Weighing house: 1 throughout. Cranes and sheer-legs at the water: 2 at 527, 4 at 550. Standing labour on working days: 40 to 55 men in the seasons 526 to 530, 70 to 110 in 546 to 550, 130 on the heaviest days of 549. Laden lighters crossing to the Roman bank: 244 in 527, 610 in 549.

The yard itself was built between 526 and 531 — that is, the target built a transfer yard on its own bank while Rome's answer on joining the lines was still unsent, and needed no one's agreement to do it. Firm, watched under construction.

**V. Weighed at Rome's own yard on the Roman bank. Firm, handled and weighed.**
Season of 549, received across the river: silk in bales 610 t, lacquerware 240 t, worked steel 780 t, worked bronze 610 t — total 2,240 t. Despatched eastward the same season: wine 900 t, oil 640 t, tin in pigs 1,010 t, glass 180 t, coin 30 t — 2,760 t weighed, and 220 head of horses counted. For comparison, 527: 940 t received, 1,180 t despatched, 96 head. Twenty-five seasons: 38,400 t received, 46,900 t despatched, 3,140 head.

This is measurement on Rome's own scales, not espionage and not estimate.

**VI. Inference, entered by the curator of the frontier rail district over the hand of its senior engineer. Inferred — the only inference in this return, and its basis is stated.**

*Basis.* The road opposite is laid to the profile, sleeper spacing and gauge of the commercial rail method this office works daily. Rome therefore knows what one such wagon bears on that profile, being 2.6 to 3.0 tonnes of merchandise, and what such a road bears in Roman service with the relays stocked, being not fewer than four laden trains a day each way.

*First.* The counted wagons and the weighed tonnage agree. Ninety-six arrivals at nine laden wagons is some 23 tonnes to the arrival; the scales weighed 2,240 tonnes the same season. The same figure reached twice by two independent means.

*Second, and stated only as a floor.* On the counted interval of one arrival in 2.2 days, and on the profile, a road of that kind with stocked relays will bear not less than 25,000 tonnes in a season of the length counted. What was delivered to the river in 549 was 2,240.

*Third.* No closer figure can be set, and the reason is specific. Two things govern such a road and neither is at the river: the draught stock held at the relays behind, and the spacing of the passing places. Both lie inland and nothing at the yard head measures them.

*Fourth.* Two counts bear on this. The interval is irregular, clustering on consignments and then standing off for a fortnight, where a road worked to its limit runs to an even interval. And trains arrive at nine laden wagons of twelve while departing at twelve of twelve. What is carried is set by what is consigned, not by what the road will bear.

## What the count establishes and what it does not

**Establishes.** The frequency, exactly. The season, exactly. The delivered tonnage by commodity, exactly, for twenty-five years. That the line to the river is a single road and has not been doubled in a generation. That a road of that profile floors at roughly ten times what is being delivered on it. That in twenty-five seasons **no troop train, no artillery, no munition, no magazine construction and no military store identifiable as such reached that terminus.**

**Does not establish.** The carrying capacity, which has a floor and no ceiling from this bank. The state of the line east of the horizon — and the target's own record shows a real constraint there, its oldest western track worn past safe loading over long stretches and now being relaid, which no watcher at the river can see. The forward magazine depth, which the target reckons not in weight but in days of sustained fire, and which it built up at its *other* frontier and not at this one. Whether the line would be turned to military use, and what it could bear if it were.

**The trap in the measurement.** The tonnage crossing the river is a true number about the Rome trade and a false number about the line. The line's principal business in this period is not the Rome trade at all but the settlement of the black earth behind it — 210,000 households along the rail towns, 9,000 iron ploughs and 54,000 draft oxen carried west from the interior foundries and heartland provinces, and grain moving back east — all of which terminates at rail towns short of the river and never appears at the terminus Rome is counting. Rome's premise, that a supply line to a frontier is a supply line to an army, is sound doctrine. At this frontier in this period it is measuring a settlement and trade line.

## Nil returns

- **Twenty-five seasons, no military traffic of any kind at the terminus.** A nil, purpose-sought, and the most informative single line in the return.
- **No second track, no new engine shed, no isolated or fireproofed building behind the yard, no depot of the kind that stores powder.** Nil across the period, and this is the nil that would have mattered most had it broken.
- **No elicitation was attempted and none succeeded.** The watch asked nothing of anyone. That is recorded as a nil and, on the coordinator's note below, as the operation's best decision.

# NOTE TO THE COORDINATOR

**On Operation 1.**

*One success that its owner will misread.* The Canaries and Madeira watch is the operation's best item and the easiest to read backwards. A purpose-built watch reads as conviction. It is not conviction: the Roman file has moved *away* from the reading that Egypt is racing west, on the strength of that very watch returning nothing for twenty-five years. If the council takes the watch as proof that Rome believes Egypt is across, it will have inverted the finding. The correct reading is narrow and should be stated in those words to the council: **Rome has not closed the question, and the evidence Rome itself has gathered points away from us.**

*A second inversion, in the other direction.* The twenty-five-year null is Egypt's evidence about Rome as much as Rome's evidence about Egypt. It is tempting to read it as proof that Egyptian concealment works. It is that. It is not proof that Rome is unaware of the African coast; Rome knows that coast in detail, by its own eyes, for three thousand miles, and holds no doubt at all about whose it is.

*No plant to warn of.* I looked for one and there is none. Rome ran no deception channel against Egypt in this period and sent Egypt no message at all. Its concealment is passive — routes and charts held close — rather than a feed. The port take can therefore be treated at face value, which is an unusual thing to be able to say and should be said explicitly, because it will not always be true.

*One live hazard the council should not confuse with a plant.* Rome kept its Alexandria network standing and quarantined the branch that fell silent, walking no replacement into it. If that branch is in our hands and is being held to feed Rome, it has no listener: Rome is not reading that line. This is adjacent to the present operation and is entered so the two are not run together.

*On the boundary of the tasking.* Item 8 — a state powder industry of fourteen mills, announced by Rome in its own published law in 540, and pieces mounted in Roman warships from 544 — was not sought and could not have been missed. The council should note that it arrived free and that nothing was risked to get it.

**On Operation 2.**

*The instrument is right and the tasking is half-right.* Say so to the legate. The half he asked that could be answered was answered better than any court in the world could answer it about its own line. The half he asked that could not be answered was floored honestly and left uncapped, which is the correct handling, and the staff should be told not to let a later season quietly convert the floor into a figure.

*A tripwire is live and Rome has not yet touched it.* The target entered a standing observation order in 526 and returns a fixed seasonal form carrying four counts, the fourth being *any inquiry received concerning its interior distances, supply intervals or routes*. Its return to 550 records none. That is an empty ledger line waiting for a Roman name. The officer Rome appoints to the yard is the best collection position in the entire operation and is also the fastest way to fill that line: one question from him about intervals, distances or what the line carries converts a lawful counting watch into a logged intelligence approach in a single season. He must go on arranging consignments and nothing else. Correspondence is closed to the question by policy in any case — the target entered a standing bar in 527 against discussing extent, distances or supply intervals with any court, and stated plainly in 549 that it publishes no timetable and no tonnage.

*Rome is being counted back.* The target's own seasonal form records depot and yard work observed at two points on the Roman bank, at present not distinguishable from ordinary commercial building. Enlargement beyond what the trade warrants is what would stop it reading as commerce.

*The counts will keep rising and keep answering the wrong question.* The target completed a trunk line to its southern mill counties in 548 and is now putting northern coal into the silk works there. Output in those counties will rise and arrivals at the river will rise with it for decades. Patience refines the measurement of the trade indefinitely and never converges on the capacity.

*The genuine forward indicator is not tonnage.* Where this state stocks a frontier for fighting, it stocks it in a form Rome could actually see: magazines must stand outside walls or in fireproofed works under a policy in force since a city fire in the 420s, which produces isolated, heavily built, separately sited structures; and since 536 it stocks and reports them against a stated number of days of sustained fire. In this period it did all of that at its other frontier and none of it here. **One isolated fireproofed building behind the eastern yard would be worth more than any number of counted trains**, and the existing form already catches it under head IV. The legate should be told to weight that head accordingly.

*One thing the count got for free and should be trusted.* This state maintains deliberate display at several of its frontiers, siting works where they can be seen, running drill when traffic is heaviest, marching relief columns in by day and out by night so a garrison is counted twice. It does none of that at this river. The count Rome has taken here is honest, and if Rome later learns of the display practice elsewhere it should be careful not to discount this return retrospectively.
