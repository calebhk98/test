# Observation return to Rome, standing watch on Han China at the Dnieper, 525–550

Adjudicated by a pass holding only China's own files. Not a clandestine network: a standing watch by the frontier legate's staff from Roman-held ground, plus Rome's own transshipment yard on its own bank. Rome tasked the carrying capacity of the line to the river, and the frequency of its arrivals.

**Coordinator note, not for the receiving court.**

*Frequency: tasked and established correctly*, firmly, with seasonality and trend. This half succeeds outright.

*Capacity: not established.* What Rome obtains instead is a correct and unusually exact **floor with no ceiling** — exact because it comes off Rome's own scales, and a floor because the far yard accepted every consignment Rome's factors ever offered and never once declined for want of carriage. The ceiling was never touched because Roman merchant demand never approached it, so it was never measured. Four reasons stack in the same direction: the railhead is not the line, since much of the line's traffic terminates inland in the Pontic settlement towns and never reaches the crossing; the crossing trade is high-value and low-bulk, silk and tolls together standing at nine percent of Chinese revenue; **China's ruling of 527 deliberately suppressed the two aggregate figures Rome was tasked to get**, on its own reasoning that a tonnage plus an arrival schedule is the lift of the line behind the yard; and the line's real limits — relay animals, forage, depots, and the empire's iron and coal allocation — sit inland and out of sight.

*A related quantity established correctly in place of the tasked one:* the throughput of the crossing and the two yards, which Rome's engineer bounds well and correctly marks as a bound on the crossing rather than on the road behind it.

*A real risk that a correct measurement misleads.* Rome's stated premise was that a supply line to a frontier is a supply line to an army. On this frontier in these years the line carried colonists, ploughs, ox teams, grain and trade, and the empire's military effort and magazining went to the other frontier entirely. China does not reckon frontier lift in tonnes per season at all; it reckons in resupply interval and in days of sustained fire held forward. A tonnage honestly weighed at a trade yard and converted into legions-fed will understate the line badly, because in a war the commercial traffic is the first thing displaced. Rome's own staff flag the limit correctly; whether Rome heeds the flag is Rome's business.

*No deception.* China withholds and does not stage. The contrast is its own conduct at the Syr Darya, where its observers note that a garrison wishing a number to be seen can arrange to be counted, and rate their own count a floor for that reason. At the Dnieper the yard, the weighing house and the scales are all genuine, and Chinese and Roman weights agree. Two consequences of Chinese openness work in Rome's favour: China invited Rome to appoint an officer to the far yard, and that officer can lay a rod across the track — and the gauge, profile and tie spacing are **Rome's own commercial specification**, the rail method having passed from Rome to China in the agreements of the 340s to 370s. Rome is reading a line built to its own drawings.

*Live tripwire, carried forward.* China's standing observation order of 526 has its Dnieper garrisons return a fixed form with four counts, the fourth being any inquiry received concerning interior distances, supply intervals or routes. By 550 it records none received. **Rome's watch survives twenty-five years because it asks nothing.** The moment Roman staff or factors begin putting questions at the yard about intervals, routes or interior distances, that count is triggered and China's rating of Rome moves.

**RETURN OF THE STANDING WATCH AT THE RIVER CROSSING**
Office of the frontier legate. Fixed seasonal form, consolidated for the twenty-five years. Returned each season, including seasons with nothing to enter.

Entries are marked by source. **[W]** counted from this bank by the watch. **[Y]** handled at our transshipment yard on this bank, or attended at the far yard by the officer this command appoints to it. **[E]** reasoned by the office of the camp engineer of this command, marked as inference, with its basis stated.

## A. The season, and arrivals counted

**[W]** The far yard works from the thaw to the first hard frost. The working season was counted at six to seven months in most years of the period, and at five months in three years. The yard stands idle from the first hard frost to the thaw, and the watch's form for those months carries no arrivals to enter.

**[W]** Arrivals of trains at the far railhead, counted in the open season:

| | arrivals counted in a season |
|---|---|
| first five seasons of the period | 7 to 10 |
| middle seasons | 12 to 16 |
| last five seasons | 19 to 24 |

**[W]** Total counted across the twenty-five open seasons: three hundred and seventy-one arrivals.

**[W]** Days between arrivals, counted: 18 to 26 days in the early seasons; 8 to 12 days in the late seasons. Shortest interval counted, three days, occurring twice, both in the last decade. Arrivals fall at even intervals through a season rather than in bunches. No arrival has been counted at night in any season of this period.

**[W]** Wagons to a train, counted at the far railhead where trains are made up and broken in view: 6 to 12 in the early seasons, 10 to 18 in the late. Longest single train counted, twenty-four wagons.

**[W]** Draught. The road to the river is worked by animals and by no engine. Teams counted at two to six head to the wagon, oxen predominating in the early seasons and horses more common latterly. A relay barn and paddock stand on the far bank; standing animals in the paddock counted at forty to ninety head across the seasons.

**[W]** Loaded and empty running. Wagons are counted arriving loaded and departing loaded in most late seasons. In the early seasons a third to a half of departing wagons ran under tare. From the middle years of the period, grain in sack is counted loading eastward out of the far yard, in rising quantity.

**[W]** The far yard, measured by triangulation from two stations of this watch: a covered store of some ninety paces by thirty; a weighing house; a spur to the water and the ferry apron. Roads into the store counted at two at the opening of the period, a third laid in the middle years, a fourth laid in the last five seasons. The fourth road has been counted standing unworked in every season since it was laid.

**[W]** Standing labour at the far yard, counted at the exchange: 70 to 90 men in the early seasons, 160 to 200 in the late.

## B. Handled at the yards

**[Y]** Our yard and its scale house have worked from their second year. All weights below are our own scale's.

**[Y]** Weight landed at our scale house: eight thousand three hundred hundredweight in the first full season of the house; twenty-four thousand six hundred hundredweight in the last season of the period. The rise across the seasons between is even and tracks the arrival count in section A.

**[Y]** Composition by weight of what we take up: worked steel and worked bronze carry the greater part of the weight; silk and lacquerware carry the greater part of the value and little of the weight, counted in crates and bolts. Composition by weight of what we send across: wine and oil in amphorae, glass in crate, tin in ingot, coin in chest, horses walked over by the apron.

**[Y]** In twenty-five seasons the far yard accepted every consignment our factors offered, and completed each within the season in which it was offered. Three part-consignments were entered over to the following season; the far yard's officers entered each of the three against the closing of the ground.

**[Y]** Consignments are settled at the far yard itself, season by season, between its officers and the officer this command appoints, under the dispute officials of the standing agreement.

**[Y]** The far yard weighs each consignment in its own house and gives a tally. Their house's weights and our scale's have agreed within the ordinary allowance in every season of the period.

**[Y]** Taken with the rod by our appointed officer at the far railhead, at exchanges across the period: the road's gauge, the rail's profile, the tie spacing and the wheel-flange pattern are those of our own commercial road, within the allowance of the rod.

## C. Reasoned by the camp engineer of this command

Marked as inference. Each item carries its basis.

**[E] 1.** The road to the river is animal-worked to its terminus. *Basis:* the counted teams, the relay barn and paddock, and the standing animals of section A.

**[E] 2.** The load a wagon on that road carries is the load our own tables give for the same gauge, profile and tie spacing, drawn by teams of the size counted. *Basis:* the rod measurements of section B against this command's own commercial tables. This is the firmest of the inferences here, because the road is built to the profile we ourselves work.

**[E] 3.** The crossing could pass several times the weight now landed within the same open season, without new track being laid. *Basis:* the longest train counted, the shortest interval counted, the four roads into the far store, and the store's measured floor, worked against the tables in item 2. Marked as the engineer's judgment and not as a measurement.

**[E] 4.** The crossing is not at present being pressed. *Basis:* arrivals fall at even intervals rather than in bunches; nothing is run in darkness; the fourth road into the far store stands unworked in every season since it was laid; and the store's measured floor is not filled at any exchange our officer has attended. It follows that the weights in section B are the weight landed and not the weight that could be landed.

**[E] 5.** The limit of item 3 is stated so it is not read wider than it holds. It is a bound upon the crossing and upon the two yards. It is not a bound upon the road behind the crossing. The animals in relay, the barns that hold them, the forage that feeds them and the depots that stage a road's freight are what set a road's rate, and on this road they stand inland beyond the reach of this watch, which counts what arrives at the water and cannot count what is loaded behind it. *Basis:* the geography of the watch's stations.

**[E] 6.** A count of trade wagons in an open season is a count of that traffic. The rate the same road would show under other demands is not established by it. *Entered as a caution on the use of sections A and B, not as a finding.*

---

Form continues on the standing order. Returned whether or not there is anything to enter.

**Continuation.** The arrangement continues into the next period unchanged. The yard remains open through the passable season, the standing agreement remains in force and unaltered, Rome's appointed officer keeps his place at the far yard.
