# Intelligence return to the Maurya Realm, operation against Egypt on the East African coast, 525–550

> **SUPERSEDED.** This adjudication was run against a state of Egypt's turn that no longer exists. It is replaced by `against-egypt-525-550.md`, which adjudicates all four operations against Egypt against the settled document. Kept for the record only; do not distribute from this file.

Adjudicated by a pass holding only Egypt's own files.

**Coordinator note, not for the receiving court.** This was the strongest of the three positions run against Egypt in the period. Resident factors of twenty-five to fifty years' standing, buying gum, hides and coastal grain at Egyptian East African harbours, are in the *same market* as the crown's provisioning agents. They do not merely observe the provisioning, they feel it in their own prices and their own short deliveries, which is the most reliable form of commercial reporting there is. Egypt's ocean-hull yards have stood on that coast beside the hardwood since the first century, so timber and cordage flow is native to the place and legible.

Outcome: substantially correct and complete within the cover's reach; coarse on quantity, as a merchant's account must be; one to two seasons old on arrival, which the tasking already anticipated. Out of reach: naval orders, crown paper, any count beyond the factors' own harbours, and where the hulls go after they turn south. The crossing is a closed compartment.

Egypt did not feed anything false here. Its two retained standing accounts concern coffee origin and electrical research, neither of which touches this ground, and its new doctrine forbids maintaining a false account about an observable harbour at all. The council has formally accepted that its colonial programme cannot be hidden and refuses to slow it in order to try. Egypt ran no operation against the Maurya this period and named none on that coast; its watchfulness there is directed against Mauryan *ships*, the patrol line having been pushed to thirty degrees south after the earlier naval probe, and not against resident merchant houses of that age. The new officer, placed as a junior man on the accounts of a long-established house, is well hidden. The fragility the court identified in using never-before-used commercial factories is real but was not tested this period. All three elements survive.

The tasked question that is answered decisively is the last one, and the answer is that there is no second provisioning because the first never stopped.

**Return of the factors on the African coast, carried by the season's ships. The account closes with the last completed season.**

**Coastal grain, our own buying, felt and seen.** Grain has not been had at the old price for years. At three of the four harbours we work, the crown's agents contract the standing crop before harvest, and what reaches open market is the remainder. We have paid over half again what this house paid when the elder factor took the books. Twice we have filled the Oman order short and made it up with millet we would not formerly have bought. The agents pay in coin on the spot and do not haggle.

**Gum** we buy as we always did; that trade is untouched. **Hides** are dearer and harder, the crown's people taking the heavy ox hides for harness and gear before we see them.

**Timber, seen.** The yards on this coast have always cut hardwood. What is new to our eyes is how much goes aboard as cargo rather than into the hulls on the stocks: squared baulks and sawn plank stacked on the quay under guard and lifted into transports. One quay we pass daily was clear ground when the junior man came and has been stacked timber every loading season since.

**Cordage, seen and handled.** The rope-walks work into the night, and the crown's rope is spoken for before it is laid. This house has twice bought cordage in Oman and carried it down here to serve its own ships, which no factor of this house has done before.

**Water, seen.** The coopers take no private work in the loading months. Casks stand by the row at the watering places; the writer counted rows and gave it up. Above two of the harbours the springs have been walled and piped down to the quay within the last twenty years, and the pipe is stone and iron, not a made ditch.

**What sailed, and in how many hulls.** In the harbour where the writer sits, over the loading months of the last completed season, he watched eleven deep sailing transports load and stand away south, and two steam vessels in company with them at different times. In earlier seasons he has counted fewer, and in one, more. He offers no number for the harbours he does not sit in; the factor at the second house reports the same kind of loading there and keeps no tally.

**What went aboard, seen.** Whole families of the inland provinces, brought down by river and by rail with their tools and their stock. Gangs of men carrying papers in their hands. Soldiers, in smaller numbers than the families. Cattle, goats and fowl. Ploughs, mattocks, nails. Seed in sack. Grain, salt fish and oil for the passage. And in the last two seasons, horses in stalls built up on deck, brought down from the highland studs, which we had not seen in the earlier loadings.

*Told,* by the men themselves: the families say they come from the copper and the gold country. The men with papers say they have finished terms in the mines and are owed land where they are going.

**The season.** The loading falls in the same months each year, at the turn of the wind toward the south, and the harbours are empty of those hulls before the rains. It does not vary, and the crown's buying of grain and cordage runs ahead of it by about a quarter of the year.

**Whether the hulls come back.** They do, but not in the season they left and not together. Hulls this house watched go out have come in again two and three seasons later, riding light, with their own crews and with men aboard who are done and coming home. Some we watched go out we have not seen again, which the mates say of any long voyage. The returning men spend money in the town and talk: of a long passage, of fever at the new places heavy in the first year and less after, and of land given out and held.

**Whether a second provisioning is under way.** It is not a second. It has not stopped since the elder factor's time. It is greater now than in the years the court asks after: more hulls in a season, more of the grain taken before market, the rope and the timber harder to come by, and the horses new.

*Notes by the writer.* All of the above the writer or the factors saw themselves or bought and paid for themselves, except the talk of the far places, which is what returning men say in wine shops, and the inland origin of the families, which is what the families say of themselves. Nothing here comes from any officer of the crown or any crown paper; the factors have never been shown an order and did not ask to be. This account leaves with the season's ships and will be a season behind at reading.
