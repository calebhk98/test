# Intelligence return to Egypt, operation against Rome, 525–550

Adjudicated against the rebuilt Rome turn by a pass holding only Rome's own files. Supersedes the earlier adjudication, which was run against a turn that no longer exists.

**Coordinator note, not for the receiving court.**

*Question one — does Rome hold that Egypt has crossed?* Rome holds **neither** proposition Egypt offered. The Senate logged the two anomalies from the southern survey as unresolved and nothing in twenty-five years bore on either. It keeps two readings alive: a race to the Americas, and a centuries-old Atlantic gold artery running metal north with its return leg offshore. It certainly does not hold Egypt confined to the African coast; that belief died with the survey. The honest answer is "open, and taken seriously enough to spend against."

The formal disposition sits in the Senate file out of a port network's reach. But Rome's *conduct under* the uncertainty sits almost entirely in ports: the standing warship authority of 531, masonry batteries at the western harbours from 546 laid expressly against ships rather than islanders, and a standing watch with glass and balloon on the Canaries and Madeira counting hulls passing north and west. Add Rome's documentary habit — commercial gazettes, and the settled practice whereby the faction that loses prints its case. The fiscal conservatives lost the 536 motion to cap the western charge; losing arguments about western spending necessarily rehearse the reason for the spending. The shape of a closed deliberation is legible from a market stall.

*Question two — the lost ships.* Emphatically yes, and the easier of the two. Rome concluded in the previous turn that the power which destroyed five vessels over fifty years was Egypt or Egyptian subjects, that it lost them to a defended Egyptian coast, and that Egypt spent a century ensuring Rome blamed a fiction. This is not a technical secret but a grievance, and grievances live in ports.

*Outcome.* Substantially correct on both, coarse in texture: no figures, no dates, and the network cannot fully separate "government has not decided" from "port talk has decided for it" — so it reports both and marks which channel carries which. No detection. Egypt's untasked military-technical placement in the northern industrial provinces was neither used, approached, nor implicated; every item is sourced to a quayside, customs house, underwriters' table or printed sheet.

**From the trade-corridor stations of the Roman Atlantic and Iberian ports. Two channels, checked against one another.**

## On what the Roman government holds about the western ocean and ourselves

*[Both channels agree, and this is the firm part of the return.]* The question is talked about openly in the ports, and it is talked about as a question. It is not talked about as a thing the government knows. Men on the quays at Gades and Olisipo will tell you plainly that they do not know whether we are already across, and will then tell you what they think, and no two of them think the same.

*[Insider, at second hand to him — he had it from those who come down from the capital with the sailing orders.]* He is told that the matter stands in the office papers as not decided. His words were that they have it written both ways: that our ships going north up the African shore may be going to the crossing, and may equally be carrying metal home the way they always have, and that the men whose business it is to say have declined to say. He hears no order given anywhere that proceeds from a settled belief that we are on the far shore, and he hears no order that proceeds from a settled belief that we are not.

*[Resident, read in printed sheets and confirmed in the coffee houses.]* The argument over the money for the western sailings is fought in print, and the side that lost it printed its case. Their complaint is the cost. But their case sets out, to argue against it, the reason the money is voted, and that reason is us: that Egypt learned of the western land almost at once, that Egyptian yards went to a great building, and that Egyptian ships were seen moving north in numbers toward the latitude a fleet crosses from. The sheets that answer them do not deny any of it. *[The agent notes he is passing what was printed, not what was decided.]*

*[Resident, heard directly at the Canaries station, and carried by this channel only.]* There are watchers set on the Canaries and on Madeira with glasses, and a tethered balloon flown from the high ground, and their business is to count large hulls passing north and west. Captains on the western run are instructed to report any such hull, its heading and its size, on making port. This is not concealed; it is a standing instruction and the clerks speak of it as routine.

*[Insider, heard directly from a lading clerk.]* Cut stone and heavy pieces have been going out in the western transports these last several seasons for works at the harbours on the far side. The clerk's own words, when asked why so much for a country of people who work no metal, were that they are laid for ships and not for islanders.

*[The agent's inference, offered as inference.]* A government that has genuinely not decided is nonetheless spending as though the worse of its two readings were the true one. He would put it that Rome has not concluded we are across, has entirely ceased to believe we are penned on the African coast, and has chosen to build against the possibility rather than wait to learn. *[He notes that the insider's account and the printed argument agree on this, and that he cannot get behind them to the deliberation itself.]*

## On the ships lost south of Morocco

*[Both channels agree, and this is the harder-edged of the two returns.]* The old account is dead in the ports. For a generation the loss was laid to reefs, to the current that carries a crippled hull away so that no wreckage comes home, and to shallow boats of coast peoples. No one on the Atlantic runs credits that any longer.

*[Resident, at second hand, from captains and shipowners in the Iberian ports who had it from men who sailed.]* The crews of the ships that went south and came home talk, and their talk has gone everywhere. What they say is that the coast is built — harbour works, towers throwing mirrored light, towns of cut stone one after another, smoke and hammering on the waterfronts, warships lying among the cargo hulls. What is said of the losses now follows straight from that: that no canoe people did it, that the coast has masters, and that the masters are ours. It is said further that a single ship kept company with them the whole length of the voyage, was relieved by another and another, never closed, never fired, and let them go home; and the men who repeat this repeat it as the thing that settled the question for them.

*[Insider, heard directly among the naval clerks.]* He hears the same thing said in the offices, and hears it said without argument, as a matter no longer discussed. He adds one thing the quayside does not: that there is real bitterness in it, because the account Rome had believed for a century was, they now say, given to Rome deliberately and was false. That grievance is also in the printed sheets.

*[Resident, heard directly in the ordinary way of his trade, and carried by this channel only.]* No shipowner will now put a hull down that coast at any charter, and the underwriters will not price the risk. This, he says, is the plainest measure of how hard the belief has set, harder than anything a man says aloud.

*[The agent's inference.]* He judges the belief settled and judges it attached exactly where the tasking asks: not to a hazard of water, but to the works, the towers and the garrisons of that coast, and to the power that raised them.
