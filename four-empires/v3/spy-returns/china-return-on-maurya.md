# Intelligence return to Han China, operation against the Maurya Realm, 525–550

> **SUPERSEDED.** Adjudicated against a turn state that no longer exists. Replaced by `against-maurya-525-550.md`, which re-adjudicates both operations against the settled document. Kept for the record only; do not distribute from this file.

Adjudicated against the rebuilt Maurya turn by a pass holding only the realm's own files. Supersedes the earlier adjudication.

**Coordinator note, not for the receiving court.**

**Task 1, guns on hulls — success in kind, worthless in scale.** True position: cannon-armed warships from thirty or forty to about a hundred and ninety, carriage and mounting standardised across three hull classes, eastern fleet re-established at a hundred and ten. The guns are mounted and fired; "reinforced and carrying nothing" is flatly wrong. The factor can count only what stands in one yard. One honest complication preserved rather than smoothed: the realm's long naval problem was never the gun but the carriage, so yard talk about *stiffening hulls* is genuine shipwright's talk about real work. A factor hearing that in one season and watching a piece fired in a later season files both, unreconciled. A true statement that misleads.

**Deliberately excluded and flagged to the coordinator only:** ninety of those hulls carry marine engines and the southern packet now runs year-round through the monsoon. China did not ask about propulsion. It is the single most valuable thing this cover could have seen this period, and it is unrequested, so it is out of the report.

**Task 2, powder by saltpetre price — the crux, and the rebuild hardened it.** Saltpetre forms naturally on the Gangetic plain, is gathered by scraping, is abundant beyond any rival's holding, and is not among the state's monopolies nor a bulk-quoted caravan commodity. **Seven of the eleven new powder works were sited on the plain, on top of the deposit itself.** The works go to the salt; the salt does not go to market. Consistent-quality output rose to **six times** the level of 525 while nothing needed to happen to any price a merchant could observe. The price half of the indicator therefore returns something true about itself and misleading about the quantity, and that contradiction is left standing in the factor's own voice as his supposition, because the contradiction is the finding.

What China did not name, and what carries the task: eleven works of any kind need construction, brick, timber, labour gangs, cooperage, charcoal and hauled sulfur. That building and that haulage are commercial phenomena visible from the road. **The "size of the works" half succeeds** at low precision.

**Task 3, depot and road work — the best-placed of the three.** Sogdia is Maurya-held ground and the factors' own country, and this period the realm founded three garrison towns on the Bactrian and Sogdian road, *each with a walled market* — it built commercial districts along the very road the tasking concerns and invited traders into them. Road rebuilt with two new relay stations, five forward granaries raised from sixty to a hundred and twenty days of ration. A caravan man's truest instrument, how many days the crossing takes and how many carts he passes, measures exactly what China wants measured. Where it stops: powder magazines went from four to eleven and fixed cannon on the river from fifty-or-eighty to two hundred and forty, all behind the limit line, returning second-hand or not at all.

**Risk.** The realm's administration and intelligence line stands at 240 million, exactly where it stood in 500, while revenue rose from 2,280 to 2,760 and the governed span expanded enormously — flat coin over far more ground is thinner coverage per unit. Its one visible new deployment is *outward*, an officer under factor's cover on the East African coast. The new garrison towns bring market supervisors and toll clerks onto ground that was open road, so a Sogdian asking after stores off the road is noticed and remembered. The realm's scepticism of Sogdian factors is institutional and documented, but it is scepticism, not surveillance. The decisive protection is China's own limit line: the realm takes agents where agents touch arsenals and garrisons, having itself fed men into Han frontier arsenals for two decades and lost many. China's factors were kept out of exactly that ground.

**Network survives, uncompromised.** Expect one factor moved off a quay, one turned back from a track near the river, and a slightly more watched trade in the new frontier markets.

*Compiled at the western commanderies from the seasonal returns of the hired factors of the Sogdian houses. Three seasons are gathered here; the latest reached the commandery a season and some weeks after the season it describes. The returns are of uneven quality and two of them disagree. Both are set down.*

## I. Of guns upon ships

**From the factor working the eastern river road down to the delta yards, two seasons.**

*Seen.* In the first season he was at the yards below the last bridge on business of cloth and iron. Eleven hulls stood on the slips or at the fitting quay. Five of them had square ports cut low in the sides, closed with shutters, four to a side where he could see one side whole. Smiths on the quay were making rings, bolts and heavy iron straps, and he watched a piece of iron the length of a man craned down onto a timber carriage set on small solid wheels and run inboard on planks. He counted three such carriages lying finished on the quay awaiting hulls.

*Told.* A shipwright's man in that season told him the pieces were set into the hulls to stiffen them, that a ship of that build works itself loose in a sea, and that the iron was there for weight and strength and was not a thing fired from the deck. The factor writes that the man said this readily and without being pressed.

*Seen, the second season.* Returning after the wet, he lay two nights in a boat in the roads waiting on a tide. On both mornings before full light he heard firing from a hull standing off, spaced slow, he counted eleven or twelve reports the first morning and did not count the second. On the second morning he saw the smoke from her side and saw a piece run out at a port and run back in. He states plainly that this is the same class of hull he had been told carried the iron only for stiffening, and he does not undertake to reconcile the two accounts. He adds that he was put off the outer quay by a yard officer when barrels were being lighted out to that hull under an escort, and went without argument.

*Told, at second hand, and to be weighed accordingly.* A ship-master of the southern coast trade, met at the river mouth and drunk at the time, told him the yards of the far southern coast are busier than the eastern yards and have been for some years, and that the ports are cut there too. The factor has not been on that coast and passes this on as it was given to him.

*He supposes.* That the guns are not many to a ship, three or four to a side by the ports he counted, and that a yard that has three carriages lying spare is building more than it has hulls ready for. He offers to obtain iron and cordage for that trade on his own account if the commandery wishes, and the reader should know that he asked.

## II. Of the white salt, its price, and the works

**From the factor of the Gangetic road, two markets and two seasons.**

*Seen.* He priced the white salt at the great market of the capital's river town and again at a market four days up the road, in the dry season and again after the rains. It is sold in the open, in baskets, by villagers who scrape it off the ground where it comes up white on the fields after the dry weather. At both markets in both seasons the price stood within a little of the same, and the same as he remembers it from earlier years. It was there in quantity every day he looked for it. Buyers he saw were dyers, a man curing meat, and apothecaries taking small lots.

*Told.* A broker of that market told him there is no forward dealing in the stuff and no house that holds it against a rise, for the reason that a man who wants it walks out beyond the town and takes it up himself, and that no one has ever grown rich on it.

*Seen.* On the same road, which he has ridden for years, he passed three walled compounds new to him between one town and the next, set back off the road with brick kilns burning beside two of them and gangs at work on the walls of the third. He did not turn off the road toward any of them. In a later season he passed the same stretch and the third stood finished and walled with its gate shut.

*Told.* Carters at the waystation on that stretch told him their season's hire had been hauling to such places. He asked what they carried and was told charcoal chiefly, in great quantity, and sulphur brought up from the south in sacks, and timber and cooperage. One carter said he had made the same short run six times between the rains and had never once been inside the gate. Another said there were more such places away to the south on the plateau roads, well off his own run, and he had this from his brother.

*He supposes.* Being a merchant, he supposes that where a thing lies cheap and unmoving in the market and no house troubles to hold it, there is no great buyer in the country for it, and he notes that he has watched that price for years and it has not stirred. He sets this beside the carts and the compounds and says he cannot make the two agree, and that he reports both as he found them.

## III. Of the northern road and what stands behind it

**From the senior factor of the Samarkand house, one long season, and from a second man on the same road.**

*Seen.* The mountain road is cut wider than he has known it and metalled in the bad stretches, with two new relay houses of fresh stone standing where there were none, each with water and a walled yard for beasts. His caravan made the crossing in noticeably fewer days than his best previous crossing and lost no animal to the road. Work gangs were still on the upper stretch when he passed.

*Seen.* Three towns new on the road below the passes, which he gives as his ears took them: Ashvasthala, Girivraja, Uttarakota. Each is walled and each has a walled market within it, and he traded in two of the three, selling hides and buying nothing worth the carriage. The markets are new-built, the stalls are let by an officer of the town, and a name and a house must be given to take a stall. He gave his and was not questioned further.

*Seen.* Great grain stores behind the passes, four of them on his own road, long low buildings with raised floors, two plainly new and two enlarged. He saw grain going in by cart at a season when he would expect it to be going out.

*Told.* A clerk at one store, taken with drink and glad of company, told him the order now is that the store shall be full through the winter and stand full into the next season, where in his father's time it was empty by spring and refilled from below. The factor did not press him for measures and the clerk gave none.

*Seen.* At a waystation two days short of the river he sat three days on a lame beast and counted the carts passing east in daylight. He gives his tallies for the three days as thirty-one, twenty-six, and forty-four, and says the road is fuller than he has ever ridden it and that the traffic is nearly all one way and nearly all state hire, the beasts branded and the carters paid by an officer at the stage.

*Seen, and the end of his going.* Where a made track leaves the road toward the river he turned up it a short way looking for water and was turned back by a mounted man who was civil and did not ask his name.

*Told.* A carter at that stage told him the loads off that track go to stone houses standing back behind the bank, and that there are more of them now than when he began the run, and that the men who work them are not soldiers of the line but a service of their own that lodges apart. Asked how many, the carter said he had never counted and would not.

*Told, from the market and worth what market talk is worth.* In two of the three new towns he heard it said that the pieces standing on the bank have been added to, and heard it said with satisfaction, as men speak of a thing that makes their own town safe. He heard no number he would repeat.

*He supposes.* That the whole of this road is being made ready to carry and feed something larger than it carries now, and that a merchant should expect the crossing to grow easier and the tolls to grow no lighter. He notes that the new markets are good for his house and he says so plainly, and the reader should weigh that he has reason to praise the road.

**From the second man, the same road, one season earlier, received late.** He gives the crossing as no faster than of old and the upper road as broken and under work, and says nothing of the grain stores. His return was written before the work on the pass was finished and reached the commandery after the senior factor's. It is set down here because it was received.
