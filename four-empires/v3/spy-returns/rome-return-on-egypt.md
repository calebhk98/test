# Intelligence returns to Rome, two operations against Egypt, 525–550

Adjudicated by a pass holding only Egypt's own files.

**Coordinator note, not for the receiving court.**

**Operation A, the standing Alexandria network.** Yards: *correct but coarse, and understating without the observer knowing it.* There is a structural limit the agent cannot perceive — Alexandria is not where most of Egypt's modern hull work happens. Ocean hulls have been built in the East African hardwood yards since the first century and the armour plate comes from arc furnaces roughly a thousand miles up the Congo. An Alexandria-only measurement of "Egypt's home yards" is true about Alexandria and materially understates the empire. Westward provisioning: *substantially correct* at the level of large, continuous, families, farm stock and seed. The crossing route, its latitudes, currents and timings: *nothing*, and not obtainable at a quayside.

Egypt fed nothing false here. It formally abandoned standing false accounts on anything a rival can go and look at, retaining them only for coffee origin and electrical research, and its instrument on harbours is now refusal to discuss — which does not stop a merchant watching a quay. Egypt also assumes as a matter of course that the Roman mission collects, having counted some thirty collectors in the 365 relief fleet, so ordinary commercial nosing draws no special action.

**On the silent branch: Rome's quarantine worked.** Egypt caught a Roman agent in port in the previous period whose questions were all directed at the Atlantic coast, Egyptian ships there, and any Egyptian knowledge of a western landmass. That is almost certainly the branch that went silent. Because Rome walked no replacement into the slot, Egypt's lead dead-ended. Not detected this period.

**Operation B, the Berber gold traders, new in 548.** The tasked question of what the northbound ships carry **has no observable answer from this cover** — a Berber gold trader has no business on a garrisoned Egyptian Atlantic quay, no sight of a manifest, and no reason to be there. The return is therefore silent on it rather than wrong about it; no plausible answer has been manufactured. What returns is one to two seasons of one route, not a survey, which is what Rome's own expectation of nothing for a decade correctly anticipated. Routes: first-hand and correct. Volume: coarse and correct in direction.

True position on the gold: Egypt holds its output deliberately moderate. It has the recovery chemistry and the plant to drive its three fields far harder and declines to, specifically so that a rival cannot weigh its bullion. The stream sits near a tenth of revenue and is roughly flat. Heavy northbound Atlantic shipping is predominantly metal from the interior works, latex, timber and returning colonial transports; gold moves by that road too, but the bulk of it is not gold.

Both networks survive to the next period, intact and unremarked.

# A. From the office of the mission at Alexandria

## The yards

*Seen.* On the open ground east of the commercial basin, where the ways are not walled, four hulls in frame and two more planked and being fastened, on the day the writer walked it. All broad and deep, of the build that carries cargo and people, not the low fast sort. Two years ago that ground carried fewer, and the sheds behind it were open where they are now roofed.

*Seen.* From a lighter crossing the basin, three large hulls lying alongside within the war harbour with sheer legs over them and iron being worked on two. The writer offers three because three is what he counted; the wall cuts the view and lighters are not suffered to lie there.

*Seen.* Flat cut iron plate, in pieces two men cannot lift, coming ashore from vessels arriving from the south and going by dray toward the yards. Our own people remark that the pieces are all of a size, as though cut to one pattern.

*Handled.* Two Roman houses here were asked this year for hemp, tar and sailcloth in quantities they could not fill. Cordage and sailcloth in this city cost more than last year and delivery runs months out. A third house declined a contract for cooperage timber on the ground that it could not get the wood.

*Told,* by a yard labourer paid by our clerk: shipwrights' wages are up, men have been brought in from the Delta towns, and the yard now sets a man to make one piece over and over against a wooden pattern rather than fit each piece to its own hull.

*The writer's own guess, offered as a guess:* that more transport than warship is being built, on the count of what stands in the open; and that the warship work is being armoured, on the plate.

## Westward provisioning

*Seen.* Recruiters taking names in the Rhakotis quarter, offering land, passage and remission of tax to families willing to go to a temperate country across the ocean where wheat, the vine and the olive are said to grow. Two houses have had steady customers wind up their affairs and sell their stock to go.

*Seen,* at the western basin over the loading months: families with children and household goods; sheep and cattle penned on deck; ploughs, mattocks, nails and cooperage; seed grain in sack, and vine and olive stock in tubs packed in damp straw; and water in more casks than any run to the strait would want.

*Seen.* Such convoys forming and standing west out of the harbour, four to six merchantmen together with a steam vessel in company. These are counts of what our people watched from the mole on the days they were there.

*Told,* by a ship's mate paid for his talk: he has made the passage twice; the ships go through the strait and down the outer coast of Africa to a harbour on that coast, where people and stores are put into other bottoms; he was not carried further. He would say nothing of winds or reckoning and claimed not to have been on deck for that part.

*Quay talk, unverified:* that the country grows wheat and the vine, and that men have wintered there.

*Note by the writer.* The yard matter cannot be pushed further without approaching the walled harbour. Our people cannot go there and will not attempt it.

# B. Return through the gold purchase, two seasons

Questions put as a buyer's curiosity.

## Where the roads run, from their own travel, seen

The southern end of every road they use lies in country under Alexandria's administration. Gold is not picked up loose. It is sold to them at the southern termini by licensed brokers who weigh it out, at a price the broker sets, against a written allotment. Toll is taken at the stages, and the stages are the wells; the wells are held, with a walled post and a few soldiers at each of the larger.

Their own roads come north and west to us. There is another mouth to the same trade bending east through a chain of desert wells to the coast the Egyptians hold beyond Cyrenaica, and the eastern mouth takes a share of the metal that never comes our way.

At three of the stages, and at both termini, they have seen towers with mirrors that are worked by day, and poles carrying wire alongside the track for part of the way. They were told the mirrors carry messages faster than a rider. One trader has watched a mirror worked and says the flashes went on a long while.

They report the roads have not moved in their lifetime. The wells govern the road, and the wells do not move.

## Volume, from their own seasons and from what older men told them

Their own two seasons: as many caravans on the road as they expected, and the wells crowded at the stages where two roads meet.

*Told,* by three caravan masters of an age to remember their fathers' trade: there are as many camels on these roads as there were, and more, but the loads are not what they were. Salt, grain, hides and worked goods have grown. The metal in a season has not. One said his father's house was allotted about what his own house is allotted now, and that the broker's answer has been the same all his life: this is the weight, take it or leave it.

*Told,* by a broker at a southern terminus, to one of our traders over a settled account: a part of each season's metal is taken by the administration itself and is never offered at market, and that part is not smaller than it was.

*Their own reading, offered as a trader's guess:* that the weight they are permitted to buy is set for them and does not follow the ground, because in a bad season and a good one alike the allotment scarcely moves.

*Note.* These are two seasons on one road. The traders will carry the same questions again and expect to answer them better in five or six years than in two.
