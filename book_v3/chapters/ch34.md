# Chapter 34: From Scratch

The man who flew was no use to me anymore, so I went looking for work as the man who could add.

The autumn the new emperor finally came up the road into the city, I started again at the bottom of doors I would not have crossed the street for five years before. Not senators; I had learned what a senator's open door was worth that spring. I went to the men who keep the men who keep the rolls. Procurators' freedmen. Clerks of bureaus whose names I had to ask twice and still got wrong. Functionaries with a half-room and a chest of tablets, who in the old days would have crossed a yard to be presented to the Thulean and now kept me standing while they finished a column of figures, because keeping a famous man standing while you finish a column is one of the few pleasures a small office allows.

I learned the geography of waiting. The portico, where the cold comes up through the stone into the soles of your feet and you keep shifting your weight and pretending you are not. The inner room you sometimes reach, where the clerk talks past you to the doorway, watching for someone better. The message left and not answered, which is its own room you wait in for days without leaving your house. I was thirty-seven, and my knees had begun to keep their own account of the cold. Day after day I was told the procurator was at his villa, that the bureau did not see private men in the morning, that my matter had been noted and would be raised at a convenient time, the Roman for never.

---

The enterprise no longer needed me to be anybody, which was the reason any lamp burned at all while I stood in porticoes being nobody.

Marcia had seen to that, the spring the city went quiet. The contracts came under her name, *Marcia, liberta*, the widow of Caepio, a respectable nothing on the rolls, a woman who factored cloth and rented dry storage and had a little press that printed schoolmasters' sheets. The new figures were hers to license. The contest belonged on paper to nobody, dedicated to a goddess, run by a widow and a freedwoman, which the men who watched such things found beneath their watching. I had spent twenty years making my name the hinge everything turned on, and now the most useful thing about me was that my name was nowhere on it. It worked because the two of them were better at it than I had ever been, and I had been good.

I came back from a wasted morning at the grain-bureau door and found them in the tally-room with a carter who was being loud about a short load. Marcia let him be loud. She had the two day-books open side by side, hers and the wharf's, both in the new figures, and when he ran out of breath she laid her ink-stained finger on a line.

"Eleven loads booked in. Nine delivered to weight. One short by a third, which I am charging you. The eleventh you will tell me about now or I will have Tyche find it, and Tyche enjoys finding things, which you will not enjoy."

The carter looked at Tyche. She was at the wall with a tablet and did not look up from it. "It went out the Ostian gate the same morning under a different bill," she said, in the flat clean city Latin she has never once slowed for anyone. "Same mules, same driver. One man paid twice for one load." She turned the tablet around, the columns ranked and totaled. "He's not arguing the third. He's arguing the eleventh, and the eleventh is the one that hangs him."

The carter paid the third and was gone inside the time it would have taken me to find the right tablet.

I said something with my name in it, about how the contract had been mine before it was hers. Marcia put the books square on the table with the edges lined up, which is what she does instead of raising her voice. "It was yours. And yours is a word that gets a man's house looked at this year. So it is mine, and the bureau when it comes will see a widow with clean accounts and no dead emperor in her family. You can have your name back when having it is not something men die of." She picked up the stylus and went back to the column. "Go wash your feet. You smell like the portico."

Tyche, at the wall, said nothing, but the corner of her mouth moved. She had moved the box again that month, the leaves in the homeland script, the ratios only she could read, and had not told me where. I had stopped needing to know, and minded it less than a younger man would have.

---

I went to Heras because I could not afford to, and could afford the staying away even less.

I had kept away from his door for the better part of a year. I told myself it was protection. Heras was old, sixty-five and more, his hands no longer steady on the lancet, his household small, and to be seen my friend could cost him in that season. It was also the plain cowardice of not wanting to see his face when I came at last to ask. I had let the months go by trading that against my need, and my need had won.

He was in his garden, a strip behind the house with a fig tree and pots of the herbs he still grew himself though he could have bought them. He watched me come through the gate and did not get up.

"The Thulean remembers the road to my house," he said. "I had a wager with myself about the season. I have lost; I said winter."

"Heras."

"Sit. You look like a man who has been standing." He poured posca without asking, the cheap sour stuff he drank to spite the wine his patients sent him. "You stayed away to keep the smell off me. I worked that out in the summer. It was thoughtful and it was insulting, and we will not discuss it. You have come to ask me for something. Let us get to it."

I told him the arrangement. That the work survived under Marcia's name but could go on printing schoolmasters' sheets for fifty years and never again get near a man who could decide anything. That the new figures were the one thing I had that a man cleaning up a war's debts might want, and that I could not get them in front of such a man, because every door to him was guarded by men whose whole art was keeping the marked out, and I was as marked as a man could be.

Heras let me finish. He ate a fig, slowly, which from him was a form of argument.

"You want a Greek," he said.

"I want a way in."

"Those are the same sentence, in this city, this year." He wiped his fingers on the cloth at his belt. "The boy emperor keeps Greek freedmen at his accounts, because they are literate and cheap and have nowhere to run, and the rolls of his treasury are thick with men who learned their letters in Pergamon and Alexandria and would no more trust a Roman senator than fly." He looked at me sideways. "You will appreciate the figure of speech."

"Do you know one."

"I know a man who knows one." He said it without weight, an old man passing something across a garden, and it was the most valuable sentence anyone had said to me in a year. "A patient. A liver I keep an eye on. He has a man he calls a nephew, the way we call any younger man from the same town a nephew when we are far from it. The younger one is in the *fiscus*." He gave the Latin its full dry weight. The treasury. "A clerk who keeps accounts of accounts. Not a great man, but one who is in the room when the great men decide how much there is, which this year is the only room that matters, because the boy has just struck a line through more debt than I can count and somebody has to know what is left." He picked up another fig and did not eat it. "I will not pretend I am glad to do this. You will go in clean, as a man with a method, and leave my name and the patient's out of it, and if it goes wrong you will not have heard the word *fiscus* in this garden. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Then I will send a word." He ate the fig after all. "It is good you came. I am too old to be left out of the dangerous part of your life. It is the only interesting part."

---

The man's name was Eudemus.

I had it from a slave who came to the wharf with no message, only a place and an hour, a side door behind the imperial offices, an hour after they closed and a small man could be seen by another small man without either being noticed.

He was younger than I expected, not yet forty, a long careful Greek face, ink to the second knuckle on both hands, a freedman of the emperor's house by the cut of his plain good tunic. He did not stand when I came in. Tablets in racks to the ceiling, the smell of wax and old papyrus, a single lamp trimmed close because oil is reckoned even in the emperor's bureau, especially this year.

"You are the Thulean," he said. In Greek. A test, I understood later. I answered in the careful slow Greek Heras had spent twenty years beating into me, and he heard the gaps and went on in Latin, which told me he wanted no part of pretending we were countrymen. "The one who flew. The one with the burning tube. The one whose marks the fish-sellers use. And the one who is on a list, in a chest, in a building I could walk to from here, as a freedman of the dead emperor's house with too many friends among the wrong men."

"I had friends among the wrong men," I said. "Most of them are dead or have stopped knowing me. It has been a quiet year for my friendships."

The recognition one careful man gives another who has said the true hard thing first moved behind the long face, calm as a figure in a column. He went on as if I had not spoken.

"I have read your marks," he said. He pulled a tablet from the rack without looking, a man who knows where everything is. Two columns, the same sum done two ways, the Roman numerals crabbed and the new figures clean beside them, written in his own ink. "I taught myself in an evening. A child could learn it in a week. That is the first thing I want to say to you. It is not clever. A clever thing I would not trust, because a clever thing is a place to hide a lie." He set the tablet between us. "You gave Rome a thing that cannot hide one. Put the figures in a column and a man cannot bill a load twice without the column showing a hole where the load should be. With the old marks a clerk who knows them can make any sum say what his master wants, and his master is at his mercy, and the emperor is at the mercy of every clerk in every province." He folded his ink-stained hands. "Do you know what the emperor did this year, before he reached the city?"

"He cancelled debts to the treasury." I had heard it forty times, the last from a fish-seller. "All of it. Burned the bonds in the Forum, they say, so no one could quietly un-cancel them."

"He cancelled what was owed because he could not be sure what was owed," Eudemus said, and for the first time there was something under the dryness. "A war's worth of accounts in a dozen hands and a dozen scripts, every one cookable, none checkable against another. You cannot collect a debt you cannot prove. So you forgive the whole and look generous doing it, and then go looking for a way to keep accounts a man cannot cook." He looked at me. "And a physician's patient mentions to him, over a bad liver, that the Thulean's marks are exactly such a way."

We sat with that.

"I want to be plain with you," he said, "because you were plain first. I am not your friend. I will not be your patron; I have nothing to patronize you with. I will risk nothing for you. If you are taken up next month I will have met you once on a matter of figures and remembered nothing else." He turned the lamp a fraction, eking the wick. "What I can do is this. The men I serve want exactly the thing you happen to have made, for their own reasons, which have nothing to do with you. If your marks can be shown to do for a province's accounts what they do for a fish-seller's slate, a day will come, not soon, when a man higher than me asks where they came from, and I will be in the room, and I will say a name. Yours, or the widow's; probably the widow's, it travels better this year." He let that sit, the casual knowledge that my own name had become a liability a careful man would route around. "A name said in a room you will not be in, to men you have not met, on a day I do not control. It is a crack in a door. It is not the door."

"A crack in a door," I said, "is more than I have had since spring."

"Then we understand each other." He pushed a clean tablet and the stylus across the table. "Show me the marks doing something a clerk would call hard. A real account, with a remainder and a fraction and a running balance. If it is a trick, I will know inside an hour, and you will have wasted an evening, which is the cheapest thing you can waste this year."

I did the figures. My hand was not as steady as Tyche's; I flubbed a carry and caught it and ground it out, the way I had in front of Heras in a cell twenty years before, and he watched me catch my own error with something almost like approval, a man being shown a method that has no hiding place in it even for the one who brought it. When I finished he looked at the column a long time, then scraped the wax flat with the back of the stylus, every line, until the tablet was blank.

"I keep nothing in your hand," he said. "I will redo it in mine. You will not come here again unless I send. You do not know my name."

"I do not know your name," I agreed, and went out into the dark with a crack in a door behind me, which I carried the way you carry a coal begged off a fire, cupped, afraid of the wind.

---

I told Marcia and Tyche that night, with the lamp trimmed close out of a habit I had caught off the clerk that very evening. Lucanus was asleep; the wharf had gone quiet but for the river working at the piles.

"A clerk," Marcia said, when I had given it to her flat. "A treasury secretary who will say a name he has not yet chosen, on a day he does not control, to men you will never meet." She had her hands flat on the table. "You walked home happy from that."

"I walked home with a crack in a door."

"You walked home with a clerk who was honest with you, which you are starving for, and which is not yet a way in." But she did not say it to crush it. "The figures are right for it. A treasury that cannot trust its own sums will buy a device that cannot lie, and pay in the only coin we want, the attention of a man who can decide things." She moved a tablet a finger's width, lining the edge. "So we feed the clerk. Quietly. A real account, a province's worth, both ways, until the right liver gets reminded. That is months. That is patient, and you are not good at patient. Go to the bureau's portico, not the senators'. Be the dull man with the dull marks. Let the marks be the famous one."

Tyche had been quiet, building something behind her eyes.

"He'll want the purpose under the marks, in the end," she said, and came down off the wall and sat, which she does not often do. "He doesn't want a better way to add. He wants to know the true extent of a thing too big to hold in one head, and he can't, because the treasury can't see its own size." She picked at the wax with a thumbnail. "And that's a different want than the dead emperor had. That one wanted more. This one wants to know exactly what he has, down to the last as, and stop there, and keep it. He's not a hungry man. He's a man taking an inventory."

I did not answer her at once, because something had turned over in me, a stone I had set down a year before. The map. The western lands across the blank ocean, the gambit I had carried back from the east and filed away, because you do not sell a man locking his doors a country a year's sail past the edge of the world.

But a man taking an inventory.

"Say that again," I said to Tyche. "About knowing the extent of a thing too big to hold in one head."

She said it again, slower, watching me.

"That's not adding," I said. "That's a map." The word came out too loud, and I brought it down. "It's the same want. The treasury can't see its own size; Rome can't see the world's. What if the thing no one has ever counted is the reach of the sea, and what lies past it?"

Marcia had gone very still, the way she goes when a number is doing something she has not yet decided she likes.

"You can't sell him a conquest," I said. The words came slow, finding their order. "He just paid to end one. But I was selling the wrong idea all along. You don't say, take the lands. You say, no one alive knows the true extent of the world. A man who knew it would be that man forever, the way Alexander is the man who reached the edge of the east. You sell him knowing. You sell him the inventory."

"And the army," Tyche said, ahead of me again.

"And the army." She had reached it before I had. "He's got legions gone idle, men he won't spend on conquest, and idle soldiers turn on the nearest object. You point them at a voyage instead. Ships, timber-yards, rope by the mile, a mapping. A great reaching-out that takes no province and bleeds no city. You don't ask him to expand." I sat back down; I had got to my feet without meaning to. "You ask him to find out."

The lamp guttered. Nobody trimmed it.

"That," Marcia said at last, "is a different idea than you brought back from the east. That was a war you dressed as a voyage. This is a voyage. He might hear a voyage. From a man he trusts, which you are not, which is why we feed a clerk for a year first."

"I'm not pitching it," I said. "There's no one to pitch it to. I just see how it would have to be said, if there were ever a room to say it in." The smallness of that sat in me, a man who had worked out the perfect words for a door that did not exist. "It goes in the box. With the rest of the things I can't build yet."

Tyche got up, took down a fresh tablet, and sat to write, in the homeland script only she could read, setting it down before I forgot it.

"Knowing, not taking," she said, writing. "Prestige, not province. The idle legions at a yard, not a wall. I'll keep it where I keep the rest." The stylus moved. "You should sleep. You have a portico in the morning."

---

I stood at the bureau's portico most of the mornings of that winter, a widow's agent with a fistful of sheets. Twice a doorman who had turned me away in the spring let me past without a flicker, because the man he had turned away had been somebody and this one plainly was not. I sent no tablets to great men. I waited on a crack in a door behind a clerk whose name I did not know, and once a fortnight ruined good wax working out, again, the exact words for a voyage I had no one to propose it to.

One of those mornings the slave who had brought me the place and the hour came again with no message, only a place and an hour, the same side door. I sent the boy back with a word that I would come, washed the ink off my hands at the well, and went back inside.

Lucanus was by the rack, sounding out a printed sheet, getting the seven and the one the wrong way round as always.

"That's a seven," I said, and turned his finger to it.

He set his chin down over the tablet and tried again, lips moving, working it out slow. I watched him for a moment, the particular concentration of a boy who has decided a thing matters to him. Then I went out to keep my appointment with a man who was not my friend.
