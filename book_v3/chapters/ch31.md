# Chapter 31: Vacuum

The sea did me the one kindness it had. It was fast.

The voyage I remember as a single long held breath broken into watches. I bought passage off a grain master running empty hulls back west to load again, the trade not yet caring that an emperor had died, money being the one currency in the world that does not stop to mourn. We coasted Cilicia and stood off Crete in a wind I would have called a gift if I still believed the sky took requests, and we made Puteoli in something near five weeks, which is a crossing sailors cross themselves over, and I knew better than to call it luck out loud, because the men who say a crossing was easy are the men the next sea drowns. I slept on deck against the cargo and did the only arithmetic the boat would let me do, the run of the keel against the run of the days, and every morning I made the number of days remaining smaller by one and was no calmer for it.

Celer's strap I had worked into my own kit at the shoulder, where my hand found it without my asking, and on the bad nights my hand asked.

We raised the bay at dawn, the cone of the mountain above the towns with its thread of smoke standing straight up in the still air, and the whole curve of the shore already crowded with masts. I stood at the rail and felt the want come up again, that steady frightening want to have my hands on the business before other men did, and made myself wait for the gangway like a person who had not lost his mind.

---

I went up the road to Rome with the post traffic and a hired mule, and the city told me what had changed before any man did.

It was in the gates. The gates of Rome are never quiet, the toll men shouting, the press of carts and drovers, and the toll men were shouting still, but there were more of them, and behind the toll men were soldiers with the particular stillness of men who have been told to watch rather than act. Guard, by their boots. They were not searching carts. They were watching faces, and letting you see them watch, which is the whole exercise - a man at a gate with a sword he is not using is a sentence the city reads all day without anyone speaking it aloud. I went through with the grain wagons and was not stopped, and was sweating through my tunic by the time the arch was behind me, and there had been no reason given for the sweat that a magistrate could have written down.

The markets were loud with the particular loudness of a room where everyone is talking past the subject. Too loud in the wrong registers, and then holes in the middle of the noise where no one wanted to be the one heard. I bought bread off a stall on the cattle-market side out of old habit, the same stall, and the woman who had sold me bread for years took my coin and did not gossip, and she had always gossiped, it was half of what the as bought. She looked past my shoulder while she gave me the loaf. Everyone on that street had taken up the trick of looking past the man in front of them, to see who stood behind him and whether they should be seen talking to him at all. I had been five weeks at sea and the city had learned a new way of holding its face in that time and was already fluent in it.

A wall by the meat sellers had a name scrawled fresh on it and limed half over again, somebody's name and a word, the kind of word the colleges would call impiety and the Guard would call sedition depending which got to it first, and the lime had gone on before the scrawl was even dry, by a shopkeeper who did not want that name on his wall when the wrong man walked past. I could read it now, every cursed letter, twenty years too fluent. I did not stop. Stopping to read a wall was, that month, a way of being a man who reads such things.

The emperor's name was on fresh stone and in the right temples, and the man it belonged to was somewhere east of the sea I had just crossed, on a road, coming, not here. Into the place where an emperor is supposed to stand, which is the place a whole city leans against without knowing it, other people had quietly put their hands. You felt the city find nothing solid and shift its weight, a man going downstairs in the dark putting his foot for a step that was not there.

---

The wharf yard was the wharf yard. That undid me worse than the gates had.

Pamphilus was at the gate before I was off the mule, a dozen years past his collar and the mark of it long faded, and he had a man's restraint about him that he had not had when I bought him, and he took the mule's head and looked at my face and said only, "You're thin," which is what you say to a man whose other news you are afraid of. The yard smelled of the river and of wet hemp and of the press oil, ink and lamp soot, the smell I had carried east in my head as the smell of the one place I was something other than a tool. Stichus had grown into the shoulders he used to trip over. The clepsydra still dripped in the tally-room and still kept its lying time. It was all exactly where I had left it, and that was the weight my chest could not hold, that it had gone on being itself for three years while I learned in a tent how a friend dies of a wound I had cleaned with my own hands.

Lucanus did not know me. I had done arithmetic about it on the boat and had thought I was ready and was not. He had been a baby on a hip when I marched. He was a boy now, just turned six, with Marcia's square set to his face and the bulla on its cord at his throat, and he came to the doorway because there was a stranger in the yard and stood behind his mother's leg with the frank weighing consideration children give a stranger, deciding whether I was a danger, and I understood that to him I was a man off a boat with a sea-burnt face he had never seen. Marcia put her hand flat on his head and said, "This is your father," and he considered it and was not convinced and went back inside to whatever he had been doing, and that was correct of him, and it took the legs out from under me more than the soldiers at the gate had.

Marcia did not embrace me long. She is not built for it and I was past it. She held the sides of my arms and looked at me the way she looks at a column of figures she suspects of lying, and then she sent Pamphilus for the mule and Stichus out of the room and took me into the tally-room and shut the door. Marcia says she loves you by clearing the room of everyone but the problem.

"You know," she said. Not a question. The news had beaten me by a fortnight; it had come on a faster hull. "We've known three weeks. I've had three weeks to count it. You've had how long."

"Five."

"Then you're ahead of where I thought." She sat, a wax tablet in front of her, and did not write on it. "I'll give it straight, as I'd give it to a partner who'd been at sea. You want it soft or you want it right."

"Right."

"We are worth more dead than alive to four men I can name and a college I can't sue." She turned the tablet a quarter, lining its edge to the table's edge, an old habit. "Your name is the security on every contract this house holds. Your name was good because of one man, and that man is being carried to the Campus in a jar to be burned. The Ulpius doesn't stop meaning what it means the day he dies. It stops meaning it the day the next man decides whose names he honors and whose he forgets, and we don't know that man, and the men who do know that man are not friends of ours."

"The press is fine," she went on. "The press makes money and the contest makes friends and none of that is the problem." She stood and walked to the shelf where the tally scrolls lived, her hand resting on them the way she steadied a cup set too near an edge. "The problem is the dedication. We gave the press and the figures and the contest into Minerva's keeping to get you off a charge. That was the price and I'd pay it again. But the colleges hold that now - a right of consult and a rite they say over our work every spring. In a reign that wants something to give, a right of consult is a door already standing open. We handed a man who hasn't arrived yet a way to take the whole engine and look pious doing it. Crispus has known that since the day we did it. He's been waiting for the lock to change. The lock's changing."

I sat down across from her. The clepsydra dripped. I thought of Vibenius coming on foot to this yard, telling me his house did not lean, and I thought: his house may have found a wind worth leaning into. I did not say it. She had got there before me and was waiting for me to catch up.

"And Scaeva," I said.

"And Scaeva," she said, and for the first time something moved behind the figures. "You're his man. That was your shield once. I'm not sure whose man Scaeva is, this month, and neither is he, and a man who isn't sure whose man he is, is a man you do not want holding your leash when the scores get settled." She put two fingers flat on the dead tablet. "I need the full account of what you owe him. The sulfur, the gate, the rest. Because if he goes into the fire, we are tied to him at the wrist, and I would like to know how long the rope is before I find out by being pulled."

I told her. The sulfur, the gate he had fixed, the powder he had said he wanted pointed somewhere by a friend. She listened to all of it without writing any of it, which was how I knew it was bad, because Marcia writes down the bearable things.

"Burn nothing," she said when I was done. "Not the ratios, not the cipher, nothing. A house that has burned its books in a bad week is a house that had something to burn, and the Guard can read ash." She stood. "Eat. You look like the war. The boy will come round to you. He came round to the wet-nurse and she was a horror." She put her hand once, briefly, against the side of my neck, and went to find food, leaving me with the dripping clock and the smell of ink and a sea-legs sway in a room that was not moving.

There was a dispatch on the corner of the tally bench. I had been ignoring it the way you ignore a bruise you haven't looked at yet. I unrolled it. From the overseer at the Hispanian mine, three months old by the postmark. His hand was rough but the new figures made it legible. Three days of water pouring out before the gallery opened, he had written. The pump runs. He had no other word for it; he was a man who reported facts and the fact was that the pump ran and the mine was accessible and the men were going down the ladder into dry rock for the first time in eleven years. I had been waiting for that dispatch for most of a decade. I sat with it in my lap and the clepsydra dripped and outside I could hear Pamphilus's feet going back and forth across the yard, and I thought about water running out of a hole in a hill in Hispania at the speed of physics, not caring who ran the government, and did not know what to do with the feeling, so I set the dispatch down and picked it up again and set it down.

---

Tyche I found in the press room, and she did not turn from the form she was setting. She finished locking the line before she looked, which was its own greeting - the work first, always. She was thirty-two and there was gray starting at her temple that had not been there, and her eyes went over me once, the strap at my shoulder, the burnt face, the thinness, all of it filed in a breath. She did not ask about Celer. She knew Celer. I had written her of the army in the cipher for years, the only person on earth to whom I could write a true sentence. She looked at the strap and back at my face and what she said was, "The box is where you left it. I move it when the floor needs sweeping and I put it back." Meaning the dead glass, the citizenship tablet, the powder ratios under the board only she knew. Then she said, "Marcia tell you about the dedication," and I said yes, and she said, "Good," and went back to the form.

There was a contest entry on the pile beside the press. A strip of rag paper, neatly lettered in a hand I didn't know, submitted without a name - just a mark, a circle with a cross through it. Tyche said, without looking up, "He's a dock rope-seller. Writes at night. Can't put his name on it."

"It's good?"

"Better than half the ones with names on them."

I turned it over. A story about a man who dreamed a waterway through a high desert pass, with no engines and no magic, only a team of men with shovels working both ends of the mountain at once. Plain sentences. A practical dream, the kind a man with a rope-seller's education can imagine because it is all labor, no theory. I thought about what it would take to judge it fairly against a senator's son's entry with better Latin and a worse idea, and I thought about names, and about what is lost when only the named can be heard, and I set it back on the pile.

"He should enter properly," I said.

"He's afraid of Crispus knowing where he works."

I stood with that a moment. Then: "We should have a way in without names. Let the panel read what it is before they know who sent it."

She turned from the form then, one full beat of her attention. "You're saying that now."

"I'm saying it now."

She turned back. I stood in the ink smell and was, for one breath, less alone than I had been in three years.

---

I went to Macer because there is no not going to Macer, and because his fear, when I found it, told me more than the gates had.

He was at his town house and he was old. He had always been old to me and now he was old with the solidity of a wall that has begun to settle, sixty-nine and big still in the shoulders but gone heavy at the jaw, the broken-veined face fallen in a little. He turned the gold ring with his thumb when I came in and kept turning it the whole while. He did not stop.

"The Thulean," he said. "Off the boat. Of course you are. Caesar dies and the Thulean comes home like a cat through a window." There was no meat on the joke. He was listening past me toward the door, a man who has started hearing things in his own house, in his own atrium. He was watching his own doors.

I had come for the bond between us, whatever it was, the cage and the shield, the man who had owned me and sold me to an emperor and called it being glad for me. In the bad business with the colleges he had stood at a wall and spent nothing to save me, and I had not forgiven it and had not broken with him over it, because you do not break with weather. I had expected, coming up his steps, to find that weather, the cold of a man calculating his own skin. I found the cold. I had not expected the fear under it.

"You contracted the legions for thirty years," he said, before I had said three sentences, and it came out of him sideways, almost to the ring. "Leather, timber, the grain. You know whose army that was. A man does not feed an emperor's wars for thirty years and come out the far side smelling of bread. I'm in the rolls. Everything I am is in some clerk's roll as the old man's man, and clerks' rolls don't burn, boy, that's the whole point of a clerk." He looked at me then, full on, and his eyes were a frightened man's eyes in a hard man's face, and I had known him eighteen years and never seen it. "They'll go through the houses. They always go through the houses, after. The only question is whose, and how high, and a man my age in those rolls has had a long time to make a tidy line for a new prefect to draw straight through."

"Who is they," I said.

"You don't know." He almost laughed and it died. "You've been at the bottom of the sea. The wife and the prefect run the city till the boy gets here." He never once that day said Hadrian's name; the emperor was the boy, with a contempt and a caution wrapped in the one word. "The wife. Plotina. And Attianus with the Guard, and the two of them between them hold every door in Rome, and they are not waiting on the boy to start putting their house in order, because by the time the boy comes the house had better look like he chose it. You understand what that means."

"It means they settle things before he can be blamed for them."

"It means," Macer said, and now he leaned in, the ring stilled under his thumb, "that there are names being said. In the right houses, behind the right doors. That certain men of consular rank, men who took the cities in the east, men who wanted the purple and made no secret they thought they had a better claim than a bookish ward, are going to be found to have plotted. That's the word that's already loose. A plot. Against the boy. Four of them, it's settled now, though some still say it's nonsense and nobody will touch a consular without a trial, and the man who told me that last is a fool who has not been alive as long as I have." He sat back. The breath went out of him. "Palma. Celsus. The Moor, the cavalry one, Quietus, who Caesar broke command from already. And Nigrinus - a fourth name, a long name, the one nobody was saying in the winter and now everyone is saying." He looked at his hand flat on the arm of the chair. "When the list runs that long, a prefect is counting, and a man in the count is a man going through a door he didn't choose to open."

"And that touches you how," I said, and I knew how; I wanted to hear him say it, because a frightened Macer was a thing I needed to account for.

"It doesn't touch me," he said, too fast. "I'm a contractor. I sold them rope and ate at their tables because that is the trade." He turned the ring once. "But you sit close enough to a fire and your beard smells of smoke whether you lit it or not, and a man drawing a line through the rolls does not stop to ask whose beard caught honest. So. I'm tucking in my edges. I'm calling in what's owed me quiet and owing nobody loud, and I'd give you the same advice if I thought you'd take it, which I don't, because you've never in your life known when to be small." He looked at the strap at my shoulder, the soldier's strap, and his face did something I could not read. "You came back smelling of the army. That's worse than nothing this month. Whose was it."

"A friend's."

"Friends," Macer said, like the word cost him, and did not ask the rest. And then, because he was Macer, and because the bond was a real arrangement even after the Caelian had bent it, he said, not looking at me, "If they come to my door for you, Thulean, I will tell them where you are. You know that. I've a wife of my own and an estate and a name to keep alive. I'm too old to be brave at someone else's funeral." He set the ring flat against his palm and looked at it. "But I'll tell them slow. I'll tell them at the speed of an old man's memory. That's what I've got to give you and it's more than I gave you on the Caelian, and we both know it, so don't make me say more about that than that."

He set the ring down on the chair arm and sat back, and I took what he had offered, because slow was real and I had nothing better.

---

I walked back to the wharf in the late light. Scaeva, whose man I was, did not know whose man he was. Macer would betray me slowly. The colleges held a door into everything I had built, and the lock at the top of the city was being changed by a wife and a soldier I had never seen, settling scores against names I had heard for the first time that afternoon. That was my standing. I had come five weeks across the sea to stand on it.

At the yard the lamps were lit and Lucanus, who did not know me, fell asleep in the corner over a wooden horse while the grown people talked low, and at some point Marcia carried him to bed and I watched her do it, my son carried past a father he had not chosen and would have to learn, and I thought, with the cold clear part of me the war had ground sharp, that everything in this room was something I could lose. The press. The boy. The woman. The figures loose in the city. All of it propped, for twenty years, on a dead man's favor, and the favor was being burned on the Campus, and the man who would decide whether the law that held my life still held it was a name on fresh stone and a beard I half-remembered being insulted in a forum, somewhere east of here, on a road, coming.

I went up to the flat roof of the shed where I sometimes went to think, where the river wind came off the water and you could see the bend of the Tiber going dark and the city behind it putting out its lamps house by house. I stood there a long while. The smoke from the mountain at Puteoli I had left five weeks behind me, and there was no smoke here, only the ordinary haze of a city cooking its supper, but I stood at the rail of that roof as I had stood at the rail of the boat, looking the one way I had to look, west and then up the road east of it where a reign I knew nothing of was walking toward us at the speed of an army, and I could no more turn it back than I could turn back the tide I had ridden in on, and I had not the first idea, standing there, what it would want of me, or whether the wanting would leave me alive.
