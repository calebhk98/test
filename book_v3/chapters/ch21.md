# Chapter 21: The Cannon That Bursts

The niter shed was a leased ruin two streets back from the wharf, far enough from anything I cared about burning, close enough that I could walk it in ten minutes and pretend I was not walking toward what I was walking toward.

I had nailed the lid on the manned balloon and meant it. I had told an emperor to his face that I would not hang another man under a bag of fire, and I had told myself a quieter version of the same promise about the white powder I'd written "???" beside on the second night I was ever in Rome, when I couldn't remember the saltpeter. Not yet. Maybe not ever. I'd been pleased with how grown-up that sounded.

What I had not understood about a vow is that you don't break it in one piece. You sell it off in slivers, each one cheap enough that you can pretend you haven't spent it.

The first sliver was the steel.

Hermes and I had been at the crucibles for going on two years and we were exactly where we'd started, which is to say we could make a blade that would shave the hair off your arm and we could make, with the identical method on the identical day, a blade that came out of the quench in pieces like a dropped cup. I would stand in his forge under the brick arch behind the cattle market with the heat coming off the fire in a wall and watch him pull a glowing billet and judge it by color, and I would have nothing to give him. No number. The whole problem was a temperature neither of us could see. There is a temperature, and we could not measure it, and so we were two men trying to bake by the smell of the oven, and one of us at least should have known better.

"The fire is the master," Hermes said, which he had said before and which had stopped being a complaint and become a kind of prayer he muttered while he worked. He was past forty, gray as iron filings, and his forearms had a written history of every spark that had ever found him.

I told him more than once that if we could get the powerful men of Rome to care about steel the way they cared about marble and frontiers and their own faces in bronze, we would have the money and the men to chase the temperature until we caught it. He didn't disagree. He also didn't see how a man got a senator to lie awake at night over the carbon in a crucible, and neither did I, for a long time.

That was the second sliver. The realization, which arrived slowly and which I welcomed because it was useful to me, that the steel chokepoint gated everything. Not just blades. Springs, which I'd wrecked a small fortune of soft Roman copper trying to make. Fine instruments. A clock that would keep time, which I could not build and was beginning to suspect I would never build. The whole ladder I told myself I was climbing for the saving things, the clean water and the medicine and the books, all of it ran up against the same wall: nobody with money believed metal was a problem worth solving, because their metal held their swords and roofed their temples and did everything they had ever asked of it. I needed to ask it to do something it couldn't.

The third sliver I am least proud of, because it was a lie I told myself on purpose to make the rest go down. I told myself the secret wasn't mine to keep. The Seres had it, I was fairly sure, the silk people at the eastern edge of the maps, China, they'd had fire-powder for centuries, I'd read it somewhere or seen it somewhere, in the half-life of facts a high-schooler carries, and if a whole civilization already had it then a man holding his breath in Rome was not the cork in the bottle. He was just a coward pretending to be a wall.

I believed that, standing in my own yard at the wharf with money in my peculium for the first time in my life, money I'd earned and not been allowed to keep until a year ago. I have wondered since whether the Seres had it then at all. I think now that I was wrong, that I'd done what I always did, slid a fact two or three hundred years out of its place because the timeline in my head was a smear. But I didn't know that then, and I went into the niter shed anyway.

---

So. Niter.

I had three of the words. Charcoal I had by the cartload, the same charcoal Hermes burned. Sulfur came up from the volcano country, yellow and stinking, a curing-house and dyer's good that nobody thought twice about my buying, and I bought it through Pamphilus so my name stayed off it. The white salt I had nothing of. *Sal petrae*, Heras called it when I asked him sideways, the salt of the stones, the bloom you scrape off old cellar walls and the underside of stable floors, a curiosity that some cooks used to redden meat and otherwise good for nothing.

I sent men to scrape walls. It was the obvious move and it was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that cost me the better part of a year before I understood why. We boiled the scrapings and leached them and crystallized them and got a salt that would not work. It sucked water out of the air until the powder was a paste; it fizzled and smoked and would not bang. I didn't have the chemistry to know the word for what I'd made, but I know now it was the wrong nitrate, the calcium one, the lime in the old walls poisoning it. I only knew then that the white salt off a wall was no good, and that the white salt I needed came from a different and far worse place.

Dung.

You make a niter bed by assembling the worst compost heap in the history of the world and then tending it like a vineyard. Manure, and the bedding straw, and ash from the wood fires, and lime, and over all of it, urine, as much as you can collect, which means you become a man who buys other men's piss. We built the beds in the leased shed two streets back from the wharf and we turned them and watered them and waited, and the smell got into the timber and the timber held it and the whole street learned to walk on the other side. You wait months. You wait through a summer that bakes the stink into a presence with weight. Then you leach the rotted beds through ash, wood-ash and water dripping slow through a barrel with a false bottom, and you boil the brown liquor down, and skim it, and let it crystallize, and you get, if the gods and the bacteria you don't have a word for are kind, a handful of long white needles that taste cold on the tongue and burn the right way.

It took most of two years to get a quantity worth the name. I told the men it was for medicine. For Heras, for a cooling salt, which was not entirely a lie because I was learning everything from Heras's books and Heras's patience, and Heras knew exactly what I was doing and hated it and helped me anyway, in the careful way he did things he disapproved of, which was to make sure I did them less stupidly than I would have alone.

"You've decided," he said one evening, not a question. We were in the shed and he had a cloth over his nose and mouth and his eyes were watering above it.

"I've decided to be able to," I said. "Using it is a different question."

"Is it." He looked at the beds, at the white forming at the edges where the wet liquor had splashed and dried on a board. "You're a man who built a wall and now spends his evenings learning where the gate is. Tell yourself the gate is still shut if you like. I've watched you find this many gates."

I didn't answer him. There wasn't an answer that wasn't the lie about the Seres, and I didn't want to say the lie out loud to Heras, who would have known it for one.

---

The ratios I had to find by killing powder.

I knew there were three parts and that the white salt was most of it and the other two were small, and that is genuinely all I had: a proportion without numbers. I had a memory of three-quarters and I clung to it, and I was lucky that the memory was close. I made small batches in a stone mortar, ground fine, and burned a pinch on a tile, and most of it whooshed and stank and did nothing, and slowly, over a winter, the pinches started to crack instead of hiss.

I had a man on the grinding. His name was Geta. He was one of the wharf hands, a freedman like Pamphilus now, a wages man, broad and slow-spoken and good with a pestle, and I had told him the powder was dangerous and I had not told him enough, because I didn't know enough. I thought dry powder ground gently was safe to grind. I had read, somewhere in the smear, that you ground it wet or you ground the parts apart, and I had a memory of the warning and not of the reason, and I let Geta grind a dried cake fine in a stone mortar in a closed shed in February because I wanted a finer powder and I was impatient and I had begun, by then, to think of the work as nearly solved.

I was across the yard when it went. There was a blow to the air, a flat hard slap felt in the chest before the ears registered it, and then the shed door came off and a column of dirty smoke stood up over the roof, and I was running before I'd decided to.

Geta was alive. I will say that first because it is the fact I most needed as I crossed that yard. He was on his back in the spilled char screaming in a voice I'd never heard a grown man use, and his hands were wrong. The pestle had still been in the mortar and the mortar had still been mostly whole and the flash had gone up and out, and it had taken his face on one side and it had taken his hands, the left one worse, the fingers of it gone or going, the right one burned to the bone across the back. His eyebrows were gone. His beard was gone in a patch and smoking. The smell was the balloon smell, the burned-grease-and-hair smell from the Loaf, the one I will not describe again.

Heras came. He packed the hands and the face and gave him poppy and worked through the night, and Geta did not die, which I had no right to and took anyway. He lost the left hand at the wrist in the end, and three fingers of the right, and the sight in one eye. He could not grind anymore. He could not do most things anymore. I put him on wages for life out of my own coin and I built him a room and I have paid it every month I've had a month to pay it in, and none of that is a thing I get to feel good about. I bought a man his hands and then I burned them off teaching myself a number I could have learned from a book if the book had survived to the century I needed it in.

When Geta could talk, days later, gray and shrunken in the cot, the bandaged stumps held up off the blanket on a roll of cloth, he asked me if the powder had worked. I told him it had. He said good, in the cracked voice, and shut his one eye, and I sat with him until he slept.

You can't un-know a thing for the world and you can't un-burn a man's hands. I had wanted, when I started keeping the manure, to make the powerful men of Rome feel something true about metal. The first person my powder made feel anything was Geta, and what he felt was his own hands going.

---

I went on.

I ground the rest of it wet, with the parts kept apart until the last, as I should have from the start, and I corned it, pressed it damp into cakes and broke the cakes back to grains so the powder breathed fire instead of smothering itself, which I'd remembered too late to do Geta any good.

Then I built the cannon, knowing it would fail. That was the entire point of it. Hermes and I built it in bronze, the good gun-bronze the Romans used for the best of everything, because the argument I was making was about exactly that bronze. But before I had Hermes pour it, I spent a week writing out what the failure would prove, and what it would prove was specific: that THIS configuration failed. Not bronze. Configuration. A tube too long, a bore too wide, walls too thin for the chamber pressure, mounted in a mobile cradle that absorbed none of the recoil. I wanted them to watch a design choice fail, not a material fail. A fixed siege bombard, short-tubed, thick-walled, staked or mortared into position, could hold the force. The Seres had figured this out through centuries of trial. All I was doing was staging a wrong version to show them the right version's requirements.

Hermes hated it regardless. He'd cast ballista parts and temple doors and he knew metal the way Heras knew a fever, and he kept running his thumb along the cooling bronze and telling me the walls were too thin for what I was telling him would happen inside, and I kept telling him that was the point and watching him fail to believe me, because no craftsman wants to build a thing to break.

"You're paying me to make a flawed casting," he said.

"I'm paying you to make a perfect casting of a thing this design cannot do," I said. "When it fails, it fails the design. Not you."

He didn't like that either. But he poured it true, beautifully true, a clean tube without a flaw in it, and that was important, because a man could look at it afterward and not say it broke because the smith was a fool.

While we waited on the cooling I told him what I wanted to do after: post a prize for a smith who could bore a cylinder to a tolerance I'd define with a measuring rod, tight enough that no hair fit between the bore and the ball. I wanted no venting. I wanted a smith who could achieve that consistency twice in a row.

Hermes chewed on that. "For the tube," he said.

"For the tube. And for other things that need the same tolerance."

He turned one of the test scrap pieces in his hands. He was already thinking about how. That was the useful thing about Hermes: you did not have to explain why. You told him the number and he worried about the number.

---

I had Celer get the army men there, and Celer's word and my own name as the emperor's freedman got me people who mattered, officers of the Guard and an engineer or two and a man from the imperial household whose job I never learned, standing under an awning on a stretch of bank well downriver of everything, on a gray morning with the cold coming off the water. I'd had the cradle staked into the mud sixty paces out, and the line where the spectators stood I'd set myself, and then I'd moved it back twenty paces farther because I was the only one there who knew what was coming and I wasn't going to kill an officer of the Guard to win an argument about steel.

Celer stood by me. He looked at the squat bronze tube in the mud and then at me.

"This is the powder," he said. Not a question. He'd been on the Loaf. He knew the look of me building toward a cost.

"This is the powder."

"You swore off it."

"I swore off hanging men under fire," I said. "I didn't keep the rest of it." He let that sit a moment, and I added, because he'd earned the full account: "There's no man on this one, Celer. Just the metal."

I loaded it heavy. I loaded it heavier than the argument strictly needed, because I wanted no chance of a fizzle in front of those faces, a stone ball seated on a charge I'd packed to do exactly what it did. We touched it off with a slow-match on a long pole and everyone flinched back and the morning came apart.

I have tried to find the words for the sound. A flat enormous blow hit you in the chest and in the teeth at the same instant, before the ears finished deciding what had happened, and the bronze tube was simply not there anymore, it had opened like a flower made of knives and gone in every direction, and a piece of it the size of a fist went over our heads with a noise like a torn sky and into the river, and the stake and the cradle were gone, and the stone ball, the stone ball had gone first and gone true, out over the water, and we heard later it had crossed the whole width of the river and struck the far bank hard enough to throw mud.

There was a long quiet after, the kind of quiet that comes when a lot of frightened men are deciding not to have screamed.

Then they all started talking at once, and I let them, and I waited, and when they'd worked their way around to the question themselves, which was the whole craft of it, I gave them the answer they'd already arrived at. Here is a force that will throw a stone through a wall. Here is the best bronze in the world, poured without a flaw by the best smith I could find, and it could not hold that force for the space of a heartbeat. It tore like wet bread. But bronze is not the limit. Bronze is what I had to work with, and the limit is this design: too long a tube, too wide a bore, walls that spread the force sideways instead of driving it forward. A thick-walled short tube, fixed to the ground so it doesn't move, with a bore tight enough that the ball has nowhere to go but out, and that does not yet exist because we cannot bore a cylinder to the tolerances it requires. So what you need, if you want the thing that throws the stone, is a metal that can be bored and fitted to that precision, and the only place in Rome anyone is chasing that question is a forge behind the cattle market run by a freedman named Hermes, who is out of money.

I had the wrecked pieces gathered up and laid out on a cloth so they could handle them. A Guard officer picked up the fist-sized chunk that had gone over our heads and turned it in his hands and went pale around the mouth, and I knew I had them, and I knew exactly what I had cost to get them, and the two facts sat in me side by side.

---

It is loose now. That is the thing I can't take back and the thing I keep coming around to in the dark.

Before that morning, the powder was a secret in a stinking shed and a ratio in my head and a man with no hands. After it, the powder was a thing that powerful men had seen with their own eyes throw a stone across a river, and powerful men do not unsee a weapon. They went away from that bank knowing it could be done, and knowing roughly what it ate, charcoal and brimstone and a white salt that comes from a slow filthy patience. I had told myself the Seres already had it so it wasn't mine to hold. Whether or not that was ever true, it is true now that Rome has it, and Rome got it from me.

And the niter-bed men knew more than I'd intended. Geta knew the rough ratios before the accident, because I'd talked while he ground. Two of the other workers had been turning the beds for most of a year, asking no questions I could hear. One of the engineers who'd watched the demonstration lingered at the wrecked cradle long after the others left, picking through the pieces with a face I recognized as the face of a man measuring distances in his head. The knowledge was no longer in one frightened freedman's keeping. I'd thought I was deciding when to release it; by the time of the demonstration, some of it was already out, already moving through hands I hadn't counted.

The steel question is theirs now too, which was the point, and which has turned out to be a heavier weight than I bargained for. Men who would not have crossed the street for a crucible are sending to ask about Hermes's forge. The household man whose job I never learned came back twice. I had wanted them to care about metal and now they care about metal the way an army cares, and I have made myself the man they ask about it, which means I am more useful and more watched than I was before.

Hermes was glad, in his way. The money is coming and the money buys fuel and the fuel buys attempts and the attempts might, someday, with resources I didn't have before, catch the temperature. He has not caught it. The steel is exactly as unsolved this morning as it was the morning we cast the cannon to argue for it. I have spent a man's hands and a great deal of my own peace to buy the right to keep failing at it with better funding.

The boring prize is out now too, the specification posted: a smith who can bore a cylinder tight enough that no hair slides between the bore and a rod of the matching dimension, twice in a row from the same process, wins a hundred denarii. Hermes's apprentice is already working on it. Two smiths from the Subura sent word they were interested. I had written the specification for the cannon. But when I wrote it I had also written it for the steam cylinder I had been failing to seal for two years, and for the valve fitting that needed that same precision to seat without hand-grinding, and for gear teeth precise enough to drive a clock escapement that would actually keep count. One prize, three problems with the same root. I had not explained this to anyone. I did not need to. The borers would bore and eventually someone would bore it right, and then all three doors would open from the same key.

---

I went to see Geta after, the evening of the demonstration, because I didn't want to drink wine with officers and be congratulated. He was sitting up. The room I'd built him had a window and he had it open to the cold and the river smell, and the bandages were off the worse arm finally, the wrist healed over smooth and wrong. He heard me come in and turned the good side of his face to me.

I sat down on the stool by the cot and didn't say anything for a while, and neither did he, and the wharf made its evening noises down the lane, the carts starting up now that the day-ban was lifting, the river slapping the pilings. After a while he asked me whether the powerful men had been frightened. I told him they had. He was quiet. Then he raised the smooth healed stump a little off the blanket, out of the habit the body hadn't lost yet, and he said that was good, then. Said he hoped they stayed that way. His one good eye was on the window and the river.

I sat with him until the light went. There was a wax tablet in my lap that I'd carried from the bank without remembering picking it up, and on it, in my own English that no one in the world but Tyche could read, I'd written the ratio I'd killed his hands to learn, the three numbers, finally right. My thumb was over the figures. I did not rub them out.
