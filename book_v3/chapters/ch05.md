# Chapter 5: Latin and the Little Ones

The first word I owned outright was *aqua*. Water.

I got it the way I got everything that winter, by being thirsty and stupid in front of people. The boy who carried water for the workshop, a knock-kneed kid called Stichus, maybe ten, who did not like me, set a jug down by my bench, and I pointed at it and made a noise, and he looked at me like I'd asked him to explain fire and said the word slow, the way you talk to a dog. *Aqua.* I said it back wrong. He said it again. I got it closer. He picked up the jug and walked off, and I sat there turning the one word over like a coin, because it was the first thing I'd taken whole instead of guessing at the front of it.

That was how it went, for months. No wise old man teaching me by lamplight. Being thirsty and cold and humiliated, pointing at things, getting the word wrong, getting laughed at by a ten-year-old, trying again. The brain does it whether you want to or not, if you're drowning in the sound long enough and the alternative is staying deaf. The workshop ran on talk, the two men at the benches arguing about a joint or a price, the woman of the household, the boy, the people who came through the courtyard, a river of sound that ran over me ten hours a day, and I stood in it like a stone until the stone wore smooth enough to hold words.

I built a grammar in the loft at night, on the inside of my own skull, because I had no tablet of my own and nothing to write with that wasn't theirs. I'm decent at patterns. That's the math talking. Two years of high-school French turned out to be the most useful thing my whole education handed me, more than the calculus, more than anything, and it had nothing to do with the French words. It was one idea: that a word could *bend*, that *amo* and *amat* were the same animal wearing different collars, and the collar told you who was doing the loving. Latin bent harder. It bent six ways and then bent the bending. I called myself by the wrong collar for weeks, used the form that meant someone was doing a thing *to* Daniel when I meant Daniel was doing the thing, so I went around announcing myself as the object of every sentence, which, fair.

The sounds were their own private joke at my expense. Their *c* was always hard, a *k*, no exceptions, so every soft *c* I'd ever read came out wrong in my mouth and right in theirs, and I had to unlearn a thousand words I'd never said out loud, only read. And the *v*. The *v* killed me. They said it like a *w*. I'd point at the wine, *vinum*, and say it with the buzzing *v* of home, and they'd squint, and Heras would say it back soft, *winum*, and the whole language tilted half a degree away from everything I'd assumed. I kept slipping the *v* back in for a year. It marked me foreign before I'd finished the first word, and all I could do was flatten it down a degree at a time.

---

Heras came every few days. I had his name by the time the rains set in, and getting it was its own small idiot triumph.

He'd been "the physician" in my head for weeks, the clean-handed Greek who'd caught me lying about long division and decided he liked me for it. He came and went and I never had the language to ask the one thing you ask a person, which is who you are. Then one grey afternoon he was leaning over my bench correcting a figure I'd written, and the man who ran the workshop called across the courtyard to him by name, and I heard it land clean. Two syllables. I'd heard it a dozen times and never cut it out of the noise. This time I had enough words around it to know it was a name and not a thing.

I pointed at him. "Heras."

He straightened up and looked at me the way a man looks at a parrot that's said something true by accident, and then he put a finger on his own chest and said it again, properly, the Greek shape of it, and made me say it until I had the vowels close, and then he touched my chest and said my name, the mangled middle of it, *Danihel*, the version they could all pronounce, and we stood there formally introducing ourselves four months after we met. He found this funny. He had a way of finding things funny that never once involved laughing. The mouth went up a hair on one side and the eyes did something dry, and that was the whole performance, and I'd already learned to chase it, because it was the only applause in my life.

He talked to me in Latin mostly, slow and clean, because Latin was what I needed to survive in the street. But when a Latin word annoyed him he slid into Greek, and over the months I picked up Greek too, slower, sideways. Greek had the roots I'd half-recognized from the start, the *therm* and the *photo* buried in the long words, the bones of a hundred English words I'd never thought about. I'd reach for a Greek word and find an English one wearing it as a mask. Heras never knew the language he was handing me had already gone ahead of him by two thousand years and come back wearing my clothes. There was no way to tell him. There was no way to tell anyone anything, and that was the whole problem. It didn't get better as the words came. The problem just got more specific.

The egg man showed me that. The marketplace, Heras buying, a fishmonger's neighbor watching, and out of nowhere, out of four months of swallowed words, I hear myself say it. I point at the eggs. In my broken collar-wrong Latin, the case probably wrong, I ask whether the hen laid them or Caesar did. Because it was an emperor's price. That was the joke. Barely a joke. But it lands, the case wrong and the *v* buzzing and all, because the fishmonger next door barks out a laugh and the egg man goes red, and Heras turns and looks at me with that dry uptick of the mouth gone wider than I'd ever earned. I got my eggs at the right price and laughed all the way back to the workshop like a man let out of a cell.

Up in the loft that night the high came off it and left the drop. The people I'd have called to tell about the egg man would never be born. I lay there with the dead phone against my hip out of habit and didn't take it out, because there was nothing on it anymore, and went to sleep counting eggs in Latin to keep from counting anything else.

---

The little ones nearly got me killed, and I walked into it on purpose, because a kid was dying and I thought I knew something.

It was deep winter, the wet kind, everyone coughing, and the boy who worked the next bench cut his hand badly on a chisel and the cut went wrong. A wound here was a coin flip with death on the other side, and this one came up death's face. The hand swelled and went red and the red ran up his arm in a thin angry line crawling toward the elbow, and the boy went hot and stupid with fever, and they sent for Heras, and I, with my four months of Latin and my half a memory of a science class, decided this was the moment.

I'd been trying to get the idea across for weeks, in pieces, badly. Now I had a sick boy and a reason. When Heras came I waited until he'd looked at the arm and done the things he did, the smelling, the pressing, the muttering about the humors, and then I got the wax tablet and tried to draw it. You can't draw what I was trying to draw. I drew dots. Crowds of them, swarming, going into the cut, into the red line, up the arm. I said the words I had. *Small. Living.* I made them with my fingers, tiny, crawling, walked my fingers up his arm like little legs, and said, in the Latin of a four-month-old, that they were small living ones, that they went in, that they climbed inside you and ate. I didn't have the word for invisible, so I said small past seeing. And because I'd spent four months listening to people swear by gods at every turn, I added the only cover I had. I pointed up. The gods send them. Tiny living creatures the gods send to climb inside you and eat you from the middle out.

Heras let me finish. He had the patience of a man watching a child build a tower of blocks so he could see exactly how it fell. And when I was done he took the stylus out of my hand, gently, and he took me apart.

I got maybe half the words and all of the contempt, which needs no translation. He pointed at the swollen arm and said the word I'd learned for bad air, the air of marshes, low and rotten. *This* is what sickens. You got fever from bad air and bad places and the wrong stars, ask anyone. He pressed his own four fingers to his own arm and named them: the wet, the dry, the hot, the cold - the humors, the balance of the body, and named the imbalance that made a fever, and this he said slowly and twice, because it was real to him, it was Hippocrates, the best medical thinking on the planet earth in that year, and he held it as firmly as I held the quadratic formula. And then the gods angry, a curse, a thing done wrong at an altar. Air, humors, the gods' wrath. Three good frames, the frames every educated man from Britain to Syria used, and into the middle of them I'd dropped a story about invisible animals that crawl into cuts. He looked at my drawing of the swarming dots and said a word I didn't know, and the man at the bench snorted, and Heras said it at me again, and from the way he said it I understood it meant something near *witch-talk*, the thing old women in the hills believed. He was telling me, as a friend, that I'd just said the kind of thing that got people killed here, that invisible things sneaking inside you was the language of curses and the evil eye and the magic the law had teeth about, and I should, for my own sake, shut up about my little animals.

I'd known it was a risk. I hadn't known how much. I'd reached for the one true thing I had, the half-memory of a video, swarming dots under a microscope I'd seen once on a screen, and I'd reached for it because the kid was dying and I was sure of it - certain down in my bones, with certainty I had no right to - and Heras had just shown me that certainty was worth exactly nothing. I had a story and no way on earth to make him see the dots. I had the truth and no ladder to it.

So I kept the chores and let the story go.

---

I couldn't make him believe in the little ones. But I could make him *do the practices*, because the practices worked, and Heras, whatever else he was, would do what worked even if he hated the explanation.

I took the water he meant to wash the boy's arm with and I wouldn't let him use it cold from the jug. I made him boil it. I had no word for *sterilize* and there's no such concept here anyway, so I framed it the one way that didn't sound like witchcraft: hot water is clean, fire cleans, you wouldn't eat off a dirty plate, don't wash a wound with dirty water, the fire makes it clean past what the eye can judge. That, he could take. Cleanliness he understood. The Greeks were big on the regimen and the bath, and *clean* slotted in with no demons attached. He boiled it, let it cool, and we washed the arm properly and washed it again, and washed our own hands first, which I made a thing of, which he thought was fussy and did anyway because the fussy foreigner wouldn't stop until he did. We kept the wound open and clean instead of packing it with the muck they'd packed it with. He knew his own herbs, the ones that helped, and I didn't argue any of that, because he'd cut up more bodies than I'd seen and I wasn't about to play doctor at him. I owned the water and the hands. He kept everything else.

The boy lived. Boys with red lines up their arms sometimes live no matter what you do and sometimes die no matter what you do, and one kid is not a number you can stand on. I knew that even then, lying awake doing the math I couldn't do, no count, no control, no second boy untreated to set him against, just one kid and a coin flip and my story dressed up as cleanliness. I'd never, in this place, have proof. I'd have the practice that worked and the explanation that didn't, forever, and I'd have to learn to live in the gap.

But Heras saw the boy keep his arm. And weeks later, a different patient, he sent the household girl to boil the water before I'd said a word, and washed his hands first without being told, and when I caught his eye he gave me nothing, no credit, no concession, just went on as if he'd always done it this way. He never once said I was right about the little ones. To the end of that winter and probably the end of his life he thought my invisible animals were a superstition I'd carried down off the ice, a barbarian's ghost story. He just quietly started boiling his water and washing his hands, because he could *see* it helped, and the story he hung on it was his own business and the doing of it was mine.

I told him the soldiers' *posca*, sour wine and water, washed a scrape cleaner than plain water, because I half-remembered the acid did something, and I couldn't tell him what and he didn't ask anymore.

He went on without comment. We both did.

---

Around this same time I also noticed there was no soap in the household.

There was oil, used for cleaning the skin the Roman way - rub, scrape, rinse. There was ash, used to clean pots. But no soap, which is what you get when you combine fat and ash-lye in the right amounts and cook them together. I knew this from somewhere - science class or YouTube, a fact absorbed without ever needing to use it.

I needed to use it now.

I rendered tallow from the kitchen scraps, the fat trimmed off meat that would otherwise go to the dogs. I made lye by leaching wood ash through water and boiling the liquid down. I combined them, heated them, and got a soft, gray-brown sludge that smelled like something had died in a swamp and burned like a second wound on any cut or scrape it touched. I gave some to Heras with no explanation. He smelled it, made a face, touched it to the back of his hand, made a different face, and set it down.

Three tries later I had a cake that was merely unpleasant rather than actively hostile. It cleaned well. It did not dissolve on contact with water like the first batch. Heras started using it without acknowledging it had come from me. The household eventually started using it too, for their own reasons, and I never heard anyone call it anything but "the Thulean's muck." Which was fine. I didn't need them to call it soap. I needed them to wash their hands.

This was the same winter I noticed the pipes.

The household had lead pipes for water. So did, as far as I could tell, every house I'd been in since I arrived. The Romans ran their water in lead because lead was cheap and workable and held a seal. And lead, in sufficient quantity over sufficient time, will rot a mind from the inside out. Every schoolchild from the twentieth century onward knew it: the Romans poisoned themselves and they never knew why. I was now one of them, eating from lead-lined vessels, drinking from lead pipes, and there was not a single thing I could do about it at scale.

What I could do was stop using the lead pots in my corner of the kitchen for my own food. I borrowed a clay one instead. I told Heras about defrutum - grape must boiled in lead vessels, sweet and poisonous - and said I thought it made people slow. His face went to the expression he reserved for my witch-talk. I didn't push it. He was already adopting my practices without my theories; asking him to abandon a relish the whole city used because of invisible damage to invisible thought was a fight I'd only lose. I filed it under the same category as the lead pipes themselves: real, significant, and beyond my reach for now.

My corner of the kitchen had clay pots. That was the size of my victory.

---

Spring came around again. I'd been here a year. Eighteen, now, by my own count off the seasons, though no one here cared and there was no candle and no cake and no one to tell.

I could hold a conversation about a thing in front of us - a tool, a price, a body, the weather, the work. I could follow Heras at his slow and lose him entirely when he got going at his real speed with another educated man, the Greek flying, the long words stacking, and I'd sit there catching one word in five and feel the deafness come back like a tide. I made errors that cost me, wrong cases that turned a sentence sideways, the *v* that buzzed and marked me foreign in the first three words. I could barely read, only the figures and a few words. A clever man behind a child's tongue is its own specific problem, because people hear the broken Latin and price your whole mind at the level of your worst sentence, and you have to claw their estimate back up joke by joke until they forget the accent and talk to the part of you that's actually there. Heras had skipped all that. He'd priced my mind off a long-division error in week one and never re-priced it down for the tongue. That was the only reason I could stand it.

But one person is not enough, and a year in I knew the dimensions of my situation with a clarity I hadn't had when I was just trying to live. I was lodged in a workshop off a household that wasn't mine, fed by people who watched the door, useful enough to keep and not free to leave, a foreigner with a homeland nobody believed in.

So I made my first quiet move, and it cost me nothing, which was the whole point.

Somewhere in the weeks after the arm healed, I started cooking for myself in the evenings when the kitchen fire was still lit and the household had eaten. I had eggs, always eggs, the hens in the yard producing reliably. Oil. Whatever herbs the cook left bundled by the wall. I beat the eggs in a clay bowl and poured them into the pan in the hot oil and watched them set from the edge inward - eggs over a gas burner, the most ordinary thing I'd ever done, over a burner I would never see again. I folded one side over. I slid it out.

The cook's assistant, a girl of maybe twelve, had been watching from the doorway. She looked at it on the plate, then at me.

"That," she said, in the considered manner of someone making a judgment, "is how barbarians eat."

She said it as if this settled something. Then she asked if there was more.

I gave her half. She ate it standing up and didn't say anything else about it.

---

The garum was its own education, slower.

My first month I ate around it. Moved it to the edge of whatever I was handed. Breathed through my mouth. The smell was honest-to-god rotting fish, or that was how I read it, and somewhere in my head was the knowledge that this was intentional, this was the seasoning, this was what went on everything. I knew, in the abstract way you know things you've read online, that people in Southeast Asia used something similar. That knowledge and the smell were two completely different facts that refused to merge.

They merged eventually. I can't say exactly when. There was a morning that winter when they put a bowl of something down in front of me, lentils and cumin and something I didn't bother to identify, and I ate it all the way to the bottom without noticing I'd done it. Later I thought back and realized: garum. The whole depth of it, the back of the throat, the quality that had been missing from everything I'd cooked when I tried to cook without it. Just fish sauce. Southeast Asia had known this for a long time. Rome had figured it out independently and further, and I was the last one to get there.

That morning I was making my eggs, this time with a little lovage - which was available and sharp and slightly like celery had a fight with pepper - and I seasoned them with garum without making a face. Three drops from the small jug Stichus kept near the fire. The eggs came out better.

I didn't make a thing of it. There was nobody there to make a thing with anyway.

And sometimes, eating something that was this far from what I'd grown up eating, I thought about the distance between here and where the food I missed most was actually growing. Tomatoes. Potatoes. Corn. Chocolate, if you could get to the right coast. I'd drawn those western landmasses on the magistrate's map. I knew roughly where they were. A ship, the right kind of guidance, enough time. A direction, not a plan. Hunger with a compass. I filed it away with the lead pipes and the lack of soap: real, significant, and not yet within my reach.

---

The small wins were not going to be enough. I knew this at the year mark with a clarity that had nowhere to go, because there was still no one to tell. One person on earth who heard past the accent, an arm that healed, some laughter at a fish stall, a jug of garum I'd stopped avoiding. That was my dent in the world. I was lodged in someone else's household, useful enough to keep, not free to leave, and not yet worth enough to matter to anyone who could make things different.

I needed an argument no one could dismiss. Heras had taught me that cold with the little ones. You can't win them with a story you can't show. You win them with a demonstration their own eyes won't let them call a lie.

Around this same time I started thinking about the glass.

In the workshop there were always scraps of the poor greenish stuff, thick and full of bubbles, next to useless for anything fine. I needed better and knew I'd need better for a long time, and I had no particular means to produce it myself. I wrote out what I wanted on a wax tablet in my broken but improving Latin: a pane of glass, one handspan across, through which a man could read a line of text from twice arm's length without the letters swimming in color. I added a figure. One month's skilled wages to the first man who brought me such a thing and told me how he'd made it.

I did not sign it with my name. I gave it to a quiet capable person at the guild contacts - a man named Drusus whose function seemed to be knowing where things were sourced - and said I'd like it posted among the glassworkers through whichever channels he used. Drusus asked if I wanted to know the attempts as they came. I said I only wanted to know when someone succeeded.

He gave me a look that meant nothing in particular and took the tablet.

Three craftsmen asked after it over the following months, according to what I heard through Drusus. None of them had the answer yet. But the question was out there now, working on its own, and that had cost me nothing but the prize if someone won. A problem released into the world, paid only on arrival.

---

It was still winter when I landed on the other thing. The big one.

I was up in the loft. A draft came up under the tiles, the smoke off the household fire below, warm air pushing through the gaps so you could feel it on your face if you lay close to the floor, the heat rising, the smoke rising, always up. I'd known the reason for that since I was twelve. Hot air is lighter. Hot air climbs. The gods don't send it up, it just goes, and nobody in this entire world knew the reason and I did, I had that one for free, and I lay there with my hand in the rising warm and thought about a bag.

A bag full of hot air. Light enough, full enough of fire-warmed air that it would do what the smoke did, what I'd watched a thousand times over a campfire and a candle and never once thought about. It would go *up*. Off the ground, into the sky, in front of everyone, a made thing of cloth and fire rising on a warm wind, smoke-simple, same principle, just bigger. I didn't know how to build it. I didn't know what it cost or what it weighed or whether the cloth would hold or burn, and it would turn out I didn't know the half of how much I didn't know. But I knew it would *rise* - knew it the same deep way I'd known the eggs were an emperor's price, and this time the fact I was sure of was true, and better than true, it was visible, the most visible thing in the world, climbing into the open sky where every eye in the town would have to look up and watch the Thulean make the air carry fire, and not one of them would have a word for calling it a lie.

I lay there with my face in the warm draft a long time, doing the only math I had, not the engineering, that came later and cost me, but the simple animal arithmetic of a cornered man. Cheap. Mine. The hot air gave me both at once, and the third quality too, the one you need when the argument's already settled: the thing that will be there in the air for anyone to see.

I went to sleep with my hand still up in the rising heat, and for the first night in a year I didn't reach for the dead phone at all.
