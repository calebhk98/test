# Chapter 19: The Contest

Rome in that winter was drunk on a scale I had no frame for.

The gold I'd counted in a tent on a mountain had come down the road ahead of me and turned into spectacle: games announced that went on for a span of days I didn't believe when somebody first said the number, money given away to the citizens, bread and oil and meat. The wine shops had their amphorae stacked in the street, and a man could not walk to the wharf without somebody pressing a cup on him, because the dead king's gold was paying for it and you'd be a fool to refuse free wine bought with somebody else's war. I drank it in a crowd that loved the emperor and loved that the war had paid, and I thought about the slope, and I drank another.

So when I tell you that this was the winter I finally did the contest, understand that part of why I did it then was that I needed, badly, to make something that wasn't that. I had spent a summer keeping men alive with water and an autumn killing a city with water, the same hand, and I wanted to use the hand on something that just made people imagine. That's not a noble reason to found an institution. It's the true one.

Also, in fairness: the gold floating around Rome's economy was doing something very useful to the gambling houses, and I had been watching them.

---

I had noticed Rome's betting culture early, almost my first month in the city. Romans bet on chariot races and gladiatorial fights the way other people breathed; dice games ran in every doorway; a man could find a taker for almost any proposition if he put it in the right wine-shop. I'd been filing the sportsbook idea somewhere in the back of my head for years. I understood expected value in a way nobody here did, and expected value was the only thing you actually needed to run a house.

The Dacian gold made the timing right. Rich men from all over the city were throwing money around in new ways, and there was enough of it moving that setting up a proper betting shop — fixed odds, a clear ledger, a house edge that was small enough to keep people happy and large enough to keep me solvent — was now a sensible business rather than a scheme. I found a room near the Circus not quite in the shadow of the big operators, hired two men who could count and keep a straight face, and opened it quietly.

Marcia had not entered the partnership yet. I did the accounts myself for the first three months, which meant Tyche did them, which meant they were correctly done. The edge held. The room did not advertise. It did not need to.

I will say only this about the betting shop and then leave it: it was clean, it paid on time, and the men I hired never cheated the house or the customers, because I had explained the mathematics to them in patient detail and made clear that cheating the house was the option that ended their wages. Whether the whole operation was an honorable use of my knowledge of expected value is a question I did not ask myself as carefully as I should have. The money went into the contest.

---

The contest. What I wanted was simple to say and a nightmare to arrange: a prize, real money, every year, on a fixed day, for the best tale of a machine that never was. A cart that ran with no horse and no slave to push it. A glass that showed the little ones I'd spent five years trying to talk Romans into believing in. A ship that crossed the sea with no wind and no men chained to the oars. An engine that did the work of a hundred men.

I had a head full of machines I could describe and not build, and I'd understood somewhere on that road north that the machines themselves weren't the gift. I was never going to live to see a horseless cart. The gift was getting a few thousand people to want one, to think it ordinary to want one, so the wanting would still be there in the heads of people not yet born when the steel finally came good.

I tried to explain this to Heras and got about three sentences in before he held up a hand.

"You want to pay people," he said, "to lie."

"To imagine."

"In Rome," Heras said, "those are close cousins, and the family is not respectable." He was grayer than when I'd met him, and I was selfishly glad of one face that hadn't been on a mountain. "A man may write of gods doing impossible things, because the gods did them. A man who writes of a cart doing impossible things has written a fable, and a fable is for children and Greeks." He paused. "I am a Greek. I am allowed to say it."

"You want," he said, after a moment, "to make men homesick for a country that doesn't exist yet. That's either the wisest thing you've said to me or the saddest, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

He was right, which was his entire function in my life. A foreigner, the man some salon-going people were still being told was a fraud and a possible blasphemer, could not simply announce that he was paying silver for the best made-up story about a machine. The approach came from the city itself. Rich men endowed things — games and feasts and a fountain with their name on it, *so-and-so paid for this, remember so-and-so* — because in a city with a short memory, your name carved on a thing was how you went on existing. The prize was normal. The problem was whose name was on it.

So it couldn't be mine.

---

I went to Macer.

He was my patron now in the proper Roman sense, which meant I attended his door in the gray morning with his other clients, an indignity he enjoyed visibly and I endured, because the emperor's freedman queuing in Macer's atrium was a thing Macer could show people, and showing people things was half of what wealth was for. I made the pitch in his study, the room that still smelled of the leather he sold the legions, with him turning the heavy gold ring on his finger while deciding whether a thing would make money or make him a fool, which to Macer were the only two outcomes worth considering.

"You want me," he said, "to put my name on a prize for stories."

"I want your name on a benefaction. The Macer prize. Given by Titus Flavius Macer, every year, for the best Latin telling of a marvel."

"What marvel."

"A machine. A device. A thing of craft that does something men can't do." Macer did not care about a civilization dreaming forward. Macer cared about leather contracts and his own immortality and being seen to be a man of taste, which he was not, and knew he was not, and minded. "Apollodorus builds bridges across rivers nobody could bridge, and Caesar gives him glory for it. You can't build a bridge. But you can be the man whose name is on the contest where Rome dreams up the bridges nobody's built yet. You'll have your name on the new thing."

"You want my name," he said, "because nobody'll come to a prize given by a barbarian freedman who'll be telling them to wash their hands at it."

"Yes. And the silver's mine. I pay, you're seen to pay. It costs you nothing but lending the name and letting it be read out under your roof once a year."

He saw a low-cost way to be a literary patron without having to read anything, plus the pleasure of the emperor's clever Thulean funding glory for the name Flavius Macer. Those two things were sufficient.

"What do I have to do," he said.

"Host the reading. Sit at the front. Look like a man of taste."

"I can't look like a man of taste," Macer said. "I look like a man who sells the army boots."

"Then look like a man who sells the army its boots and also funds poetry. It's a better look. Half the senate would kill for it and can't afford the boots."

He laughed at that, the short ugly bark he had, and I knew I had him, because the one flattery that always worked on Macer was the one that was also a little insulting and also true.

"Write me the rules," he said. "Short. And keep your name off the front of it. You're the judge if you want, the Thulean who knows the foreign marvels, that's fine, that's a flavor, people like a tame barbarian to weigh the entries. But the prize is mine."

"It's yours," I said, and meant it.

---

We set the day at the *Quinquatrus*, the festival of Minerva in the spring, which Heras suggested and which was perfect, because Minerva is the goddess of craftsmen and the schools, and a prize for marvels of craft given on her festival had the gods covered, which mattered more than I can say with Vibenius still out there somewhere weighing me. You did not, in Rome, launch a strange new thing about impossible machines without nailing it firmly to a god first.

The silver was a hundred denarii to the winner, a third of a legionary's yearly pay, enough that a poor man with a quick imagination and somebody to write his words down would walk a long way to try for it. I could have made it more, the Dacian gold had everybody throwing money around, but I didn't want senators' sons entering for sport. I wanted the schoolmaster, the surveyor, the freedman clerk, the kid in the Subura who'd never owned anything. A hundred denarii to them was the moon. To a senator it was a tip.

The entries came in on whatever people had. Most people in Rome can't write, and most who can can't afford clean papyrus, so the marvels came on wax tablets and potsherds and the backs of old accounts and once on a strip of board that had been part of a wine crate, the grain showing through the soot-ink. A man brought his on a borrowed tablet and stood over me while I read it because he had to take it back. I kept a clerk just to copy entries off tablets people needed returned, and the clerk was overwhelmed inside a week.

Tyche kept the records in the tally-room, each entry numbered and noted in the new figures. She had made a column for the judge's notes and left it three inches wide, which turned out to be exactly enough.

---

The first marvels.

I have kept some of them. I'll put a few down exactly, because somebody finding this should know what the inside of an ordinary Roman head looked like when you gave it permission to dream.

A man who drove a cart for a builder wrote about a chariot pulled by trained eagles, in little harnesses. There was the problem of how you steered them, which he solved: a man sat behind the driver holding pieces of meat on long poles, one to each side, so you guided the team by where you dangled lunch. He'd done a sum, in Roman numerals, of how many eagles a loaded cart would need, and the sum was wrong, but he'd done it. At the end he'd written that the trouble was getting the eagles to come down once they were up, that he hadn't solved that part, and that maybe a wiser man could. I read that and laughed out loud and then sat with the *maybe a wiser man could* for a while, because that little sentence was the entire thing I was farming for.

A baker's wife sent in a bronze man who ground grain. Life-sized, wound up somehow, she was vague on the winding, and he turned the quern all day and never tired and never had to be fed. She'd thought hardest about the part that was hers, that the bronze man would never steal flour, never get sick, never need watching, and could grind through the night so the bread was ready at dawn. There was nothing in it about how the bronze man worked, because the how wasn't her dream. The not-having-to-own-the-grinder was the dream. I gave her tablet to Tyche, who read it twice and said nothing and gave it back.

There was a boy, maybe twelve, who'd had a grammarian's slave write his out in exchange for errands, and his marvel was a glass that let you see the moon as a country, with mountains, because he'd reasoned, as a sharp kid reasons, that if it threw back light the same as a hill throws back light then it had hillsides, and a thing with hillsides is a country. I had a single-lens magnifier in a drawer that would have done nothing for the moon and a head full of a telescope I couldn't build because Roman glass was full of bubbles, and this child had walked in and asked for the one marvel I knew was actually coming, someday, for somebody who wasn't either of us. I asked how he'd thought of the mountains. He looked at me like I was slow and said the moon wasn't smooth, anybody could see it wasn't smooth, what did I think the dark parts were. I told him it was a good marvel, which it was.

Then there was a soldier.

---

He wrote in a military man's flat Latin, no subordinate clauses, no ornament, the kind of language you'd use to report a damaged road. His marvel was an observation platform high enough that a man with a war-bow could loose arrows down onto an enemy formation without ever seeing their faces. He described the platform in careful detail — a large bag of heated air, he'd seen one, though his man was in it rather than hanging under it on a rope — and he had worked out the angle that made a dropped arrow go faster than a thrown one, and he knew how far you'd need to be to stay above a javelin. He wrote that a man at height could kill without the killed knowing where death came from. The best kind, he wrote. Clean. No mud on your boots.

He was not wrong about any of it. He had seen the tethered bag at the Danube and understood it faster than the engineers had. He was describing something I had refused to build specifically because I could see this use clearly before anyone else named it.

I sat with his entry a long time. He was cheerful about it in the professional soldier's way — there was no malice in it, just craft, the pleasure of a man who knows his trade running a new tool through its paces in his head. I could not find a principled reason to exclude him, given what I'd let through. I put him in the second round and went on with the stack. I didn't annotate the ledger entry.

There was a slave's satirical portrait of a man's household.

---

The slave who wrote it had good letters and no illusions. He had written about a senator's house — not any specific senator, he was careful about that, just *a* senator — where the master spent his mornings receiving clients while the clever slave who had been trained at the master's expense and did the master's actual work stood three steps behind his chair and heard everything and said nothing and was never asked. The marvel in his entry was a device that made the clever slave visible, so the clients could see who was actually running the household. He was precise about the device, he wanted it to be a story about a machine, but the machine was not the point. The story was the point.

It was mean and accurate and very good.

I sat with it too. The question was whether "open" meant "open to entries I found comfortable" or actually open. I put it in the second round and did not annotate the ledger entry and did not read it a third time, because the second time had been enough.

---

And there was the ship.

The ship's tale came in on the back of a grain-factor's discarded day-sheet, in a hand that was careful and a little cramped, the hand of somebody who'd learned letters late and was proud of them. It was signed with one name, the way of a man who has only one name to give, and the name was Felix.

Felix's marvel was a ship that needed no wind and no oars and no rowers. So far, the same dream as a dozen others, the no-rowers dream, which I'd noticed came from people who had reason to think about rowers and never from people who'd never been one. But Felix didn't wave his hand at how the ship moved. He wrote that everyone knew steam, the breath that comes off a boiling pot and lifts the lid and rattles it, anyone who'd watched a pot knew the lid wanted to fly. And if the breath off a pot could rattle a heavy lid, then a very great pot, fire under it always, its breath caught and aimed, could surely turn a wheel, and a wheel turned in the water would drive a ship, and the ship would carry its own fire and its own pot and its own sea, and go where it liked with no wind, against the wind, in a dead calm, forever, as long as there was fire.

I read it twice, then sat very still with the wharf noise coming through the door, and read it a third time, because I had shown Heras, years before, a toy I'd had a coppersmith make, Hero of Alexandria's spinning ball, the *aeolipile,* the hollow ball that whirls when you boil water under it and the steam jets out, and I'd said, *imagine this strong enough to pull a hundred carts,* and Heras had been completely unmoved, because it was a toy, it did one thing, it spun. And here was a man called Felix who'd never seen my toy, who'd watched a pot lid rattle in some kitchen and reasoned his way, on the back of a grain-sheet, to the engine I knew was the door to everything.

He'd gotten the outline of it. Not the working, the outline. And he'd hit the real problem without naming it, because he wrote, near the end, that no pot he knew could be made strong enough and large enough and not burst, that you'd need a metal nobody had, a metal that didn't yet exist.

The moon-boy wanted the glass I knew was coming. Felix wanted the steel I knew was the wall, the thing Hermes and I cracked our heads on in the forge behind the cattle market batch after ruined batch, gorgeous one firing and crystalline garbage the next. They were both standing at the same locked door I was standing at.

---

Before that day arrived, something else arrived with it.

Through the same guild contacts I used for the prize postings, the workshop had a standing offer on a bronze cylinder spec: a component I needed for the steam program, interior surface ground to within a tolerance I could measure with the go/no-go gauges Hermes had helped me build. The spec had been open for seven months.

A bronzesmith named Glabrio had come closest.

His cylinder arrived in a sacking cloth, set down on the counting table by a guild messenger who clearly had no idea what was inside. I ran the gauges myself. Glabrio's interior was smooth, genuinely smooth, the kind of smooth that comes from a craftsman who knows what he's doing and spent real time on it. But the final pass had taken off a hair's breadth too much. The bore was fractionally oversize, enough that the valve fitting would have to compensate, enough that under pressure the seal would eventually weep.

I set the cylinder down and looked at the ledger Tyche kept of the prize attempts. Glabrio had submitted twice before on different specs, getting close each time. The same pattern: excellent work, the finest adjustments done last, and the final pass a fraction too eager. A man who had spent his career working bronze by eye and feel, whose instincts pushed him to fit the surface just a little more, just one more stroke of the file. His hands were trained for a tolerance they couldn't see.

I wrote his name in the prize ledger under the column for near-misses. The winner's column stayed blank.

---

The first contest also received a submission from someone calling himself "Mercator Thulensis" — a Thulean merchant, or possibly a dealer from the north, the Latin was ambiguous. The entry was unlike anything else in the stack.

It was a story about two young people from families at war with each other, doomed from the moment they met, and the marvel in it was not a machine at all, strictly speaking. The marvel was language: the story was told in a mode I'd never quite seen in Rome, closer to tragedy than to the prose marvels and engineering fables the contest was meant to generate. Two families, two names, an ancient grudge, and the people who died for it. The ending was not a victory. There was no engineering. I don't know what made me include it.

I read it and then sat with it for a long time, and then I put it in a separate pile from the others.

We gave two prizes that year. The judges' prize — a thing I hadn't planned on, but which the room required after reading — went to the Thulean merchant's story, because it did something none of the engineering marvels did: it made the room go quiet. The main prize, the hundred denarii, I'd already decided.

I gave that to Felix.

---

It caused exactly the trouble I should have expected.

Felix turned out to be a slave, the property of a man who imported African grain and kept a counting-house of literate slaves, where Felix had learned his careful late letters doing his master's sums. The prize money, by every law in Rome, belonged to a grain importer who had not written a word about a ship.

I bought the result with maneuvering and Macer's name and some of the silver going where I'd rather it hadn't. The grain importer was happy to be flattered as the master of a slave clever enough to win the Macer prize, and happy to take his owner's cut. In the end Felix got to keep, with his master's gracious permission, a portion toward his own *peculium,* toward, someday, if the master stayed gracious, buying himself. I'd turned a hundred denarii for the best marvel into a down payment on a man's own freedom, filtered through a master's vanity and a master's cut, and it was a win, and it was also the bronze grinder run backward.

I read his tale out at Macer's, on the Quinquatrus, with Macer at the front looking like a man who sells the army its boots and also funds poetry, the room fuller than the salon had ever been. People laughed in the good places, and went quiet at the metal that didn't yet exist, and a man near the back said, not unkindly, "Well, and when the gods give us that metal," and got a laugh, and I thought, *a wiser man could,* and that Felix was at that moment in a counting-house adding up another man's grain, and I said none of it.

---

Crispus came.

Of course he came, oiled and elegant and with his scroll open on the table beside him, three fingers of Falernian and a face arranged into something close to approval. He had learned the last time that fraud and geography and the graver word were a terrain I could fight on, so he did not go back to it. He had something different. He had come as a man grieved.

He waited until the reading was done and the room had settled into its talk, and he called for the room's attention, a kind of gravitational pull he'd always been able to manage, and he praised it. Lavishly. The eagles were delightful, genuinely, and the bronze grinder was a lovely piece of Roman ingenuity, and the boy's moon, well, one was reminded of the best tradition of the old philosophical imagination. And then he came to the prizes.

He set down his cup. He expressed his admiration for the spirit of the whole enterprise. And then he wondered, in a voice of careful sorrow, whether the judges had paused to consider what it meant for the first Macer prize to pass to a slave. He was not, he wanted the room to understand, raising any question about the quality of the tale itself, which he found charming in its way. But he wondered. A prize for Roman literary excellence, on the festival of the goddess of learning herself, given to a man who was — and he used this word gently — property. Had Rome come to a point where it could not find, among its free citizens, a more worthy recipient? Was the Thulean's enthusiasm for opening the doors perhaps a little wider than the tradition could bear? There were men of standing in this room who had not submitted entries, he noted. He imagined they had observed the rules of the contest as given. He only asked whether those rules, in hindsight, might be sharpened.

He sat down to a scattered, uncertain murmur, and I saw what he'd done. He had not attacked the contest. He had raised the standing of the room against the standing of the winner. The next year's entries would remember that a slave had won, and half of them would write to prove they were not slaves, and the other half would stay home rather than be in the same contest that had lowered itself.

I stood up.

I thanked him. I said I'd heard his concern, and that it was a real concern, one any man who loved the tradition should weigh. Then I said that I'd gone back to the oldest Roman law I knew about prizes, which was the Olympic precedent — not Roman, I knew, but the oldest — and that in those games a man who won had earned the crown by the standard of the task, and no one had checked his grandfather's grandfather at the gate. The gods of craft did not ask for a free man's wax tablet or a slave's. They asked for the best work. Minerva had watched a slave reach for her festival's crown with his hands and nothing else, and the goddess's festival was still standing, the column still upright, and no one had been struck. If the goddess had a complaint, I trusted she would make it.

The room laughed, harder than his had been, and Macer laughed his ugly bark from the front, and Crispus smiled the smile that did not move above the lips and said something gracious about the Thulean's command of the tradition, and we were done.

Walking home I kept seeing that smile. He had not gotten the prize. The resentment had found a new landing spot, and it was Felix, who had no protection at all.

---

Then we printed them.

A prize read out once at a rich man's dinner and then forgotten is a dinner. The dream had to spread and outlast me.

So Eros, my letterer, carved the winners onto pearwood. Not all of them; the press was crude and gray and we wasted a sheet in four. But the ship and the eagles and the bronze grinder and the boy's moon and the Thulean merchant's story of two doomed young people, inked and pressed fifty crooked impressions at a time, sold for an as or two to schoolmasters and whoever wanted them, because a marvel that won the Macer prize was a thing people wanted to own. The press that had started its life making times-tables for a Subura schoolmaster now made marvels, and they went out on cheap gray sheets, and got copied by hand by people who couldn't buy even the cheap sheet, and started turning up, Tyche told me, in the mouths of children playing at the wharf, who'd decided among themselves that the bronze grinder was real and lived somewhere and could be visited.

A generation playing at machines that didn't exist. I knew when I planted it that the field was a hundred years long and I'd be in the ground for most of it, and I planted it anyway, on a borrowed name, on Minerva's festival, with a slave's tale of a ship in the front row.

---

Felix sent word, near the end of that first year, through the grain importer's steward. He'd been allowed paper for his next, and he wanted me to know he was already working on it, that with proper paper and time, he thought he could fix the pot. The metal that didn't yet exist. He thought he could fix it.

I sat in the tally-room with the steward's tablet in my hand, the wharf going on outside, Tyche at the other table running the day's figures in the new marks, Pamphilus down on the dock bellowing at a crane crew with the collar long gone off his neck, and I read the slave's message that he thought he could fix the pot, the one thing in all of Rome I knew for certain could not be fixed, not by him, not by me, not by Hermes and every ruined batch behind the cattle market.

I took up the stylus and wrote back, in my plain accented Latin, that I would very much like to read it, that he should take all the time he needed with the pot, and that the metal would come. Then I smoothed the wax flat with my thumb where I'd pressed too hard, and gave it back, and went out onto the dock to see what Pamphilus was shouting about.
