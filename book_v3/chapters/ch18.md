# Chapter 18: The Siege

The price of Pamphilus was four thousand sesterces, which Macer named to me across his own atrium with the gold ring turning on his thumb, and which was, he wanted me to understand, robbery in my favor.

"He's a strong back going gray," Macer said. He was leaning over the tallying-table, not looking at me, using a stylus to correct some carter's manifest. "Ten years of carry left in him if his knees hold. I'd get more at the block off a man who didn't know his joints. You I'll bleed slow because you'll come back for the girl and I want you to remember I was kind." He had a steward bring the tablet, the bill of sale, and he had me read it through, the careful law-words, *Pamphilus, servus,* a collared porter of the household of Titus Flavius Macer, sold for the named sum to Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified house, and there it was on the wax, a man bought and a man buying, both of us his once. I counted out the coin from my own peculium that was mine now in law and not in sufferance, heavy bitten silver, and Macer's clerk weighed it and bit two of the aurei and wrote the receipt, and Pamphilus belonged to me.

That was the morning. The freeing I did myself in the yard, badly, because I had never done it and the right form requires a praetor's presence and I had neither.

You are supposed to go to a magistrate. The full thing, a rod laid on the slave's head, a citizen made in a morning by a man with the power to do it. I did not have eight days and a praetor's patience, I had a war to march to, so I did the household thing, the cheap door, which makes a free man but a lesser-free man, only the start of citizenship. I sat Pamphilus down on the bench by the well and said the words wrong twice and got Heras, who was there because Heras was always there when a thing mattered, to feed them to me quietly, and I declared before him and before Tyche and a wharf-clerk I had dragged over for a witness that this man was free.

The collar was the trouble.

It was riveted. It had been riveted for the eleven years Pamphilus had worn it and the iron had thinned where it rode his collarbone and gone green-black in the creases of his neck, and you do not unlock a thing like that, you cut it. I had not thought about that part, as I had not thought about a great many parts. In my head freeing a man was a sentence said and a tablet signed and the man stood up different. The man did not stand up different. He sat on the bench with his hands on his knees while Hermes set a cold chisel against the rivet at the back of his neck. Hermes looked at me once, flat, asking without words was I sure, because a slip there opens the big vessel and a freed man bleeds out on his first free morning. Pamphilus held still, stiller than I have ever seen a living man hold, and Hermes struck the chisel twice and the rivet sheared and the collar came away in two halves and left a band of paler skin, soft, never weathered, like the underside of a stone lifted from damp earth.

Pamphilus put his hand up to his own neck and felt the bare skin there.

"You free," he said, after a while, to nobody in particular, and took his hand down.

He had said *you* when he meant *I.* Eleven years of Latin learned in a house where somebody else was the subject.

I had imagined, God help me, that he would weep, or grip my arm, or do some version of the thing that happens in the story when the chains come off. What I wanted was the clean feeling, the one clean thing, after Sabinus and the boring water that keeps men healthy enough to be killed and all the rest of the ledger I was carrying. I wanted Pamphilus to make me feel like I had done a good thing.

What he did was ask whether he was still to cut the cloth.

"You don't have to cut anything," I said. "You're free. You can go."

"Go where," Pamphilus said.

He was asking. A plain question. He was a man past forty with a band of pale skin on his neck and no family that had survived being sold twice and no trade but carrying and lifting and cutting bolt-cloth, and the question was real: *go where.* I had freed him into being my client. I had moved him one rung and called it the sky. The chain you can't see is the better fitted of the two, and I had been the one to fit it, and I did not have an answer that was not the answer the world gives every freed man, which is: nowhere you didn't come from, stay near the man who freed you because you owe him the duties a freedman owes and outside his door is a city that eats men with no door.

"You stay," I said. "On wages. Real ones, into your own hand, your own peculium that's yours now, not sufferance. You cut the cloth because I'll pay you to cut the cloth, and when you don't want to cut it you tell me and we'll find what else. But Pamphilus" - and here my Latin failed me and I said it too plain - "nobody owns you. That is all I have."

He looked at the two halves of the collar lying in the dirt by the well. Tyche had gone very still by the shed wall, watching, because she had asked *can you free us,* us before herself, and she was watching what free turned out to be.

Pamphilus rubbed the pale band with two fingers. Picked up the shears. Went to the cloth. Because that was the morning's work and he was a man who did the morning's work, and I stood there holding a freedom that had cost four thousand sesterces and still came up light.

Heras took the collar halves away. He said he wanted them for something. He never said what. I think he simply did not want them in the yard where I would keep looking at them.

---

I marched north in the spring with my name on the orders.

That is the difference I cannot make small enough, between the first war and this one. The first time I went up the Via Flaminia I went as a thing on loan, Macer's property lent to Celer's command, a slave with a clever toy, riding in the baggage and boiling my own water and watching the machine of the army move and being moved by it like a parcel. This time I went as Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified house, with a warrant that requisitioned timber and lime and digging-men in my name, and a mule of my own, and Pamphilus and two hired wharf-men on wages, and a sheaf of the new figures the quartermasters were now ordered to suffer.

The army was bigger. I keep wanting to say the war was bigger but that is the historian's word and the true one is that the army was bigger, doubled and more than doubled, legions pulled off the Rhine and out of the East, a flood of men and mules and the smell of them going north all spring, the road never empty, the dust never settling. Walking in it I understood that the client-king peace of four years before - Decebalus keeping his head and his hills, the relief and the anticlimax - had been a thing Trajan always meant to come back and finish. An intermission, not an ending. The whole vast machine had been quietly oiling itself the entire time I had been in Rome teaching shopkeepers to count. Macer had known. Macer had been stockpiling leather for tents. The flux talks, he'd said, and the flux had talked, and here was the answer in a column you could not see the end of.

And there was the bridge.

I had seen Apollodorus build the first bridges, the wood on the cofferdams, the river fought back a span at a time, and I had thought that was the great thing. The great thing was at Drobeta. He had put stone across the Danube. Twenty piers of dressed stone standing in the river that had drowned his cofferdam-men in the first war, the timber arches riding up off them, the thing running bank to bank wide enough for the army to cross marching, and the legions tramping over it in their thousands as if crossing a river that size were a thing a man simply did on a Tuesday.

I found him on the Dacian bank, plumb in hand, watching a pier with the patient suspicion of a man who has been given reason not to trust something he built.

"You came back," he said, not turning. "They freed you. I heard." He said *freed* the way you'd say a man had been promoted into a worse job, which was about right. "Marcus Ulpius now. The emperor collects useful Greeks and useful barbarians the way other men collect bronzes."

"It's a good bridge," I said, because it was, and because I wanted to see what he would do with a true thing said plainly.

He turned then. He had gone grayer and the sun had worked his face harder and his hands were the same scarred thick instruments. He looked at the bridge, his bridge, and there was no false modesty in him and no boasting either, just a man looking at a sum that had come out.

"It will outlast the province," he said. "They'll take the country and lose it in two lifetimes, three, and the bridge will still stand when there's no Rome on either bank to use it." He said it flat, a fact, the cold long view. Then he looked at me. "Your flying toy."

"Still does the one thing."

"Good. Do the one thing." He turned back to his pier. "Don't put a man under it. I heard about that too." And that was Apollodorus, who did not forgive a botched demonstration and did not pretend the dead man had been anything but my fault, and I respected him more than men who would have been kinder, and I left him to his bridge and went up into the hills to do the one thing.

---

What I did in that war, set down plainly:

I sited camps. That is the dullest sentence I will ever write and it kept more men alive than anything I have built. I walked ground ahead of the engineers and said put the latrines here, downstream, downslope, lime them, dig them deep, and the water-point here, upstream, above the horse-lines, above the fouling, and I made them mean it, because the emperor's warrant meant I could make a centurion move a latrine. A centurion does not forget being made to move a latrine by a barbarian in work boots, and I paid for that in small ways all summer. I set the barrel-battery filters at the water-points and made the water boiled by century and not by whim. Where the men did it the flux thinned. Where the men were lazy the flux did what the flux does. I watched both happen in the same army in the same month and had no way to count it, only the latrine pits, fuller in one camp than another, and the carts of the sick, lighter where I had been listened to.

I sent up the tethered eye on still mornings. Just the bag on its hundred feet of rope, a code of colored cloth, a soldier with good distance-sight in a basket no higher than a man could survive falling from. It saw what it saw and was useless in wind and Celer's successors used it and cursed it and used it again.

And then, between the second and third month of the campaign, the other thing.

I am setting this down because it needs to be set down.

By the time we moved against Sarmizegetusa, I had two teams of operators who had been flying tethered for the better part of two years, fifty-plus hours each, voluntary, trained, double-basket construction with a second man to manage the fire-bowl from below. The second man's basket hung low, deliberately, below the sway-point. I had rebuilt the fire-management around that second basket because the thing that killed Sabinus was a shifting fire-bowl and a single hang-point and no one to counter the weight. These were not untested designs with rushed men inside them. These were men who had practiced the particular way the basket heeled in a crosswind and how to read the rope tension before the sway turned into a problem. Celer had watched every hour of it. He had his own notes in the new figures. When he read the warrant with my name on it he said, "They made you real," with something under the dryness I won't name, and the first day he checked the basket-rigging himself and found the two-man rig and said only, "Longer gap between the baskets next time." He was not wrong.

So there were manned flights at Sarmizegetusa. I need to say it plain. The vow I made standing in the Danube mud four years ago was I won't rush untrained people into untested designs, and I had kept that vow. What I had also done, in the years between, was build the design past the point of failure. I had built it until it did not need the vow anymore. That is either the honorable version or the way a man tells himself it is, and I have not decided which.

The crossbowmen were light cavalry volunteers. From altitude you could see troop concentrations in the mountain passes that ground observers could not. The crossbow bolts were standardized, pre-spanned, the same quarrel from any basket. The first time a patrol of Dacian cavalry walked out from behind a ridge and found crossbow bolts falling out of a basket they could barely see, the word went through the mountain like a fire through dry wood. After that the passes emptied ahead of the column without a fight. I have wondered whether those men were grateful or terrified and I think probably both.

Celer was alive through the whole war. I am setting that down because I spent every month of it braced for the other thing, and the other thing did not come, not in Dacia. He checked, always, that the fire-management rigs were right. We never said Sabinus's name.

And I did the figures. The grain and the fodder and the days of march and the men to feed, columns of it, the new marks racing ahead of the old freedmen with their abacuses. A quartermaster who had fought me in the spring came to me by autumn with a sheaf of his own done in the marks and a column that wouldn't balance. He did not ask me to teach him. He said: *find my error.* A man too proud to learn and too pressed not to. I found his transposed pair - the kind the marks catch because you can't quietly thicken a stroke - and he took the sheaf back without thanks and used the marks the rest of the war. Need spread the figures. The grudging man's version of it, taken through the camp on gritted teeth, but it spread.

Pamphilus had come north with me, carrying and lifting and cutting whatever needed cutting. He did not complain about the road. He did not complain about the cold. One evening in camp, after I had moved a latrine trench for the fourth time and a junior centurion had spent ten minutes explaining to me in close detail what I could do with my warrant, Pamphilus looked over from where he was sorting the rope-coils.

"That man," he said. "Loud."

He thought about it a moment.

"You want I move the ropes so he trips."

He had not said it as a joke. It was a practical offer, delivered flat, and it was the kindest thing anyone said to me that week.

"No," I said. "Don't."

"Right," Pamphilus said, and went back to the rope.

---

Sarmizegetusa sat up in the mountains in the cold thin air with the walls of *murus dacicus* that the engineers admired and hated - stone faces packed with rubble and tied with squared timber so the rams bit and the wall ate the bite - and behind it the Dacians and their king and their gold and their gods, and around it the army dug in, the lines of circumvallation going up, the ballistae hauled into the heights piece by cursing piece. A siege is patience and engineering and starving a place, and I had not understood it until that autumn, watching men dig lines around other men to kill them slowly.

The water came into the place from springs, channeled in pipes and covered runs along the contours of the hills, ceramic and lead and hollowed stone, the same craft I had spent four years admiring in Rome. An engineer of Apollodorus's, a young hard Greek with a survey-staff, found the lines on the slope below the walls, traced them, and brought the question up the chain, and the question came to me, because I was the emperor's water-man and the warrant said so.

Could it be cut.

I read the slope. I knew where the springs had to be and how the runs would have to fall to feed a fort that high, and I said cut here, and here, where the contour forces the run, and where you can't reach the buried part, foul it - put the camp's filth above their intake, do to their water what I spend my life stopping us from doing to ours - and I heard myself say it in my plain accented Latin to a man with a survey-staff, the exact inversion of every word I had said to the emperor in the cold room. The young Greek wrote it on his tablet and it was good engineering and it was the cleverest thing I did in that war and it broke a city.

It did not break it cleanly. They had cisterns, they had rain, they had the discipline of a people who had held that hill against Rome for a generation. The cut water worked as the flux works: slowly, and on the weakest first, and out of sight. I did not see them die of it. That is what I keep coming back to: I did the water and never saw the water do its work, the same as I have never seen the boiled water save a man, no number either way, only the result that came down the mountain in the end. The place broke. The fight went out of it. A city full of people ran dry on a high cold hill because a man from a country that did not exist had read the slope right.

There was an inch where I could have been slow, vague, wrong on purpose - the inch I had used for two years to drag my feet on the powder. I did not use it. A freedman of the deified house who is slow and vague at a siege is a freedman who has discovered what the state does with tools that lie to it. I read the slope. I said cut here. I chose to live, and I am not calling that a moral act.

Apollodorus came to see the cut once, professionally. He stood looking at the fouled run with his plumb-line still in his hand out of habit, and said, "Neat," and nothing else for a while. Then: "It's the same knowledge." He said it the way he had said the bridge would outlast Rome - a fact about the world that he had no intention of softening. "The same hand that keeps your men off the flux fouls theirs. You didn't think it would stay on the one side."

"No," I said.

"Then don't make the face," he said, and walked off down the slope.

---

The place fell. The fight went out of it and the lines went in and the city that had never been taken was taken, and the king, Decebalus, who I never saw, who was a name to me and a reason for all of it, did not let himself be led down that mountain in chains. He cut his own throat. A cavalry scout found him doing it, or just done, the story changed in the telling as every soldier's story changes, but the throat was the constant of it. They cut off his head and his right hand to carry south as proof, because the state needs proof, the state does cold sums and a head is a sum that balances.

And then the gold.

I had heard there was gold. Everyone had heard there was gold, Dacia's gold, the king's treasure, stories all summer of the river dammed, the hoard buried, slaves killed to keep the secret. What I know is what came down the mountain in the autumn, cart after cart under guard, more guard than I had ever seen put on anything, and the men knew what was in the carts, they always know, and the mood of the army changed around it, a greed coming off the columns like heat. Every man in that machine knew a haul like that meant a donative, coin in the hand, and meant games, and meant the war had paid, that the dying in the valleys had been an investment and the investment had come good. You could feel the whole vast thing turn from a war into a triumph while it was still standing on the conquered ground.

I did the figures on some of it. They put the emperor's water-man and his quick marks onto the reckoning of part of the haul, and I sat in a tent counting Dacian gold by weight into the columns of the new figures I had carried up that road to keep men from dying of bad water. The column of grain that had kept a cohort off the flux and the column of looted gold that the same cohort had died and killed to take - the same marks, the same hand, and I balanced the column, because it balanced, because the marks always balance, that is the whole virtue of them.

Dacia was a province now. The country with the bridge across the great river was Rome's, the gold was Rome's, the king's head was going to Rome, and Rome was going to spend the gold on the longest games anyone could remember. I would hear about those games from the city when I got back down, the days and days of them, the killing made into a holiday, paid for out of the carts I had helped count.

---

I came down off that mountain a different man than the one who had gone up, and I do not entirely have the measure of the difference even now, writing it. I had freed one man in the spring and found that freeing was smaller than I had dreamed. I had spent the summer keeping men alive with water and the autumn killing a city with the same knowledge and the one hand. And somewhere in the middle of it, doing the cut, a thought came that I have never been able to put back in its box. The first war had ended in a client-king peace, an intermission. This one ended in annexation and a dead king and the gold, finished, as I half-remembered from a history class two thousand years off that it had ended. Had it ended that way. Was I remembering it or watching it. Had any of it gone otherwise than it would have without a barbarian by the timber wharf who read a slope, and I had no way to know, no number, no second world to lay beside this one and check the column.

We crossed back over Apollodorus's stone bridge in the cold, the army and the carts and the head and the gold and me, twenty piers standing in the river that had drowned the men who built them. I stopped at the rail a moment in the press of the column and looked down at the gray water going under, fast, indifferent, the water I knew better than anything in the world. I put my hand flat on the cold stone. Then a mule behind me balked and the column shoved and I went on across to the Roman bank, where my warrant was still good and my name was still the emperor's, and there was a winter of figures waiting, and a city full of gold, and a girl at the wharf I had promised, next, to buy.
