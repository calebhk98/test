# Chapter 26: The Forum

On the ninth day we named him Lucanus, and the house breathed out.

I had wanted to give him something of my own and could not. There is no Latin for the name I was born with that does not sound like a man clearing his throat, and in any case the name is the law's, not the father's. So he became Marcus Ulpius Lucanus, the praenomen mine, the nomen the emperor's, the cognomen a soft Roman word a child could grow into without it ever once weighing on him the way "Thulean" had weighed on me. Marcia chose Lucanus. She said it sat well in the mouth of a man calling an account, which was how she chose most things. The priest-of-the-house rites I let happen around me, the salt and the purified water and the small bird, and I held my son when they put him in my hands, freeborn, citizen at his first breath, able by the bare fact of his birth to stand for offices and wear stripes that I, who had taught his city to count, would die a freedman barred from. I had built him a higher floor than I would ever stand on.

What I felt, holding him while the bulla went round his neck against the evil that comes for a noticed child, was the cold arithmetic that I had now given the slope below my house one more thing to roll downhill at.

He gripped my finger. They do that; it means nothing, it is a reflex left over from clinging to fur. I knew it meant nothing and let it mean something anyway, because there was no one I could say it to.

That same week the contest returned its prize, as it did each year. The winner's name was VINIA SECUNDA, a tanner's widow in the Transtiberim, who had submitted her entry through the collegium under an initial and had asked Eros to keep her name off the broadsheet. I had expected the argument (from Crispus, from the priestly college men, from someone) and it did not come. The contest stewards read her name at the reading, Eros printed it, and the room went on. A woman had won. The sun came up the next morning at the same height it always had. I had assumed this would matter more than it did, and my assumption had been wrong, and that wrongness sat with me pleasantly for several days. I sent Vinia Secunda her prize by messenger, and she sent back a polite acknowledgment, and that was that.

---

The Forum opened to Rome in the high summer, and the city went out of its mind in the orderly, enormous Roman way.

I had seen Trajan's men widening the cut behind the old fora for years, the whole shoulder of the Quirinal eaten back, the spoil hauled south past my own wharf in carts I had sometimes counted out of habit. I knew the project the way you know a noise you have stopped hearing. Then they took the hoardings down, and the noise had been a building, and the building was a thing I did not have the right words for in either of my languages.

You came in under an arch and the space simply refused to end. A court paved in colored stone, wide enough to drown the Subura in, ringed in columns the color of old honey and gray storm, and at the far end the basilica with its roof of gilt bronze throwing the sun back so hard you put a hand up without deciding to. Statues stood in the upper register looking down, captured Dacians in their trousers and soft caps, carved over and over, a stone army of the defeated holding up the architrave with their patience. In the center the emperor rode a bronze horse bigger than any horse, and the horse's raised hoof hung over the heads of the crowd as if it had been stopped in the instant before it came down, and would come down, in the carver's intention, on us.

I stood in it and was, for a moment, the seventeen-year-old in the stubble field again, doing his sums to keep from screaming. Six hundred feet, I thought. More. The columns are forty if they're a foot. I did the arithmetic, and it steadied me, and then it stopped steadying me, because the number I arrived at was simply the truth, and the truth was that these people had done this on purpose, with ramps and ropes and ten thousand hands, in a few years, while I was failing to make a clock keep time.

I was not anonymous in that crowd. Men I half-knew turned and named me to men I did not know, the Thulean, Caesar's Thulean, the one with the flying bag, the one with the figures. Near the basilica steps, a young officer I had never met stepped back before I reached him and held the path open, chin down, waiting. He stepped sideways to avoid me. A perfumer's wife found me afterward and wanted to know whether the moon was truly a country with mountains; her husband had bought one of Demetrios's miserable spyglasses and seen nothing through it but colored fog, and she suspected, correctly, that she had been cheated, and was too well-bred to say so to a man Caesar favored. I told her the fault was in our glass and not in the moon, which was true and pleased no one. Scaeva's people found me twice in the press of bodies, not Scaeva himself, only a freedman of his who put a hand at my elbow and asked after my health and my son's and let me feel, through the warm words, the length of the leash. I had a customs man unmolested at the river gate now. I had paid for that.

A man in the crowd behind me, watching the bronze horse, said something to his neighbor about the Thulean's fire-bag having been on the wall, and what would the soldiers have done without the eye it gave them, and the neighbor said he'd heard it was really a fishing-float that the barbarian had dressed up, and the first man said he hadn't heard that, and they argued the merits in the same unhurried way they argued charioteer form. I did not turn around.

---

The column they had not finished, and the column was the thing that undid me.

It stood behind the basilica in its own small court between two libraries, and it was already up, white as a peeled bone, banded top to bottom in a carved ribbon that wound round and round like the rind cut from an apple in one unbroken strip. Men on a scaffold were still working the lower turns with the libraries' colonnades half-hiding them, fine little chisels going at the stone, and the stone was the war. My war. Both of them.

You read it from the bottom and the band climbed. There was the river with its god leaning out of the bank to watch the legions cross, the boats lashed into a bridge, the wall of shields. There were the camps going up, the men cutting turf, hauling timber, the orderly violence of Roman work that I had marched inside of and learned to fear more than any battle. Higher up, where I had to crane and could only half-see past the scaffold, the war went on, the burning of villages, a Dacian woman with her hands up, the king at the last with the cavalry closing, the head being carried off on a tray.

The carver had made the column true and left out what happened.

I stood under it a long time. Somewhere in those turns was the Loaf, the hill with the Dacians massed behind it that a column was to advance past in three days, and somewhere in that white unbroken ribbon there was no man falling a hundred feet inside burning cloth because the wind off the river took the basket the way I had said it would, and was overruled, because the no of a slave is a sound and not an event. The carver had not put him there. There was no place for him in the rind of the apple. The army had recorded him as a volunteer and it had not been a lie, and the column recorded nothing of him at all, and both were true, and standing under it I understood for the first time what the difference was between a thing that happened and a thing that is remembered, and that the second is made by hands holding chisels, on purpose, for money, by men told what to leave out. The river god leaned from the bank, kind and enormous, watching the legions cross. He had watched a great many things from that bank and the stone had been told to show him watching this.

I do not know how long I would have stood there. A voice behind me said, in fast Greek-cornered Latin, "It's wrong, of course. The bridge. They've drawn it too narrow and left out a whole course of piling so the boats would fit the band. Tell a story long enough and someone shortens it for the wall."

---

Apollodorus had not changed in the particular fashion of the truly proud. The world had gone on arranging itself around him and confirmed him in everything. He was past sixty now and built like a thing that had been left out in weather for a long time and not weakened by it, the gray gone white, the hands worse than I remembered, two knuckles on the right thickened wrong from some old break he had never let set. He had the emperor's whole Forum behind him as a thing he had made, and he wore it lightly, certain you would notice.

"It's still standing," I said, of the bridge. "The real one. I came back over it."

"I know you did. I built it to stand. That up there," and he tipped his white head at the column without looking, dismissing a likeness of his own work, "is a poem about it. They asked me to approve the marching order of the reliefs and I told them I am an engineer and not a man who arranges processions, and they were offended, and I let them be." A short hard sound that on another man would have been a laugh. "You're looking at it like it bit you."

"I served on it," I said. "Both of them."

"So did I. Mine's the part that holds water." He came and stood by me and looked up at the band with me, and for a moment we were two men reading the same lie and knowing different halves of it, which is as close to friendship as we ever came, and was not friendship. "Your bag is up there. Did you see? Two turns above the scaffold, a little round thing over the camp with strings on it. The carver didn't know what it was. He'd been told a Thulean device watched from the air, and he made a fishing-float with a man drawn small beneath it, looking up." He found it for me with a blunt finger, traced the air. "They asked me what it was meant to be. I told them a clever toy that does one thing."

He had said that to me on the Danube eleven years before, of the same toy, before it killed a man he never bothered to learn the name of. He said it now without malice and without memory of having said it, because to Apollodorus my balloon had always been the one true sentence and he had no reason to vary it.

"It does one thing," I said. "You build the one thing that holds water and I build the one thing that floats on air. We're the same trade, you just got the better element."

This pleased him. Vanity is the door into a proud man and I had learned where his hinges were. "Water is the only honest element," he said. "It finds its level and it tells no lies and if your work is wrong it shows you on the first day, not the tenth. Fire flatters you." He looked at me sidelong, the old appraisal, the one that had never once warmed past wary. "You're rich now. They tell me you're rich and courted and a senator keeps your name in his mouth. You've gotten fat in the standing-still way. When did you last fail at something with your own hands?"

It landed because it was fair. "A clock," I said. "I cannot make a clock keep true time. The thing that lets the wheel go tooth by tooth instead of running down all at once. I had it in my head and it's gone." I had never said this aloud to anyone but Hermes, who had only grunted, and Tyche, who had written it down in a script no one else could read. To say it to Apollodorus was to hand a blade to a man I knew would take it.

He took it. "Then you've reached the bottom of the well you brought with you," he said, simply, not unkindly, which made it worse. "Every man does. I reached mine years ago and have been getting clever about the same depth of water ever since. The difference is I know how deep my well goes. You keep dropping the bucket as if you'll be surprised." He turned from the column. "Build the things you can hold in your hands until the day you die and let the rest go. The men who chase the thing they can almost remember go mad, or get poor, or get killed for promising it."

---

There was a stir at the basilica end, the particular ripple a crowd makes when men of weight are moving through it, and Apollodorus's face did a thing I had not seen it do before, which was close, like a hand making a fist out of itself.

A knot of men came along the colonnade, the kind of knot the Forum was full of that day, the great walking and being seen to walk. I knew few of them by sight. One I half-knew, a Spaniard of the emperor's own blood and circle, somewhere near forty, full-bearded in the Greek manner when no Roman of standing wore a beard, which told you before he opened his mouth that he intended you to find him learned. He had a draughtsman's roll under his arm in a crowd where no one carried work, and he came toward the column deliberately, a man who had opinions forming in advance of his arrival, but there was something in how he read the proportions first, eyes going up the shaft, across to the library colonnades, back, measuring without a rod, that was not performance. He understood the building before he spoke about it. He stopped. He looked up at the column, and then he looked at the libraries flanking it, and he said something to the man beside him about the proportion of the colonnade, gesturing, sketching a different cornice in the air with two fingers as if the air were a wax tablet.

Apollodorus watched him as you watch a child rearrange tools on a bench you have spent forty years learning.

"He designs," Apollodorus said to me, low, not low enough. "He draws gourds. Domes like cut melons, one swallowing another, lines no stone wants to make. He showed Caesar a temple once with the roof in segments like a squash and was very proud of it. I told him to go and paint his gourds, and leave temples to men who know what holds a thing up." The contempt in it was total and serene, the contempt of a man who has never once been wrong about water. "He has not forgiven me. Men like that keep a ledger. They put a small mark in it and carry it for thirty years and one day they are in a position to settle, and they settle the whole column at once."

The bearded man's eye traveled the crowd as men of that rank let their eyes travel, owning what they crossed, and found Apollodorus, and there was a held instant where the two of them looked at each other across the white shadow of the column, and the bearded man inclined his head a precise small distance, courteous, cold, the bow you give a man whose work you mean someday to improve upon, and Apollodorus gave him back nothing at all, not a nod, not a word, only the flat blank stare he gave a course of piling that had been laid wrong. Then the knot moved on toward the libraries and the moment closed and the bearded man laughed at something one of his circle said, easily, a man entirely at home in the world.

I felt the cold of it go up my back. I had stood in enough rooms by then to know the size of a powerful man being despised by a prouder one in front of witnesses. Apollodorus had the truth about water and the certainty that truth was enough. The bearded man had the long memory and the ledger and, one day, time. I had watched a man I respected spit, gently, into a well he might one day have to drink from, and do it with the clean conscience of someone who had never imagined the well belonging to anyone but himself.

"You shouldn't have said that loud enough for him to hear," I said.

"I have been saying true things loud enough to be heard for sixty years," Apollodorus said, "and it has built me everything you see." He looked up at his Forum, at his column wrapped in its shortened poem, at the gilt roof flinging the sun, and there was no crack in him anywhere, no place the doubt could get a finger in. "A man who softens his true things to suit the listener is building on sand. You of all men should know what water does to sand."

---

He went off to inspect something about the basilica drains, which were his and which delighted him. I was turning back toward the column when I heard my name, and looked, and there was Crispus moving through the crowd toward me at the particular pace of a man who has been watching you and waited for the right moment to arrive.

He looked well. Not the strained-looking Crispus of the salons who prosecuted each argument as if the alternative were execution, but a man who had arrived somewhere and set down the bag. He wore his good mantle and the silver ring he favored, and he came up to me and made the conventional greeting, and then he stood there beside me for a moment in companionable silence, looking up at the column.

"A fine work," he said. "Trajan's men did well with it."

I said nothing useful. We had nothing to say to each other that was not a continuation of the argument, and that day I did not want the argument.

He did not want it either, which was the odd thing. He said: "I had occasion last month to visit my patron's works in Puteoli. He has a glass concern there, decorative vessels, modest operation. While I was going over the accounts with his man, I found an entry I could not place. A prize payment. A sum rather larger than you would expect for finished glass, not for a commission, nothing like that, for meeting a specification. A clarity standard. This man produced glass that reads to a stated distance without a color fringe, and was paid a year's wages for it."

I waited.

"I was curious enough to ask. He told me the prize had come through the guild, quietly, under a collegium name I recognized from your press accounts." Crispus turned to look at me. He was not angry. That was the second thing. "I began to inquire elsewhere. A farrier in Capua, a measurement table. A bronzesmith, I believe his name is Glabrio, on cylinder tolerances. Eight or ten more over the past several years, scattered across trades, none of them knowing the others exist." He paused. "Each man solving one piece of a design no single man among them can see complete."

The fountain near the basilica portico was running loud. Two senators were moving along the colonnade in conversation and I watched them until they passed.

"You are farming it," Crispus said. "The knowledge. You post the target and the whole city works on it, each man spending his own time and silver, and you pay only the one who arrives. No one bears the full cost. You bear none of the failures." Something moved across his face, a controlled expression I had never seen on him before, the look of a man who has just understood something that required real work to understand. "The guild structures depend on knowledge staying within the trade. A glassman's knowledge is a glassman's. It is what he sells; it is what makes him a master rather than an apprentice. You are paying craftsmen to make their private knowledge visible, and then it is visible, and it does not go back inside the trade." He let that sit a moment. "You have been doing this for years, and I have been arguing about your figures and your fire, and those were not the thing to argue about at all."

I said: "I pay fair prizes to men who do good work. I have done nothing a guild master with money couldn't do."

"No guild master would. They have no reason to. The prize benefits the whole enterprise and the cost comes from one man's purse." He shook his head, the fractional gesture of a man correcting an error in his own notes. "The objection is not fraud. I withdraw fraud." He said it as flatly as returning a borrowed tool. "The objection is this: you are rewriting what a craft is. A trade is men and a tradition. You are turning it into a set of targets anyone can run at. The son of a freedman who has never held a hammer can post a specification for a bronze cylinder and a third-generation bronzesmith will work a year to meet it. The bronzesmith does not own his knowledge after that. You do."

"The bronzesmith was paid."

"Yes." He looked up at the column again. "That's the elegant part."

He made the leaving-gesture, the conventional thing, and moved off through the crowd. Not angry. Not threatening. The thing he had meant to communicate was communicated and he was done with the scene. I watched him go.

I had known men argue against the prize competitions before, always on the fraud angle or the impiety angle, the numerals-are-foreign or the flying-is-unnatural register. This was different. He was not arguing I had cheated. He was arguing I had built a machine, and his argument was that the machine was wrong in a way I could not simply refute, and he was going to come back with that argument in a place where it had consequences, and he had just told me to my face that he was going to.

---

I stood a while longer under the column in the wheeling noise of the dedication, a rich and courted man among marvels, named and pointed at, with a senator's hand at my elbow through a freedman and a senator's knowledge of where my walls were thin, with a word in the priestly colleges that had a courtroom at the end of its slope and an old priest somewhere in the city not deciding what he would say about my fire, and with a son nine days old at home who could rise as high as Rome went and was, for exactly that reason, the largest stone I had yet set rolling.

The sun came off the bronze roof and I put my hand up. All around me Rome looked up at the white ribbon of the war and read the story it had been given, the crossing, the king run down, the head on the tray, the river god watching kindly from the bank, and none of them looked for the man who was not carved there because there had never been a place for him in the rind of the apple, and I thought that this, more than the figures or the press or the powder, was the real engine of the world, the chisel that decides what the future will be allowed to remember, and that I did not own it, and that the men who did were even now walking the colonnade ahead of me, settling their ledgers, deciding which fires were prayers and which were offenses, and that all my cleverness had bought me was a higher place to stand while they decided.

I went home along the wharf in the cooling evening. The river had the same teeth in its wind it had carried all that winter, and I drew my cloak and let myself in at my own gate, and the boy was awake and making his small blind sounds at a ceiling he did not yet know he could one day be carved on.

Marcia had the tally open. "Well?" she said, not looking up. "Is it as big as they say."

"Bigger," I said. "And mostly true."

She made a mark and turned the tablet over. "Then it'll do," she said, and I sat down across from her in the lamplight to count the day's loads.

Outside on the slope the river went on telling the wharf the same flat thing it told every stone it passed, which was nothing, which was that it had found its level and would keep it, and did not care in the least whose walls were thin.
