## Prediction (written before reading ch13)

The most likely single biggest plot turn in chapter 13 is that Daniel is ordered — or effectively coerced by Celer and Macer's expectations — to put a man in a crewed, fire-carrying basket for a manned ascent, and the flight ends in serious disaster: either the man is killed, or the balloon is destroyed in a way that strips Daniel of his protected status, causing Macer to wash his hands of him and leaving Daniel exposed to whatever Vibenius (the haruspex) or Crispus has been quietly preparing. In other words: the pressure to build the deadly manned balloon finally breaks, someone pays with their life or Daniel pays with his freedom, and the cage of Macer's "protection" snaps open — for worse.

## Actual ch13 turn

The army demands Daniel launch a free (untethered) manned flight so a soldier can see over the Loaf hill and count the Dacians before a column advances. Despite Daniel's explicit warnings about the wind and the danger, tribune Celer allows it and a volunteer named Marcus Aurelius Sabinus goes up. At a hundred feet, the river wind catches the bag, swings the fire-bowl sideways, the flame escapes the bowl and runs up the greased seam, and the bag comes apart. Sabinus falls a hundred feet with burning cloth coming down over him and is killed on impact. The chapter ends with Daniel kneeling in the dirt holding a scrap of scorched linen, Naso placing his one good hand briefly over Daniel's, Celer accepting his own share of the blame and promising no more men go up, and Pamphilus quietly boiling water by the river while Daniel finally burns the rag.

## Score: PARTIALLY CORRECT

The core of the prediction was correct: the manned free flight happens, it ends in catastrophic disaster, and a man dies. That central turn landed exactly. The partial miss is on the consequence half of the prediction. Daniel does not lose Macer's protection in this chapter, nor does Vibenius or Crispus move against him. Celer, unexpectedly, absorbs his share of the guilt plainly and honestly, and the aftermath is grief and moral reckoning rather than a political or legal collapse of Daniel's position. The cage does not snap open — it stays intact, which is arguably a more devastating outcome than losing protection, because Daniel must go on building inside it.

## What telegraphed it

Several specific setups in ch01–12 made this turn highly foreseeable:

1. **The Naso accident (ch11)** is the direct rehearsal — the same physics, the same fire-runs-up-the-seam mechanism, and Daniel's own interior voice: "this is how I kill somebody" (ch11). The chapter ends with Celer saying "it works... we'll do it again with a smaller fire and a longer basket." The sequel was written there.

2. **The cliff edge of the tethered balloon (ch12)** — Daniel stands on the bald hill feeling Celer's boots coming up the path to tell him "what they wanted me to fly next," and the chapter closes on that dread without resolving it. The forward momentum could only discharge in ch13.

3. **Daniel's repeated formula "a thing you cannot argue with" (ch05–06)** — the novel has spent eight chapters showing that the balloon's undeniable visibility is also undeniable leverage for the army; once the army wanted something the balloon could only almost do, coercion was inevitable.

4. **Celer's specific arithmetic (ch11–12)** — he watches Naso burn and says "it works" and counts the rafts on the far bank; the novel shows a military mind that registers a burned soldier as a price, not a verdict. His arrival on the hill in ch12's final line signals the demand is coming.

5. **The fire-bowl physics, established over three chapters (ch06, ch07, ch11)** — the seam-catches-the-fire mechanism has been shown, re-shown, and burned into Daniel (literally). Any attentive reader knows exactly what will happen if the bowl tips.

What was not foreseeable: Celer's moral clarity and honesty in the aftermath, and the emotional register of the ending (Naso's hand on Daniel's, Pamphilus boiling the water). The novel earned an unexpected grace note inside the predicted catastrophe.
