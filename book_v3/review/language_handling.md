# Language Handling Audit

## 1. Which Languages Daniel Learns, and Who Speaks What

Daniel encounters **Latin** as the street vernacular from day one. The physician
Heras — a Greek freedman from Pergamon — is the first person to treat him as a
mind; he initially speaks Latin to Daniel, switches to Greek on a hunch when
Latin produces nothing, and continues to use both throughout their relationship.

Latin is established as Daniel's survival language and the one he works to master
first. Greek comes second, picked up "slower, sideways, the way you learn a thing
standing next to someone who knows it" (ch05), principally through Heras. By ch10
Daniel explicitly cannot follow the educated symposium chatter — "my Latin had
gotten good enough to order cloth and pay women and curse at a fire. It was
nowhere near good enough for this, for the allusions, the half-lines of poetry
everyone finished in their heads, the Greek tags dropped in to show they could."
He explicitly states "I don't read Greek" to the antagonist Crispus (ch10), which
is both true and used against him. The bilingual educated world (Latin street /
Greek scholarship) is accurately rendered throughout: military men and traders
speak Latin; physicians, architects (Apollodorus, ch12), and the emperor Hadrian
(ch35) slip between Latin and Greek.

Daniel's English is treated as a private cipher. He keeps his private encyclopedia
in "the homeland script" that no one around him can read, used for the technical
books he is building for future readers (ch20, ch40). Tyche eventually learns to
read and write it (ch40), and in the death scenes (ch50) she reads the books aloud
to him in "the homeland tongue."

## 2. Acquisition: Learning Curve

The acquisition curve is shown in explicit, sustained detail across chapters 1–5
and continues to leave marks through chapter 10 and beyond.

**Days 1–3 (ch01–ch03):** Total incomprehension. "A whole language was happening
around me all the time, wet and fast and full of those hard k-sounds where I
expected soft ones, and none of it stuck. It washed off" (ch03). Daniel
understands gestures only. He explicitly cannot catch even the optio's name from
the stream of speech.

**Day 3 (ch04):** First footholds via Greek roots embedded in English: "pou"
(where), "therm-" (heat), "photo-" (light) — single words only, zero sentence
comprehension. He explicitly notes: "I never once understood a sentence. People
who tell you they picked up a language in an afternoon are lying or they're
geniuses, and I was neither. I had *where*, and *heat*, and *light*, and the
optio's name, and the mangled middle of my own" (ch04).

**~Months 1–4 (ch05):** Latin acquired the hard way — "being thirsty and cold and
humiliated, pointing at things, getting the word wrong, getting laughed at by a
ten-year-old, trying again." The chapter details the phonological problems: the
hard *c*, and especially the *v* pronounced as *w*, which marks him as foreign
"in the first three words." Case endings defeated him for weeks ("I went around
announcing myself as the object of every sentence"). He builds a mental grammar by
pattern-recognition, aided by two years of high-school French giving him the
concept of inflection. Greek is absorbed slower, "sideways," through exposure to
Heras.

**~1 year in (ch05 end / ch09):** "Functional broken Latin" — can hold a
conversation about concrete present things, gets lost at speed, "could barely
read, only the figures and a few words." Under pressure in ch09, "My Latin came
out the way it always did under pressure, the cases sliding, the v turning to w in
my mouth before I could stop it." Heras speaks to him "slow and clean" because
that is what he can follow.

**Year 2–3 and beyond:** Conversational, but not literary. He describes "toga-Latin
I had drilled into myself" (ch25), a register he consciously learned for formal
settings. He never stops being identifiable as a foreigner by his accent and
occasional case slips. At the Macer dinner party (ch10) he is publicly humiliated
by the rhetorician Crispus precisely because his Latin fails him "at exactly the
speed I needed it."

**Late book (ch40, ch50):** The bible's promised drift ("by the late book he thinks
partly in Latin and has to reach for the English word") is realised in ch50:
"when the room went strange and the Latin would not come and I reached past it, in
the dark behind my eyes, for the words of the other tongue, the first tongue, the
one I had not spoken aloud to a living soul in fifty-seven years, and found that
those were going too."

## 3. Timeline and Consistency

The progression is internally consistent and carefully tracked:

| Stage | Chapter | Competence |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | ch01 | Zero comprehension; gestures only |
| Day 2–3 | ch02–ch03 | Gestures; no usable words; recognises name-sounds only |
| Day 3 | ch04 | Greek single-word footholds; zero sentence comprehension |
| ~4 months | ch05 | Broken functional Latin; barely literate; v/w accent; case errors |
| ~1 year | ch05 end / ch09 | Conversational for concrete topics; still fails under pressure |
| ~2–3 years | ch10 | Good for trade/work; inadequate for literary Latin or dinner-party Greek |
| ~7 years | ch25 | Formal "toga-Latin" drilled; still heard as a foreigner |
| ~35 years | ch35 | Emperor tests him by switching mid-sentence between Greek and Latin |
| ~57 years | ch50 | Latin dominant; English deteriorating; both languages failing with health |

There are no apparent scenes where Daniel understands Latin before he should. The
canon log (bible/08_canon_log.md) explicitly flags this as a rule: "Do not let
him eavesdrop on plot-critical Latin he couldn't yet understand" (bible/05_world_rules.md).
The early chapters honour this — in ch03 he watches two soldiers talk all night
and "could not have told you whether they were arguing or joking."

His foreigner status persists at every social level. He is called "the Thulean"
and "the barbarian" by everyone from the equestrian Macer to the emperor Hadrian
across thirty-plus years of chapters. Crispus uses his accent and his inability
to read Greek as live ammunition against him (ch10, ch25). In ch35, Hadrian tests
him by opening in Greek before switching to Latin — a sophisticate's check on
which language he reaches for first.

**Literacy** is handled distinctly from speech and consistently. He arrives
illiterate in Latin (ch04 he can draw numbers but not read words), is described
at the one-year mark as "barely read, only the figures and a few words" (ch05),
and admits he cannot read Greek even years later (ch10). His technical books are
written in English — the private cipher — precisely because his Latin, while
spoken fluently enough, is insufficient for the sophisticated written register,
and because secrecy demands it (ch40: "there would be three copies of the whole,
in the homeland script, and a fourth begun in Latin only as far up as the shapes,
because past the shapes the Latin failed me and the cipher was the safer keeping
anyway"). This is a notable and well-handled consistency: spoken fluency and full
literacy are not conflated.

His teaching of Arabic numerals to others is framed around the written symbol,
not spoken Latin — he demonstrates on wax tablets, and others understand the
marks without needing his words (ch03–ch04). The printing-press work (ch10) uses
Greek letters first (cut by a professional letter-cutter) because Daniel does not
yet read Latin well enough to proof the blocks — this too is consistent.

## 4. Realism Verdict

**The language handling is a genuine strength of the book** and represents one of
its most carefully maintained worldbuilding commitments. It avoids every major
pitfall of the genre:

- No instant or unexplained fluency.
- No hand-wave ("somehow he learned the language"). The mechanism — survival
  immersion, pattern-recognition from two years of high-school French, Greek root
  recognition via English cognates, and a physician willing to speak slowly — is
  plausible and shown scene by scene.
- The phonological and grammatical specifics are accurate: classical Latin's hard
  *c*, the *v*/*w* distinction, the complex case system, the diglossia of Latin
  (street) vs Greek (educated). These are not decorative; they generate plot
  (the accent that costs him in negotiations, the inability to read Greek used
  against him in a public ambush).
- The accent persists, permanently. He never "passes" as a native, even after
  fifty-seven years, which is realistic and thematically load-bearing: his
  perpetual outsider status is inseparable from his outsider influence.
- Literacy is correctly distinguished from oral fluency, and his inability to
  read/write Latin to a literary standard is correctly reflected in his decision
  to write the technical books in English.
- The long-term language drift — English eroding as Latin becomes dominant — is
  foreshadowed in the style guide and realised in ch50 with quiet effectiveness.

The one modest underexamination: Greek acquisition is less dramatised than Latin,
handled largely by summary ("over the months I picked up Greek too, slower,
sideways"). Given that educated Roman discourse is bilingual and Greek is the
language of the physician who is his closest ally, a scene or two of Greek
incomprehension analogous to the Latin acquisition scenes might have been earned.
This is a minor gap, not a plot hole. The architecture is sound.
