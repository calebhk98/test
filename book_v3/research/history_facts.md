# Canon Reference: Rome under Trajan & Hadrian, 98-138 AD

Compiled for the novel. Where facts are debated or estimated, they are flagged
[DEBATED] or [EST]. Dates are AD unless noted. Web access was available during
compilation; core dates were checked against standard sources (Cassius Dio, the
Historia Augusta, Pliny, modern syntheses). Ancient sources disagree more than a
modern reader expects, so treat round numbers as approximations.

---

## 1. POLITICAL TIMELINE 96-138

- **96, 18 Sept:** Domitian assassinated. Senate proclaims the elderly senator
  **Nerva** emperor the same day. Damnatio memoriae on Domitian.
- **97:** Nerva is weak; the Praetorian Guard mutinies and forces him to execute
  Domitian's killers. To shore up his position he **adopts Trajan** (governor of
  Upper Germany, commanding Rhine legions) as son and heir, late 97.
- **98, ~27/28 Jan:** **Nerva dies** (natural causes, after ~16 months). Trajan
  succeeds smoothly - the first emperor adopted on merit, not blood.
- **98-99:** Crucially, Trajan **does NOT rush to Rome.** He stays on the
  **Rhine and Danube frontier** through 98, inspecting defenses and preparing.
  He finally **enters Rome in 99**, on foot, greeting senators as equals - a
  deliberately modest *adventus* contrasted with Domitian. [Useful for plot: in
  98 the emperor is physically absent from the capital.]
- **101-102: First Dacian War.** Trajan crosses the Danube (pontoon bridge by
  the engineer **Apollodorus of Damascus**) against King **Decebalus**. Hard
  fighting; Decebalus sues for terms, accepts a client-king peace.
- **105-106: Second Dacian War.** Decebalus breaks terms. Trajan crosses on
  Apollodorus's great **stone bridge at Drobeta**, storms the capital
  **Sarmizegetusa** (106). Decebalus flees and **commits suicide.**
- **106:** **Dacia annexed** as a province (modern Romania). Enormous gold/silver
  loot funds building in Rome. **107:** 123 days of games, thousands of
  gladiators/beasts [ancient figures, treat as inflated].
- **~106:** Annexation of the Nabataean kingdom as province **Arabia Petra.**
- **112-113:** **Trajan's Forum and Column** dedicated in Rome (the Column's
  spiral relief narrates the Dacian wars; it survives today).
- **113-117: Parthian War.** Trajan marches east, annexes **Armenia** and
  **Mesopotamia**, takes the Parthian capital **Ctesiphon** (116), reaches the
  **Persian Gulf** - the furthest east Rome's frontier ever extended. Then
  revolts erupt in the rear (Mesopotamia, plus the **Kitos War**, a major Jewish
  diaspora revolt 115-117 in Cyrene, Egypt, Cyprus, brutally suppressed).
- **117, 8 Aug:** **Trajan dies** at **Selinus in Cilicia** (south coast of
  modern Turkey) of a stroke, while withdrawing toward Rome. Age ~63.
- **117, 11 Aug:** **Hadrian** (Trajan's ward and kinsman, then governor of
  Syria) proclaimed emperor at **Antioch.** [DEBATED] A deathbed adoption was
  announced, but it was widely suspected to be engineered by Trajan's widow
  **Plotina** and the Praetorian Prefect **Acilius Attianus.** Contemporaries
  doubted it.
- **118: The "Four Consulars" affair.** Four senior ex-consuls
  (**Palma, Celsus, Nigrinus, Lusius Quietus**) are killed for an alleged
  conspiracy, without a proper Senate trial - hunted down in absentia. Hadrian
  blamed Attianus and the Senate, but the killings poisoned his relations with
  the Senate for life. This is the defining stain on his accession.
- **Hadrian's reign (117-138): consolidation, not conquest.** He **abandons
  Trajan's eastern conquests** (Mesopotamia, Armenia) back to client/buffer
  status - hugely controversial. He **travels the empire** more than any emperor
  (Britain, Gaul, Germany, Spain, Greece, Egypt, the East). He builds
  **Hadrian's Wall in Britain (begun ~122)**, the **Pantheon** (rebuilt
  ~118-128, the dome survives), his **Villa at Tivoli**, his **Mausoleum**
  (now Castel Sant'Angelo). His lover **Antinous** drowns in the Nile (130);
  Hadrian deifies him. **132-136: Bar Kokhba revolt** in Judaea, savagely
  crushed; Jerusalem refounded as **Aelia Capitolina**.
- **138, 10 July:** **Hadrian dies** at Baiae. Already adopted **Antoninus Pius**
  (who in turn was made to adopt Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus).

## 2. HOW POWER WORKED

- **The emperor (princeps):** technically "first citizen," holding a bundle of
  legal powers (tribunician power, proconsular *imperium*) rather than a single
  "king" title. In practice an autocrat backed by the army. He controlled the
  treasury, the armies, appointments, and could grant citizenship.
- **The Senate:** ~600 men. **Membership is for LIFE, not a fixed term** (this is
  a common error - clarify in the book). You enter the Senate by being elected to
  (or appointed to) the quaestorship and stay a senator unless expelled by the
  censor/emperor or you lose the property qualification. A senator's son was not
  automatically a senator; he had to win office. Senators needed a property
  qualification of **1,000,000 sesterces.**
- **Cursus honorum** (the career ladder, roughly): military tribune / *vigintivir*
  -> **quaestor** (finance, ~age 25, gets you into Senate) -> **aedile** or
  **tribune of the plebs** -> **praetor** (judicial, ~age 30+) -> **consul**
  (the top annual magistracy, two at a time, ~age 33+ but usually older;
  "suffect" consuls were appointed mid-year, so many men held it). After consul:
  governorships of major provinces, *praefectus urbi* (city prefect).
- **Key offices/men:** **Praetorian Prefect** (commander of the Guard; an
  *equestrian*, hugely powerful, e.g. Attianus). **Praefectus urbi** (senator,
  runs the city, commands the urban cohorts/police). **Praefectus annonae**
  (grain supply). **Praefectus Aegypti** (Egypt, governed by an equestrian, not
  a senator - emperor's personal grain-province).
- **The Praetorian Guard:** ~9-10 cohorts, the only major armed force in Italy,
  stationed at the **Castra Praetoria** in Rome. Better paid than legionaries.
  King-makers; they extorted donatives at each accession.
- **Equestrians (equites) vs Senators:** Equestrians = the business/military
  upper class, property qualification **400,000 sesterces.** They officered the
  army, ran finance and key prefectures. Below them: ordinary citizens, then
  freedmen, then slaves, then non-citizen foreigners (*peregrini*).
- **Patronage / clientela:** the spine of society. A **patronus** protected and
  funded **clientes**; clients gave political support, votes, and the morning
  **salutatio** (clients queue at the patron's door at dawn to greet him, get a
  small dole/*sportula*, and walk him to the Forum). EVERYONE has a patron above
  and clients below; the emperor is patron of all.
- **An outsider with no status** could do very little legally. He couldn't sue or
  be sued under full civil law, couldn't make a Roman will, couldn't legally
  marry a citizen, had no political voice. **What he COULD do:** attach himself to
  a patron, work a trade, accumulate money (foreigners could trade and own
  property). To rise: **gain a patron** by being useful (skill, knowledge, a
  novelty), then citizenship could be granted by the emperor as a favor, or
  earned via 25 years' auxiliary military service, or (for a slave) acquired at
  manumission by a citizen master. A freedman took his ex-master's family name
  and remained his patron's client.

## 3. THE CITY OF ROME c.100

- **Population: ~1,000,000** [EST; range ~800k-1.2m, [DEBATED]]. The largest city
  on earth. Densely packed, mostly in tenements.
- **Layout:** The **Forum Romanum** (civic/political heart, temples, basilicas,
  Senate house). **Palatine Hill** (emperor's palaces, the rich). **Capitoline**
  (Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus). The **Subura** (notorious crowded, noisy,
  poor/red-light district just NE of the Forum). **Campus Martius** (flat plain by
  the Tiber - theaters, baths, the Pantheon, public monuments). The **Tiber**
  (muddy, flood-prone, working river; bridges; the port quays at the foot of the
  Aventine). The **Aventine** (plebeian, trade). **Trans Tiberim** (Trastevere,
  across the river - immigrants, Jews, Syrians, workers).
- **Insulae:** apartment blocks up to ~5-7 storeys (legal height cap ~70 Roman
  feet under Trajan, ~21m). Ground floors = shops (*tabernae*); upper floors get
  worse, cheaper, more dangerous the higher you go. No running water above ground
  floor; chamber pots emptied into the street or stairwell. Fire and collapse were
  constant dangers (no good firefighting beyond the **vigiles**, the night
  watch/fire brigade).
- **Streets:** mostly narrow, winding, unlit at night, dangerous after dark.
  **Wheeled cart traffic was banned in daytime** (Julian law), so the city roared
  with delivery carts ALL NIGHT - chronic noise. Stepping-stones across streets
  fouled with mud, dung, and refuse. Open shops, hawkers, beggars, litters
  carrying the rich.
- **Smell/noise:** smoke, woodfires, baking bread, frying, the reek of the
  fullers' (laundries using stale **urine** as bleach), tanneries, butchers,
  sweat, animal dung, the public latrines, incense at temples. Constant shouting,
  hammering, schoolchildren, street performers.
- **Water:** ~9 **aqueducts** by 100 AD feeding hundreds of public **fountains**,
  public baths, and a few private homes. Water ran continuously; most people drew
  from street fountains. **Public latrines** (rows of seats over flushing
  channels, no privacy) and the great **Cloaca Maxima** sewer.
- **Colosseum (Flavian Amphitheatre):** **finished/inaugurated 80 AD**, so in 98
  it is barely 18 years old and fully intact, gleaming travertine, ~50,000
  spectators. The **Circus Maximus** (chariot racing) held far more,
  ~150,000-250,000 [EST]. **Baths of Trajan** open 109.
- **The grain dole (annona):** ~**200,000** adult male citizens held a wooden
  **tessera** entitling them to a monthly ration of free/subsidized grain
  (~35 kg unground). Restricted to registered citizens - NOT a universal handout;
  the poor foreigner gets nothing.

## 4. DAILY LIFE & MATERIAL CULTURE

- **Clothing:** Everyone wears the **tunic** (knee-length wool/linen, belted).
  The **toga** is the formal Roman-citizen-male garment - heavy, awkward, draped,
  worn for public/civic occasions; non-citizens and slaves may NOT wear it. Women
  wear the *stola* (married) and *palla* (shawl). Colors mattered: senators wore a
  broad purple stripe (*latus clavus*) on the tunic, equestrians a narrow one.
  Footwear: leather sandals (*soleae*) and boots (*caligae* for soldiers).
  - **What a Roman makes of jeans/T-shirt/sneakers:** Trousers (*bracae*) =
    barbarian, specifically Gaulish/German/Persian, faintly shameful on a
    civilized man - a teen in jeans reads as a barbarian. The T-shirt's tight knit
    weave and the rubber/synthetic sneakers would be uncanny - no elastic, no
    machine-knit cloth, no vulcanized rubber existed; the regularity and dyes
    would look impossibly fine or witch-made. Zippers, snaps = inexplicable.
- **Food:** Bread is the staple (wheat). **Garum** (fermented fish sauce) is the
  ubiquitous condiment, in everything. Porridge/puls, olives, cheese, lentils,
  vegetables; meat is occasional for the poor. **Wine** is the universal drink,
  almost always **watered** (drinking it neat = barbaric/drunkard); even children
  drank watered wine; water alone was often distrusted. *Posca* (sour wine +
  water) was the soldier's/poor man's drink. No sugar (honey instead), no
  potatoes, tomatoes, maize, coffee, tea, citrus (rare), or distilled spirits.
- **Money:** **As** (copper) -> **Sestertius** = 4 asses (brass) -> **Denarius**
  = 4 sestertii (silver) -> **Aureus** = 25 denarii (gold). Rough purchasing
  power [EST, varied by place/date]:
  - Loaf of bread: ~1-2 asses.
  - Day's wage, unskilled laborer: ~1 denarius (so a few sesterces).
  - **Legionary base pay: ~300 denarii/year** under Domitian (=1,200 sesterces),
    minus deductions for food/kit.
  - A cheap tunic: a few sesterces; a slave: hundreds to thousands of sesterces
    depending on skill (a trained scribe/doctor far more).
  - Senatorial census: 1,000,000 sesterces. So the scales between a beggar and a
    senator are vast.
- **Writing:** Everyday/ephemeral writing on **wax tablets** (*tabulae*) with a
  bone/metal **stylus** (pointed to write, flat end to erase). Permanent/literary
  writing on **papyrus** scrolls (the *codex*/bound book is just emerging, used by
  the poor and early Christians). **Ink** = soot/gum (carbon) or iron-gall, with a
  reed pen (*calamus*). Parchment exists but papyrus dominates. **No paper, no
  printing** - every book hand-copied, expensive.
- **Literacy:** [DEBATED] Probably ~10-20% functionally literate overall, higher
  among urban men, the elite, and oddly among many skilled **slaves/freedmen**
  (scribes, secretaries, accountants - literacy was often a slave's job, not a
  status marker). Many who couldn't read could recognize numbers/signs. Reading
  aloud was normal; silent reading was remarked on as unusual.
- **Language:** **Latin** in the western empire and the army/administration.
  **Greek** is the language of culture, philosophy, medicine, and the entire
  eastern Mediterranean - an educated Roman is bilingual. A newcomer in Rome would
  hear Latin on the street but Greek among the learned, plus dozens of immigrant
  tongues (Aramaic/Syriac, Punic, Gaulish, Egyptian).
- **Measurement:** **Roman foot (pes)** ~29.6 cm. 5 feet = 1 *passus* (pace,
  ~1.48 m). **1,000 paces = 1 Roman mile (mille passus)** ~1.48 km (shorter than a
  modern mile). Weight: **libra** (pound) ~327 g, divided into 12 *unciae*
  (ounces). No standard clock; time told by sundials and water-clocks; the day
  (sunrise to sunset) was split into 12 hours that LENGTHENED in summer and
  SHORTENED in winter.

## 5. SLAVERY

- **Scale:** Pervasive. [EST/DEBATED] perhaps 1 in 4 to 1 in 3 people in Italy
  were enslaved; in Rome itself a huge fraction. A modest household might have a
  few slaves; the rich owned hundreds.
- **Sources:** war captives (the Dacian and Parthian wars flooded the market),
  children of slaves (*vernae*, born in the house), the exposed/abandoned, debt
  and piracy/kidnapping, the slave trade across the frontiers and from the East
  and Africa.
- **Legal status:** A slave was **property (res)**, not a person - could be bought,
  sold, beaten, worked to death, or used sexually with impunity; could not legally
  marry, own property (though many kept a *peculium*, a fund their master let them
  use), or testify except, often, **under torture**. Killing another's slave was
  damaging another man's property.
- **Manumission:** Common and expected - freeing slaves was routine, by will or
  formal ceremony. A freed slave of a citizen usually **became a citizen** (with
  limits: freedmen couldn't hold office or join the Senate, though their freeborn
  sons could rise). The ex-master became the freedman's **patron**; the freedman
  owed continued duties (*operae*) and respect.
- **Freedmen (liberti):** could grow extremely rich and powerful (imperial
  freedmen ran the bureaucracy; some became fabulously wealthy merchants - cf.
  the fictional Trimalchio). But the *stain* of servile origin clung socially.
- **Daily visibility:** Total and immediate. A newcomer would see slaves
  everywhere - carrying litters, serving in shops and homes, chained work gangs,
  branded or collared runaways, slaves sold naked on platforms in the markets with
  placards listing origin and faults, educated Greek slaves tutoring children. The
  ordinariness of it - that no one questions it - is the shock. (Book note: keep
  the anti-slavery argument in events, not speeches.)

## 6. RELIGION

- **State religion:** polytheist, ritual-focused (correct performance matters more
  than belief or morality). The great gods: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva (the Capitoline
  triad), Mars, Venus, etc. The **Vestal Virgins** guard the sacred flame. Religion
  and state are fused; priesthoods are political honors.
- **Divination:** **Augury** (reading the flight/behavior of birds, the sacred
  chickens' feeding) and **haruspicy** (reading the entrails - especially the
  liver - of sacrificed animals, an Etruscan art) were taken seriously before any
  major public act. No campaign, election, or building began without favorable
  omens. Prodigies (lightning, monstrous births, comets) demanded expiation.
- **Priestly colleges:** **Pontifices** (headed by the **Pontifex Maximus**, a
  title the EMPEROR holds), **Augures**, the *Quindecimviri* (keepers of the
  Sibylline Books), *Epulones*, the **Flamines** and Vestals. Membership = elite
  honor.
- **Foreign gods & novelty:** Romans were tolerant of foreign cults if they
  ADDED to, and didn't subvert, the civic order - Isis, Cybele, Mithras, Jewish
  worship (a licensed exception) all coexisted. What they feared was **novelty
  that threatened public order or denied the gods their due** (this is why
  Christians and others got persecuted - "atheism," refusing sacrifice, secret
  rites). Impiety/blasphemy against the state gods or the emperor's *genius* was
  dangerous. Magic was both used and feared/illegal.
- **Imperial cult:** dead emperors were **deified** (made *divus*) by Senate vote;
  the living emperor's *genius*/numen was honored, especially in the provinces and
  East. Refusing to honor it was treasonous, not merely irreligious.
- **Disease theory (CRITICAL for the "invisible creatures cause illness"
  pitch):** Romans had NO germ theory. Prevailing ideas:
  1. **Miasma** - bad/corrupt air from swamps, rot, stench causes disease (hence
     fear of marshes and "pestilential" air).
  2. **Hippocratic/Galenic humors** - illness as imbalance of the four humors
     (blood, phlegm, black bile, yellow bile), treated by bleeding, purging, diet,
     restoring balance. (Galen, the great physician, is active ~129-216, so his
     synthesis is just dawning at the book's end.)
  3. **Divine cause** - plague as the gods' anger, requiring sacrifice/expiation;
     and personal curses, the evil eye, malign spirits (*lemures*, etc.).
  - A claim of tiny **invisible living creatures** causing disease would map onto
    NOTHING in their framework. Reactions would range from "you mean malign
    spirits/demons?" to ridicule to suspicion of magic. The ATOMIST philosophers
    (Epicurus/Lucretius) believed in invisible particles and Varro had even
    speculated about tiny swamp creatures entering the body, so an educated person
    *might* find a hook - but the burden of proof is total. This is a strong
    source of friction/danger for the protagonist.

## 7. THE ARMY

- **Legions:** ~28-30 legions (Trajan raised some for Dacia), each ~5,000 heavy
  infantry **citizens**, plus roughly an equal number of **auxiliaries**
  (*auxilia*) - non-citizen provincials who earned citizenship after 25 years.
- **Structure:** Legion -> 10 **cohorts** -> ~6 **centuries** of ~80 men each ->
  led by **centurions** (the backbone, career officers risen from the ranks).
  Senior command: the **legatus** (senatorial), six **tribunes**, the **camp
  prefect** (*praefectus castrorum*). The **standards** (*aquila*, the eagle) were
  sacred; losing one was catastrophic disgrace.
- **Auxiliaries/specialists:** cavalry (*alae*), and famous specialist units -
  **Syrian/Cretan archers**, Balearic slingers, Batavian swimmers, Numidian light
  horse. The legions themselves were heavy infantry: short sword (*gladius*),
  javelins (*pila*), large shield (*scutum*), segmented armor (*lorica
  segmentata*) or mail.
- **Supply:** a major operation. Armies lived off pre-positioned depots, requisi-
  tioned grain, river/sea transport, and long baggage trains; soldiers carried
  ~weeks of rations and dug a fortified **marching camp every night**. The Danube
  and Rhine were supply highways. Engineering (roads, bridges, forts) was a core
  army function - cf. Apollodorus's Danube bridges.
- **Siege warfare of the era:** circumvallation, ramps, **ballistae** and
  **onagers** (torsion catapults throwing bolts/stones), battering rams, siege
  towers, *testudo* (tortoise shield formation), mining/sapping, and cutting the
  water supply (how Sarmizegetusa fell). Sieges were slow, logistical, attritional.
- **What military men value in a new device:** reliability and ruggedness in the
  field, a logistics edge (food preservation, faster bridging, better roads/maps,
  signaling over distance), a morale/medical edge (treating wounds, reducing camp
  disease - dysentery killed more than battle), accuracy/range in artillery, and
  anything that saves manpower. They distrust the fragile, the fiddly, and the
  unproven. A device that wins a siege or keeps a legion healthy is worth a
  fortune and a patron's protection.

## 8. GEOGRAPHY KNOWN TO ROME c.100

- **The known world (orbis terrarum):** centered on the Mediterranean ("our sea,"
  *Mare Nostrum*). Educated Romans had Ptolemy-era/Strabo/Pliny geography: fairly
  good on the Mediterranean, vaguer outward.
- **Africa:** the North African coast was Roman (rich grain provinces - Egypt,
  Africa Proconsularis). South of the Sahara was rumor - the **Garamantes** of the
  desert, the Nile's source unknown (sought, never found), "Aethiopia" (sub-Saharan
  Africa) as a land of fabulous peoples.
- **Persia/Parthia:** the great rival empire to the east, well known and feared;
  Trajan briefly conquered into it (see timeline). Beyond it lay vaguer lands.
- **India:** known and actively **traded with via the Red Sea and monsoon
  routes** - Roman ships sailed from Egyptian ports (Berenice, Myos Hormos) to SW
  India for **pepper, spices, gems, ivory**; huge silver outflow worried moralists.
  The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* (1st c.) is a real merchant's guide. So
  India is real, distant, exotic, reachable.
- **China:** known only as **"Seres"** ("the silk people") - a near-mythical land
  at the eastern edge of the earth, source of **silk** that arrived overland (Silk
  Road) and by sea via middlemen. Romans never dealt with China directly; an
  embassy is recorded reaching China only in 166 (after the book). Silk was a
  luxury and a moralists' scandal (immodest, drains gold).
- **Thule / Ultima Thule (IMPORTANT - the protagonist's claimed origin):** the
  legendary land at the **far north**, "farthest Thule," six days' sail north of
  Britain near a "frozen/congealed sea." Reported by the Greek explorer **Pytheas
  of Massalia** (~320 BC); cited by **Pliny** (NH, 77 AD); but **Strabo openly
  called Pytheas a liar**, and most Romans treated Thule as semi-mythical, on the
  edge of geography. By 100 AD it had become a byword for "the uttermost edge of
  the world." Candidates moderns suggest: Iceland, Norway, Shetland, Orkney,
  Faroes - the Romans themselves didn't know. **Plot value: a claim to be "from
  Thule" is unfalsifiable, exotic, and credible-enough precisely BECAUSE no Roman
  has been there - but an educated skeptic (a Strabo-reader) might scoff that
  Thule may not exist at all.**
- **Beyond the maps / the Atlantic / land to the west:** The **Atlantic
  ("Ocean," *Oceanus*)** was the edge of the world, a fearsome encircling sea.
  Romans knew the Atlantic coasts of Iberia, Gaul, and Britain, and that the
  ocean was tidal (a wonder to Mediterranean folk). They did NOT know of the
  Americas. Some philosophers (and Seneca, prophetically) speculated about **lands
  across the Ocean to the west**, and a few believed the earth was a sphere so one
  could in theory sail west to the Indies - but this was speculation, not
  knowledge. The dominant image: water all the way out, the unknown, sea monsters,
  the realm where the sun sets.

## 9. NAMES (usable stock)

Roman male citizen names = **praenomen + nomen (gens/family) + cognomen**
(e.g. Gaius Julius Caesar). Common praenomina (only ~17 in use, often abbreviated):
Gaius (C.), Lucius (L.), Marcus (M.), Publius (P.), Quintus (Q.), Titus (T.),
Gnaeus (Cn.), Aulus (A.), Sextus (Sex.), Tiberius (Ti.), Decimus (D.). Women
usually bore the feminine of the nomen (Julia, Claudia, Cornelia) + sometimes a
nickname. Slaves had ONE name (often Greek). Freedmen took the master's
praenomen+nomen + their old name as cognomen.

- **Senator (grand, old-family feel):**
  - Marcus Cornelius Fronto
  - Lucius Calpurnius Piso
  - Quintus Sosius Senecio
  - Publius Aelius Hadrianus (= Hadrian himself; avoid for a fictional one)
  - Gaius Julius Severus
- **Equestrian patron (rich, ambitious, businesslike):**
  - Titus Flavius Macer
  - Lucius Annius Verus
  - Gaius Vibius Maximus
  - Sextus Attius Suburanus
  - Marcus Mettius Rufus
- **Freedman craftsman (master's name + Greek/servile cognomen):**
  - Lucius Caecilius Iucundus
  - Marcus Vipsanius Eros
  - Gaius Julius Hermes
  - Publius Clodius Philadelphus
  - Tiberius Claudius Onesimus
- **Praetorian officer (centurion/tribune, solid Italian):**
  - Gnaeus Domitius Tullus
  - Marcus Aurelius Sabinus
  - Lucius Petronius Saturninus
  - Quintus Marcius Turbo (a real one; or invent Quintus Marcius Celer)
  - Titus Aelius Naso
- **Greek physician (single Greek name, or Greek + Roman if freed/citizen):**
  - Antiochus, Heras, Demetrios, Philotas, Asclepiades
  - if a citizen-physician: Gaius Stertinius Xenophon (style)
- **Household slave (single name, often Greek, sometimes descriptive):**
  - Male: Pamphilus, Eros, Hermes, Stichus, Davus, Geta, Syrus ("the Syrian"),
    Felix, Fortunatus, Onesimus.
  - Female: Doris, Chloe, Tyche, Helena, Fortunata, Psyche, Eutychis.

## 10. WEEK-ONE DETAILS THAT WOULD SHOCK A MODERN TEENAGER

1. **The smell** before anything else - fullers' shops curing cloth in vats of
   collected stale **urine**, open sewers, dung, smoke, unwashed crowds. The city
   stinks, all the time.
2. **Slaves everywhere, treated as furniture** - and nobody thinks twice. People
   sold naked on platforms with price-placards in the market.
3. **You can't read the signs and nobody reads silently** - books are scrolls,
   unrolled by hand; people read OUT LOUD; a silent reader is stared at.
4. **Public toilets are communal benches** - rows of holes, side by side, no
   stalls, strangers chatting; a shared sponge-on-a-stick (*xylospongium*) for
   wiping.
5. **No clocks, no fixed hours** - "the third hour" is shorter in winter than
   summer; people navigate by the sun and bells/announcements.
6. **Wine, not water, all day** - watered wine even for kids; drinking water
   straight is suspect; drinking wine UNwatered marks you as a barbarian drunk.
7. **The night is pitch dark and the carts come out after sundown** - daytime cart
   ban means the city rumbles and bangs with deliveries all night; you go out
   after dark with torch-bearers and a fear of muggers.
8. **No sugar, potatoes, tomatoes, coffee, citrus** - and **garum**, fermented
   fish guts, in nearly every dish.
9. **Money is heavy metal coins you bite to test** - no notes, no credit; a fat
   purse is a real weight and a real target.
10. **Casual lethal violence as entertainment** - the arena, executions at
    midday, beast hunts; bloodshed is a civic festival, free or cheap.
11. **Everyone's clothing announces their exact rank** - the toga, the purple
    stripes; a teen in jeans and sneakers reads instantly as a foreign barbarian
    (trousers = un-Roman) wearing impossibly fine, strange cloth.
12. **The morning salutatio** - before dawn, clients line up at a patron's door to
    pay respects and collect a handout; the whole social machine runs on this
    face-to-face dependency.
