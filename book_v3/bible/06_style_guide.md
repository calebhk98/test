# Style Guide

## POV and tense
- **First person, past tense**, narrated by Daniel Mercer.
- Default is close retrospective: Daniel is telling this from some later point,
  but he does NOT use that vantage to telegraph or spoil (no "little did I
  know"). He tells it as it felt then.
- Tense/POV may shift deliberately for specific interludes (see below). Any
  shift must be flagged in the chapter brief, never done casually.

## Permitted deliberate shifts (use sparingly, only when briefed)
- **Encyclopedia/journal excerpts**: short passages in Daniel's written English,
  flatter and more instructional, used as chapter epigraphs or interludes. These
  are the texts meant to be found after his death.
- **Roman-document interludes**: rare third-person or epistolary fragments
  (a senator's letter, a market record) that let the reader see Daniel from
  outside. Use to create real dramatic irony without declaring it.

## Voice — Daniel
- A 2020s American 17-year-old. Dry, self-deprecating, undercuts his own big
  moments. Swears sometimes (period-neutral: "hell," "damn," "Christ" lands
  oddly to him here, which he notices). Reaches for modern reference points and
  then has no one to share them with - that loneliness is a running note, shown
  not stated.
- He is good at math, okay at physics, vague on chemistry and biology. He has
  half-memories from history class, YouTube, and sci-fi novels. He frequently
  cannot remember the crucial detail. He fakes confidence in public and panics
  in private.
- As years pass, his English narration slowly picks up Latin cadence and Roman
  assumptions. By the late book he thinks partly in Latin and has to reach for
  the English word. Show this drift gradually; do not announce it.

## Prose mechanics
- American spelling.
- No em dashes. Commas, normal dashes, full stops.
- Latin words italicized on first meaningful use, then roman type. Keep them
  rare and load-bearing; don't pepper the text to seem authentic.
- Numbers: spell out under 100 in narration unless it's a measurement/quantity
  Daniel is reasoning about (then numerals are fine).
- Chapter length target: 2,500-4,000 words. Some interludes shorter.

## Sensory grounding for Rome
- Lean on smell, sound, temperature, the feel of materials. Rome is loud,
  pungent, bright, crowded. Avoid generic "ancient" atmosphere; use specific
  objects (a wax tablet, a bronze stylus, garum, lamp soot, wool grease).

## What the narration must NOT do
- Must not explain Roman history to the reader as a tour guide. Daniel knows a
  little and is wrong about a lot; the reader learns Rome as he does.
- Must not lecture on the morality of slavery. The reader sees it; Daniel reacts
  as a modern teen would (revulsion, helplessness, calculation), and acts
  through invention, rarely through speeches.
- Must not step outside time to assign significance. No "I would not understand
  until years later," no "that was the last time," no future-tense predictions
  about what will or won't happen. The retrospective frame licenses knowing what
  did happen; it does not license announcing what mattered. See full prohibition
  below.
- Must not announce its own emotional weight. If the scene is working, the
  reader already knows what it means. Appending an interpretation — even an
  elegant one — tells the reader they were right to feel it, which is smaller
  than letting them feel it.
- Must not restate the takeaway at the end of a scene. End on a concrete image,
  action, line of dialogue, or silence. See the section on scene endings below.

## Prose patterns to avoid in V2 (learned from V1 analysis)

V1 was technically clean but had systematic AI signatures that compound across
53 chapters into an unmistakable machine fingerprint. This section names them
precisely, with V1 examples, so they can be caught before they accumulate.

### BANNED: The correctio / "not X, it's Y" construction

Also called epanorthosis. Any sentence that cancels its premise by naming what
something is not, then pivoting to what it is. All variants are banned:

- "It was not X. It was Y."
- "Not X. Y."
- "It wasn't X, exactly. It was more like Y."
- "Not because X. Because Y."

**Why it's banned:** The construction performs nuance without achieving it. It
sounds like the narrator is refining a thought in real time, but AI and weak
writers reach for it reflexively, for anything. The elegance is addictive; the
pattern is a clear machine signature at 25-37 instances.

**V1 violations:**
- "I did not have a plan. I had a wound with an idea in it." (ch28)
- "It was not the heart that stopped me. It was the breath." (ch50)
- "It wasn't conversation. It was two men passing a stylus back and forth."

**Instead:** State the precise thing directly and support it with a specific
detail or action. Do not define it by what it isn't.

- BAD: "It was not a plan. It was a desperation."
- BETTER: "I had three days and no money and one name I hadn't tried yet."


### BANNED: Narrator stepping outside time

Explicitly banned in the original rules and violated ~8-12 times in V1. Named
here again because the violations are subtle — they don't always announce
themselves as foreshadowing.

**What this looks like:**
- "For a long time afterward I got him wrong." (ch04)
- "I have never entirely stopped." (ch07)
- Any sentence that tells the reader the future emotional state of the narrator
  as a way of assigning weight to the present moment.

**The permitted exception:** Encyclopedia/journal interludes only — passages
explicitly marked as Daniel's written texts, meant to be read after his death.
In these, the retrospective vantage is the point. Outside them, it is banned.

**The line:** The retrospective narrator can say "I know now that X." He cannot
say "I did not know then what X would mean to me." The first reports knowledge.
The second performs dramatic irony by declaration, which is a banned move.


### HARD LIMIT: Gnomic scene-closing "wisdom buttons"

Nearly every scene in V1 closes on a portable life-lesson that restates the
scene's meaning. Individually these are often well-written. The problem is the
pattern: a reader habituated to the button stops being surprised by endings,
and the endings that don't button — the cleaner ones — lose their distinction.

**Target for V2:** No more than 30% of scenes end on a gnomic note. Aim for
a ratio closer to 20%. The rest should end on image, action, cut, or silence.

**V1 examples of the button:**
- "I can't beat it." (ch23 closing — restates the chapter's theme of limits)
- "It is the only interesting part." (ch34)
- "Some things you secure. Some things you only confess you could not." (ch49)

These are not bad sentences. The problem is universal deployment. See the
scene-endings section below for the alternatives.


### HARD LIMIT: One-sentence paragraphs

V1 has 26-27% of all paragraphs as single sentences. Literary fiction runs
5-15%. At 27%, the one-liner stops signaling "this matters more" and starts
signaling "paragraph break." The machinery is exposed; the reader anticipates
the short beat before it arrives.

**Target for V2:** Maximum 15% of paragraphs as single sentences across any
given chapter. In a 3,000-word chapter of roughly 60-80 paragraphs, that's
9-12 one-sentence paragraphs. If you are writing a 13th, it had better earn
its isolation.

**The rule:** Reserve the free-standing one-liner for genuine pivots — the
irreversible decision, the revelation, the death, the silence between two
people that changes everything. If the sentence is a narrator's processing beat
or a rhythmic pause, absorb it into the adjacent paragraph.


### HARD LIMIT: "the way" / "X the way Y does"

677 instances in V1 (~13 per chapter). The book's defining verbal tic. The
construction is genuinely useful — the narrator is an engineer who explains by
analogy. The problem is that it never rests. At 13 per chapter, the reader
stops feeling the force of individual comparisons.

**Target for V2:** Maximum 3 uses per chapter. Use only when the specific
comparison is doing genuine work that a direct statement cannot do.

- EARNED: "Tyche's thumb is moving on the back of my hand the way you smooth a
  leaf flat before you write on it." (This earns its simile; the leaf-writing
  image does something direct language cannot.)
- UNEARNED: "He handed it back the way you return something you never wanted."
  (Direct statement — "He handed it back without looking at it" — is sharper.)


### TIC-PHRASES: frequency targets

These phrases are fine in small doses. At V1 densities they become wallpaper.

| Phrase | V1 count | V2 limit |
|--------|----------|---------|
| "the way" (all uses) | 677 | 3 per chapter |
| "looked at me" | 91 | 2 per chapter |
| "the shape of" | 59 | 1 per 5 chapters |
| "the whole of" / "that was the whole" | 80 combined | 1 per 5 chapters |
| "which is to say" | 21 | 1 per 10 chapters |
| "a man who..." gnomic form | 67 combined | 1 per 3 chapters |
| Macer's ring-turning gesture | ~11 | 3 total in book |
| "Cheap. Mine. Undeniable." refrain | multiple | Retire after Part III |
| Forearm burn-scar explanation | re-explained ~every early chapter | Explain ONCE (ch01-02), then reference only |

## Scene endings: a taxonomy

This is the book's most important structural craft question. V1 defaulted to
the "wisdom button" — a portable aphorism that restates theme — for roughly
55-60% of scene closings. V2 should distribute endings across four types, with
the button used sparingly.

### TYPE 1: The image cut (preferred default)
End on a concrete sensory image. Let the reader sit with the physical thing
rather than the meaning of the thing. The image should be specific enough that
it is doing interpretive work without announcing it.

**Model:** Ch11 ends with Daniel holding the burned rope line — "for a long
time." He doesn't say what it means. The reader already knows. The act of
holding is the meaning.

**Structure:** [scene reaches its turn or disaster] → [one concrete image,
action, or object] → [end]. No interpretation. The last sentence should be
physical.

**Example shapes:**
- "The coals settled and dimmed. I did not put more wood on."
- "She set the tablet face-down on the bench. Outside, the yard was already
  loud."
- "The door was still open. I left it that way."


### TYPE 2: The dialogue cut
End on a line of dialogue — ideally one that says less than it means, or more
than the speaker intends, or whose weight the reader feels before Daniel does.
No interpretive tag after the line.

**What to avoid:** Adding a sentence after the dialogue that explains what the
line meant. If you need to explain it, the line isn't working.

**Example shapes:**
- End a scene: "'Good night,' he said, in the easy way of a young man to an
  old one."
- End a tense scene: "She said, 'Go,' and I went."
- End an ambiguous scene by letting the ambiguity stand in the last word.


### TYPE 3: The action cut (enter late, leave early)
End at the moment the scene's next action begins, but do not follow it. The
reader is left mid-movement — enough to know where it goes, not enough to watch
it arrive. This creates forward motion and withholds the landing.

**Example shapes:**
- "I folded the letter and put it in my belt and went to find him."
- "I went to keep my appointment with a man who was not my friend."
- "I started walking because standing was worse." (Daniel heading away from a
  scene; reader knows where, doesn't need to follow.)


### TYPE 4: The silence cut (model: ch42)
End in a gap — a silence, an absence, a small action that is notable for not
being the larger action the scene was building toward. The model is ch42 (Heras's
death), where grief is rendered through action and absence rather than named.
Grief does not get a sentence. What happens after grief — the small physical
fact — is the ending.

**Example shapes:**
- "[Character has just died or left or said the unsayable.] I put out the lamp."
- "[After a difficult conversation.] Macer turned the ring on his thumb. I
  counted the stones in the wall."
- "[After a failure.] I went back to the bench."

**What distinguishes this from the button:** The silence cut ends on a physical
fact. The button ends on a generalization. "I put out the lamp" is a silence
cut. "Some things you cannot say in the light" is a button.


### TYPE 5: The wisdom button (use sparingly)
A gnomic, aphoristic close that restates or crystallizes the scene's meaning
in portable form. This is not banned — it is Daniel's natural register — but
its power depends on restraint.

**Rule:** One per chapter maximum. Aim for one per three chapters. When you use
it, it should feel chosen, not habitual.

**What makes a button earn its place:** It must say something the scene didn't
already say explicitly. If the scene showed a lesson, the button restates it
and loses power. If the scene dramatized something and the button names the
unnamed principle behind it, it can land.

## What the narration must NOT do — complete list (V2 additions in bold)

- Must not explain Roman history as a tour guide.
- Must not lecture on the morality of slavery.
- Must not step outside time (see full prohibition above).
- Must not announce emotional weight ("The party moved on. He didn't." — this
  requires the reader to already carry the character's feeling; skipping the
  earning and delivering the device is empty).
- Must not restate scene takeaways. End on image, action, or silence.
- **Must not use the correctio / "not X, it's Y" construction. Zero instances.**
- **Must not deploy more than 15% one-sentence paragraphs in any chapter.**
- **Must not use "the way" more than 3 times per chapter.**
- **Must not re-explain the forearm burn scar after ch02.**
- **Must not use Macer's ring-turning gesture more than 3 times total.**
- **Must not close consecutive chapters on the same image or gesture.**
- Must not use "unexpected," "surprising," or "no one anticipated" — earn the
  surprise structurally.
- Must not state dramatic irony. Build the architecture so the gap exists.
- Must not expose subtext. Show the behavior of someone hiding a thing; do not
  name the hiding.
- Must not use dialogue to deliver information the listener already knows.
- Must not stack metaphors from different conceptual domains.

## What V1 got right — preserve these in V2

Do not over-correct these. V1 was told to avoid beat-tag clichés (nodding,
sighing, chuckling) and eliminated them entirely — zero instances across 197k
words. That over-correction creates its own uncanny valley. Characters who
never nod or sigh feel subtly inhuman. V2 should allow these **naturally and
sparingly** — 1-2 per chapter when the moment genuinely calls for a physical
beat. The goal is natural variation, not zero occurrence.

**Genuine V1 strengths to preserve:**

- **Sensory grounding**: smell, sound, temperature, materials. This is the
  book's strongest consistent quality. Keep it.
- **Oblique dialogue**: In most scenes, characters don't say what they mean
  directly. This is right. Preserve it.
- **Dramatic irony through structure**: especially ch51/53 where Roman documents
  "find" Daniel's legacy. This is the correct technique. Preserve and expand it.
- **Daniel's dry, self-deprecating narrator voice**: Never let the prose become
  earnest or solemn. The comedy of his situation is always available.
- **Deliberate motifs**: the phone (~22x), "the new figures" (~39x), "the little
  ones" (~17x) — these are intentional and well-controlled. Do not thin them
  while fixing the accidental tics.
- **Ch42 and ch50** as models for grief rendered through action and absence,
  not through statement. These are the technical high points of V1.
- **Conjunction-led sentences**: "So," "And," "But," at the start of a sentence
  is natural and human. Keep it.
- **Varied hedging**: "perhaps," "seemed" are used very sparingly (5 and 2
  instances respectively) — this is correct. Keep this restraint.
- **The retrospective frame**: The narrator is old, writing this. That frame
  licenses philosophical interludes and measured reflection. Use it, but don't
  use it to deliver analysis the scene has already completed.
