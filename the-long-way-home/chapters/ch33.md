# Chapter 33: The Terror

I felt the city stop talking.

It came on over days, a slow change in the air before you could name what changed. The wharf was loud as it always was, carters cursing and gulls fighting over guts, and then the quality of the loudness shifted - men talking close and short, without laughing at the ends of things, because that laughter is how a man on the street signals to a stranger he means him no harm. That season nobody was signaling anything. The bread-woman who would not gossip in the winter now would not take the coin from your hand without looking at who stood behind you in the line. A grain-factor I had dealt with for years, a fat easy man who told the same three jokes, came to settle an account, said nothing else, and left a gift sitting in his own basket, having decided on the walk over that it was a bad year to be seen giving the Thulean anything at all. I had the weather of it before I had the names - the whole city had taken a step back from every doorway at once, and I stood in the middle of it not knowing which way the wind was blowing, looking for Marcia.

She had the names before I did. She had them as she had everything, off the rolls and off the men who kept the rolls, the factors and the bank-table men who lend at the bridge and hear a thing the morning it is true.

"Four," she said. We were in the tally-room with the door shut, which we did not do, the lying clock dripping into its bowl. "Four of the consulars. Killed."

I did not understand the word at first in the sense she meant it. I knew consulars. Men who had held the consulship, the top of the heap, the names you put on a year. I had stood in rooms with them and not been spoken to.

"Tried?" I said. It was a fool's question. I asked it because I wanted the answer to be yes.

"No." She wrote nothing. That was how I knew. Marcia writes when a problem can be worked. She set her two hands flat on the table and looked at the wall over my head. "Taken and killed. On the prefect's word. Attianus. While the boy is still on the road." Then she dropped her eyes and set the names down one at a time, flat, to be sure of the count. Palma. Celsus. The Moor, the one the army out east had loved, Quietus. A fourth she gave and I lost - a long name, Nigrinus, that I could not have picked out of a crowd if you had paid me, and that was the one that frightened me most, because I understood I could die for being on the wrong side of a man whose name I could not hold in my head.

"For what," I said.

"A plot." She let the word sit. "There is always a plot. Four of the best men of the dead emperor's making, and a plot was found, and they were dealt with before the new one is even in the city to be asked whether he wanted it done. This is the house being cleaned before the master gets home. By the steward, so the master's hands are clean of it. Whether the boy ordered it or will arrive and grieve over it and lay it at Attianus's door does not matter to the men in the ground."

I had built a life on guessing what would please a man with power. "They were senators," I said. "Consulars. I'm a freedman who flew a bag."

"You're a freedman who flew a bag with the dead emperor's own name. Whose work is dedicated into a god's keeping with a college that can ask a question any day it likes. Who is, on three rolls I could fetch you before the lamp burns down, written as the man of a senator of the conquest party." She picked the stylus up and put it down without writing, which I had never once seen her do. "You are not a fish that size. But the net does not care what it catches on the way to the fish it wants. And there is a man in this city who has wanted you in a net for fifteen years and now has a season where nobody is asking too closely why a man ends up there."

She meant Crispus. She did not need to name him. We had both lived under that name long enough that it had stopped needing saying, the way you stop saying the name of the river when everyone in the room is wet.

She slid the tally across to me - the quarter's press income, the numbers in the new figures going on for two columns, a total that if I'd tried to write it in Roman figures would have run off the edge of the tablet and down the wall. More than most senators saw in a year. Whatever that sum was worth this spring, we could not spend it safely. The press could not print our way through what was coming. The money existed; it had no door.

Crispus moved ten days after the consulars died, which was careful timing. Marcia told me. She had it from a grain-factor's wife whose husband had been at the reading: Crispus had stood in front of a gathered house and read aloud a paper, his own, old argument dressed in new cloth, pinning the impiety charge back to the specific thing he had said at the reckoning on the Caelian six years prior. The gods of Rome, he said, did not sanction a foreign ladder used to climb past them. The dedication to Minerva had been a purchase, not a conversion. The press printed what a foreigner told it to print, under the goddess's name, as cover for whatever a foreigner thought was true. He was careful not to name me directly. He named the thing.

Marcia had a secondary report: the paper had not landed the way Crispus intended. The reading was polite. The room did not rise. A senator's freedman, in the back, had asked whether the report of the ship's return was correct - whether the Thulean's map had in fact been confirmed past the western ocean - and Crispus had said the reports were premature and not credible, and the senator's freedman had nodded once and the room had moved on to other business.

I watched Marcia's face while she told me. Her face said the same thing mine did: not yet. He was not finished. But his footing had shifted. The ship's return had circulated in enough circles that his specific argument - that I made claim to knowledge Rome did not have - had grown a crack. He had simply lost the room. I went to Scaeva the next morning.

Scaeva had leaned in on the Caelian and tipped a room and made me his. I had hated the public collar of it, *Scaeva's man*, but a collar that a strong hand holds the other end of is a comfort when the dogs are out. He held my sulfur secret, and we held debts to each other, and the arithmetic of all that was tangled but it came to something I could lean on. Or I had thought it did, for five years.

I went at the dawn hour, with the clients, in a clean toga, as a client goes. His house on the Quirinal stood open with a great man's morning openness, to show the world how many men want a word. I was not let in.

It was done so that no one could ever point at the doing of it. The doorman knew me. He had taken my name into that house a dozen times. He registered something in the region of my face, then a freedman came out - a sleek young one I half knew from the dinners, face arranged into regret - and he did not take my hand.

"The senator is not receiving this morning," he said. I said I would wait - there were thirty men waiting; waiting is what the dawn is for - and the freedman spoke again, and there was a small weight on the second saying that I understood in my body before my head caught it up: "for some while. He has a great deal of business this season. He has asked that those who can manage their own affairs manage them, and not trouble the house until the city is quieter." He glanced, very briefly, at the freedman's clothes under my citizen's toga. The regret on his face did not change at all. "I'll tell him you came. He'll be glad you're well."

I sent a tablet, two days on. A short one, nothing in it a clerk could read sideways, only that I would value a quarter of an hour on a matter of the house's business, the river-gate business, said in the words we had used for years. It came back with the seal unbroken, in the hand of a slave I did not know, with a word from the freedman that the senator's affairs at the gate were settled and would need no further attention from me, and that he advised me, as a friend advises a friend, to attend to my own and trouble no one with what could be carried alone.

The river-gate business was the sulfur. *Settled and would need no further attention from me.* He was telling me, in the clean language of a man who does not put a knife in writing, that the favor was withdrawn, the gate no longer smoothed. And under it, the warning a man hears with the back of his neck: *trouble no one*. You are not my man. You were never my man. If a clerk drawing a line through the rolls comes to this door and asks was the Thulean yours, this door will be surprised to hear the name.

He had not denounced me. A denunciation that season was a coin you could spend on your own safety, and he did not spend it. He let go of the rope and stepped back and dusted his hands where men could see him do it, so that anyone watching Scaeva that spring would see a man with no barbarian on his leash. He was a conquest-party man with a dead emperor's wars all over his career, exactly the kind the net was out for, and he had seen a smell on his own clothes and taken off the coat. I had spent two months drawing up those same sums about myself in Marcia's tally-room. The difference was that I had been the smell.

I walked home in my own toga feeling like a costume a child had put on, kept to the back ways, and found myself reading the faces of men coming the other direction to see whether any of them were reading mine.

Macer I went to last.

Twenty years I had gone to that door - the door of the man who owned me, then of my patron - and somewhere in there it had become a door I went to the way you go to a thing that will be where you left it. He was past seventy now and built like a beam that has carried a roof so long the roof would fall before the beam did. He kept me standing in the atrium, which he had never done, and came out himself rather than sending the steward, and the two wrong things together told me before he opened his mouth.

"You shouldn't have come up the hill," he said. "I needed to talk to you," I said. He said, "I know what you need," and turned his ring, the heavy gold one, his thumb working it round and round. I thought of Lucanus on the press-room floor turning the dead man's buckle the same way, learning a frightened man's trick off the men around him. "You need an old man with frontier names in his account-books to stand in a door this spring and say the Thulean is a good fellow, leave him be. That's what you need." He stopped turning the ring. "I'm not going to do it."

"Yeah. I know." I was not going to argue with him. "That's not - that's not why I came up."

"You came up the hill. That's the asking." He came a step nearer and dropped his voice - and that was the worst of it, because Macer does not drop his voice, Macer says the crude thing at the volume of a man selling timber, and a Macer who has learned to be quiet is a Macer who has been made afraid. I had only seen the edge of that in the winter and now I was seeing all of it. "Listen to me, because I'm going to say it once and then you're going to go home the back way."

He stopped. His thumb moved on the gold once, without turning it.

"There are four of the best men in this city in the river or under quicklime this month, not one of them tried, and I am a leather-contractor whose name is on the legion rolls for thirty years of feeding the dead man's army. I am exactly the size of fish a hungry steward keeps for the new master. I sold you to Caesar fair and I was glad, you know I was glad." He stopped, working something over behind his eyes. "I'm not your enemy. If a man comes to my door and asks was the Thulean ever mine, I'll tell him the truth: that the emperor bought you off me a long time ago and I've had nothing to do with your sky-bags and your burning tubes since, and that's no lie, and it'll cost you nothing and me nothing." He stopped again and something crossed behind his eyes that was not quite fear and not quite the old calculation, and settled somewhere between them. "But I will not burn for you. I've got a household and grandsons and a name to leave them and I am not setting a torch to all of it to keep one clever foreigner out of a net he flew himself into the middle of. So stop coming to my door. Make yourself small. Make yourself dull. Make yourself the kind of nothing nobody bothers to denounce, because that, boy," and the *boy* came out then, the only one, bare and quick, "is the one thing that's going to save you."

He had the steward show me out by the back, through the yard where the hides were stacked, into a lane that smelled of tanning, and the door shut behind me with the ordinary sound a door makes. In the lane, the hides were stacked. The door did not open again.

The scare came at the yard, eleven days later, and it came in through the front, smiling, which is how it comes.

He was a man of about forty, compact and soft-footed, moving through a room without appearing to decide to move - learned stillness, the kind that comes from years of watching without being watched. His tunic was good cloth but chosen plain, nothing that would catch an eye at either end of the street. He had a deep fold between his brows that he would have had since his thirties, and eyes that went somewhere slightly past your face when he spoke to you, as though reading a point on the wall behind your ear. He had a tablet under one arm and a slave behind him of the useful, forgettable sort, and a manner that was a hair too easy, the ease of a man who has practiced it until he cannot put it down. He said he wanted his account looked at, got up as a small importer, oils and the like.

Marcia took him, because Marcia takes the front, and I was at the back of the press room and saw him through the door and felt my skin go before I knew why. He was not buying oil. His questions wandered. He admired the press, asked could he see it work, asked what we printed, asked whether it was true the Thulean's marks were said before a god each spring on the Aventine, asked it all with the flat patience of a man who already knows the answers and is watching your face while you give them. He let drop that a friend of his in the colleges had said an interesting thing about the dedication, about whether everything off the Thulean's press fell rightly under the goddess's keeping or whether some of it went somewhere the rite did not cover. He used the word *impiety* once, lightly, setting it on the table to see if anyone's eyes went to it.

I did not let my eyes go to it. I do not know how. I think I was too frightened to do anything but the thing I had done my whole life, which is to put a pleasant Thulean over my face and stand behind it.

Marcia was faster than I was, and colder, and magnificent in a fashion that does not look like anything. She did not throw him out. She bored him. She answered everything long and dull, the prices of cloth, the cost of pearwood, the schoolmasters who bought the times-tables, the exact denarius the heifer had cost on the Aventine and who the priest was and how the rite went and how proud the house was to have its little marks under the goddess's eye, and would the gentleman like a sheet, here, a gift, a true account of wheat-weights any honest grain-man could use. She buried him in the harmless until the harm could not breathe in it. The press she walked him to was the school press; the man who ran the other one, the one Eros kept the cut blocks for, had been sent on an errand the moment Marcia heard the front-door manner; and the room where I kept the work that was mine, the box under the floor, the leaves in the homeland script with the ratios in them, was shut, with a woman sitting against the door of it mending a basket as if she had sat there all her life.

That was Tyche. She had moved the box the night the city went quiet, without being told. She sat against the shut door with her short-cut hair and her face that gives away exactly nothing, a freedwoman of no account at all, and she let the oil-man's slave glance over her head into the room and see a woman and a basket and shelves of ordinary scrolls, and she did not look up.

The man left. He took the gift sheet. He said he would think about the oil. We never saw him again, and I never learned whose he was - the colleges' or the prefect's or Crispus's or some independent delator working on his own account, hoping to sell what he found to whoever would buy that season. That last possibility was the worst of it, because a hungry man with no master cannot be reasoned with.

Marcia bored him and Tyche hid the dangerous half of my whole life behind a basket and a blank face, and the rest was dumb luck. He found, on the day he came, a dull cloth-house with a school-press and a proud little rite and no purchase for a man's hand to close on. If he had come the week before, with the second press running and the wrong leaves out, I would have had no answer for that. I sat on the floor of the press room after, my heart going like a fist on a door.

Bituitus's second ship had come back before the terror broke. Among the trade goods and the specimens I had spent three weeks cataloguing, it had also brought a person. A man, past thirty by the look of him, with a name in his own tongue that the captain had compressed into a sound, *Aco*, because it was the closest the captain could get. He sat in the back room of the wharf yard with a cup of posca he had accepted and set on the floor, and the set of his shoulders said he had been taking the measure of a room full of people he did not know for weeks.

I had thought, for years, about what I would say if this day came. Every draft of the thought had ended in the same place, which was that I was the one who had sent the ships, and he was the one sitting in a back room of my warehouse with no way home, and there was nothing in my Latin or his tongue that covered the distance between those two facts.

Tyche was his primary contact. She had learned seven words of his language in three weeks, more than anyone else in the compound had managed, and she used them slowly and kept her hands still when she used them. He watched her hands when she spoke. He ate what she put in front of him. When I came in he held very still and the calculation behind his eyes was one I could not read, because I did not know what variables he was working from, what I looked like from where he stood in the world he had come from. I did not know anything he knew. That was the part I had not thought through: that when he got here, he would know things I did not, had always known them, and I would be the ignorant one in the room.

I said his name, the sound the captain had given it. He did not correct me. I do not know if that meant I had it right.

We sat for a while. Tyche said one of her seven words. He answered in something longer and she worked it over and gave me three words of it, slowly, uncertain. I wrote in English afterward. The page went into the box. I wrote: *I arranged this. He did not choose to come here. I do not know yet what that means.* Then I shut the box.

For a month after the scare, I made myself small as Macer had told me to. I sent no tablets to senators. I let the contest rite be prepared exactly to the colleges' liking and stood at the back of my own enterprise and thanked the goddess in the words a priest gave me, bowing lower than I had ever bowed. I took my name off things. Marcia put the new contracts under hers, *Marcia, liberta*, the widow of the merchant Caepio, a respectable nobody of a name, and where mine had been the security on an agreement she found another name to be it. I went down to the wharf in a plain tunic and let men forget they had been afraid to be seen near me, by giving them less of me to be seen near.

I had caught myself reaching for Heras out of habit and stopped. A man who loved me was something I could no longer afford to be seen to have. Celer was three years in the eastern ground for a province struck off a map. Scaeva had taken off the coat. Macer had warned me through a shut door, which was the most any friend had given me in a month. No emperor knew my name except as a line a tired clerk wanted to draw through. The life I had spent twenty years building - the un-ignorable Thulean who flew and counted and burst tubes for great men - was the very asset that could get me killed. I wanted to be forgotten with my whole body, the way I had once wanted to be seen.

The press was running when I came up from the wharf in my plain tunic at the end of it - the school press, the only one I dared run that spring, putting wheat-weights and times-tables onto cheap gray sheets for an as apiece, the smallest and safest work the house made. Lucanus came and leaned on me while I checked a sheet. He had the buckle. He always had the buckle now. He was sounding out the figures under his finger, slow, getting the seven and the one the wrong way round as they all do at first.

"That's a seven," I said, and turned his finger to it.

He fixed it and went on counting, asking nothing, because he had not heard a frightened thing said over his head that week. We had been careful. He counted the sheet to the bottom and got it right and looked up, and I told him so, and he leaned his whole small weight against my arm and went back to the top to do it again.

I let him count. The press came down, and came down, putting the safe true small thing onto the cheap gray page. The room with the one press running was about the size of a cell. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog barked twice and went quiet.
