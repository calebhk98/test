# Chapter 40: The Books

The first page took me eleven days, and the first page was the easy one, because the first page was counting.

I had thought it would be the top that gave me trouble, the high rungs, the things I half-knew dressed up as things I knew. The bottom was where I had to be slowest. A man writing the top of a ladder can lean on the man who will read it being already most of the way up. The bottom assumes nothing. I sat at the cutting-bench Eros let me use in the slack hour after the lamp-trim, with a stack of cheap palimpsest I scraped myself because I would not waste a clerk on this, and I wrote for a reader who could not yet count past his fingers, in a language no one within a thousand years would be able to sound out, and I found I did not know how to begin something that began with nothing.

Tyche read the eleven days' work in an evening. She read it finger under the line, lips not moving, the same as she read a tally she suspected. She had been reading my homeland script for twenty years and could write it faster than I could now, my hand having gone to the same place my eyes had gone, the letters wanting to be larger every season. When she finished she set it down and said it was good, and then said the seven was wrong on the third page, I had drawn the curve of it backward, and that a child copying it would learn a backward seven and teach it to his children, and did I want a thousand-year line of people writing the seven the wrong way because the Thulean's hand shook on a winter night.

I fixed the seven.

The order was the whole of it, and everything else was a consequence. You climb a ladder by the bottom rung. There is no other place to put your foot. I had learned that over thirty years, watching numerals crawl into Rome at the speed of a fish-seller's slate while the men who could have used them best, the senators with their abacus-slaves, kept the old marks for prestige. You cannot hand a man the top of the ladder. He has nowhere to stand. So the books began where a person begins, and went up only as fast as a foot can follow a foot.

First the numbers, and the great trick of writing them so their place tells their size. Then the four workings on them, and I wrote out the long division in full, every step, because I had once flubbed it under a Greek physician's eye and ground it back to the truth by hand, and the grinding-back is the lesson. Then the unknown thing, the letter that stands for a number you do not yet have, which Tyche took to in an afternoon, and which I knew would stop most readers cold, so I built ten rungs where one would do. Then shapes and the measuring of them, the right triangle and its squared sides, the circle and the number that will not come out clean. Then the idea I never had the rigor of and could only draw the outline of, the thing about the line that touches a curve at a point, and the area trapped under a curve that you find by chopping it into pieces too thin to count, and I wrote at the head of that page, in plain words, that I did not have the proof of this and never had, that I was handing down the structure of a tool and not the making of it, and that a man who built on it without finding the proof himself was building on my word, which was the word of a man who had it secondhand from teachers he half-remembered.

Then the three laws of how things move, which I had carried since I was a boy and which I wrote down one rainy afternoon with great confidence and read back the next morning with none.

Here is the excerpt as it stood when I left it, the entry on motion, in the homeland script, set down plain for a reader I will never meet. I render it here in the homeland tongue. It is flatter than the rest of this account, because it was meant to teach and not to remember.

> *If you have read the pages before this one, you know how to weigh a thing and how to measure how far it goes and how long it takes. Now you will learn how things move, which is three rules, and a man named Newton found them, who lived in my country long after my time and long before yours, and whom I never met and have read about only a little.*
>
> *The first rule. A thing that is still stays still. A thing that moves keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed, forever, unless something pushes or pulls it. You will say this is false, because a rolling ball stops. The ball stops because the ground drags on it and the air pushes back. Take those away and it never stops. There is no such place on the earth, so you must imagine it. The imagining is the lesson.*
>
> *The second rule. To change how a thing moves, you push it. A bigger push changes it more. A heavier thing changes less for the same push. I believe these three quantities, the push, the change, and the weight, can be set in one true line of figures, and that the push equals the weight times how fast the moving changes, but I am not certain I have the form of it right, and I never could derive it, only use it. Find the true form before you trust it. Do not trust it because I wrote it.*
>
> *The third rule. When you push on a thing, it pushes back on you, the same, the other way. Stand on a boat and shove the dock and the boat goes out from under you. This one I am sure of.*

I wrote *I am not certain* into the second law and left it there. I could have smoothed it. A man writing for the unborn wants to sound like a god, and the temptation to write the second law clean and sure, to let the reader believe the Thulean had it whole, was the strongest pull I felt in the whole long labor. But a clean false rung is the worst thing a ladder can have. A missing rung a climber sees and reaches past. A false one holds his weight until it doesn't, and by then he is high up. I left the doubt where it was.

The chemistry was where the floor went out from under me.

I had meant to write how the steam engine worked. It was the keystone of the whole upper structure, the prize every contest-tale dreamed of, the horse that ate wood and never tired, the metal that did not yet exist. Felix was failing at it on my coin a quarter-mile off and I owed his grandchildren a true account of what the pot was supposed to do. I knew the structure of it cold. Fire heats water, water becomes steam, steam wants more room than water, that wanting is a push, you catch the push and make it shove a thing back and forth, and the back and forth turns a wheel. I had said this aloud a hundred times. I sat down to write the middle of it, the part where the wanting becomes the shoving, why the steam pushes and keeps pushing instead of simply lifting a lid once and going slack, what it is in the heat that becomes the motion, and I came to the middle of the explanation and there was nothing there.

I knew that heat was the little ones moving, the smallest pieces of a thing, jostling faster when hot. I had written that on an earlier page and believed it. I knew steam pushed. I could not put the one to the other. The bridge between the jostling and the push, the argument my teachers must have drawn on a board for a bored boy on a spring afternoon while he watched the clock, was simply gone, scraped clean as the palimpsest, and I sat there at forty-eight years old with a stick of charcoal in my hand and reached for it the way your tongue reaches for a name you have known your whole life and find you cannot say, and it would not come, and it has not come since.

I wrote what I had. I wrote that fire makes the little ones move faster, that the faster moving is what we feel as heat, that steam pushes hard and an engine catches the push, and that *I do not know what joins the moving to the pushing. There is knowledge here I had once and have lost. A man who finds it has found the heart of the engine. I have only the two ends of the rope and not the middle.* And I sat with that a while, because it was one thing to know the books would have holes and another to watch a hole open under my own hand in the rung I had thought the soundest.

It got worse before I made my peace with it, and I did not entirely make my peace with it. The quadratic first. I had carried the quadratic from the cell, from the second night in this world, scratched into my first panicked notes for whoever found my body, and even then I had written beside it that I was not sure I had the sign right. Thirty-one years. I sat down to set it on the page clean, the formula for the unknown that solves the squared thing, a tool I could once have written in my sleep at seventeen, and I wrote it, and the minus before the first part looked wrong, and I changed it to a plus, and the plus looked wrong, and I worked a problem I knew the answer to forward to check it and got the answer, then worked it with the sign flipped and got the answer too, because I had chosen a problem where it came out the same either way, like a fool, and I had to build three more problems by hand to trap the sign. I got it right in the end. I needed the morning for it.

The planets broke the same rule and I could not fix it by working. I had drawn the sun in the middle, which I was sure of, and the earth going round it, which had cost men their lives to say and which I gave away on a page for a child. I set the others in their order out from the sun and got to the count of them and stopped. There was an inner pair, hot and quick, then us, then the red one, then the giant, then the ringed one, and out past those a cold pair I could no longer keep straight, their order and even how many, two or three, and the names I had for them were homeland names that meant nothing and that I could not be sure I had hung on the right worlds. I tried to walk it out from the sun in my mind, stepping from one to the next in order, and found the sequence went strange past the ringed one, the rooms out of order, a turning I could not place.

So I wrote the ones I was sure of in their order and drew them to no scale, with the gaps marked as gaps, and at the cold far end I wrote *out here are more, two or three, I am no longer certain of their number or their order, and the names I knew are no use to you. I have set down only what I can swear to. Do not fill the rest from my drawing. Go and look.* A poor record, an account of the heavens with the far rooms left dark. But a man who drew the far worlds wrong would send some later astronomer hunting a planet that was not where the Thulean swore it would be, and call the Thulean a liar, and rightly, and throw out the sun in the middle along with the lie.

One evening that winter, Ulpia came to the bench with a question that had nothing to do with the tally work. She was eleven, going on twelve, and she had been watching me at the star-pages for three nights running, saying nothing. Tyche had sent her with a lamp. When she set it down she asked whether the sun was truly in the middle, or whether I had drawn it that way to make the picture work.

I told her it was truly in the middle, and that the picture was wrong about something else, but not that. I showed her the two inner ones: a hot fast pair, the one nearest the sun probably too hot even for iron, the second one close enough that on some maps the old Greeks had called it the morning star and the evening star without knowing it was the same world going around. She listened with both hands flat on the bench, frowning at the page. Then she asked how anyone had found this out, given that the sun rose and set every day like a plain fact.

That took the better part of the evening. I showed her the trick of watching where a planet rises and noting how far it drifts against the fixed stars over a season, and how the two inner ones never get far from the sun at all, always pulling back, always appearing before dawn or after dusk and nowhere else, and how that tells you something about where they are. I drew the orbits in charcoal and she corrected my circles for being lopsided, which was fair. We got to the ringed one and I told her it had a ring, which was the most I could say about it, and she said *what kind of ring* in the tone Marcia used when an invoice was incomplete, and I told her I thought it was ice or rock or both and that I was not certain and that nobody in this city was going to be certain for a very long time, and she looked at the charcoal ring I had drawn around the circle and said nothing further, which I knew meant she was deciding whether to believe me.

She went to bed without saying whether she had decided. I scraped the orbits off the palimpsest and went back to the astronomy page, and on the entry for the ringed one I made the small hooked mark before the description of the ring.

That was the governing principle, and it cost on every page. The books would say what I knew, and mark plainly where I did not, and leave the dark rooms dark rather than furnish them with guesses dressed as facts. Tyche enforced this harder than I did. She had a small flat way of reading a too-confident sentence back to me and asking, in the tone she used on a clerk's rounded-up figure, whether I knew that or only felt it, and the number of times the honest answer was *felt it* shamed me. Where I felt it, we marked it felt. We had a small mark for this in the script, a hooked stroke before a sentence, so that a reader four centuries on would know which of his rungs were oak and which were the outline of oak drawn in the air.

The biology I could barely begin. I knew bodies were built of small rooms too small to see, and that the same little ones that climbed into a wound to fester were everywhere, in water, in breath, and that creatures over long ages changed into other creatures by the slow weeding of which ones lived to breed, and that this last was a thing men had killed each other over in my country and that I believed it and could not prove a word of it. I wrote it as a story and called it a story. I wrote that I could not show him the little rooms because the glass to see them did not yet exist, that I had once claimed a glass that could and that the claim had been a lie I told to live, and that he should not believe the rooms are there because the Thulean says so, but should build the glass and look, and that the day he looks will be a greater day than any I have had.

I put in a page on the fevers that spread man to man through the air and through water, and the quarantine rules I had learned from the flux, not because I knew all of it but because I knew enough that a man reading it in a hundred years might save a city of a hundred thousand from a fever that would otherwise burn through it in a season. I had seen what the flux did to an army. I wrote what I had, with the hooked marks over everything I was guessing at.

The pages on how peoples govern themselves and how they trade I set down last, and trusted them least. I had been wrong about those things more than anything. The words I had for them were mostly homeland words that did not land right in any tongue I knew, and what I wrote was closer to a record of my own errors than a true account of anything. I wrote a page on how a republic runs and another on the distances between rich men and poor men that I had watched widen all my years in Rome, and at the head of both pages I put the hooked guessing-stroke, because I was uncertain of what it meant, and a man who mistakes his own reading of a thing for the thing itself is building on sand all the way up. I added a page on how the new figures changed markets, not from principle but from watching: what happened to prices when a factor could not hide his tally, what happened to credit when a ledger could be audited in an afternoon. These pages I trusted better than the republic page. I had seen them. I put a smaller hooked stroke on the price observations and no stroke at all on the fraud.

I wrote the sea-clock problem at the end of the navigation section, in a paragraph by itself: *I have not solved this. To know how far west you have sailed you need a clock that keeps true time at sea. The clock I built does not. The prize for this stands unclaimed and will stand after me. Whoever builds it will complete the map. I leave this rung empty.* I drew the same small hooked mark before it, though it was a fact and not a guess. The mark seemed right.

It had to live past me, and one copy in one hand is a fire away from never having been. So the plan, which was Marcia's and Tyche's far more than mine.

There would be three copies of the whole, in the homeland script, and a fourth begun in Latin only as far up as the shapes, because past the shapes the Latin failed me and the cipher was the safer keeping anyway. The three would not sit in one room. One Tyche kept, moved when the wind changed, its place hers alone since the bad year when the informer came smelling at the wharf. One went into the keeping of the printers' society, sealed, listed in their charter among the founder's papers as a thing not to be opened or sold, a brick in a wall built to stand a hundred years burying men. One we meant to put where no living interest touched it, sealed in lead and pitch and laid up in a dry place, a wall, a tomb, a foundation, against the day the upper rungs caught up to it and someone living could read them, and against the likelier day they did not and it waited longer.

And the script itself, which was the lock, had to be a lock that opened. One head holds it, one cart on the wharf and the books become stone marks that no one in the world can read. So the homeland script had to pass to more than one trusted hand, taught slowly, to someone young enough to carry it forty years.

Tyche was forty-four and the only living soul who read it whole. She said so to me one evening over the third copy, flatly, the way she stated a figure. One hand, she said. You have built the most patient thing in Rome and put its only key in one aging head, mine, and you are no younger, and if a cart takes me on the wharf the books are a box of marks and you are an old man who cannot see to read his own writing.

I said yeah, I know. I said I'd been thinking on it.

She did not say the name. She let her eyes go where the name was, which was the tally-bench in the corner where Ulpia stood most evenings now that Marcia let her come, eleven going on twelve, square and grave, working a column of the wharf's own figures faster than the second clerk and checking it twice because Tyche had drilled the checking into her until it was a motion her hand did without her. The child did not look up. She had the same flat refusal to be hurried that Tyche had, that Marcia had, the long-suffering frown of a small person doing a large person's work and finding the large persons careless. She wrote a seven, and it curved the right direction, because Tyche had taught her, and I watched my daughter draw it correctly, the same curve I had finally gotten right on the third page after drawing it backward, and said nothing, because Marcia had told me not to teach the girl to break herself against a door the law had bolted, and a counting-house was a bolted door, and the homeland script was a deeper room behind a door I had not yet decided to open.

Tyche had decided. I could see it in the patience she watched the girl with, the careful measuring stillness of a woman who has weighed something and found it sufficient. She did not say it and I did not ask it and we left it where it lay.

Ulpia wrote another column, checked it, moved the lamp closer without looking up.

I went on writing through that winter and into the spring, slower as the light failed earlier and my arm grew tired of holding the work out where my eyes could find it. Some nights I wrote a single sound rung and felt I had done a day's good work. Some nights I scraped more than I kept. The upper pages, the engine and the lights in the sky and the little rooms in the blood, filled with the hooked marks until they looked, in the lamplight, more chewed than written. The lower pages, the counting and the shapes, stood clean, oak all the way up.

Near the end of the writing season I came to a page I had left blank on purpose, the page where the chemistry should have given the names of the things the world is built from, the small unsplittable pieces and how they joined, and I had nothing for it but a handful of words and the image of a picture, little balls hooking onto little balls, that I was three-quarters sure was wrong in some way I could no longer locate. I sat with the blank page a long time. Then I wrote across the top of it, in the largest letters my hand had come to, so a reader could not miss them and call them an oversight, the one true thing I had to put there: *Here is a great room I cannot light for you. I knew there was knowledge here and I have lost it past saving. Do not take my silence for there being nothing. Take it that the Thulean reached this rung and his hand was empty. Cut this rung yourselves. You will, and on the day you do you will have passed me, and that is the whole reason I wrote any of it down.* And I left the rest of the page bare under the words, white and waiting, and set the next page after it as if the empty one were full, and went up to the house, where Marcia had kept a lamp.
