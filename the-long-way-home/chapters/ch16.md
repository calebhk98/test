# Chapter 16: The New Figures

By that summer I had stopped being able to find the new figures.

For two years they'd lived in one place I could see, the column Tyche kept on the wax tablet in the wharf yard, and a second I could half see, the practice scratchings of Macer's younger clerks, who used them on the sly with one ear cocked for Onesimus. They'd been a secret the house kept from itself. Now they were loose in the city and I could not say how far.

I found them first on a fish-seller's slate down by the Aemilian bridge, the morning's prices chalked in the figures, clean, a little 4 and a 6 and the wide-open 0 that Romans had never had and could not stop staring at. I asked her, in my w-for-v Latin that still made people decide I was simple, where she'd learned them. From her son, she said, the way she would have answered where she'd learned to breathe. He kept the book for a grain factor across the river, and the factor had them off another factor, back up a chain nobody could see the top of, because the top of it was a barrel in a yard by the timber wharf and nobody was looking there.

It had nothing to do with anyone admiring me. Heras had it right years ago when I tried to sell the figures as a wonder and he laughed at me. Nobody adopts a wonder, he said. They adopt a thing that saves them an afternoon. The figures saved everybody an afternoon. You could add a column of them straight down, which you cannot do in Roman numerals, where adding is its own small ordeal of counters and a board and a man who's done it twenty years. More than that, the part the factors loved with something close to fear: you could not cheat them as easily. A Roman ledger in the old numerals is a forest, and a clever steward can lose a herd in it. Lay the same accounts in the figures, places lined up, the running sum at the foot, and the forest turns into a field where a man skimming his master shows up plain. The merchants who took up the figures fastest were the suspicious ones, robbed by their own bookkeepers and wanting a writing that robbed harder to do.

So it spread downhill, into the low cheap places, the slates and the day-books and the back of a tile a porter scratched a tally on. The high places stayed dry. The figures were not on a single contract that mattered, not on a tombstone, not on a milestone, not on anything a man wanted to last or look dignified. Onesimus had a phrase for them that went around the counting houses: shopkeeper's marks. He meant it to bury them and instead named them true. Rome ran on shopkeepers, and the shopkeepers were teaching their sons.

I went home that day with the fish-seller's slate still sharp in my mind, thinking about how little of me was loose in this city and how much of what was loose had cost nothing and gone everywhere, while the rest - the flying and the water and the dead men - stayed locked inside a yard.

The books were slower, and they were mine in a way the figures had stopped being.

We had blocks now. Not many. The first one, the ugly gray line of Homer I'd cut on pearwood and printed crooked, with the doubled letter and the starved right edge, still sat in the pigeonhole where I kept my failures, and I'd cut a hundred after it, each a little less crooked. A Greek-letterer worked three days in five in a back corner of the yard, a freedman named Eros with a steady hand and a deep boredom, carving my blocks for a piecework wage out of my peculium, and I borrowed a pressman off an oil house when their screw stood idle. We could do a page at a time, the same page fifty times in a morning, sixty if the ink behaved, and the ink mostly did not behave, too thin and it crept or too thick and it filled the letters to blots, and we threw away one sheet in four and called that a good day.

We had a small stock of rag paper running through it now, rough and expensive, but cheaper than papyrus on a long run. Eros hated it. The rag took the ink slower, dried uneven, and smelled of old cloth in the damp. I kept buying it.

What we made were cheap copies of books that already existed. A page of Aesop. A schoolmaster's list of maxims. A grammar of the kind a boy is beaten through. The first thing that ever sold, that a stranger put a coin down for without Macer arranging it, was a single printed sheet of the multiplication a child needs, the times-tables a Roman boy spends a year crying over, laid out in the new figures, fifty copies sold to a Subura schoolmaster for two asses each. He did not care that a machine had made it or that I had made the machine. He cared that it cost two asses.

Two asses. A scroll copied by a scribe is days of a man's life and is priced like it. Our sheet was cheap because the cutting was done once and the printing nearly free after, and that gap, between the scribe's days and the press's morning, was the only revolution I ever started that I could watch turning. A schoolmaster could own fifty sheets, lend them out, get them back grubby, and not weep over the loss. Reading had been a wall with a narrow gate and the gate was money. I had not knocked the wall down, nowhere near. I'd cut one extra notch in it, low down, where the small people could get a hand through.

Heras came to look at the press ready to be unimpressed and prepared to be honest if he failed at it. He picked up a fresh-pulled sheet of the Aesop, held it at arm's length and then close, turned it over to look at the back where the ghost of the letters pushed through, and was quiet for longer than was like him.

"It's gray," he said finally. "And the line wanders."

"It's the same gray fifty times. That's the trick. The wander I'll fix. The fifty I've already got."

He set the sheet down and wiped his fingers, although there was nothing on them. "When I was a boy in Pergamon," he said, "the great library was the wealth of the city. A king's wealth, scrolls a man could not buy in a lifetime, kept behind a door, lent to scholars by favor." He looked at the fables drying on the line, common as washing. "You're proposing to drown that. To make the king's wealth common as fish."

"Yes," I said.

"Some men will hate you for it more than for the fire in the sky," Heras said, and he was not wrong.

We talked a little while longer, and I pushed the conversation somewhere it had been sitting in the back of my mind for months. I said the name Aristarchus. Heras turned with a physician's particular wariness, the look he kept for remedies that were also poisons.

"The old Samian was right," I said. "The sun stands still. The earth goes around it."

Heras was quiet for a moment. Behind us the pressman was cleaning ink off the screw, and the yard noise came in from outside.

"You understand," Heras said carefully, "that this is also the opinion of no one in particular, held nowhere, under which no reputable man publicly shelters."

"Yeah. I - yeah, I know that."

"And that a foreigner saying it, at a moment when certain individuals are already collecting your offenses, is the sort of fuel that-" He stopped. "How long have you known this?"

"Since I was twelve, I think. Maybe younger. I don't - it's just one of those things you get told and it sticks."

Another silence. He looked at the sheet of Aesop on the line, and I could see him running some calculation I was not privy to. "I will note," he said at last, "that Aristarchus was from Samos. If someone asks, you learned it from Aristarchus, not from wherever you actually learned it." He picked up another sheet to check the margins. "Don't say it again in a room with Crispus," he said, and we left it there.

The idea came to me at the press, with my hands black, because of something Tyche said.

She was eighteen or nineteen that summer and the problem I was hiding had outgrown the child I had been hiding. She could do arithmetic faster than any man in Macer's counting house, and we both knew that if Macer heard the full size of it he'd sell her to a factor for a price that would make him grin, because a slave who can keep a great house's books is worth a small farm, and I could not stop it, being property myself. So we did the work quietly and let the yard think she was a girl who kept a cloth tally.

She was setting the figures of a child's times-table for Eros to cut, copying them out large on a board, and she looked up from the sevens and said it without looking at me, flat, the way she spoke when she'd already decided something: "Who teaches them that the figures can do more?"

I didn't follow. "The schoolmaster teaches them."

"He teaches them to carry. To multiply." She set the chalk down and turned. "He teaches them because his father did. Nobody learns to carry water and then dreams of the sea." She looked at the sheets drying on the line, fifty identical pages, fifty times the same Aesop fable, and something closed down in her face around a thought she hadn't said yet. "You're printing the water-carrying. Who prints the sea."

She picked the chalk up and went back to the sevens. I stood there with ink on my hands. Then I started to talk, too fast, my Latin tripping: a contest, maybe. A prize for a story about a machine that didn't exist yet. A fixed day, every year, money out of a strongbox. Something that made the sea reachable to anyone who could imagine it. She listened with her chin down and her eyes up, head tilted slightly, deciding.

"It needs money," she said when I ran down.

"Yes."

"You have a peculium Macer lets you touch." She said it without unkindness, which was worse than if she'd been cruel. "And it needs a man to stand up in front of the room and say, I, this man, give this prize. They won't take a prize from a slave. They won't take a fable about machines from the Thulean who burned a soldier and a washerwoman." She picked the chalk back up. "You'd need to be free and respectable and rich, and you're none of those, and you're talking about it like it's tomorrow."

"It's not tomorrow," I admitted.

"No." She bent back to her sevens. But she'd stopped, for one breath, and looked at the board without seeing it, and the idea hadn't died, it had only gone somewhere to wait, in her as much as in me.

I told Heras that evening too, walking him to the gate. He pulled his cloak up and looked at the fables drying on the line a moment longer. "You want to make men homesick for a country that doesn't exist yet," he said. "That's either the wisest thing you've said to me or the saddest, and I genuinely cannot tell which. Mind it doesn't make you new enemies. The poets," he said, with the small precise distaste of a Greek physician for the Latin literary trade, "do not like plumbers."

Something else happened that month, in the back corner of the yard where I kept the things I built because I could not help it.

A musician had played at a dinner Macer sent me to - a girl with a double-pipe, a particular run of notes that hung in the room and then was gone. No way to ask for it again. No way to keep it. By the time I got back to the yard I had the rough of an idea: a stylus dragging a groove into a wax cylinder, the groove recording the vibration of the stylus, a second stylus running back through the groove and vibrating in turn. I had a lamp and a piece of wax and a bronze pin and I sat there for an hour pressing marks into the wax with the pin dragged along a turning cylinder I made from a rolled piece of lead sheet.

It did not work. The marks were too crude, the stylus too stiff, the lead unevenly round. But the principle was there, and I had not seen it until the night I needed it.

I posted a materials prize the next week, ten denarii for a smooth, uniform, formable sheet that would hold a thin impressed line without deforming at room temperature. I told no one what it was for.

The other object in that corner was newer. A box with a pinhole in one face, a screen of oiled linen at the opposite face. I'd built it in an afternoon, out of a board and some rag. Held in a doorway, it projected the street outside onto the linen screen, inverted, moving, the cart-horses walking upside-down, the vendor's awning bright orange and wrong-way-up. Tyche came in while I was looking at it.

"What does it do?" she said.

"I'm not entirely sure yet," I said.

She looked at the cart-horses walking on their ears and went back to the tally. The box stayed in the corner.

I had also, somewhere in those weeks, begun teaching the workshop hands a game. I'd cut the pieces myself from scrap bone, filed them to rough shapes, and renamed them for Rome: general, legate, cavalry, elephant, scout. The rules took an evening to learn and most of a lifetime to do anything with. Eros learned them, then the pressman, then the oil-house man who came for his screw on alternate days and sometimes stayed to eat. Within the month the game had gotten into the tavern up the lane and Gnaeus the owner had stopped waving me off when I brought him things to try in his kitchen.

He and I had an arrangement that was not quite an arrangement. I brought him a recipe - a long-dried noodle cooked briefly in broth, a pasta the workshop had been eating for months, which some of the hands liked and some found wrong - and Gnaeus tried it on his counter and told me what sold. He did not think of it as my food by the time it was on his counter. He thought of it as food. I got a small cut on certain dishes, when I remembered to collect it, and mostly I didn't remember, and that was fine. What I got was better: a man in the neighborhood who knew what I was and was not afraid of it and sent me information in the form of what his customers asked for.

The night Macer sent me back to the salon, I went more willingly than I once would have. Two years had put Latin enough in my mouth that I no longer stood there mute, and I'd let myself imagine I might walk in and be, if not feared, at least not furniture. Macer stood at the back in his good wool with no stripe, arms folded, watching his investment, which had appreciated.

Crispus had not appreciated. He had concentrated. He was oiled and easy on his couch as before, and when Mettius made his little flourish of presenting me, the Thulean, the man whose marks were on every fishwife's slate in the city now, ha, Crispus did not unfold and stroll over to play with me. He stayed put and let me come to the middle of the room, and he smiled the warm smile that had never once meant anything, and waited until the talk had quieted enough that he would not have to raise his voice, which is its own kind of power, and then he addressed the room.

"I've been thinking about our friend," he said, "since last we had him. I confess he's troubled my sleep. Not as a marvel. As a question. Bear with an old reader." He had a scroll by him on the couch, half open, and he touched it without picking it up, two fingers laid on it, so we'd know he'd done his work. "Four years ago I made a small joke about Thule. About Pytheas, the Massaliot, the great liar, and his far country no ship can reach. You'll remember." He studied me with a look I couldn't read. "I treated it as wit. I've since decided that was a failure of seriousness on my part, and I have let it go too long, as I think have all of us, charmed."

The room had gone still, that particular stillness when it scents blood and is ashamed of itself for liking the smell. I felt the heat start up my neck the old way and I made myself breathe and stand square and not look at Macer.

"Consider what we have here," Crispus said, and now he did rise, unfolding from the couch, and he was good, I'll give him that, he moved like a man who had spent his life being watched and decided to enjoy it. "A foreigner. No city, none we can write to, none that can send for him or vouch for him or claim him. He comes from a place that Strabo, the gravest geographer of the age, who measured the bones of the world, names a fiction invented to gull fools." He paced, easy, the scroll left behind, his audience caught. "He draws us seas on the far side of the earth where no keel will go to check his hand. He teaches foreign figures to fishwives and slaves and the keepers of grain-factors' books, the senators and scholars passed over, so that quietly, cheaply, beneath our notice, the accounts of Rome are kept in a script no honest Roman elder can read at a glance. He prints copies of our books fifty at a stroke, gray and crooked, cheap as fish, so any porter's brat may own a page of letters and any letter be multiplied past all controlling." He stopped. He had them. "I ask only this. By what name do we call a stateless man with no city and no patron-by-blood, who arrives from a country proven not to exist, who unsettles our commerce, who cheapens our letters, who flew a fire that the gods, in the hearing of this whole city, struck back down upon our roofs? In Greek there is a clean word. *Goēs.* A charlatan, a worker of false wonders. And there is a graver word, which I will not be the one to say first in a friend's house, except to note that the law knows it, and that the law has a long memory for foreigners who trouble the order of things and call down fire."

That last was the knife, slid in gently, and he stepped back from it so the room could look. He had not said magic. He had said the law knows a graver word, naming impiety without owning it, pointing at a man's house and saying nothing about the man. And the worst of it, the part that closed my throat, was that every separate fact he'd laid out was true. He had built the whole charge out of true bricks, and that is what makes a clever man dangerous, that he does not invent the bricks, he only chooses which ones to stack and in what order, and Crispus was a master of the order.

I could not win it as an argument, and I knew that standing there with the heat in my face. He had Strabo and Greek and forty years of standing in rooms like this, and I had broken Latin and a true country I could not name and a dead soldier I could not explain. If I argued Thule he'd quote me Pytheas line by line. If I argued the gods I'd be a foreigner lecturing Romans on their own gods, the shortest road to the graver word he'd been too careful to say.

So I asked Mettius for a cup of water.

It threw the rhythm, which was the only thing I had. Mettius blinked and a slave brought a cup and I held it up to the lamps so the room could see it, plain water, clear, drank half of it, and then said, in my w-for-v Latin, slow because slow was all I had and slow at least sounds like a man choosing his words instead of a man drowning, "Lucius Vettius Crispus has not had the flux."

It landed in the room like a dropped plate. He stared at me.

"The bloody flux," I said. "The camp sickness. It killed more of Caesar's soldiers on the Danube last year than the Dacians did, and some man here, I'd wager, has buried a son or a brother of it, of bad water." I drank the rest of the cup. "I cannot make you read my figures. I cannot make you believe my country. You'll beat me on Strabo every time we meet, and I'm not going to lose to you in Greek. But the water in that cup came clear out of a barrel I packed, off the wharf, the foulest water in Rome, and I drank it in front of you, and I am not going to die of it. Any man here with a household who wants to stop burying his slaves and his children of the summer flux, I'm in the yard by the timber wharf, and the barrel costs less than this man's dinner." I set the cup down. My hand wasn't quite steady and I set it down anyway. "Call me a charlatan when the children stop dying. I'll answer the graver word the day my barrel kills somebody. It hasn't yet. His Strabo never saved a single child from anything. Mine's a worse book and it works."

I'd known before I finished speaking that it wasn't a win. The cleverest men in the room did not suddenly love me. But I'd done the one thing his attack had no door for, which was to put a true result on the table next to his true bricks and let the room weigh them with their own dead children in the scale, and you cannot beat that with Strabo, and Crispus knew it before I'd finished. He recovered beautifully, said something light about the Thulean preferring the company of fishwives to philosophers, got a laugh, a smaller one, a divided one, and turned his shoulder to me the old way. The easy contempt was gone out of him, though, and what replaced it was worse, and I'd put it there myself. He left early, and did not finish anyone's line of Callimachus on the way out.

There was one man in that room who had not laughed at either of us.

He'd come in late and taken a couch in the shadow near the door, and I hadn't marked him until the water was done, until the room was breaking up into its knots of talk and a slave was relighting a guttered lamp. An old man, past fifty, heavy in the shoulders, with the still, weighing face of a man whose work is to read a sign in a slick of blood and tell a consul whether to march. I knew him before Mettius murmured the name. I'd seen that face once before, four years ago, across a festival crowd, looking up from a steaming gut on an altar to lock his eyes on the boy whose burning linen bag had just come down on Rome's roofs.

Aulus Vibenius did not come over. He did not send a slave. He sat in the shadow with a cup he wasn't drinking and looked across the emptying room at me, without warmth and without hate, the long patient look of a man adding a column he was in no hurry to total. He had heard Crispus name the graver word and not own it, and heard me name the dead children and the clear water and the gods I'd been careful not to lecture. He was the only man in Rome who could turn either of those into a charge the law would have to hear, or into a blessing that put the whole thing out of Crispus's reach forever, and his face told me nothing about which way he was leaning.

When I looked again the couch was empty.

Macer walked me down to the wharf himself that night, which he never did, and said nothing the whole way, and at the gate where Tyche had the lamp lit he stopped and looked at me with the broken-veined face that never moved.

"You made an enemy in there worth keeping," he said, "and the wrong man saw it." He meant the priest. "Get the barrels into a senator's house before that crow makes up his mind. A man with the flux out of his halls won't let them burn you. Move." And he went off up the dark wharf, and Tyche held the lamp up to my face and read whatever was there and didn't ask.

I went into the back room and took the ugly first block of Homer off its pigeonhole, the crooked one, the doubled letter, the starved right edge, the failure I kept. I sat under the lamp with the chalk and a clean board and wrote down what I could of what Tyche had said. The sea she meant. The machine that didn't exist yet. A contest, a prize, a story told in front of a crowd. I wrote it out in pieces, rough, more a list of questions than a plan.

Then I pulled out the other board, the one with the static machine sketched on it in chalk - two counter-rotating glass discs, the metal foil sectors, the wire brushes that would pull charge off the spinning surface. I'd been working the sketch for three weeks. The spark it would produce, the blue pop between the terminal balls when the charge had built high enough - that spark was the point. Every plate of glass I'd pressed a charge into, every amber rod I'd rubbed with wool, was trying to get enough voltage to jump a gap reliably, and this machine, if I could find someone to grind the glass true enough, would give me that voltage on demand. At the moment I did not know what I would do with reliable high-voltage sparks. I had a direction, not a plan. Something at the end of that direction involved sending a signal through the air without a wire to carry it, and the machine was the first step up toward that, the way a bottom rung is just a bottom rung until the ladder has a top.

I left both boards on the bench when the lamp went low.

Tyche came to look at them on her way to bed, stood there a moment reading, and said nothing. She picked up the chalk and added a word at the bottom of the contest board - *when* - and set the chalk down and went out.
