# Chapter 29: Overreach

The word came up the road ahead of the proof of it, carried by men who had heard it from men, so that by the time it reached the camps behind Antioch it had been polished smooth as a coin passed through too many hands. Ctesiphon was taken. The great city on the river, the Parthian's own seat, had fallen, and Caesar had stood in the king's own hall. The legions had gone down the rivers to the place where the rivers ended and the water turned bitter and went on forever, the southern sea, the edge of the maps, and there was no more east to take.

I heard the story first from a wine-factor at the quay, then from a wounded optio coming in on a cart, then from a tribune's slave who swore he had it from a man who had been there. Each telling grew bigger and emptier together. They had reached the sea. They had loaded a ship and seen it stand out toward lands that paid no tax to Parthia or to Rome or to anyone. The old man, they said, the emperor, had stood on the shore in the heat with the bitter water at his feet and watched a merchant ship go out toward India and said he wished he were younger, that he might cross over after Alexander, and the men around him had laughed because it was the kind of thing he said, and then had not laughed, because of the look on him.

I did not see it. I was a great many miles behind the shore where the old man stood wishing he were a boy, standing over a fire telling a centurion to wait for the bubbles. Everything I know of the high-water mark of the world I know from the brown line it leaves and the things it floats down to you afterward. What floated down to me was the strangeness in the men who told it. They told it as a victory and their faces told something else, the look of a man who has eaten until he is sick and is still being handed plates.

There was one small piece the rumor carried that was mine. The aerial work at Ctesiphon had named the water conduit in two days, where a ground scout would have needed three weeks. The city had still taken the city's time to fall. But the men inside the wall had known, early, that we could see over it, and that knowing is its own kind of pressure on a defense. I had been in camps, not at the siege, but the result reached me as numbers, and the numbers were different from the wars before.

They had taken everything. There was nowhere left to point. A Roman with nowhere to point is a frightening thing, like a draft horse in a stall too small to turn around in, and I stood at the bottom of the world's longest supply line and felt the whole weight of it arrive at a wall it had not known was there.

Something reached me before anything else visible: the grain. There was less of it coming up the road, and then it was coming up the road wrong, in guarded clumps with too many soldiers walking beside too few carts, and the soldiers walking beside it had the look of men who had been shot at. A supply line is a held breath. You do not notice it until it catches. Ours caught somewhere behind us, down toward the rivers and the great taken cities, and the catching came up to me as rumor and short rations and a quartermaster swearing over a tally that no longer balanced because the figures going out the far end were not coming back as anything but trouble.

The cities had risen. That much reached me, and even with a camp's worth of returning soldiers to question, I could piece together only fragments of what that meant across a dozen provinces at once. The taken places would not stay taken. They had bent the knee while the legions stood in their streets, and the legions had gone on east to the bitter sea, and there were too few of them left in the rear to hold what the front had grabbed. Garrisons cut off. A column ambushed in a place that had been Roman a month before. One piece reached me as something more specific than rumor: a ship's captain at the Antioch docks told me, flatly, that Cyrene was burning. He had put in from Alexandria and heard it there. Jewish quarter, then the whole quarter, then the neighborhood beside it, then something larger than a neighborhood. The governor's hands, the captain said, were insufficient for the size of it.

A name went with the story. Lusius Quietus, the Moorish cavalry commander, was being sent with real force to put it down. He was a man the soldiers spoke of with the particular flat respect they gave to someone who was very good at the kind of work nobody liked to name. And somewhere south and west of Cyrene more of the same, whole communities turning on their neighbors and being turned on, burning hotter than the Parthian war ever had. That was all I had. The printed maps and the standardized route orders had moved the first reserve cohort three days faster than dispatch riders would have managed, and it was still not fast enough, and three days faster was what we had instead of enough.

I heard it as a Roman heard it: as madness and treachery. I knew only that the line I kept alive with clean water now ran through country that wanted it dead, and that the further the army had reached, the thinner the hand was that held the ground behind its back, until the ground simply closed up behind it like water behind a swimmer.

Celer's cohort was in the rear, on the supply road, which had been a dull safe duty in the summer and was the most dangerous place in the east by autumn, because the road was what the rising wanted to cut, and a cohort spread thin along a road is a cohort spread thin along the exact thing everyone is trying to kill. I knew this by arithmetic, lying awake. I had been expecting to be surprised by him for weeks, which is a specific wrongness of that kind of waiting. You are ready for it and it still arrives like a fall.

They brought him on a cart, with four of his men around it who would not leave even when an optio told them to, in the gray before a dawn. He was awake. That was the first thing, and I let it be a good thing for the space of one breath before I made myself look properly. He had taken a thrust low on the left side, below the ribs, in a night action on the road where his men had held the carts and lost six and held them. Somebody had bound it on the road with whatever was to hand, which was the trouble, because whatever was to hand on a road in that country was filth. It had been two days and a half. The binding when I cut it away came off in a way I had smelled before on other men and learned to dread, and under it the edges of the wound were the wrong color and the wrong heat, and the skin around it had gone a tight shine that ran further than it should.

"Don't," Celer said. He was watching my face, weighing it. "I've seen that look on enough surgeons. Save it for someone who hasn't seen the inside of a tent before."

"It needs cleaning."

"It needs the gods," he said, "but clean it, by all means, if it gives you something to do with your hands. I always said you'd kill me with washing."

I cleaned it. I had water boiled fresh and cooled, more of it than the camp could spare, and I washed the wound and the skin around it until the cloths came away less foul, and I cut away what my knife told me was already dead, which made him swear at me steadily in three languages and grip the cart-rail with a hand that went white, and I packed it with linen boiled and dried clean and bound it loose so it could weep instead of holding the rot in as the road-binding had. I gave him posca to drink and washed it on the wound besides, the sour wine that I half believed and half only hoped did something. I built him a place off the road, by clean water I had sited myself, and I changed the dressing twice a day and boiled everything between, and I made the men who tended him scald their hands until they hated me, and I did every single thing I knew.

I knew a great deal. I knew more than any man within five hundred miles about keeping the invisible climbing things out of a body. And I knew, washing his side at noon on the first full day, watching the shine on the skin reach a thumb's width further than it had at dawn, that I had come to the wall I had spent my life pretending I could not see clearly. The little creatures were already inside him. They had been put inside him on a dark road by an iron point and two days of a dirty rag, before I ever cut the binding. There is no clean water in the world that washes out something that is already in the blood. Everything I had was for keeping them out. I had nothing for getting them back. I never had. I had been able to save the army from this for fifteen years only by reaching every man before the road did, and the road had reached Celer first.

He went down the way that wound goes down. Not steadily, which is the cruelest part of it, because it leaves you room to be a fool.

The first day he was himself and angry at being on his back. He had his optio come and report and he gave orders about the cohort and the carts and where the road was weakest and he was right about all of it, his voice flat and clipped and exactly as it had always been, and I let myself think, against everything the skin was telling me, that he was a tougher animal than the rule. The second day the fever came up, slow, certain, with nowhere built into it for it to stop. He sweated through the bedding and I changed it and boiled it. He drank the water I gave him. The shine on his side went up past the wound and around toward his back and the smell of it came through the clean dressings no matter how often I changed them, and I changed them, by lamp and by sun, and the changing did nothing but let me look at it and know.

Naso came. They had kept him in the rear with the baggage and the mules, his ruined hand curled to his chest, his ankle still putting its hitch in his walk, and he came and sat where he could be useful, which was holding water and carrying out the foul cloths to be burned, the one good hand doing the work of the bad one's absence. He did not say much. He had buried things in his own way years ago and he knew the weight of a man sitting with another man's dying. He kept the small fire I needed and he kept it clean and he kept his face still, and once, when I came back from being sick behind the tent, he had a cup of the posca ready and he put it in my hand without a word.

On the third day Celer stopped being angry, which frightened me worse than the fever, because a Celer who has stopped fighting is a Celer who has done his arithmetic and got an answer. He was clear-eyed between the fevers. That is the nature of that death, that it leaves the mind alone for too long. He looked at the dressing when I changed it and he looked at the shine that had reached his back and he looked at my face doing the thing faces do when they are working very hard to be steady.

"You've gone gray over it," he said. "When did you go gray. You were a boy."

"You've gone grayer."

"I've earned mine." He let his head go back. "How long. And don't lie to me to be kind, I'll know, and I'll think less of you, and I've thought a great deal of you, which is a thing I'd as soon not undo at the end of it."

I held it for a moment. Nothing to measure the silence with except my own pulse going in my ears. "Not long," I said. "A day. Two. The fever takes more of you each time it comes."

"Good," he said. "Not the fever. The knowing." He worked his dry mouth. "I've watched men go who didn't get told. They spend their last day on plans. It's an obscene thing to watch, a dying man making plans." He looked at me, and I saw the man he had been at the horse-lines calculating what he could afford to say. "Tell me a thing. The bag. You never put another man up, after the boy. Did you ever want to? After. When they pressed you."

"Every time," I said. "I wanted to give it to them. To be done arguing."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"There," he said, and something eased in his ruined face that was as close to peace as I saw on it. "You did decide, then. What kind of man builds a thing like that. You decided it with your no, every time, for fifteen years, and never once told yourself that was the deciding." He closed his eyes. "Mind your drain," he said, which was a joke we had, and then he was asleep, or under, and the fever came up again that night and took more of him, and the day after that he was not clear anymore, and talked to men who were not there, a centurion long dead, his mother by the sound of it, and I sat with him and washed him and did the useless tender work that is all there is left to do, and Naso fed the fire, and the road outside went on moving in the dark.

That wound takes the words first and the breath after. The breath went out long and ragged and did not come back in, before dawn on the fifth day, while I held the cup to his lips. I sat with the cup still at his lips for longer than made any sense, the way I had knelt by the burned cloth with my hands out and nothing for them to do, and then Naso reached over with his one good hand and pressed my arm, and I set the cup down.

I washed him. There was no need to wash a dead man and I washed him anyway, the wound that had won and the rest of him the wound had wasted, and I closed his eyes with my thumb. I straightened the kit-strap he had worried at the last night we ate together, the one his hands had wanted to keep busy on, and I laid it neat across him because his hands could not do it now and somebody's should. His optio came and his men came, the four who would not leave the cart, and they stood in the gray with their helmets off, and I gave him over to them, because he was theirs more than mine. They wrote it down that the prefect Quintus Marcius Celer fell of a wound taken in a night action holding the supply road, which was true, every word of it.

I went back to the drains. There is always work, and the work that morning was a centurion's century waiting for clean water on a road that wanted them dead, and I went and stood over the fire and waited for the bubbles, and my hands shook on the dipper so that the centurion noticed and looked away from it to give me the manners of not having seen, which was the kindest thing a soldier did for me that month.

I did the arithmetic, because I could not stop doing the arithmetic. It is the habit my hands run to when the rest of me cannot be trusted. I had kept thousands of men from dying of the thing that killed him. I had it in figures, near enough, the men who would have gone down to the flux and the foul water in two wars and had not, because of clean cloth and cooled boiled water and a tedium of standing over fires. Strangers, every one, men whose names I never learned and whose children's names I was spared. And I could put every one of those saved strangers in one pan of a scale and the one man in the other and the scale would slam, not balance. The strangers were saved before the climbing things got into them. Celer was brought to me after, and there was no cleverness in any head in that century of the world that could reach back across that little distance of two days and pull the damage out of his blood.

My hands kept shaking even when I set the dipper down. The centurion had walked off to give me the manners of not watching. I stood at the fire until my hands were steady again and I could not have told you how long that took.

My whole life's gift was a wall built to keep the invisible things out. I had no door in it for going in after the ones already inside. I never built one. I did not know how. No one would know how for longer than my life, longer than the lives of everyone burning a foul dressing on that road. The saving work had a floor in it, and the floor was where my friend was.

The army would not hold what it had taken. I knew it in the body before the mind would say it, watching the grain come up the road in guarded clumps and the empty carts come back full of the gray-faced and the worse-than-gray-faced from places that had been ours a month before. Something reaches as far as it can hold and then it reaches a little further, because reaching is the only thing it knows how to do, and the little further is the part that closes up behind it like water. Someone, some day, was going to have to stand at the front of all that overreached length and do the thing no Roman in my hearing had ever once said aloud, which was to let go of ground that had been taken, to pull the arm back out of the wall, to draw a line somewhere short of everywhere and write here, and not past here. I did not know who. I knew only that it had to come, and that out here, on this road, in this autumn, we had found the end of both at once.

I did not point the hunger west to anyone. The thought was in me harder than it had been, hardened by the smell of a clean dressing over a wound that had already won, by the brown enormous emptiness the empty basket had hung over, by the figures of grain that would not come back as anything but trouble. The east was a dead end, desert and rivers with foreign names and cities that would not stay knelt, and it had eaten the old man's wish to be young again and it had eaten Celer below the ribs. Every instinct I had said the want would have to go somewhere or it would go on grinding up the people it could reach. I knew a place it could go that I had drawn empty on a tablet in a cell before I could speak. But there was no one to say it to. Celer was the one man in the east I might have tried it on, and the road had reached him first.

So I kept it. I watched the fires.

Naso left with the next baggage train going back toward Antioch, mended enough, alive, his hand curled to his chest, his ankle hitching him up onto the cart. He stopped beside me before he climbed up. He looked at me a while with the face that had stopped being young a long way north. Then he took, from inside his tunic, the kit-strap I had laid neat across the body, the one Celer's hands had worried at, and he put it into my hand and folded my fingers over it with his one good hand, and he climbed up onto the cart and did not say anything, because there was nothing the right size to say, and the train pulled out east to west along the road that the rising would cut again before the moon changed.

I worked the strap into my own kit, where my hands could find it in the dark on the long marches. The fires needed watching. I went back down the line.
