# Chapter 30: The Blindside

The trumpets went wrong before anything else did.

A camp keeps time by horn. You stop hearing it after long enough, same as you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and then one morning the heart does something it has never done and you hear nothing but that. I was at the fires, where I was always at the fires, on a stretch of road north of the city where the army was pulling itself back westward in long sullen lengths, more carts every week, fewer men beside each cart, all of it leaning the same direction now, away from the rivers, toward the sea and home. The dawn watch should have blown and changed. Instead the horns went up out of order, two at once and then a third that came in late and broke off, and the men around my fire stopped with their bowls halfway to their mouths and looked east down the road, which is the direction you look when you are afraid the enemy has done something in the night.

A rider had come up the road in the dark, a dispatch rider on a blown horse, and he had gone straight to the legate's quarter without stopping. The men who carry water and empty pots carry news faster than any rider, and the news came down the line cold through a wall, from the inside out, a man going still before the man beside him knew why. I had it from a baggage clerk I had taught the new figures, a sharp Greek-tongued boy who did the grain tallies, and he did not say it as news. He said it as a question, because he could not make it sit flat enough to be a fact.

"The Thulean," he said. "Is it true Caesar is dead." I told him he shouldn't repeat things he heard in the dark, and he looked at me, and I saw from his face that he already knew it was true and had only wanted me to say otherwise.

For the length of several breaths I did not believe him, and the not-believing had no grief in it yet, only the simple animal refusal of a thing that does not fit the arrangements of the world. I had been in this country nineteen years. The emperor had been emperor the entire time. He had bought me out of a leather-merchant's yard with his own coin and set his own family's name on me, and in all those years the sun had come up over the marching camps and Trajan had been Caesar, and the two facts had grown together in some place under thought where I did not examine them, so that the clerk might as well have told me the sun had been recalled. I knew old men died. I knew this one was old and had been sick on the road west, everyone knew that, it was the reason the whole army was crawling home instead of marching, the emperor was unwell and turning back. I had filed it in the same drawer as other facts I was not ready to act on. I had let myself believe, without ever once putting it in words where I would have laughed at it, that the old soldier was a fixed point. That he would outlast the war. That he would outlast me.

He had died at a town on the coast called Selinus, in Cilicia, with the sea on one side and the mountains shutting down to the water on the other, turning back toward Rome with the conquests souring behind him. A seizure, they said, a stroke that took one side of him and then the rest over a few days. The eighth day of the month. The news had walked up the coast and inland, mouth to mouth, so that by the time it reached my fire the man had been dead the better part of a fortnight and the world had already gone on being a world without him in it, only nobody on my stretch of road had been told yet that they were living in it.

I did the only thing my hands knew. I went on tending the fire, told the waiting century to wait for the bubbles, and under the steady stupid work of it the bottom went out of me, not for the dead man, who I had stood in front of exactly once in my life and who had been kind to me in the cold transactional way the state is kind to a useful tool, but for me. I am not proud of how fast it turned to me. It turned to me before the water boiled.

Everything I was, in law, came from him, and standing over the fire on a road that wanted us dead, the morning I learned the emperor was a body being carried somewhere west, I did the arithmetic while the century's water came to temperature. I was not Daniel out here. Daniel was a sound men could not say and a boy who had stopped existing in a stubble field a lifetime ago. I was Marcus Ulpius Danihel, freedman of the deified house, and every word of that name was the dead man's gift. The Marcus he had hung on me like a coat. The Ulpius was his own blood, his gens, the name his sisters carried and his father had carried, lent to a stateless foreigner he had decided was worth more enfranchised than enslaved. That name was the wall I lived behind. It was the reason a haruspex with a grudge had read me a sound liver instead of a guilty one. It was the reason a grammarian who hated me to the marrow had only ever been able to talk, and never to act, because you do not move on a man the emperor has personally named unless you want to explain to the emperor why. Macer had said it to me plainly, years back, the night the citizenship tablet went inside my tunic against the dead glass. No haruspex reads a bad sign over a man Caesar's just named. The man Caesar had named was a man Caesar protected. Caesar was a body on a cart.

The worst of it was how slowly the wall came apart. That was what I understood, standing there. A dead patron does not unmake your freedom; the law is not that crude. I was still free. The name was still mine. But a name is only worth what stands behind it, and the thing that had stood behind mine had stopped breathing on the coast of Cilicia, and the men who had feared to move on me had feared him, not the seven letters of his family. With him gone they did not have to be afraid of a sound. They had to be afraid of whoever stood behind it next, and nobody yet knew who that was, and a man nobody yet knew was a man with no debts to me and no reason on earth to keep an old emperor's former barbarian safe.

I went down the list the way you check the lashings on a load before a steep road. Crispus, in Rome, who had wanted me ruined for the better part of two decades and had been beaten back every time by the same fact, and who would now wake up one of these mornings to a courier and a different world. The priestly colleges, who held a permanent hook in my press and my contest and my figures since the impiety business, who had been content to be consulted because consultation was as far as the wind let them reach, and who could feel a wind change as well as any haruspex alive. Vibenius, who held a debt I could never pay and had told me to my face that his house does not lean, and whose house, in a new reign, might find a new direction worth leaning. The complaint had been quieted, not killed. Nothing in this country was ever killed. It was buried in shallow ground and the ground here was about to be turned over by other men's spades.

And I was the whole width of the sea from any of it, on a dead-end campaign, on a road in revolt, carrying a dead friend's strap worked into my kit and a dead emperor's name worked into my law. And the one thing in the world I wanted, with a want that came up so fast it frightened me worse than the news had, was to be in Rome. To have my hands on what I had built before the men back there got their hands on it. To be standing in front of Marcia and Tyche, who were running all of it on a wharf a month's hard sail away and did not yet know, could not yet know, that the floor had gone out from under all of us.

I thought of the box, the one under the floorboard that only Tyche knew to keep. The dead glass in its wrapping of oiled wool. The citizenship tablet beside it. Two relics of two countries that couldn't write to each other, and one of them had just gone further away.

The other leash had not died. I thought of that next, and it was a cold practical thought and I was glad to have it, because it was something I could hold. Scaeva was alive. The senator was alive in Rome and the senator had spent real weight to make me his, had leaned in across a borrowed room and tipped a college of priests and made me, in front of witnesses, the senator's man. Whatever else had fallen, that had not. The emperor's hand had loosed me; the senator's had not. And a senator with an emperor freshly dead was a man doing his own arithmetic that morning, faster and colder than mine, because senators had been doing exactly this arithmetic since before there were emperors. Scaeva would already be sorting the new reign for handholds. He knew I imported sulfur, knew how much, knew through which gate. He knew the powder worked and roughly what it ate. He had once said, with that beautiful unhurried voice, that he wanted the powder pointed somewhere by a friend once the steel was solved. A change of master at the top did not make a senator want power less. It made him want it more, and faster, and with fewer of the old restraints, because the man who had set the restraints was being carried home in a jar. A loosened grip does not free a tool. It gives the other hand room to swing it wherever it likes.

By midday the rumor had grown its second head, and the second was worse than the first because the first had at least been a single clean blow. I stood in the middle of the not-believing and tried to find what stayed the same across all three versions, sorting them for error as I sorted grain reports: what came different in each telling, and what held.

What stayed the same was a name, Hadrian, and a place, Antioch, where the legions of Syria had hailed him. What changed in every telling was how. The governor of Syria, a kinsman of the old man's, who had been sitting in Antioch the whole while the old man died up the coast. Adopted on the deathbed, the official word ran, named son and heir in the last days, the seal still warm. And under that word, already, the same morning, in three soldiers' mouths out of four, the other word: that the empress had been at Selinus, and the prefect of the Guard, Attianus, had been at Selinus, and that a dying man with one side of his face gone could be made to have said a great many useful things, or could be reported to have said them after he could no longer say anything at all. That a freedman of the household had announced the adoption in the right order, and that the freedman had then conveniently died. People said it in those particular flat soldier tones that mean a man is repeating what he heard and not signing his name to it. One of the men at the third fire said it shorter. "Dead man's mouth and his wife's thumb," he said to the fire, not to anyone, then turned back to his bowl. Another one, further down: "Bookish kinsman. Some of us were hoping for a killer." Nobody argued. I noted it, filed it among the things I could not weigh.

I had no way to know. I was a man in a field hearing about a flood in the next valley. The new emperor was a name and the manner of his making was a quarrel between people I had never seen, in a city I had passed through once, and there was no man within a hundred miles whose word on it I would have trusted with a coin. A new emperor with a name I knew roughly, barely, about as well as I knew the name of any city official, and with unknown priorities, and a making that half his own army called a fix in the same breath they cheered it. The men cheered where the officers could see them, and muttered where the officers could not, and the muttering said the army's old hard core had wanted one of the marshals, the killers who had taken the cities, and had got instead a bookish kinsman the old man had kept close and never quite given the thing to, until the thing was given in a sickroom with only the wife to hear.

There was a moment, standing there, when the name and the talk of a bookish man caught on something at the back of my skull and would not come loose. A man I had seen. A beard, a roll of drawings under an arm, a cold exchanged bow in a crowd at the Forum a few years back, and Apollodorus's voice loud and contemptuous over the top of it. Go and paint his gourds. I had watched a proud man humiliate a powerful one that day and thought, idly, that the powerful one had the longer memory. The catch was there and then it was gone. I could not make it sit. There were a great many bearded men of letters around an emperor, and I had seen that one for the length of an insult, and the road in front of me was full of grain that would not come back and water that needed boiling, and I let it go and went back down the line.

I stood over the next fire and the next century and made grown men wait for the water, as I always made them wait, and I did the work because the work was the one thing the death of an emperor could not change in the next hour. The invisible climbing things did not care whose name was on the standard. They killed a soldier of Trajan and a soldier of Hadrian by the same method and were beaten by the same method, with clean cloth and cooled boiled water and the tedium of standing over a flame.

The thing I had not been able to plan for was this: the work kept going in Rome without me. I had built it to do exactly that, and it had never stopped doing it, not for a single day of the war years, and knowing that was the first solid thing I had felt since the baggage clerk asked his flat question. Tyche had the cipher. She had the contest, the tally, the press count for the fortnight - the contest entries had been arriving since early spring, she had written me that, the broadsheet runs were on schedule. Eros had the blocks and Marcia had the books and between them they had kept the whole engine running through two wars and an impiety trial without waiting for me to come home and make a decision. I had never, until that moment on a road in revolt, fully understood how much work it had taken to arrange things so that my absence was survivable. It did not make me feel better. It made me feel the distance differently, as a thing that had a length I could calculate, rather than a gap I could only stand in front of. They were running it. It was running. I needed to get there before anyone else did.

Lucanus was five that year, and I was far enough from him that I could not even do the counting I always did when I was frightened, the counting of his age against how much longer this campaign would run, whether the road would let me back in time. I put him in the back of it with the rest and kept working.

The army would crawl west now whether the emperor was a god or a corpse or a quarrel between a wife and a guard, because there was nothing left east to crawl toward, and somewhere ahead of all of us, in a city on the sea, a man I could not place was being made into the thing that would decide whether the law that held my life still held it. I needed to get to Rome with a steadiness in the wanting that frightened me more than panic would have. Marcia had the books and Tyche had the cipher and between them they had every dangerous thing I had ever made or written, the powder ratios in a script only she could read, the press, the contest, the figures, the whole engine, sitting on a wharf in a city that was, this morning, no longer certain to be safe for the man whose name was on all of it. They did not know yet. The news would take weeks to reach them by sea, just as it had taken weeks to reach me by road, and there was a stretch of the world's width between us in which they would go on running the enterprise of a dead emperor's freedman without knowing the emperor was dead, balancing the tally, crowning the contest, while the ground under all of it had already given way and only had not finished falling.

I worked the dead man's strap once between my fingers, where my hands could find it now in the dark, and made the century wait for the boil, and turned the problem over the only way I knew how to turn anything, which was to start counting the days it would take a man to get from this road to that wharf, and to find that the number was very large, and to begin, while the water came up to its bubbles, to make it smaller.
