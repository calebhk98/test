# Chapter 24: High Ground

The pressmen had gone, Eros last, ink to the elbow, holding a sheet of the Minerva-prize tales away from his tunic the way a man carries a baby he isn't sure of yet. The river was going from pewter to nothing. Somewhere down the wharf a man was loading grain by torchlight and cursing the man above him, the easy fluent cursing of people who do it every night and mean nothing by it. In the shed the water clock ticked its lie, off by a quarter of an hour and gaining, and Tyche had already corrected it by the sun and would correct it again at dawn - a motion she now made with one hand while holding something else in the other.

I stood by the well with a cup of my own filtered water, which still tasted of charcoal and of being right, and I counted. Eleven men and women on the wages, not counting the wharf-women who stitched piecework and the carters. A press that still ate one sheet in four and still threw off more printed paper in a week than the whole city of Rome had owned in writing the year I fell into it. Three months running on rag paper now, cheaper to make than the papyrus it had replaced, and I had not stopped to note when that had become ordinary. A forge across town that great men sent money to because of a bronze tube I had made burst on purpose in front of them. A filter in two of the public fountains, where it mattered. A spyglass that did not work, sold to people who did not know it did not work. A contest that put a baker's dream of a self-grinding mill into the hands of children who now drew, in the dust of the Subura, carts with no mules and ships with no oars. And underneath all of it, unfinished, a pile of copper discs and zinc strips and shallow vinegar bowls I had been assembling for a week and had not yet shown Heras, because I wanted to be sure before I gave him something new and half-wrong. I had counted myself once, on a wax tablet in a cell, for whoever found my body. This was the other list. Twelve years long, and I stood in the dark over my own well and could not make any of it feel like mine. From the roof of the shed you could see a long way across the city: rooftops still, the river black, a million small fires pretending to be sleeping.

I had walked through the markets that morning, which I did sometimes to be among the city without being asked anything by it. Coming up from the wharf through the cattle market, the new figures were everywhere, spreading into Rome's low commercial life through every crack and low seam. A fish-seller had them chalked on a slate, the morning's count and the price per measure, in the quick hooked hand the slate-writers had developed, which I would not have recognized as my own digits if I hadn't known to look. A boy ran them off on his fingers for a woman buying eels and got it right faster than the woman's own slave with his counters, and the slave gave the boy a look I knew from the inside. Nobody had taught that boy from a book. The marks had simply gotten into the city by being easier to carry than what they replaced, and now they were the city's marks, not mine.

In a colonnade off the cattle market a man was selling small painted clay figures off a board - gods and gladiators and lucky phalluses for the festivals - and among them, between a Hercules and a little Venus, were two of mine. A balloon, fat and gray-painted, with a stick man hung under it in a basket the size of a thumbnail. And a device with a long body and many little wheels under it, painted with a smoking stack at the front, that I had never built and never would and that the potter had copied off a printed prize-tale because it sold. A horseless cart. A train, in everything but the rails, which the potter had left off because rails would have looked like nothing to a buyer and a smoking stack looked like power.

I stood there longer than I meant to. The man took me for a buyer and started his patter: the smoking one was a Thulean engine, a wonder from the far north where the cold makes men clever, it would one day drag the grain of all Egypt up the Tiber with no men and no beasts, his sister's boy had seen the drawings. A good story, and it had drifted a long way from mine, picked up a sister's boy and the grain of Egypt and come out the far end as a two-as toy. I bought the train. I gave him more than he asked and told him to keep the patter, it was a good patter, and he thought I was a soft fool and I let him.

I have it still. It sits where I can see it. A clay engine that pulls nothing, made by a man who copied a dream printed by my press from a tale entered in my contest by a boy who will never know my name, sold to me as a marvel from a country that does not exist by a man who will go to his grave certain that somewhere in the cold north it is true.

The contest reading that year was held, as always, in Macer's house, under Macer's name, the Macer Prize, paid out of my coin and crowned with my judgment, with Macer at the head of the room taking the credit for a thing he privately thought was a waste of a perfectly good dinner. I had stopped minding. Letting Macer own it publicly was the price of its being allowed to exist. Letting the gods own the water filter was the price of the filter. Letting Heras call the boiling "Greek wisdom" was the price of the boiling. I had a lot of children, and not one of them had my name on it, and most days I told myself that was the design.

A girl read that year. Eleven, twelve, freeborn, the daughter of a wool-factor who had brought her along because the wife was sick and he didn't know what else to do with her, and she'd heard them reading and she'd written something on the back of one of her father's tally-sheets, in the new figures where she'd run out of words, and somebody had put it in the basket as a joke. It was about a ladder. A ladder of knowing. She'd written that a man could not jump to the moon but he could build a step, and on the step his son could build a step, and she had drawn it, a stair going up into a sky full of the little painted stars children draw, and a stick figure on the bottom step holding up a torch for the ones above him who weren't born yet. The perspective was wrong. The stick man's torch was bigger than his head.

I had to go and stand by the wine for a while. Crispus was there, oiled and elegant, and he made a remark about the spectacle of a barbarian teaching Roman children to dream of staircases to heaven, the impiety of it, the bad taste. The room laughed, and I parried it with a joke against myself that let everyone feel generous. I won the room. I always won the room now, because Caesar had named me and no man of letters wants to be the one who turns out to have been mocking the emperor's wonder.

But I had drawn that ladder myself, in the dark, in a script no one in that room could read, years before that girl was old enough to hold a stylus. I had drawn it as the whole point of everything. A child I would never meet had reached up out of her own head and grabbed the exact design of it and drawn the stick man holding the torch for the ones not born yet, and she did not know, she could not know, that the man clapping for her by the wine was the one who'd lit the torch, and that he was standing in a room full of people and there was no one in it, not one, he could turn to and say: she got it. She got the whole thing. Look.

I gave her the prize. A child's tale beat a baker's machine and a senator's son's war-engine, and I made the case for it cleanly and Macer grumbled that there was no profit in stairs and paid out the hundred denarii into the wool-factor's astonished hand. The girl regarded me with the flat incuriosity of a child who has received exactly what she expected. I told her the torch was too big. She told me it had to be, so the ones above could see it. Then she went to find her father.

I keep the phone. By now even I have to reach for what it is. A flat piece of black glass and dark metal, the size of my palm, heavier than it looks, with a single eye of glass at the back and rounded corners worn smooth where my thumb used to ride. Romans rub a worn coin or a god's bronze foot, a rubbing they do without thinking, and I have done it to the phone for twelve years without meaning to, and the metal there is brighter than the rest.

It does nothing. It is whole. There is not a scratch on the glass that matters. It sits asleep with no current in it and no fire I can build will wake it, no wheel I can turn, no clever Roman trick and no clever Thulean one, because what it needs is a current I cannot make and could not explain and have given up grieving for, the way you give up grieving for a country.

That night, after the well and the counting, I took it out. There is danger in it; a man who held that up in a market would be a witch by sundown and a dead witch by the next festival, and I keep it wrapped in oiled wool in a box under a board only Tyche knows about, next to a wax tablet that says Marcus Ulpius Danihel and made me a citizen, two relics of two countries that cannot write to each other. I held it and rubbed the bright place with my thumb and tried to remember a song.

There was a song. There were a thousand songs; the glass was full of them once, I had carried a whole shouting world of music in my pocket and never once thought it a wonder. Now I cannot keep one. I can keep the arrangement, the rise and fall, the feeling in my chest where the chorus went, but the words come up in pieces and the pieces are wrong - I know they are wrong, I am misremembering them as I misremembered the clock and the third powder and the boiling-point of water on a mountain - and there is nothing in this world that can write the true words back to me. The song is in a place that cannot send to here. Same as the current. Same as my mother's voice, which I can no longer be sure I am remembering rather than inventing. I hummed a few bars of the wrong song over a dead piece of glass in the dark of my own rich house, with my freeborn wife asleep down the hall - Marcia, six months along now, a fact I would forget for an hour and then remember again like a door I kept walking into - and eleven people on my wages and the emperor's favor on my name. The rule of this whole place is that you do not get to say the loud true thing out loud: you make a toy and a filter and a contest and let the gods take the credit, and the loud true thing stays in a box under a board.

I put the phone back. I put the citizenship tablet on top of it, then took the tablet off again because it felt wrong, the new country lying on top of the old one like dirt over a grave, and I put the phone on top instead, and that felt worse. I left them side by side in the dark, two flat things that knew nothing of each other.

A few days later a commission arrived from a farrier in the Capua district, brought by a carter who'd heard we paid for quality data in iron. The farrier's name was Statius. He hadn't come himself - he'd sent his nephew with a roll of wax tablets and a covering letter in the rough hand of a man who writes sometimes rather than often. The letter said he'd heard the workshop paid for tables of iron consistency. The tablets held a list: iron color against readiness for working. His grandfather had started the table and his father had kept it and Statius had added twenty years of his own horseshoes to it, fifty years of heat readings calibrated against consequence, meaning when he got it wrong a horse went lame on a mountain road and a rider broke his neck. Cherry, bright cherry, orange, lemon-yellow, white - against each color, what the iron does under the hammer, whether it will crack at the fold, how long it stays workable before it cools past use. Field readings, not laboratory ones.

His color scale was rougher than mine, built from a working fire rather than controlled tests, but it covered more real situations than mine had. Statius's table had already been wrong in every way it could be wrong, and it had been corrected against consequence. Mine had been wrong in a clean workshop with nobody's neck on the line.

I paid the prize - a year's skilled wages into the nephew's startled hands - and copied everything. Then I went and got Heras, who had come by that afternoon on no particular errand, which was itself the errand. He read the tablets carefully, without expression, drinking the wine I'd poured him without looking at the cup.

"Your farrier has done a century of work," he said eventually. "More carefully than your workshop has managed in five years."

"Forty years, technically. With his grandfather."

He set the tablets down and looked at me over the rim of the cup. "You're telling me a man who shoes horses has better temperature data than you do. And you're surprised." He gave me the look he reserved for patients who were sure they knew what was wrong with them.

I said I was. A little.

"Why? The man has been solving the same problem every day for forty years. The problem was: how hot is the iron, and will it hold the nail, and will the horse be lame next week. You have been solving the problem three days a month in a clean shed. Who do you expect to know more?" He set the cup down. "The city is full of Statiuses. Every trade. Every generation. Men who have been solving the same problem under real conditions for so long that their knowledge has a different quality than yours. You've built a mechanism for paying them to show it to you. That's the useful part. The access."

He did not say what the mechanism was called or where I'd gotten it from. Neither did I. I added the tablets to the notes that night, with a cross-reference against Hermes's forge data and a note about source and method, in the hooked-mark notation I used for things I was certain of. Statius would not know that entry was there. He would go to his grave thinking he'd sold a table to a rich man's workshop for a year's wages.

Heras came back a few days after the farrier's tablets, at dusk, with a flask of something Greek and resinous. He sat on the bench by the dead forge-corner and we drank and watched Pamphilus's wife hang washing in the last light and said nothing for a good while, which between Heras and me was most of the conversation. He had the cleanest hands I had ever seen, still - age-spotted now, and they never quite stopped being a doctor's. He turned the flask in them, looking at the river.

"The little wool-factor's girl," he said eventually. "With the stairs. Everyone heard about it. Crispus has a phrase now - 'the barbarian's heaven by ladder.' It's not bad, as phrases go. He'll wear it out." He tasted his wine. "You gave a child a hundred denarii for a drawing of a staircase. Macer is telling people it proves you've gone soft."

"Macer thinks crop rotation is a soft idea."

Heras smiled the faint way he did. "He asked me what you're for." He did not look at me when it mattered; he looked at the washing. "He's owned you, he's sold you, he's dined off you for twelve years, and he sat in his own house watching you crown a child's drawing and he leaned over to me and asked, in earnest, with money on his mind: what is the man for. What is the thing under all the things." He drank. "I told him I didn't know. Which is the truest thing I've said all year."

I should have made a joke. I had one. I felt it come up and be wrong and I let it go past.

"You've made the figures," Heras said, mild. "I'll grant you the figures. The boiling, the water, the washing of wounds - I've stood over the people it's saving, I'll grant you that and I'll thank you for it as long as I draw breath. Those are gifts." He set the flask down and held up a finger, then a second, then a third, counting. "And then there's the rest of it. The tube that bursts. The white salt that took two years and Geta's hand. The drawings of carts that carry the grain of Egypt with no men, which means no men working and no men eating. The ships with no oars, which a child draws now because you taught a city to draw them." He stopped, and the river finished going dark while the silence held. "I'm a man who has watched a great many people die, Daniel, and I have learned to be suspicious of any gift addressed to people who can't yet say no to it."

"You think it's pride," I said.

"I think," Heras answered carefully, "that I cannot tell the difference, from where I sit, between a man doing a duty no one asked him to do and a man who has decided he knows better than the whole world that made him. I have known you twelve years and I cannot tell the difference. I don't think you can either. And I think that's the most interesting thing about you and the most frightening, and I am too old to be frightened of much." He picked the flask back up. "Macer wants to know what you're for so he can price it. I want to know because I'm fond of you and I'd like to be sure, before I die, that I helped a man and not a fire."

I could have told him. Sitting there in the dark with the one man alive who came closest - I could have said: I'm from a place where the staircase is built, all of it, up past the moon, and I saw it, and I fell off it into your stubble field, and every gift and every tube and every loose number is me trying to lay one more step under your people's feet because I cannot get home and the only thing worse than being stuck at the bottom of the staircase is being the only one who knows the staircase is there. I could have said it. He would not have believed the from-a-place part. He would have believed the rest.

"I'm not for anything," I said. "A guy who got lucky with a map and never stopped running."

Heras regarded me for a long moment with those clean old eyes that had punctured my germs and my Thule and my every comfortable lie for twelve years, and he did not puncture this one, which was how I knew he'd heard the size of it. "All right," he said, and drank. "A guy who got lucky."

He left when the wine was gone. He patted the bench twice when he stood, the wood not me, told me to drink less of his country's wine and more of my own boiled water, and went up the wharf into the torchlight, an old man walking carefully on stones.

I went back to the workbench, where the Volta pile was still set out from that morning: the copper discs, the zinc strips, the shallow bowls of vinegar. I'd touched the wire to my own tongue twice to verify the current was real - the small sharp bite of it, a live thing with no body - but I had not shown Heras. I wanted one clean demonstration, not a fumbled one. So I ran back up the wharf and caught him at the first corner.

He came without complaint, in the easy manner of old men who have stopped arguing with unlikely requests. He stood over the bench while I held the two ends of the wire to the tip of my tongue.

"A current," I said. "From the metals and the vinegar. No flame. No bellows."

He looked at the pile of discs, then at me, with the expression he used for things that were too small to be interesting until they were. He reached out and touched the wire ends himself, just for a moment, face unchanged. "Again," he said, so I held the ends to his fingertips this time. He felt it - I could see it in the slight tightening at his eyes. He pulled his hand back and looked at the pile.

"You've made a current," he said. "Without fire."

When I confirmed it, he was quiet, looking at the copper and zinc the way he looked at a liver that was showing him something he hadn't expected. Then: "And what does it do."

I brought out the compass needle on its pivot - a magnetized needle mounted so it could turn freely - and held it near the wire while the current ran. The needle swung. Forty degrees and held there, trembling, while the current flowed. When I pulled the wire away it swung back.

"Every time?" he asked.

Every time.

He was quiet for a long moment. "I don't know what this is," he said, which from Heras was the nearest thing to reverence you were going to get. "I know you're going to tell me it's something."

"Not tonight," I said. "Tonight it's just a needle that moves."

He looked at it one more time, then walked back up the wharf more carefully than before in the dark. I stayed at the bench after he was gone and wrote by lamplight: *first demonstration to Heras. compass needle deflects forty degrees at one palm-length from the wire. held for thirty breaths before the vinegar weakened. he asked what it does. I said: tonight, just a needle that moves.* Then I added the Statius tablets to the entry they belonged in, cross-referenced against Hermes's forge data and my own, and put the lamp out.

A few nights after that I stayed out late, until Pamphilus's wife had banked the kitchen fire and the only light was the watchman's brazier at the gate and the city's dirty glow over the rooftops. I could see a long way from the roof of the shed. The press would run tomorrow, the figures would keep spreading, the notes were building in secret rung by rung in a script only one freedwoman could read. A wife down the hall, a child coming, freed people who chose to stay, money I couldn't feel, the emperor's name laid over my own, and a city drawing my dreams in the dust without knowing they were dreams or whose. All of it laid out below me like the lamps of the wharf.

High ground has a military logic to it.
