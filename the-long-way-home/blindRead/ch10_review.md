# Chapter 10: "The Workshop" — Review

## Summary
Daniel takes possession of the yard. Pamphilus arrives wearing a collar Daniel can't remove. Tyche reveals she absorbed place-value and long division from one viewing — Daniel immediately hides her capability ("Good. Do the next one alone") to protect her from being sold. Macer's accountant Onesimus resists the new figures (faster with his abacus after 40 years; the young ones adopt it). The printing press: first carved block, Homer, genuinely ugly, but reproducible. Daniel recognizes saltpeter in a list — burns the list ("Not yet. Maybe not ever"). Water-wheel bellows for the forge: Heras warns about the aqueduct being the emperor's. A salon at Mettius's house: Lucius Vettius Crispus dismantles Daniel in front of Rome's intellectuals using Pytheas, Strabo, and wit. Daniel wilts. Comes home and cuts a second printing block.

---

## The Gunpowder Scene: The Book's Most Important Moral Beat
This is the most significant moment in the book so far and it's given the fewest words. Daniel recognizes saltpeter. He has all three components. He writes the list. He burns it. "Not yet. That was what I filed it under. I knew what those three things became, put together in the right proportions. I knew what that meant. Not yet, maybe not ever."

Three sentences. No drama. No speech about responsibility. No agonized choice. Just: list, fire, back to drawing seam-angles.

This is the right way to handle it. Gunpowder in 98 AD would not be "a thing that flies." It would be a thing that kills. Daniel knows this. His burning the list is not virtue signaling — it's a strategic decision: he needs demonstrations people can't dismiss as threats. A cannon in Rome in 99 AD would get him executed, not celebrated. The ABOUT.md says he eventually "bursts a cannon on purpose to argue for a steel he cannot make." That's the controlled deployment: not a weapon, a demonstration of a concept. This scene is setting up that future choice from the opposite direction — he knows what it becomes, and he chooses to wait.

## Tyche: The Most Important Character Development in the Book
One demonstration of place value. One viewing of long division done wrong and right. She does it herself, correctly, the next day, with a remainder set off with the *r* he showed her once. The long division that humiliated Daniel in front of Heras in chapter 4.

"A quick mind in a body the law called property. The value I could put on her by teaching her was a price somebody could pay."

Daniel's response is the correct moral one given the constraints: hide her capability, let her learn in the dark, say as little as possible about what she's doing. "Good. Do the next one alone." The restraint of that line is everything.

They are now running a parallel deception of Macer's system: Daniel pretending to be less than he is (the Thule cover story), and Tyche pretending to be less capable than she is (the keep-her-head-down survival strategy). Both concealing from the same ledger.

## The Printing Press: Honest Progress
"It is genuinely ugly. I kept it."

The first print — smear, then better, then legible but crooked, doubled letter, skewed line. This is right. The insight that matters isn't quality; it's reproducibility: "the same error every time, which a copyist cannot do, which means you can fix the error in the block and then it is fixed forever." Heras's verdict is correct and fair: "Make it not ugly, and you will have done something, and not before."

The press will take years. This is the right framing. The book is avoiding the "genius solves it first try" pattern consistently.

---

## Onesimus: Correct Technology Adoption Dynamics
Onesimus is faster with his abacus after 40 years. He's right. Daniel can't beat him on speed. But the young accountant sees what Onesimus doesn't: "a number you can audit." The spreadsheet vs. the abacus — one leaves a trail, one doesn't. The adoption starts at the bottom with the juniors, who get trusted with the checking, who use the new method because they want to be trusted. Onesimus dies keeping his board, training two more boys on it out of spite.

This is exactly how technological transition works in large institutions. The author understands this.

## Crispus: The First Real Defeat
Lucius Vettius Crispus deploys the Pytheas argument against Daniel with precision:
- Pytheas claimed Thule (Strabo called him a liar)
- Daniel claims to be from Thule
- Thule is conveniently unreachable
- The balloon "caught fire and fell on a washerwoman"

The argument is internally coherent and rhetorically devastating. Daniel has no comeback because his Latin fails him "at exactly the speed he needed it." He is right, and he loses anyway, because being right requires language to deploy it.

"He'd come in ready to be threatened by Macer's famous foreigner and found a tongue-tied boy in a too-short tunic." This is a precise account of what it feels like to be outgunned socially in a language you don't fully own.

Crispus's attack is legitimate. Daniel's Thule story IS exactly what Crispus describes — a country no one can reach or check. The critique is correct. The story is a lie. Daniel has no high ground here.

The ending: Tyche at the gate, lamp lit, "Bad?" — Daniel doesn't answer. He gets the ugly Homer block and starts cutting a second one. He can't out-argue Crispus in a salon. He can build something Crispus can't. The block as response to rhetoric is the whole book in one gesture.

## Research Check
- **Pytheas and Strabo**: Historically accurate and specific. Pytheas of Massalia (~325 BC) claimed to have sailed to Thule. Strabo (64 BC–24 AD) was explicitly skeptical, calling Pytheas a liar. The "sea-lung" (thalattia pneumon — the jelly-like mixture of sea, land, and air) is real from Pytheas's account. Crispus quoting this is correctly researched.
- **Roman aqueducts as imperial property**: Accurate. The water supply belonged to the emperor and tapping it required permission. Heras's warning is historically appropriate.
- **Onesimus and the abacus**: The Roman abacus was a real instrument. Freedmen accountants using it for decades is accurate.
- **Moldboard plow**: Known in China; not in Roman Europe. Daniel introducing it is another historical transplant.
- **Water mill for forge bellows**: Romans had water mills. Using one to power bellows was technically feasible. This is a plausible innovation for the period.
- **Woodblock printing**: Han Dynasty China (100 BC–220 AD) roughly contemporaneous with Daniel's situation. Europe: much later. Accurate as a Roman "first."

**Research flag**: The "gunpowder proportions" that Daniel implies he knows — the historical proportions are approximately 75% saltpeter, 15% charcoal, 10% sulfur. Does Daniel know this? He doesn't say. He only says he knows what the three things "become." The proportions are harder to know from memory than the ingredients. A real person might know "sulfur, charcoal, saltpeter" without knowing the exact mix. This is plausible.

## The Pamphilus Problem
Daniel can't remove the collar. He can say "sit here" and "you eat when I eat." This is the correct response: not grand resistance, small humanizing actions within the constraint. The book is not pretending Daniel can fix systemic slavery. He can only make small choices about how he treats the person in front of him.

"I had asked for free hands." He'd said this to Macer. Macer sent him a slave to make a point. The point: free hands cost wages; slaves cost nothing. The economics of the institution. Daniel can see it clearly and can't change it.
