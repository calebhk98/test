# Chapter 14: "What It Costs" — Review

## Summary
The First Dacian War ends not with triumph but with treaty — Decebalus keeps his head and hills, gives back Roman deserters and standards, calls himself Rome's friend. Nobody cheers. Daniel crates the tethered balloon (approved) and buries the manned-flight plans under it (to stay unfound). Naso watches; Daniel arranges for Naso to come south with the baggage. Celer at the horse-lines, both men looking at horses, not each other, drinking bad wine: he left Daniel's name out of "the part that mattered" in the legate's report. "Build me something that doesn't need a man hung off it. Or build me nothing. Either one." On the road south, Daniel makes a two-column list: press, numerals, filter, rag paper on one side; the powder on the other, filed under "not yet, maybe not ever." Rome from the haze — he cries, a few ugly tears, before Pamphilus sees. Heras at the wharf yard waiting on an upturned crate with a scroll he wasn't reading. Doesn't ask about Sabinus. Takes Daniel to the bath. Keeps the letter's passage about the flux and the water. Tyche held the yard for two years — cloth stacked, columns balanced in the new figures, her small fierce hand. Daniel starts drawing a slow sand filter, gets the layers wrong twice, starts again. Tyche brings bread.

---

## The Celer Scene at the Horse-Lines
Neither man faces the other. They look at the horses. Celer doesn't offer the wineskin until he's said the important thing.

"I wrote the letter. The legate has it. Can't be done from that side." — He protected Daniel in the report. Left his name out of "the part that mattered." A soldier's death; soldiers die. He didn't have to do this. He did it anyway.

"Build me something that doesn't need a man hung off it. Or build me nothing. Either one." — This is Celer at his most human. He's telling Daniel he doesn't have to build weapons. The army doesn't need him to. He's giving Daniel a choice that, technically, Daniel doesn't have as property — but Celer is offering it anyway, from the horse-line, drinking bad wine, not looking at him.

"We did not say his name, Sabinus, we did not say it, and the not-saying of it was the realest thing that passed between us." Perfect.

---

## The Two-Column List: The Book's Clearest Statement of Daniel's Ethics
Press, numerals, filter, rag paper on one side.
The powder on the other.

He's not committing to never building the powder. He's committing to an order of priority: things that keep men out of the dirt first. Things that put them in it, last or never. This is not a moral stance he announces to anyone — it's a private sorting, made on a road south while the flux kills men two fires over. He starts "making the list in his head, testing each item for what column it belonged in, the way you sort grain from chaff without quite deciding to."

That last phrase is important: "without quite deciding to." It's not a deliberate ethical framework. It's an instinct becoming a commitment by degrees.

## Heras: The Definition of a Good Friend
"You've gotten thin. And you stink. The frontier agrees with no one."

He doesn't ask about Sabinus. He doesn't offer the consolations Daniel knows are true and useless. He takes Daniel to the bath, talks about Epicurus. He had kept the letter passage about the water and the flux — quietly planning the next step while Daniel was still on the Danube.

"He set the man down between us by naming the water, and we both knew it, and we left him there, present and unspoken."

This is the most precise sentence in the book about grief. Heras places Sabinus in the room without naming him, by naming what Daniel came back wanting to build. They both know this is acknowledgment and forward motion simultaneously. They leave him there — present, unspoken — and go wash the frontier off.

A lesser writer would have Heras say the right thing. This author has Heras say a different right thing — the next step — and lets the rest be silence.

---

## Tyche: The Yard Held
The tally on the wall in the new figures. Columns that balanced for two years with nobody checking her and Onesimus probably sneering. Cloth stacked. The yard Tyche's now "in the clean sense of a place run by whoever didn't leave."

She reads Daniel's mood at the door, skips the consolations. Says "you ate on the road" like an accusation. Brings bread without being asked when he starts drawing.

She's 17 now. She was 14 when she first appeared. The two years are visible in her — still the slave's quick read of a man's mood before committing to a word, but the yard behind her is proof of something those two years built.

The author doesn't have a scene where Daniel acknowledges what she's done. He just sees it — the wall of figures — and the seeing is the acknowledgment.

## The Slow Sand Filter: The New Direction
Daniel gets the layers wrong twice. He knows sand doesn't sterilize — you still have to boil. But the filter addresses the real barrier: "a man who would drink it was a man you could keep out of the dirt." The flux kills men because they won't drink boiled water (it tastes of nothing) and they drink from wherever the army stops. If he can make the water look and taste clean, they'll drink it.

This is the same pivot as the germ theory/cleanliness move in chapter 5: he can't sell the truth (boil the water because of invisible creatures). He can sell the visible result (clear water tastes better). The filter is his tool for selling the visible result.

He gets the layers wrong twice and starts again. That's the right kind of failure.

---

## The War Ending: Historical Realism
"They're saying we winter south of the river." Not: "we won." Saturninus, who buried too many to the flux to think in those terms. The men receiving the news with suspicion, having been told three times they were going home and not going home. "The third time a man threw his cup at the tent pole and got flogged for it."

This is the honest version of a war ending — not triumph, not ceremony, just the machine slowing and turning south, and men going home the same way they came, with the flux still with them.

## Research Check
- **First Dacian War peace terms (102 AD)**: Accurate. Decebalus was allowed to remain as a client king, returned Roman deserters and captured engineers, acknowledged Roman suzerainty. This was widely understood as temporary (Trajan fought the Second Dacian War 105-106 AD). The soldiers' skepticism is historically appropriate.
- **Slow sand filter**: Real water filtration technology. The principle was used in early forms throughout history; formalized in the 19th century (Scotland, 1804, John Gibb). The layers (gravel, sand, finer sand — Daniel keeps getting the order wrong) are correct. The filter doesn't sterilize — it removes turbidity and some pathogens through mechanical filtration and biological action. Daniel's note that "sand does not sterilize" and that boiling is still needed is medically correct.
- **The flux (dysentery) on the road south**: Historically documented. Disease followed armies on both legs of the march. Accurate.

## Hidden Message Pattern
Tyche's held yard is the pattern inverted: her competence is now being stated by Daniel rather than hidden. "She had held my whole small enterprise together by sheer stubborn competence." But she still operates within the slave's posture — the "watching look, the slave's quick read of a man's mood before she committed to a single word." The capability is visible; the posture it operates from is still suppressed.

---

## Naso's Resolution
Daniel arranges for Naso to come south with the baggage. A word to Saturninus, carried to whoever has the tablet. "A man with one good hand can still mind mules." Small, concrete, within his power. He can't fix the hand. He can make sure the man has work that earns him a place.

This is the same pattern as the collar: he can't remove it, but he can put bread on the bench. He can't undo the hand, but he can make sure there's work.

---

## What the Author Is Doing
The chapter is the pivot between the balloon arc and the water arc. Daniel commits to a direction: things that keep men out of the dirt. He files the powder. He starts the filter. He comes home to Heras and Tyche, who have been holding his world together.

The chapter's title is "What It Costs." The costs are specific and carried: Sabinus's wife and children, Naso's hand, the manned-flight plans buried under a crate lid, the two columns of things that feed men and things that kill them.

## Overall: A Chapter That Earns Its Quiet
No dramatic revelations, no new inventions, no confrontations. The whole chapter is aftermath. And it's some of the best writing in the book. The horse-line scene and the Heras bath scene are the two best quiet moments. Tyche's wall of figures is the right way to show what two years look like. The filter is the right next step.
