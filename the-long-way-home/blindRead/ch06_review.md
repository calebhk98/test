# Chapter 6: "Cheap, Mine, Undeniable" — Review

## Summary
Daniel attempts to build a hot-air balloon across three iterations: (1) linen scraps — too heavy, never lifts; (2) worn-thin rag — flies as a toy on a string but leaks all heat; (3) Heras-funded greased linen — fills, swells, then the grease burns and the bag collapses in a stinking fire, burning Daniel's arm. He loses the yard. He loses Heras's linen. A fourth compromise attempt (lighter seal, lower fire) flies for ten breaths before the seam rips. Chapter ends with Daniel kneeling in the slaughter-lot, hand against the still-warm cloth, feeling the heat leave.

---

## What the Chapter Is Doing Right
This is the book's first sustained failure arc, and it's executed with real honesty. Four attempts, four partial results, no triumph. The "cheap, mine, undeniable" thesis from chapter 5 is immediately tested against reality: the fire isn't really his, the cloth isn't really his, and the demonstrations are still private (a yard, a slaughter-lot, a ten-year-old holding a string).

The engineering friction is real and specific: you need light cloth (won't fly if too heavy) and you need sealed cloth (won't hold heat if porous). These requirements are genuinely contradictory with available period materials. The Montgolfier brothers in 1783 used taffeta and paper — the author has correctly identified why linen doesn't work well (it's too heavy). Daniel doesn't know what the right material is. He only knows what he wants the material to do. This is the "useful incomplete knowledge" thesis applied to engineering.

The financial bootstrap problem is stated clearly: the balloon is supposed to earn him the money to build the balloon. The loop closes clean as a noose. This is a real structural problem for someone in his position, and the author doesn't hand him a solution.

---

## The Best Line in the Book: Heras
"He said, in the dry voice, that the air had carried the fire very well."

That's it. That's the perfect response. Heras has watched his linen get set on fire, his prediction proven correct, and the only thing he says is a technically accurate, completely understated observation. This line captures an entire personality.

---

## The Ending
"I knelt down and put my hand against the cloth to feel the heat go."

No words. No summary. No Daniel-reflecting-on-what-this-means. Just the physical sensation of failure. This is the best chapter ending in the book so far — it resists the urge to tell the reader what to feel. The heat going out of the torn balloon is all you need.

This is a significant improvement over the previous "close on stillness" pattern (which always resolved to a composed resignation). This ending is still still, but it's *grieving* still, not *resigned* still.

---

## The Bootstrap Problem: The Book's Real Conflict
The balloon chapter reveals what the book's structural problem actually is. Daniel isn't stopped by knowledge gaps (he knows hot air rises). He's stopped by resources. He has no money, no freedom, no property. He can't buy cloth. He can't run a sustained fire. He needs an arm's length of good linen and it takes a month of begging to get one.

This is more interesting than "Daniel doesn't know how to do X." It's "Daniel knows what to do but is owned." The obstacle is social/economic, not intellectual. This is historically accurate to what actually limits change: not the idea, but the capital.

---

## Hidden Message Pattern
Continues but deepens. The balloon arc shows Daniel doing something that actually *works* (the toy flew, the fourth version flew for ten breaths) while everything else fails. His knowledge is real. The obstacles are material, not cognitive. The pattern is now operating at two levels:

1. **Competence hidden behind self-deprecation** (earlier chapters): "a B-minus in art class" → draws a map that changes a room
2. **Real competence blocked by material lack** (this chapter): knows the physics, can't execute because he's effectively a slave with no capital

This second level is more interesting because it's not about perception. Daniel doesn't underestimate himself here. He accurately identifies the problem. The obstruction is real.

## Stichus: Growing
The water-boy who doesn't like Daniel has now:
- Brought him rag scraps (for a promised "thing the gods do")
- Held the string on three balloon attempts
- Screamed the right scream ("good scream, the gods-do-a-thing scream")

He's becoming something like an apprentice, though neither of them would use that word. He's still "the going opinion" about Daniel's sanity, but he keeps showing up. Good slow character development.

## Research Check
- **Balloon materials**: Historically accurate problem. The Montgolfiers used linen + taffeta + paper; silk and later rubberized fabrics. Plain linen is too heavy. The author knows this.
- **Sealing with grease**: The greasing-to-prevent-heat-leakage is a reasonable improvisation, but historically early balloonists used different approaches (alum solution, varnish). The grease catching fire is a realistic consequence.
- **Fire in Roman cities**: Correctly noted — Rome was terrified of fire. The city burned regularly (the Great Fire of 64 AD was living memory in 98 AD). The kitchen woman's reaction is historically appropriate.
- **Burn treatment**: Daniel treating his own burn with boiled water is consistent with his established practices.

## What the Author Is Doing
The chapter title names the thesis that's being dismantled: "Cheap, Mine, Undeniable" — none of those three things are fully true yet. The fire isn't cheap (it cost Heras's linen and Daniel's arm). The balloon isn't his (everything is borrowed). And it's not yet undeniable (still private, still a string and a slaughter-lot). The title is ironic.

The author is being careful not to give Daniel a triumph yet. The balloon is real but not enough. The money problem has no solution yet. The world is still very small.
