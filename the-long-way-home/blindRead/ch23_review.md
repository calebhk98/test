# Chapter 23: "The Things That Don't Work" — Review

## Summary
Three failures. The clock: Daniel has money and Hermes's gears but can't remember how an escapement works. He can picture the anchor-shaped device from a documentary — "I could see it and I could not build it, because seeing a thing swing is not knowing how its faces are angled." He builds a water clock instead. Gorgeous, on the wall of the tally-room, the painted pointer going round. Doesn't keep time. Wanders "a fistful of marks off the sun." Heras: "It is very beautiful" — the same words he used for the first balloon that wouldn't lift. The glass: Three years of the contest created a demand for telescopes. A boy wrote a story where Daniel builds the glass to show the invisible ones. He can't. Roman glass is full of striae and bubbles. Demetrios the glass-cutter grinds by eye; twelve blanks produce two usable discs with unfixable ghosts. The tube: the man on the far bank is two times closer, also dim and wearing a rainbow. Marcia sells ten to rich men who want the newest toy even if it's bad. "A bad toy that no one else has is still the only one." The boy who wrote the story will wait until he dies. The rails: the demonstration is perfect. Four men walk a block down the slope that took twenty-six. Maximus sees it work, calls it clever, sends them home. The arithmetic Marcia does: he owns the men. The slaves are a sunk cost, not a running cost. Rails cost iron and silver. A back costs less than a wheel. "It lost, fair and square, in the open court of arithmetic." Side threads: Subura fire (fourteen dead, Daniel goes to the aedile, the aedile asks who owns the buildings — senators and an equestrian — rolls the tablet back); the voltaic pile (twenty copper/zinc disc pairs, cloth soaked in brine, a jolt that puts stars in Daniel's eyes, a wire wound around a nail that lifts iron filings — "it has become a lodestone; yes, and it stops when I lift the wire"); the compass written in the cipher for whoever comes after to navigate by. Ending: Tyche working on English letters, Daniel writing about rails in the small flat hand for the books, the flange drawn twice before the lip is on the right side, men outside hauling the old way. "I wrote it down."

## "You Don't Know How It Argues": The Escapement Problem

The clock section is the book's most precise statement of the difference between knowing something conceptually and knowing it mechanically.

"I, the man from the land of clocks, the wizard who drew the whole world and made fire fly, could not describe to an honest smith the one small bronze argument at the center of every mechanical clock a clockmaker would ever have to build."

He can see the documentary. He can see the man's hands, the brass swinging, the anchor-shaped bit rocking on a pin. He cannot build it because he cannot reconstruct the angles from having watched them. "Seeing a thing swing is not knowing how its faces are angled, how deep the points bite, how the rocking gets fed by the wheel it's holding back. The angles were the secret and the angles had never gone into my head. I had watched it pleasantly, learning nothing."

This is the most specific and most honest account in the book of the limits of Daniel's knowledge. Every other failure has been about resources or materials or political economy. This one is about attention. He saw the escapement a hundred times and learned nothing about it because he wasn't paying attention. The page was torn out before he arrived, and he tore it out himself, watching pleasantly.

Hermes, watching him fail: "It wants to go or it wants to stop. You are asking it to do both." / "What does it look like, in your country, the part that quarrels?" And Daniel opens his mouth and has nothing.

"A specific flavor of shame, knowing a true thing and being unable to use it. When you don't know, you can go find out. There was no finding out here."

The water clock on the wall is the right resolution. Tyche corrects the pointer twice a day with her thumb. The man from the land of clocks can make water crawl around a circle and cannot make the wheels argue properly.

## The Glass: Chromatic Aberration and the Limits of Roman Glass

"Every bubble bends the light its own wrong way and every thread smears it, and a lens is nothing but a thing for bending light all one right way at once."

The striae (threads of varying glass composition) and bubbles are correctly identified as the problem. The chromatic aberration (the rainbow edge) is correctly described — when light passes through an imperfect lens with varying refractive index, different wavelengths bend different amounts, producing colored fringes. The man on the far bank "wore a rainbow."

The moral weight: he told everyone about the invisible ones for ten years. The contest produced a story where Daniel builds the glass to prove his own claim. He can't. The boy who wrote the story "will wait until he dies." 

"I told a true thing I cannot prove and dressed it up as a marvel I cannot make, and the dressing-up is the part I'll answer for."

This is the glass thread (open since ch05) coming to rest at an honest stopping point. Not abandoned — explicitly addressed: it doesn't work with Roman glass, it won't work until someone makes better glass, Daniel is not that person, and the story he told that he can't prove is a debt he carries.

Marcia's intervention is characteristically Marcia: she doesn't make him throw it out. She sells ten to rich men. "A bad toy that no one else has is still the only one." She doesn't pretend it's something it isn't; she finds what it actually is worth (status, novelty, the first in a category) and prices accordingly.

## The Rails: Technology vs. Slave Economy

The rails section is the book's most sustained economic argument, and the most structurally important.

The demonstration: four men walk a block down that took twenty-six. Perfect. "A true machine doing a true job in front of a man who can use it." Daniel lets himself have one clean breath of the feeling he almost never gets.

Then Marcia does the arithmetic: "You own the men, Maximus."

The full argument: Maximus bought his slaves years ago — a sunk cost, long since paid and forgotten. A slave who hauls stone costs a bowl of grain and a wall. If one dies under a block, there are more off the boats from Dacia, cheaper this year because Rome just dragged a whole nation south in chains. The slaves are a cost he has already paid, will pay again in bodies, not coin. The rails are a new cost in iron and silver he has now, to replace labor he already owns and doesn't have to buy twice.

Maximus sees it work. He calls it clever. He sends them home.

"My rails were not [free to him]. [...] A back costs less than a wheel."

Then the crystalline moral observation: "I have built things that hurt people. I put a man up in a balloon and watched him burn out of the sky. I made a powder that took Geta's hand and his eye in a shed I built. Those are heavy and I carry them. The rails were a different weight, and lighter, and somehow they have worn a deeper groove. Nobody died. Nothing burned. I built a clean, true, simple machine that took the killing labor off men's backs, and it lost, fair and square, in the open court of arithmetic, to the plain fact that a man already owned the backs and a back costs less than a wheel."

The rails are worse than the balloon and the powder because they are entirely morally clean — no death, no fire, no crossed line — and they failed because of the economic structure of the world Daniel cannot change. The balloon and the powder carry weight he holds as guilt. The rails carry weight he holds as defeat.

This is the book's most important historical-economic argument: labor-saving technology is only economically rational in an economy where labor has a marginal cost. In a slave economy, bought labor is a sunk cost. The rail is built for a world that doesn't exist yet.

## The Electromagnet: The Most Important Side Thread

The voltaic pile section is brief — three paragraphs — and contains what may be the most consequential thing Daniel builds in this chapter.

Twenty copper and zinc disc pairs, cloth soaked in brine, pressed together in a tower. A wire touching his tongue produces a jolt. A wire wound around an iron nail: the nail lifts iron filings off the bench.

"It has become a lodestone."
"Yes. And it stops when I lift the wire."
Heras: "It has very little obvious use." Then goes to think about it for a month.

The electromagnet — a wire carrying current producing a magnetic field — is the key component for electric motors, electric generators, and the telegraph. Daniel writes it in the cipher for whoever comes after. He doesn't name what it will be for. He knows he's at the bottom rung of a ladder that goes somewhere he can see in outline but cannot build toward. The compass is written in the cipher alongside it — a needle that points north, for navigating when there's no shore.

The deliberate restraint: "He did not ask me what I was going to do with it." Neither does the text. The electromagnet sits in the cipher, next to the compass, next to the steam pump, next to the boring prize, next to the Wimshurst machine sketch. A cluster of things that will eventually converge into a technology neither Daniel nor anyone in this century can reach. He is writing it for people not yet born.

## The Subura Fire: The Aedile's Face

"I would be very interested if any of those men were interested."

The flat voice of a man who gives the bad news often enough that it no longer costs him anything. The buildings are owned by senators and an equestrian. The fire code requires them to spend money making their own property less valuable in the short term. The aedile cannot compel them. He rolls the tablet back.

Daniel writes the fire prevention notes and gives them to Tyche to copy into the files. "Which was what I could do."

This is the smallest version of the rails problem: useful information that cannot be deployed because the people with the authority to act on it have no incentive to do so. No drama, no confrontation. Just the flat face and the rolled tablet, and Daniel putting the notes in the files where they'll wait.

## Research Check
- **The verge escapement**: The verge-and-foliot escapement was the first successful mechanical clock escapement, dating from the 13th century AD. Daniel cannot remember how it works because he watched it in a documentary and didn't absorb the angles. The description — "an anchor-shaped bit rocking on a pin, two little points dipping into a toothed wheel" — is a reasonable description of a verge escapement's visual behavior. The anchor escapement he's describing is actually closer to the lever escapement (17th century), but the visual impression of an anchor shape on a rocking pivot is consistent. Either way, not knowing the angles is the problem.
- **Roman water clocks (clepsydra)**: Accurately described. The variable-flow problem (a full vessel pushes harder than an empty one) was well-known to the Romans; they had solutions of varying sophistication. The constant-head design (overflow vessel maintaining fixed pressure) is the correct engineering fix. Daniel's float-and-cord-and-drum mechanism for displaying the hour is plausible Roman engineering.
- **Chromatic aberration**: The rainbow fringing of early lenses is real — caused by different wavelengths of light refracting at different angles (dispersion). This is why Newton used a reflecting telescope rather than a refracting one. The striae and bubbles in Roman glass are historically accurate. The combination makes a useful optical telescope essentially impossible with period materials.
- **The galvanic pile / voltaic pile**: Alessandro Volta's battery (1800) was exactly this: alternating zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth. The shock (Daniel's stars), the electromagnetic effect (the nail/lodestone), and the correct observation that it "stops when I lift the wire" are all accurate. Daniel is about 1,700 years early.
- **The electromagnet**: Hans Christian Ørsted discovered electromagnetism in 1820; Faraday and Henry built working electromagnets. Daniel's wire-around-nail construction is correct. The key insight — that the magnetism stops when the current stops — is the crucial distinction from permanent magnets.
- **Railway economics in a slave economy**: The economic argument is historically accurate. One of the reasons ancient civilizations with slave labor didn't develop labor-saving machinery at scale is exactly this: when labor is free (sunk cost), reducing labor costs is not economically rational. The historical parallel to Roman slave-based economics and why they didn't produce the Industrial Revolution is real and well-documented.

## Hidden Message Pattern

The water clock and the spyglass sit side by side in the tally-room, "the two prettiest things I have made that don't do what I said they would." Both visible failures, displayed. Daniel is not hiding these. He's put them on the wall.

The hidden message of the chapter is in the ending: the flange drawn twice before getting the lip on the right side, then written down in the small flat hand for the books. The rails failed economically. The knowledge of how they work doesn't fail — it goes into the books. Everything that fails in this chapter goes into the cipher or the files or the books. The failures are the message: this is what the next person needs to know to get further.

## Predictions Updated
- **The escapement**: Eventually someone will solve it. The boring prize winner (who masters cylinder tolerance) may also have the metalwork skill to solve the escapement's angles. Or Felix. Or an unknown craftsman. The clock is still open.
- **The glass arc**: The chromatic aberration problem and the glass quality problem are now both named and addressed. The solution (better glass, and later: achromatic doublet lenses) is centuries away. The book has closed this thread honestly.
- **The electromagnet**: In the cipher. This is the most forward-pointing object in the chapter. The path from electromagnet to telegraph to radio is decades of work — but the first rung is in the cipher now.
- **The compass**: Also in the cipher. Future navigators will use it.
- **The rails**: Won't work in a slave economy. But the knowledge is in the books. If Rome ever shifts toward wage labor (it won't, in Daniel's lifetime), the rails become useful.
- **Felix**: Still working on the pot. The steam pump is running (barely). The boring prize is out. The convergence is getting close.
- **Heras**: The electromagnet makes him think for a month. Still here. Still the witness.

## Overall: The Book's Most Technically Honest Chapter

Three failures: one because Daniel's memory failed him (clock), one because the materials don't exist yet (glass), one because the economic structure of the world makes the right machine irrelevant (rails). All three are honest accounts of real categories of technological failure. The chapter doesn't try to make any of them more comfortable than they are.

The rails section is the book's most important historical argument. A technology that would be world-changing in a wage-labor economy is useless in a slave economy, not because it doesn't work but because it doesn't need to work — the backs are already bought and paid for. Daniel has built the future and the present has no place for it, in the same open court of arithmetic where he balanced the grain column and the looted gold column, the same marks, the same hand.

"I wrote it down" is the chapter's last sentence. It's everything.
