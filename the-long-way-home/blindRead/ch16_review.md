# Chapter 16: "The New Figures" — Review

## Summary
Summer, Daniel somewhere around 22-23. The numerals have escaped the yard — found on a fish-seller's slate, spread downward through the commercial class by utility (adding a column of figures vs. the ordeal of Roman numerals with counters and a board). "Shopkeeper's marks" — Onesimus's contempt became their name and their truth. The press is operational: Eros the freedman letterer, rag paper, a pressman borrowed from an oil house. First external sale — fifty copies of a times-table sheet to a Subura schoolmaster, two asses each. Heras comes to inspect the press, delivers: "You're proposing to drown that. To make the king's wealth common as fish." Daniel reveals heliocentrism; Heras constructs the cover story ("if someone asks, you learned it from Aristarchus"). Tyche at 18-19 delivers the chapter's central line: "You're printing the water-carrying. Who prints the sea?" — sparking the idea of an annual prize contest for imagining machines that don't exist yet. Tyche's immediate deflation: "You'd need to be free and respectable and rich, and you're none of those." Heras: "You want to make men homesick for a country that doesn't exist yet." In the back corner of the yard: a wax cylinder phonograph attempt (doesn't work, posts a materials prize); a camera obscura (pinhole box projecting the street inverted onto oiled linen — Tyche looks at it, goes back to the tally). Chess renamed for Rome, spread to the neighborhood tavern. Pasta arrangement with the tavern owner Gnaeus. The Crispus confrontation at the salon — Crispus builds an impiety case from true facts (goēs, then the graver word unnamed); Daniel's counter: asks for water, drinks it, names the dead children from the flux, invites anyone with a household to the barrel. Not a win but a different kind of result: "you cannot beat that with Strabo." Vibenius present and watching, says nothing, leaves. Macer's warning: "Get the barrels into a senator's house before that crow makes up his mind." Back in the yard, Daniel sits with two boards: the prize contest notes and the Wimshurst machine sketch (counter-rotating glass discs, the direction is wireless signal transmission, "a bottom rung is just a bottom rung until the ladder has a top"). Tyche writes "when" on the contest board and goes to bed.

---

## The Numerals: The Book's Clearest Model of How Innovation Actually Spreads

"It had nothing to do with anyone admiring me. Heras had it right years ago when I tried to sell the figures as a wonder and he laughed at me. Nobody adopts a wonder. They adopt a thing that saves them an afternoon."

The chain: Tyche's wax tablet → Macer's younger clerks doing it on the sly → a grain factor → another factor → a fish-seller's slate by the Aemilian bridge. Nobody is teaching them as a wonder. They spread because they save an afternoon and make cheating harder to hide.

"The merchants who took up the figures fastest were the suspicious ones, robbed by their own bookkeepers." This is the shrewdest observation about adoption in the book. The numerals spread fastest among people who already had a reason to want better visibility into their own accounts. The innovation solved a problem people already felt.

"Shopkeeper's marks" — Onesimus's contempt becomes the thing's identity and its strength. It doesn't reach the high places yet. Not on contracts, tombstones, milestones, anything a man wants to look dignified. But Rome ran on shopkeepers, and the shopkeepers were teaching their sons. This is how things actually spread — not from the top, but seeping down through utility into the places that can't say no to a saved afternoon.

## The Crispus Confrontation: The Book's Best Rhetorical Scene

Crispus builds his attack from true bricks: Daniel is stateless, from a country Strabo calls a fiction, his maps cannot be checked, his figures are taught to slaves and fishwives and no honest Roman elder can read the resulting accounts at a glance, his press cheapens letters, his fire came down on Roman roofs. He doesn't say magic. He says "the law knows a graver word" and then steps back from it.

"He had built the whole charge out of true bricks, and that is what makes a clever man dangerous, that he does not invent the bricks, he only chooses which ones to stack and in what order."

Daniel's counter: he asks for water. Drinks it. Slow Latin because slow is all he has and "slow at least sounds like a man choosing his words instead of a man drowning." Then names the flux. Invites anyone who's buried a slave or a child of the summer sickness to the barrel by the timber wharf. "Call me a charlatan when the children stop dying."

This is the only available counter to true bricks: not arguing the bricks but placing a true result next to them. Not Thule vs. Strabo (Crispus wins). Not Roman gods vs. foreign gods (the shortest road to the graver word). Instead: here is the water, here is what it does, here are your dead children in the scale.

"It wasn't a win." Crispus recovers, gets a smaller laugh, turns his shoulder. But the easy contempt is gone. "What replaced it was worse." Crispus leaves early, doesn't finish anyone's Callimachus. The scene is the most precise political writing in the book.

## Tyche: The Chapter's Moral Center

"Nobody learns to carry water and then dreams of the sea." / "You're printing the water-carrying. Who prints the sea?"

This is the sharpest insight in any chapter so far. It comes from Tyche, flat, while she's doing the sevens, without looking at Daniel. She has understood something Daniel hasn't fully articulated even to himself: the press is printing tools and grammar and already-existing knowledge. The wonder of imagining what doesn't exist yet is not on the sheets drying on the line. The press is a vehicle. She's asking what it should carry.

Then, immediately, the deflation: "You'd need to be free and respectable and rich." Delivered without unkindness, "which was worse than if she'd been cruel." She's not dismissing the idea. She's identifying the obstacle she personally cannot clear from this position. "It hasn't died, it has only gone somewhere to wait, in her as much as in me."

"When" — the single word she writes on the contest board before she goes to bed. Not whether. When.

Tyche is now the character doing the sharpest thinking in this book. She's 18-19, running a yard, hiding her capability, and when she speaks, it lands.

## The Back-Corner Inventory: Three Enormous Things in Two Pages

**The phonograph/gramophone sketch**: A stylus dragging a groove into a wax cylinder, recording vibration, a second stylus running back and vibrating in turn. The concept is correct — this is the core principle of Thomas Edison's phonograph (1877). It doesn't work — marks too crude, stylus too stiff, lead cylinder unevenly round. Daniel posts a materials prize for a smooth, uniform, formable sheet that holds a thin impressed line. He tells no one what it's for.

This is the author's most ambitious back-corner donation so far. Not a weapon, not a tool — the recording of sound. The principle is real. The materials problem (the wax cylinder, the precision of the groove) is real. That it doesn't work yet is honest. The materials prize is Daniel's indirect method — he doesn't know how to solve the materials problem directly, so he posts a prize and waits.

**The camera obscura**: Pinhole in one face of a box, screen of oiled linen at the opposite face. The street outside projected inverted and moving. Tyche looks at the cart-horses walking on their ears and goes back to the tally. This is the right response to a camera obscura — it is arresting and unclear. "I'm not entirely sure yet" is Daniel's honest answer. He doesn't know what it's for. The box stays in the corner.

Camera obscura is historically attested in ancient China and Greece as a phenomenon (pinhole projections), and systematically described by Alhazen in the 11th century. Daniel's version is not impossible but is ahead of any documented systematic use in Rome. It's in the corner, not deployed, which is the right staging.

**The Wimshurst/static machine**: Two counter-rotating glass discs, metal foil sectors, wire brushes pulling charge off the spinning surface. The direction stated explicitly: wireless signal transmission. Radio. Daniel doesn't have a plan, only "a direction, not a plan. Something at the end of that direction involved sending a signal through the air without a wire to carry it." The machine is the first rung of a ladder whose top he can't see yet.

The Wimshurst machine (1882) generates high-voltage static electricity. The path from there to radio (Hertz 1887, Marconi 1895) requires: understanding of electromagnetic waves (Maxwell 1865), the spark-gap transmitter, the coherer as receiver. Daniel is at the bottom rung, doesn't know where it goes. This is the right framing for a 1st-century attempt.

## The Heras Scenes: Two of the Chapter's Best

**The press inspection**: Heras picks up the sheet, holds it at arm's length, then close, turns it over to look at the ghost of the letters. "It's gray. And the line wanders." / "It's the same gray fifty times. That's the trick." He wipes his fingers on nothing. The Pergamon library speech — "A king's wealth, scrolls a man could not buy in a lifetime, kept behind a door, lent to scholars by favor." Looking at the fables drying on the line, "common as washing." "You're proposing to drown that." / "Yes." "Some men will hate you for it more than for the fire in the sky."

This is the book's clearest statement of what the press means and who it threatens. Heras is right about both the wonder and the danger.

**The heliocentrism conversation**: Daniel says "The old Samian was right. The sun stands still. The earth goes around it." Heras's physician's wariness — "the look he kept for remedies that were also poisons." The cover story: "If someone asks, you learned it from Aristarchus, not from wherever you actually learned it." And: "Don't say it again in a room with Crispus."

This is Heras doing the one thing Daniel can't do for himself — constructing the historical cover. Aristarchus of Samos did propose heliocentrism (~280 BC). The fact that it was rejected doesn't make it heretical in the way the Christian sense later would be — it makes it eccentric, Pythagorean, a known fringe view. "The opinion of no one in particular, held nowhere, under which no reputable man publicly shelters." A defensible position framed correctly. Heras gives Daniel the frame.

"Since I was twelve." — A small but significant line. Daniel has known since childhood. He's carried it as private knowledge for years. The heliocentrism filing from ch11 (cipher, "for me, not for them, useless until the machines need explaining") is now out of the cipher and in conversation with Heras.

## Vibenius: The Long Patience

The haruspex from ch08 — "something in the entrails that shouldn't be" — is back. He was present at the balloon demonstration when the fire came down. Now he's at the salon, uninvited or at least unannounced, taking a couch in the shadow by the door. He doesn't join the Crispus attack. He doesn't applaud the water defense. He watches with "the long patient look of a man adding a column he was in no hurry to total."

"He was the only man in Rome who could turn either of those into a charge the law would have to hear, or into a blessing that put the whole thing out of Crispus's reach forever."

This is the book's most sustained ambiguity about a character. Vibenius could destroy Daniel or protect him. The face tells nothing. He leaves without engaging. Macer's read: "The wrong man saw it." Get a senator's household free of the flux before that crow makes up his mind.

The framing is exact: Vibenius is a priest whose function is to read divine signs in biological matter. He has been reading Daniel since the balloon fire. The column in his head is not totaled yet. But it's being added.

## The Side Thread Inventory (New): Chess, Pasta, Cultural Diffusion

**Chess** (renamed for Rome: general, legate, cavalry, elephant, scout): The game — clearly chess — takes an evening to learn and "most of a lifetime to do anything with." It spreads from the workshop to the tavern. No dramatic scene, no revelation. Just a game introduced, adopted, diffused. The author is modeling cultural diffusion in microcosm — the way things spread without anyone intending to spread them.

**Pasta**: A long-dried noodle cooked in broth. Workshop food that spread to the tavern via the arrangement with Gnaeus. "He did not think of it as my food by the time it was on his counter. He thought of it as food." Daniel doesn't collect his small cut most of the time. What he collects instead is better: information from a man in the neighborhood who knows what he is and isn't afraid of it.

Both of these are small-scale models of the book's larger thesis about how things spread. Not through credit. Not through admiration. Through use.

## Research Check
- **Roman numerals as obstacle to calculation**: Accurate. The difficulty of arithmetic in Roman numerals (requiring a counting board, an abacus, or a specialist) is well-documented. Place-value arithmetic is significantly faster for columnar addition.
- **Heliocentrism / Aristarchus of Samos**: Historical, ~280 BC. He proposed that the Earth orbits the sun. His view was minority and his work mostly lost; only Archimedes's reference survives in detail. Heras's framing (eccentric, not heretical) is historically appropriate.
- **Camera obscura**: The phenomenon (light through a pinhole producing an inverted image) was noted by Mozi (~470-391 BC) and Aristotle. Alhazen's systematic treatment is 11th century. An educated person in Rome could know of the phenomenon. Daniel constructing a deliberate apparatus is slightly ahead of documented history but not impossible.
- **Phonograph principle**: Thomas Edison's phonograph (1877) uses exactly this principle. The physics is correct. The materials problem (groove precision, stylus behavior) is correctly identified as the obstacle.
- **Wimshurst machine / static electricity**: The Wimshurst machine (1882) generates high-voltage static by rotating glass discs. The components Daniel sketches (glass discs, metal foil sectors, wire brushes) are correct. The direction toward radio is historically real: high-voltage sparks → Hertz → Marconi.
- **Pasta in Rome**: Long noodles are documented in Roman-era contexts (lagana, from which lasagna derives). The "dried noodle cooked in broth" is plausible as an existing practice Daniel is popularizing, not inventing.
- **Wimshurst machine in 1st century**: The glass grinding required (true, smooth, uniform discs) would be a serious obstacle given Roman glass quality. Daniel posts a prize for the material first, which is the right move.

## AI/LLM Tells
- Three section breaks — within the established pattern
- The back-corner inventory (phonograph, camera obscura, Wimshurst machine) has the checklist quality: three significant items, each described in one or two paragraphs, in sequence. The author is clearly working from notes about what should appear in this chapter.
- But the Crispus scene is excellent and specific — the slow Latin ("slow at least sounds like a man choosing his words instead of a man drowning"), the water cup, the not-quite-steady hand, Crispus leaving early without finishing the Callimachus. These are human details.
- Tyche's "when" is the kind of single-word ending that requires knowing the scene so well you can trust the reader to feel it. That's not generated.
- The pasta/chess passages (cultural diffusion in microcosm) feel like conscious authorial technique, not generation.

## Hidden Message Pattern
Two versions in this chapter:
1. Tyche's arithmetic capability is still hidden from Macer — "a slave who can keep a great house's books is worth a small farm, and I could not stop it." The capability is now explicitly at risk if discovered. The hiding has become a protection, not just a habit.
2. Daniel's water defense at the salon — he downplays ("broken Latin," "I'm not going to lose to you in Greek") and then delivers a result Crispus cannot answer. Same structure as always: the downplay before the demonstration. Here the downplay is strategic, not instinctive. He's learned the technique.

## Predictions Updated
- **The Wimshurst machine**: This needs true glass discs. The glass challenge (lens, still open) and the glass needed for the static machine may converge. Better glass = both the lens and the electrostatic generator.
- **The phonograph materials prize**: Will appear in some later chapter as a strange commission a craftsman brings back.
- **The camera obscura**: Unclear what it's for. Possibly an intermediate step toward optics, possibly toward a projector or toward something connected to printing (precise reproduction of images). Its presence in the corner is deliberate.
- **Vibenius**: He heard both Crispus's attack and Daniel's counter. The column in his head is still being added. Macer's "get the barrels into a senator's house" is the actionable recommendation. Daniel has maybe weeks or months before Vibenius totals the column.
- **Tyche's "when"**: The prize contest is in her future as much as Daniel's. If the book ends with a freed slave's heir reading Daniel's record, the contest (imagining machines that don't exist yet) may be the seed.
- **The senator's household**: The next move is clear — get the water treatment into a home with political protection. This is Daniel's immediate task.
- **Crispus's next move**: "What replaced [the contempt] was worse." Crispus is now seriously engaged. He will not leave this as a rhetorical loss. The impiety case may move from accumulation to action.

---

## Overall: The Book's Most Densely Packed Chapter
Five major threads (numerals spread, press operational, Crispus salon, Vibenius watching, back-corner inventory) plus Tyche's insight and the Heras conversations. Structurally the busiest chapter in the book. It holds together because each thread is in conversation with the others: the numerals spread by utility → the press spreads letters by utility → the Crispus attack names that utility as a threat → Daniel's counter uses a different utility (clear water) → Vibenius watches the entire exchange → the back-corner sketches are the private laboratory underneath the public argument.

"You're printing the water-carrying. Who prints the sea?" — this is now the book's second defining line, after "A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day."
