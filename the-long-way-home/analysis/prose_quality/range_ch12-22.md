# Prose Quality Audit: ch12-ch22

Scope: chapters 12-22. Criteria from `/home/user/test/Bad writing.txt` and `/home/user/test/book/bible/06_style_guide.md`.
Tags: [BLOCKER] / [MAJOR] / [MINOR]. Recurring tics flagged where the same device appears across chapters.

Overall: the prose is strong and largely renders experience rather than labeling it. The dominant problems are (1) a heavy, recurring "I want to be exact/honest/clear about X" metanarrative tic, (2) gnomic aphoristic scene-closings on a hand-on-an-object beat, and (3) some narrator-from-the-future annotation. The ch13 death is the most serious predictability concern: it is foreshadowed and pre-grieved so insistently it telegraphs itself.

---

## 1. Narrator Stepping Outside Time (retroactive annotation)

This is the most frequent style-guide violation. The bible explicitly forbids using the retrospective vantage to telegraph ("no 'little did I know'"). The narration repeatedly arrives from the future to manage weight.

- ch12, l.31: "the hand that held me had been holding tools like me for a long time and would keep holding them after I broke." Future-knowledge of his own breaking, appended to a scene. [MAJOR]
- ch12, l.121: "A Greek bridge-builder gave it to me ... and he never knew he'd given me anything, and I never told him." Annotating significance from later. [MINOR]
- ch13, l.35: "I have hated him and envied him in the same breath more times than I can count and that morning I did both at once" - reaching from the future to pre-frame. [MINOR]
- ch13, l.47: "he told me their names and I made myself not hear them and I heard them anyway and I have them still." Pre-loads the death's grief before it happens. [MAJOR] (see Predictability)
- ch13, l.63: "for a count of maybe forty he was a man flying and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever built and I knew with my whole body what was coming." Explicit telegraph mid-scene. [BLOCKER] - the narrator announces the disaster a beat before it lands.
- ch18, l.69: "he stayed alive all that war, which I am setting down because I spent the whole of it braced for the other thing, and the other thing did not come, not in Dacia." Future-vantage reassurance that also defuses tension. [MINOR]
- ch21, l.61: "the one I will not describe again because I described it once and that was enough for one life." Narrator managing from outside. [MINOR]

Recurring tic, not one-off. Cluster densest in ch13.

## 2. Announcing Emotional Weight / Scene Closings That Restate the Takeaway

Chapter and scene endings repeatedly land on a summarizing emotional declaration rather than a concrete image left to resonate. Frequently paired with a hand-on-object gesture (see also Beat tags).

- ch12, l.151 (chapter close): "I held the rope, and I did not let go of it, and below me on the path I heard Celer's boots coming up the hill to tell me what they wanted me to fly next." Borderline; the rope image works but the "to tell me what they wanted me to fly next" appends the thematic point. [MINOR]
- ch13, l.101 (chapter close): "and that smell I will not get out of the back of my throat as long as I live." Restated takeaway / future-vantage. [MAJOR]
- ch15, l.105 (chapter close): "the deep red of a thing cooling, which was the one color in that shed I could read for certain, because it meant the work was over and we still did not know why." Closes by restating the chapter's thesis. [MAJOR]
- ch17, l.145-147 (chapter close): "I tried to feel like a free man and felt instead like a tool that had been picked up off the table and found worth keeping" - explicitly names the meaning. [MAJOR] Recurring image: "tool picked up off the table" repeats the ch12 "tool being carried" motif and the ch17 earlier "hefting it with his eyes" - intentional but verging on over-stated.
- ch20, l.149 (chapter close): ends on Tyche making the letter E "until she had it the way she wanted it" - good concrete close. [OK, counter-example]
- ch16, l.13: "and how much of what was loose was the one cheap thing I'd given away free in a cell four years ago, while everything since ... had stayed locked in a yard." Summarized takeaway mid-chapter. [MINOR]

Recurring tic.

## 3. AI Tell: "I want to be exact/honest/clear about X" metanarrative frame

This is the single most pervasive recurring tic in the range. The narrator repeatedly steps back to announce that he is about to be precise/honest, which is the style guide's core sin ("the writing steps back to label, annotate, or explain what the scene is doing"). It also functions as uniform hedging applied to dramatic beats.

- ch13, l.41: "I am going to set down what I did ... because it is the only part of this I get to keep."
- ch14, l.13: "I want to be precise about this, because it mattered, and because I did it cold and on purpose."
- ch15, l.51: "I am going to tell the steel honestly, because it is the truest thing in this whole account."
- ch17, l.111: "I'd been waiting two and a half years to hear it, and when it came I didn't feel what I'd thought I'd feel." (cf. l.77 "I want to be exact, because it would be easy to write that I felt joy.")
- ch18, l.63: "What I did in that war, set down plainly, so that whoever finds this knows what the work was."
- ch18, l.83: "I want to be exact about my own hands here, because it would be easy to write it so I was only asked."
- ch18, l.89: "I did not refuse. I want that on the wax too."
- ch19, l.75: "I'll put a few down exactly, because somebody finding this should know."
- ch20, l.43: "I am not going to dress up what I felt sitting there."
- ch21, l.3: "I want to put down clearly that I knew what I was doing."
- ch21, l.77: "I want that understood, because what I staged was a kind of theater."
- ch21, l.119: "That is the honest shape of what I did."
- ch22, l.13 (and ch22, l.89): "I want to be honest about how it came about."

[MAJOR] as a recurring tic. Individually most are defensible as Daniel's confessional-document voice (the bible permits journal register), but the density (12+ instances across 10 chapters, often opening a section) is templated and flattens conviction. Strongest single offenders: ch15 l.51, ch18 l.83/89, ch21 l.3 - all open scenes with near-identical "I want to be exact/honest" scaffolding.

## 4. AI Tell: "not X, it was Y" correctio / negation-as-emphasis

The style guide explicitly bans epanorthosis/correctio ("It's not X, it's Y"). Several instances:

- ch13, l.77: "The fall had done it. The fire was just the fire." (correctio-adjacent, and a "X. Y." punch) [MINOR]
- ch16, l.91: "there was no contempt in it now, which was worse, because what was there was a workman's flat tired anger." [MINOR]
- ch18, l.71: "That is how it spreads. Not gratitude. Need." Textbook negation-emphasis tricolon. [MAJOR]
- ch20, l.31: "It was a senator's furniture, a small farm, a frontier officer's pay for more years than Celer had served." (escalating list, fine) but cf. ch20, l.105 "Freeing me was the easy part. ... Now there's the rest of my life" - declarative subtext-exposing.
- ch13, l.83: "He didn't say I told you ... He didn't say anything." Negative narration (telling what isn't said). [MINOR] - though restrained.
- ch22, l.91: "she could not fix that, no one could, but ... that turned out to be the next best thing to being known." correctio shape. [MINOR]

Recurring but lower-frequency than #3.

## 5. AI Tell: "X. Y. Z." short tricolons / punchy declared-depth fragments

- ch16, l.9: "The figures saved everybody an afternoon." then l.18 "you could not cheat them as easily." - fine.
- ch18, l.71: "Not gratitude. Need." [see #4]
- ch15, l.79: "'That,' he said, 'is a blade.'" - earned, dialogue. [OK]
- ch21, l.73: "I went on. That is the worst sentence in this whole account and I'm going to leave it short." Self-conscious about the device, which both excuses and exposes it. [MINOR]
- ch17, l.119: "Marcus Ulpius Danihel. The man who hated this whole machine ... had just been bought and freed and named for the machine itself." Declared-irony summary. [MAJOR] (see #6)

## 6. Dramatic Irony by Declaration / Exposing Subtext

Generally the book structures irony well (the Apollodorus "demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day" payoff is architectural). But some moments name the irony rather than letting it stand:

- ch17, l.119: "named for the machine itself, the way you'd burn a man's brand off and burn your own on in the same hour." States the ironic gap outright. [MAJOR]
- ch18, l.85-91: the "same knowledge ... fouls theirs" theme is stated by Apollodorus AND then re-underlined by the narrator ("It's the same knowledge"). Apollodorus saying it is good; the narrator's surrounding gloss (l.87 "do to their water what I spend my life stopping us from doing to ours") exposes the subtext the dialogue already carried. [MINOR]
- ch20, l.41: "the bill, when it came, was a bill for her mind, which I had loved like the one piece of home" - names the buried feeling directly. [MINOR]
- ch16, l.81: "that is what makes a clever man dangerous, that he does not invent the bricks, he only chooses which ones to stack" - explaining the antagonist's method as commentary. [MINOR]

## 7. Repetition Across Scenes

- "A demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day" (ch12 l.119) is re-quoted ch17 l.51. Intentional motif, acceptable, but watch frequency.
- "the one thing / a clever toy that does one thing" recurs ch12 l.97, ch13 l.31, ch18 l.55-59. Deliberate Apollodorus refrain; borderline over-used by ch18. [MINOR]
- "tool picked up off the table / hefting with his eyes / a tool being carried" - ch12 l.31, ch17 l.59 & l.145. Thematic but stacking. [MINOR]
- "the wrong size / got the size of it wrong" - ch13 l.81, ch14 l.51-55 (used 3x in one passage). [MINOR]
- Niter/powder vow "Not yet. Maybe not ever." restated ch14 l.97, ch21 l.5. Intentional. [OK]
- The hand-flat-on-a-cold-object closing gesture recurs: ch18 l.113 (hand flat on bridge stone), ch20 l.101 / ch22 l.101 & l.123 (Marcia's hand flat on the table). By ch22 the "hand flat on / near, not on" beat is a signature move used three times with the same partner. [MINOR-MAJOR] borderline cliché by repetition.

## 8. Beat-Tag Clichés

Largely avoided; gestures are mostly specific (Macer's gold ring, the ink-stained hand, Naso's claw hand). A few generic ones:

- ch12, l.101: "nodding at the river" - placeholder nod. [MINOR]
- ch16, l.93: "got a laugh, a smaller one, a divided one" - fine.
- ring-turning ("turned the gold ring on his thumb") appears many times for Macer (ch15 l.27, ch17 l.27, l.39, ch19 l.33, l.47, ch20 l.9). This is a deliberate character signature but is leaned on as the default Macer beat-tag and recurs to the point of punctuation. [MINOR] recurring.

## 9. Fake/Vague Details

Rare. The prose is strongly specific (garum, wool grease, mutton grease in the weave, murus dacicus, niter needles "cold on the tongue", pepper-and-damp-rope warehouse). A few category-labels:

- ch17, l.57: "the most powerful human being on the surface of the earth" - category claim, though deliberately undercut by the plain-centurion description that follows. [OK/MINOR]
- ch14, l.3: "in sweat and small mercies" - mildly vague. [MINOR]
No BLOCKER/MAJOR here. This is a strength of the range.

## 10. Outline-Notes-as-Prose

- ch18, l.63: "What I did in that war, set down plainly" then a list ("I sited camps. ... I sent up the tethered eye. ... And I did the figures.") - this reads close to an outline of functions, each paragraph a labeled beat. Justifiable as the journal-register, but it is describing the shape of the work rather than rendering scenes. [MINOR-MAJOR]
- ch16, l.51: the contest concept arrives as a bulleted concept ("A contest. A prize. Money out of a strongbox ...") - fragmentary planning-note rhythm, though framed as the idea "arriving whole." [MINOR]

## 11. Info-Delivering Dialogue

Dialogue is generally good - characters deflect (Tyche's "You ate on the road," Macer's arithmetic-as-affection). Some functional exposition:

- ch17, l.39: Macer's speech ("He wants the water ... I sold the leather for the tents they died in") delivers backstory/stakes cleanly but is somewhat on-the-nose plot delivery. [MINOR]
- ch22, l.95: Marcia's marriage proposal is a long, fully articulated, perfectly reasoned monologue laying out every legal/practical benefit. It is in-character (she "said the hard words first") but is the clearest case of a character saying exactly and completely what she means in an emotionally charged scene. [MINOR-MAJOR]

## 12. Uniform Hedging

Not pervasive, but the "I want to be exact / I have no number / no way to count" refrain (ch12 l.59, ch18 l.65 & l.87, ch19) applies the same epistemic caution to many beats. Thematically motivated (his lack of measurement is the book's spine) but creates some flatness of conviction across dramatic and mundane moments alike. [MINOR]

## PREDICTABILITY / ch13 death check (priority)

The ch13 death of Sabinus is the range's biggest predictability problem. It is heavily telegraphed and pre-annotated:

- ch12 ends already framing the manned flight as a coming horror (l.141 "Naso's ruined hand ... being counted as a price"; l.135 the near-disaster dry run).
- ch13 opens (l.1 title "The Fall") and l.7 "the way you have to be fair to a thing that is going to crush you" - declares catastrophe in the first paragraph. [BLOCKER for telegraphing]
- ch13 l.16: "'No,' I said. 'Don't.'" / l.41 "I built it as safe as a thing like that gets, which is to say I built it to kill more slowly" - the narrator states it will kill before it does. [BLOCKER]
- ch13 l.43-51: every safety measure is itemized (wet clay, the lid cord, "I made him say it back to me twice"), plus Naso's "Make him keep his seat" and the children's names "I have them still" - the text checks off every Chekhov device, so the reader knows precisely which one fails. The fire-bowl tipping on the swing (l.65) is fully predicted by the loving setup.
- ch13 l.63: "I knew with my whole body what was coming" - the narrator annotates the death one beat early.

Net: the death itself is rendered powerfully and concretely (the fall, the cloth "tucked in," the rag burned at the river). But it is the opposite of unexpected - it is announced, foreshadowed, and pre-grieved. Per the style guide and Bad-writing.txt "Telegraphing Twists" / "Narrator Stepping Outside Time," the emotional management arrives from the future repeatedly. The death is not a twist (it isn't meant to be), but the telegraphing drains suspense from the ascent that the prose otherwise builds well. Recommend trimming the pre-death future-vantage tells (esp. l.7, l.41, l.63) and letting the itemized safety rig do the dread silently.

Other predictable/clichéd beats:
- ch16/ch19: Crispus as the recurring oiled-rhetorician antagonist who "files it away" and "smiled the smile that meant" is a familiar type; his salon takedowns follow a predictable rhythm (he builds a case, Daniel answers with a concrete demonstration, "got a better laugh"). The ch16 water-cup and ch19 Minerva rebuttals are structurally the same beat twice. [MINOR-MAJOR recurring]
- ch20: Macer's "buy her a comb" gossip and the "one story Rome tells" is signposted then explained at length (l.63-69), telegraphing its own theme.
- ch22: the Tyche/Marcia rivalry that resolves via a shared discovered error (l.79-81) is a predictable "two competent women bond over competence" beat, and the narrator flags the resolution mechanism in advance (l.75 "they were, for about a month, and then they weren't").
