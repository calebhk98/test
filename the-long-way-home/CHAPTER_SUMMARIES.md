# Chapter Summaries — *The Long Way Home* (book_v5)

Rolling per-chapter summaries. Each chapter was summarized by a fresh Sonnet
subagent that received the running summary of prior chapters for continuity,
then read only its assigned chapter. Detail loss/drift between the source text
and these summaries is expected and is itself diagnostic.

---

## Chapter 1
Danny, a young man with roughly forty dollars to his name, gets ready for an early morning shift at Brentwood Hardware where his manager Rick has already cut his hours twice. After tripping in the store's parking lot around 7:15 a.m., he blacks out and wakes up — twenty-five minutes later by his phone — in an open field of cut stubble, with no roads, power lines, or any modern infrastructure in sight. He walks toward a settlement of mud-and-stone buildings, where the inhabitants wear rough, hand-dyed clothing with no zippers or printed labels and speak an incomprehensible language, and a man with a knife turns it visibly in his hand as Danny passes. From a hillside above the settlement, Danny spots a vast city in the valley and recognizes at its edge a large, round, arched structure he knows from textbooks — the Colosseum — except this one is completely intact and new, confirming with stomach-dropping certainty that he has somehow ended up in ancient Rome. The chapter ends with four armed soldiers closing around him on the hillside, grabbing him by the arm and neck, and marching him down toward the city while he holds his hands up and can say nothing in his own defense.

## Chapter 2
The soldiers march Danny — who is 17 years old and identifies himself as Daniel Mercer — into the city and lock him in a stone-and-brick cell with a barred window and a heavy plank door. Alone, Danny secretly retrieves his phone (at 98% battery), switches it to airplane mode to conserve power, and spends a frantic session in Notes writing down everything he fears forgetting: scientific facts, his mother's phone number, and his best estimate of the date as "ROME 98 AD." He also takes a selfie, creating two photos on the phone — the Colosseum and his own face — as proof he was alive. A curious off-duty guard inspects the phone without understanding it and hands it back, prompting Danny to note he must hide it in his tunic. That night, a higher-ranking soldier arrives, cuts the rope from Danny's wrists, and exchanges names with him — the soldier's name is given with two falling syllables, though Danny doesn't yet record it clearly — before leaving Danny in the dark with a bowl of grain mush and fish sauce, the first food he has eaten since before the blackout.

## Chapter 3
Danny is brought at dawn from his cell by the optio and two spearmen to a building where a senior official (roughly fifty years old, wearing a tunic with a red band) questions him in a fruitless interrogation, since Danny understands no Latin. When the official gestures to have him removed, Danny gambles on showing rather than speaking: he borrows the clerk's wax tablet and stylus and draws a world map from memory — the Mediterranean and Italy (which the optio recognizes), Africa with a southern tip, Asia, and finally, fatally, two landmasses across the blank western ocean (the Americas). The official's demeanor shifts from contempt to calculation; he takes the tablet, has the clerk copy the coastlines, and dispatches a soldier to fetch a healer and foreigner to help communicate. Back in his cell, Danny acknowledges his blunder (revealing the Americas fourteen centuries early) but consoles himself that the drawing upgraded his status from disposable prisoner to a curiosity worth keeping alive; he plans to use his phone's map app (now at eleven percent battery) to improve future maps, and makes a private note about variolation, deferring it as not yet feasible.

## Chapter 4
Danny is brought from his cell to a furnished room and introduced to the physician — a roughly 45-year-old freed slave with medical knowledge and Greek — who examines him physically and begins attempting communication. When the physician switches from Latin to Greek, Danny catches a word rooted in Greek (*pou*, "where") and uses gestures to convey that he comes from a far northern, cold place, building the cover story that he is from some unknown land beyond Britain. The two spend the morning and into the afternoon exchanging knowledge through a wax tablet: Danny demonstrates Hindu-Arabic numerals and place value (stumbling embarrassingly through a long-division problem but recovering), and the physician is so intrigued that he copies Danny's numeral "7" into his own notes. As a result of this exchange, Danny is moved from the cell to a workshop loft — better conditions, but still clearly captivity. That night, after checking his phone one last time, the battery dies completely, taking with it his photos, his notes, and his only connection to home.

## Chapter 5
Spanning roughly a year from Danny's arrival through his first winter to age 18, Danny painstakingly acquires Latin (and some Greek) through immersion at the workshop, helped by two years of high-school French teaching him how inflected languages work, while Heras visits regularly and the ten-year-old workshop boy Stichus becomes an inadvertent teacher. Danny makes a failed attempt to explain germ theory to Heras when a workshop boy develops a dangerous infected wound with red streaking up the arm, but Heras dismisses the "little animals" as superstition; Danny then successfully reframes hygiene practices — boiling water, hand-washing, keeping the wound clean — in terms Heras can accept, and the boy survives, after which Heras quietly adopts the practices without crediting Danny. By year's end Danny has also improvised a crude soap, begun avoiding lead cookware and warned Heras about lead poisoning (dismissed), sketched an improved horse-collar harness for a leatherworker (who predicts no one will want it), and put out a standing bounty through a guild contact named Drusus for a craftsman who can produce a clear optical-quality glass pane. Recognizing small wins are not enough and that he needs a public, undeniable demonstration, the chapter closes with Danny lying in the loft, feeling warm air rise through the floor-gaps, and conceiving the idea of a hot-air balloon.

## Chapter 6
Danny spends weeks attempting to build a functioning hot-air balloon as his "cheap, mine, and undeniable" proof of knowledge, working with only scavenged rags and the help of ten-year-old Stichus. His first small model — sewn from worn linen scraps — lifts off on a tethered string in the yard, thrilling Stichus and drawing a dry but intrigued reaction from Heras, though Danny is banished from the yard by the unnamed kitchen woman for fire safety. Heras, motivated by curiosity, funds a second larger attempt with linen and mutton grease; Danny seals the cloth with grease to prevent heat leakage, but the added weight prevents lift, and when he stokes the fire higher the greased seam catches and the whole bag burns, leaving Danny with a blistered forearm (the burn scar) and the ruined project in the slaughter-lot. He grasps the core engineering trap — cloth must be light to lift but sealed/heavy to hold heat — and recognizes money is the missing key, yet the balloon was meant to earn that money, a closed loop. He builds a third compromise-sized balloon that lifts above Stichus's head and holds for about ten breaths before a seam tears and it settles back, unresolved but proven.

## Chapter 7
Covering the following spring, Danny solves two major engineering problems: perfecting the seams (folded triple-thick, grease-sealed needle holes) and developing a "fill and release" technique using a ground fire to inflate the bag before pinching it shut and letting it fly free. After a fire accident burns his right hand and nearly destroys part of the household lean-to, Danny achieves his first untethered free flight, watched by a visibly shaken Heras. Heras then takes Danny into Rome for the triumphal entry of the emperor, where Danny is spotted with unsettling intensity by an important haruspex named Aulus Vibenius. On the road home, Heras reveals he has told the household's unnamed owner about the flying bag, and the owner now wants a demonstration — setting up a high-stakes public flight before a wealthy Roman audience that will determine Danny's fate and value. (Note: the outline intends this emperor to be Trajan arriving ~99 AD as a normal entry, not a full triumph — a flagged revision point.)

## Chapter 8
Danny publicly launches his balloon in a cramped Subura square, assisted by the slave Davus and the boy Stichus, while Heras watches and the household steward observes for the still-unseen master. The balloon rises roughly sixty to seventy feet above the rooflines to a stunned crowd, but without a sustained heat source it drifts, clips a rooftop, drags across washing, and comes down smoldering — burning a tunic and alarming the neighborhood without killing anyone. The haruspex Aulus Vibenius steps forward, inspects the fallen bag, and delivers a carefully ambiguous public verdict: the gods let the fire rise (good omen) but turned it back on Rome's rooftops (possible warning), leaving the crowd to tip narrowly toward wonder rather than violence. Afterward the steward informs Danny the master will see him immediately — the first time Danny will meet the equestrian who has owned him for over a year. A brief aside reveals Danny has also been quietly running a small bookmaking operation on chariot races near the Circus, exploiting his understanding of implied probability to guarantee a margin on every race.

## Chapter 9
Danny is escorted with Heras to the home of Titus Flavius Macer, an equestrian contractor who supplies leather and timber to the legions in Dacia and who legally owns Danny. Macer — a weathered, calculating man — delivers a cold power briefing: the balloon launch has made Danny visible and dangerous (a senator's freedman is already at the door), so he can no longer afford obscurity. Macer offers a deal: a walled yard by the timber wharf, cloth by the bolt, two slave assistants, forge and materials on account, and a personal peculium, in exchange for a working balloon and teaching the slaves enough to protect Macer's investment against Danny dying. Danny plays his one card — only he knows why the balloon failed and how to fix it — then rashly asks for freedom if it succeeds; Macer dismisses it flatly, saying citizen-freedom is not his to grant. In the corridor Danny is assigned a fourteen-year-old slave girl named Tyche as a personal attendant; she has been silently watching and absorbing everything, and she tells Danny the yard is by the wharf and to eat his bread on the walk.

## Chapter 10
Danny is installed in the walled wharf-side yard and begins serious work on a full-scale balloon, hiring free women to sew seams and working alongside the collared slave Pamphilus as his cutter. Tyche quickly proves a prodigy with the Hindu-Arabic numerals Danny taught her, independently reconciling the workshop accounts and catching a deliberate shortfall from one seamstress. Danny also experiments with a printing block — carving a line of Homer in relief, inking it, pressing papyrus — producing ugly but reproducible impressions, which impresses Heras. Near the chapter's end Macer brings Danny to a literary salon hosted by an equestrian named Mettius, where a polished, contemptuous man of letters, Lucius Vettius Crispus, publicly humiliates Danny by comparing him to Pytheas of Massalia and implying he is a fraud; Danny's Latin is too slow to fight back, and he returns to the yard and silently begins cutting a second block.

## Chapter 11
Danny's balloon project attracts Quintus Marcius Celer, a hard-edged frontier tribune who frames the balloon as a battlefield observation tool, asking how far it can be seen. A contract is negotiated — 80,000 sesterces to Macer, 40,000 direct to Danny — to build a man-carrying version. Over weeks Celer visits regularly; Danny sketches stirrups and a stirrup-cocking mechanism for the arcuballista crossbow (passed to Celer's aide "Hermes"), plus a counterweight trebuchet, and argues heliocentrism to a skeptical Heras using shadow sticks. The chapter climaxes with a tethered manned trial: a young recruit, Titus Aelius Naso, climbs into a willow basket and the bag lifts him to head height, but Naso stands, the basket heels, the fire-bowl catches the greased cloth, and the bag burns and falls on him — leaving severe burns, a destroyed left hand, and a broken ankle. Celer's cold verdict is that it worked (he saw the far riverbank) and he orders the trial repeated with a smaller fire and longer basket.

## Chapter 12
Danny and Pamphilus (leaving Tyche, now 16, in Rome) travel north along the Via Flaminia to join Trajan's army at the Danube frontier, a multi-week march that forces Danny to reckon with Rome's vast military logistics and its limits: dysentery kills more soldiers than any enemy — Danny silently boils his own water knowing the cause but lacking standing to act — and he quietly cures a tentmate's scurvy with lemon rinds. At the Danube crossing he meets Apollodorus of Damascus, the emperor's chief engineer building the great stone-pier bridge; they do not become friends (Apollodorus dismisses Danny's pile-driver idea and calls the balloon a "clever toy that does one thing"), but the Greek's precision impresses Danny, who records his maxim ("a demonstration is a lie you tell on a good day") in English. The tethered observation balloon is flown unmanned over a ridge to count Dacian fires for Celer, but nearly escapes in a wind gust and is reliable only on calm mornings, leaving Danny under mounting pressure from Celer and Macer to make it carry a man over enemy territory — Naso's ruined hand already recast as a price rather than a verdict.

## Chapter 13
At Trajan's Dacian camp, Danny is ordered to fly a volunteer legionary, Marcus Aurelius Sabinus (~25), in a free untethered balloon over a hill ("the Loaf") to count Dacians, because a column must advance in three days regardless. Danny protests the wrong wind and danger, but as a slave his objections carry no weight; Apollodorus condemns the plan before returning to his bridge, and Naso warns Sabinus to stay seated. The balloon rises to about a hundred feet, but the river wind catches it broadside, tilts the fire-bowl, and burning grease ignites the envelope; Sabinus falls to his death, the burning cloth settling over him. Celer finds Danny kneeling in the dirt, takes responsibility for overriding the warnings, and declares no more men will fly from his orders; alone with Pamphilus by the river that night, Danny burns the last scrap of scorched envelope and resolves never again to put an untrained person in an untested free-flight design simply because he was told to.

## Chapter 14
Following Rome's negotiated peace with Decebalus, Danny packs up his balloon equipment at the Dacian front, sealing the bag and his improved designs into a crate he treats as a private ceremony. Naso, now maimed, watches him nail the crate shut and accepts Danny's vow to resume manned flight only when both design and pilot are truly ready; Danny secures Naso a place with the baggage mules south. Celer tells Danny he wrote the official report to protect him, listing Sabinus as a soldier's death and omitting Danny's culpability. On the march south Danny watches a muleteer sicken from unboiled water and begins sorting future projects into two columns — clean tech (press, numerals, water filter, rag paper) versus gunpowder, which he refuses. Back in Rome, Heras steers him toward water filtration as the new work; at the yard Danny finds Tyche, now 17, has kept the business running with meticulous tally-columns in his numerals, and he spends his first morning back sketching a slow sand-filter while she sets bread beside him.

## Chapter 15
In the spring of Danny's twenty-second year, back in Rome, he keeps working with Heras on water filtration and recognizes that bronze surgical knives reveal a critical limitation — bronze stays rust-free but dulls, iron holds an edge but rusts — forcing him to confront that better steel underlies every precision instrument he wants. Macer introduces him to Gaius Julius Hermes, a gruff veteran freedman smith behind the cattle market, and Danny shares his theory of crucible steel (carbon from charcoal, sealed in clay pots, fully melted, for uniform hard steel). Their second batch yields one extraordinary blade, but the third, done identically, shatters in the quench — exposing that Danny can't supply the numbers Hermes needs (how much carbon, how hot, how long). During this period Danny also builds a simple battery (matching a Syrian trader's gilding device), designs calipers and go/no-go gauges, and makes a mercury thermometer calibrated against boiling water (sending a second to a naval contact as a storm-predictor), but the chapter ends on the forge impasse: without instruments to measure temperature or carbon, fine steelmaking stays ungovernable.

## Chapter 16
During Danny's twenty-second summer, Arabic numerals have already spread through Rome's merchant class without his direct involvement — he finds them on fish-sellers' slates and traces the chain back to his yard, noting they spread because they made fraud harder to hide. Danny has built a rudimentary printing press, staffed by a freedman letterer named Eros, selling cheap printed sheets (including a times-table) to Subura schoolmasters; Heras warns that cheapening books will make new enemies. Tyche, now ~18-19 and still hiding the full extent of her arithmetic to avoid being sold, challenges him to think beyond rote printing and asks "who prints the sea," sparking his idea for an annual prize competition rewarding imaginative machine-thinking. At Macer's salon Crispus mounts a formal rhetorical attack calling Danny a stateless charlatan and nearly naming him impious under Roman law; Danny defuses it by publicly drinking his filtered water and daring anyone to call him a fraud after his barrel stops killing people. Haruspex Aulus Vibenius watches silently and leaves; Macer urges Danny to get his water filter into a senator's household before the priest reaches a verdict. The chapter ends with Danny sketching both a prize-contest plan and a static-electricity machine (a first step toward wireless signaling), and Tyche adding the word "when" to his contest board.

## Chapter 17
Danny, now 22 and still a slave at Macer's yard, receives a summons to the Palatine signed by Emperor Trajan, who wants to see the balloon, water filter, and new numerals; someone has been reporting on his work in detail, unsettling Danny and Macer. At the audience Danny demonstrates the tethered balloon, the filtration process, and the Eratosthenes stick-shadow proof of Earth's circumference, while Tyche publicly runs provisioning sums in the new figures faster than the emperor's freedman with an abacus. Trajan buys Danny from Macer and immediately manumits him, giving him the legal name Marcus Ulpius Danihel, with rights to hold property, write a will, and free slaves. Danny feels not liberated but re-bound — now an imperial freedman obligated to support the coming Dacian spring campaign with his balloon and water protocols, reckoning with the moral weight of enabling war even as he prevents disease deaths. Descending the Palatine, Tyche asks whether he can now free them — meaning Pamphilus first, then herself — and Danny promises to buy and free Pamphilus as soon as he can.

## Chapter 18
Danny purchases Pamphilus from Macer for four thousand sesterces and frees him in an improvised yard ceremony, with Heras and Tyche watching as smith Hermes chisels off the iron collar Pamphilus has worn for eleven years. Freedom falls flat — Pamphilus, now past forty with nowhere to go, simply asks whether he is still to cut the cloth, and Danny hires him on wages as a freedman client. Danny then marches north as Marcus Ulpius Danihel for Trajan's second Dacian campaign, reconnects with Apollodorus at the stone bridge at Drobeta, and deploys two-man tethered balloons and sanitation measures that thin the flux. At the siege of Sarmizegetusa he uses aerial observation and waterworks knowledge to locate and foul the city's water supply, breaking the stronghold from within; Decebalus cuts his own throat rather than be captured, and Dacia's gold is carted south. Danny ends the chapter crossing back over the bridge, haunted by the inversion of his knowledge — the same hand that kept Roman soldiers alive with clean water killed a city with bad — and by whether history unfolds as he half-remembers or because of him, with Tyche still waiting to be bought.

## Chapter 19
Now living as Marcus Ulpius Danihel, Danny uses profits from a quietly run fixed-odds betting shop (managed on paper by Tyche) to launch an annual literary prize for imagined machines, held on the festival of Minerva and fronted under his patron Macer's name for credibility. The contest draws entries from across Roman society — a carter's eagle-chariot, a baker's wife's bronze grain-grinder, a twelve-year-old's moon seen through an imagined glass, a soldier's aerial arrow-platform, a slave's satire, and a grain-factor's slave named Felix who reasons his way to a steam-powered ship and the need for a stronger metal. Danny gives the main prize to the moon-boy and a judges' prize to an anonymous "Thulean merchant's" love story; Felix, a slave whose prize would legally belong to his master, gets only a commendation, but Danny maneuvers workshop fees toward Felix's freedom fund. Rival Crispus tries to take over the contest by appealing to literary tradition but is repelled by Danny's Olympic-precedent argument; walking home, Danny notes Crispus's resentment has fixed on the vulnerable Felix. The winning entries are printed on Eros's press and spread cheaply, and the chapter ends with Felix sending word he intends to solve the pot-strength (steel) problem.

## Chapter 20
In the winter before spring, Danny meets privately with Trajan, candidly answering that a balloon can deliver fire (at the cost of envelope and pilot) and that the only counter is an enemy acquiring the same capability; Trajan announces a permanent Praetorian-trained balloon unit and asks Danny to shape its doctrine, leaving Danny disturbed that his invention is now a weapon. In spring Danny goes to buy Tyche — now 24 — and learns Macer has long known of her hidden mathematical ability; after tense negotiation he pays 38,000 sesterces (nearly all he has) plus filter service at Tibur and teaching Macer's steward's boys, for her bill of sale. He frees her informally before Pamphilus and Heras, but Tyche refuses to thank him and confronts him: he spent five years deciding how much of her Rome saw, kept her invisible "for her own good," and now decides she is free — all without her say. She sets her own terms (real wages, her name on nothing of his, a barred room, a prompt court date for proper manumission) and demands he teach her the private night-script she's seen him write — English, which he has guarded for eleven years as his only private possession and the safe vessel for knowledge he fears dying with him. He opens the little book, the dead phone falls out, and he begins teaching her the alphabet, watching her reason out a fifth letter on her own.

## Chapter 21
Danny — now a freedman with money and wharf property — breaks his long-held vow against gunpowder by secretly building a niter bed over roughly two years, rationalizing it with a false belief that the Chinese ("Seres") already had the knowledge. A worker named Geta loses his left hand at the wrist, three fingers of his right hand, and the sight in one eye when a batch of powder explodes during grinding; Danny sets him up on permanent wages and housing. Danny then deliberately casts a flawed bronze cannon with Hermes — too long, too thin-walled — and fires it before Celer, army officers, and an imperial official on the riverbank, destroying the tube spectacularly to prove that only better steel and tighter machining could contain such force. The demonstration directs military and imperial interest toward funding Hermes's forge and Danny's boring and valve prizes (with the side goal of advancing his secret steam pump and clock), but Danny closes the chapter knowing the powder secret is already loose — Geta knew the ratios, workers knew the beds, an engineer lingered at the wreckage — and sits that evening with Geta, holding a wax tablet inscribed in English with the three correct numbers.

## Chapter 22
Roughly ten years after his arrival, Danny struggles with the business and legal complexity of his growing enterprise (press, niter beds, forge, prize) until Heras directs him to Marcia, a sharp self-made freedwoman widow of about thirty-five who runs Caepio's warehouse on the Aventine wharf. Danny fails her test about his own net profit, returns with the answer, and she diagnoses every leak in his business and proposes a partnership share rather than a wage, wanting in early before men of rank notice its value. Over months, Marcia and Tyche forge a hard-edged alliance (Marcia catches a carter's year-long swindle; Tyche teaches her the figures), while Danny gets a crude steam pump running for the first time and writes notes about New World crops (tomatoes, potatoes, cacao) and citrus for scurvy. A year in, Marcia proposes marriage on practical grounds — legal protection of the joint estate, freeborn children, mutual advantage — Danny agrees, and they wed simply with Heras, Pamphilus, and Tyche as witnesses; afterward Marcia inspects the powder, cannon drawings, and the cipher book and declares that with Tyche to read it and herself to sell it, the work will survive even if Danny does not.

## Chapter 23
Danny recounts a winter of failures after Marcia takes over his accounts: he tries to build a mechanical clock but cannot remember the escapement mechanism and substitutes an inaccurate water clock (clepsydra). He hires Greek glass-cutter Demetrios to make a spyglass, but bubbly, striated Roman glass yields only a dim, rainbow-fringed ~2x magnification — useless for the war-glass or microscope he promised; Marcia salvages costs by selling the flawed tubes as luxury novelties. Most bitterly, Danny builds a working wooden rail-and-flanged-wheel tramway at stonecutter Maximus's yard, proving four men can do the work of twenty-six — but the economics collapse because Maximus already owns his slave laborers and has no reason to pay for iron rails to replace free coerced labor, so Danny tears the line out. The chapter closes with Danny building a voltaic pile (copper-zinc-brine) that produces current and turns an iron nail into an electromagnet, and sketching compass-needle notes for future navigators; Tyche sits beside him practicing her English letters as he writes down everything he knows about rail flanges before it fades.

## Chapter 24
Roughly twelve years after his arrival, Danny reflects on his accumulated achievements (press, rag paper, water filters, spreading numerals) while feeling disconnected from all of it, since none of his inventions bear his name. At the Macer Prize reading, a freeborn girl of about eleven or twelve, a wool-factor's daughter, wins with a drawing of a "ladder of knowing" — a staircase to the heavens with a torch-bearing figure at the bottom — privately devastating Danny because she independently reached his own core vision. A farrier named Statius, from Capua, sends his family's fifty-year table of iron-heat-to-color data, more accurate than Danny's own records; Danny pays him a year's wages and incorporates it. Heras visits and asks pointedly whether Danny is a man doing an unasked duty or one who has decided he knows better than the whole world; Danny deflects with a half-truth. The chapter closes with Danny demonstrating the voltaic pile to an awed Heras (a current without flame, deflecting a compass needle forty degrees), reflecting on his hidden phone and fading memories of music and his mother's voice, and the news that Marcia is six months pregnant.

## Chapter 25
Eight days after the birth of Danny and Marcia's (as-yet unnamed) son, three powerful figures visit in succession. Senator Publius Cornelius Scaeva (~57-58) arrives by grand litter and flatters Danny with genuine understanding of his enterprise — naming the press and standardized numerals, not fire or flight, as his true revolutionary tool — while maneuvering him into a patron-client relationship, extracting information about his sulfur imports, revealing he knows about Geta, and warning that when steel makes cannon viable Danny had better have a friend controlling their use. At the baths, Crispus drops his old geographic-fraud attacks for a more dangerous charge — that the "ladder of knowing" tale teaches children to ascend to heaven through their own works without gods, an impiety the priestly colleges are weighing. Finally the haruspex Vibenius, who has watched Danny silently for eleven years, appears on foot the day before the naming ceremony and lays out the whole political map: Scaeva's patronage makes Danny look like an arms-maker for a war-hungry senator, weaponizing Crispus's impiety charge, and Vibenius warns Danny he will one day have to give a reason for bringing his fire into the world that he could say "to a god's face."

## Chapter 26
At the dedication of Trajan's new Forum in high summer, Danny attends with his nine-day-old son Lucanus, freshly named. He inspects Trajan's Column, which records the Dacian wars but omits the balloon volunteer who died, and reflects bitterly on how history is shaped by whoever holds the chisel. He meets Apollodorus, now past sixty, who dismisses the column's inaccuracies and bluntly tells Danny he has "reached the bottom of the well he brought with him" — the clock-escapement failure proves it — and warns that a proud, bearded Spanish architect (a rival Apollodorus humiliated) is keeping a grudge-ledger. Most importantly, Crispus confronts Danny, having traced the scattered prize competitions (glassmen in Puteoli, the Capua farrier, bronzesmith Glabrio, and others) and understood that Danny is systematically "farming knowledge" — using guild-administered prizes to pull craft secrets into the open to be replicated and owned by him rather than the trades. Crispus withdraws the fraud charge but declares the real objection is that Danny is dismantling the nature of craft itself, and says he will bring the argument somewhere with legal consequences.

## Chapter 27
Danny faces a formal impiety hearing on a spring morning at the house of Aemilius Scaurus on the Caelian, convened by three priests of the college (including Vibenius), with Crispus as accuser. The night before, Vibenius demands Danny rehearse his defense; Danny delivers a spontaneous argument that his knowledge belongs to the gods — he only found what was already placed in the world for men to discover — which Vibenius coldly approves. At the hearing Crispus frames the "knowledge ladder," the prize contest, and the germ-theory hygiene teaching as hubris and impiety; Danny's faltering rebuttal gains ground when patron Scaeva intervenes to defend the practical benefits, tipping the room. The verdict is a public sacrifice: eight days later Danny kneels on the Aventine steps, leads a white heifer to Minerva's altar, and dedicates all his works (press, contest, figures) to the goddess, placing them under the priests' ritual oversight. Danny reflects on the triple cost — the college's leash over his prize, a public debt to Scaeva, and an inscrutable debt to Vibenius — while noting that a ship called the *Corva*, captained by a Gaul named Bituitus, cleared the Tagus that same morning on an Atlantic route Danny spent two years preparing, unwitnessed while he knelt at the altar.

## Chapter 28
Danny receives imperial orders to join Trajan's eastern campaign as a camp physician and sanitation expert, while patron Scaeva simultaneously co-opts him as an intelligence asset through pre-arranged officer connections — a leash he cannot refuse. He parts from Marcia, infant Lucanus, and Tyche, travels to Brundisium and on to Antioch, overwhelmed by the scale of Rome's war machine pushing east toward Parthia; Celer, now a greying prefect, articulates the empire's insatiable hunger ("out of world or out of us"). Six weeks in, a dispatch from his Atlantic captain (a letter in Tyche's hand) reports the *Corva* found land with cultivated crops, rubber, cacao, and sizable indigenous populations — and that a sick crew member who walked through a village caused an estimated 30–50% mortality. Danny records his guilt privately in English, acknowledging he deliberately drew the Americas as "uninhabited" to protect himself, and sends orders for a second voyage with stricter quarantine. He also notes petroleum seeps in Syrian streambeds and writes distillation instructions for an Antioch factor, deliberately omitting what the heavier fraction could be used for.

## Chapter 29
During Trajan's eastern campaign in autumn, Danny learns Rome has taken Ctesiphon and reached the Persian Gulf — the farthest the empire will ever reach — but overstretched supply lines are immediately threatened as conquered cities revolt, including a major Jewish uprising burning in Cyrene (Lusius Quietus sent to suppress it). Celer is brought to Danny on a cart with an abdominal sword-wound two and a half days old and sepsis already set in; Danny does everything he knows (boiled water, clean dressings, cutting dead tissue) but must confront that his knowledge can prevent infection, not cure it once bacteria reach the blood, and Celer dies on the fifth day. Naso sits the vigil and tends the fire. After Celer's death Danny grieves the limits of his lifelong gift — he has saved thousands of anonymous men but could not save his friend — and privately nurses a plan to redirect Rome's expansionist energy westward toward the Americas, an idea he now has no one to share, since Celer was the one man he might have told.

## Chapter 30
On a road north as the army retreats westward, Danny learns from a Greek baggage clerk that Emperor Trajan has died of a stroke at Selinus in Cilicia — news already a fortnight old. He immediately calculates the danger: his legal identity as Marcus Ulpius Danihel rested on Trajan's personal patronage, and with Trajan gone his enemies (Crispus, the priestly colleges, creditor Vibenius) face no check on moving against him. A new emperor, Hadrian, has been acclaimed by the Syrian legions at Antioch — soldiers suspect a deathbed manipulation by the empress and the Guard prefect Attianus — and Danny dimly recalls once seeing the bookish Hadrian humiliated by Apollodorus at the Forum. His urgent focus turns to reaching Rome before anyone can act against him, since Marcia and Tyche are unknowingly running his press, contest, and powder records there without yet knowing the ground has shifted; his son Lucanus is five that year, and Danny is far from both family and enterprise, counting the days to close the distance.

## Chapter 31
Danny arrives in Rome after a fast five-week sea voyage, finding the city tense under the new post-Trajan order: soldiers watch faces at the gates and a seditious wall-scrawl has been limed over. He reunites with his household at the wharf yard — Pamphilus, the grown Stichus, Tyche (now 32), and Marcia — but is devastated when his son Lucanus, just turned six, does not recognize him. Marcia lays out their precarious position: Danny's name and patronage rested on the dead Trajan; Crispus has been waiting; Scaeva's loyalty is uncertain; the Minerva dedication left an open legal door. Danny also reads a dispatch that his Spanish mine pump is finally working after a decade. He visits Macer, who is visibly frightened: four ex-consuls (Palma, Celsus, Quietus, Nigrinus) are accused of plotting against Hadrian, and Macer, fearing the purge, tells Danny he will betray him if the Guard comes — but promises to do it slowly, the most he can offer.

## Chapter 32
Danny learns from a fish-seller that Hadrian is abandoning all of Trajan's eastern conquests and withdrawing the legions — news that hits hard because Celer died holding a supply road to that now-surrendered province. He visits the elderly Heras, who describes Hadrian as a meticulous, Greek-loving, cost-counting emperor who cannot be flattered and wants a compact, well-governed empire — and points out the bitter irony that Danny has spent nearly twenty years building tools (balloon, powder weapons, supply logistics) suited to an empire of expansion, not consolidation. Back home, Marcia coldly assesses the danger — Danny is branded as the dead emperor's man and must reposition fast — and draws up a four-point plan to make him indispensable to Hadrian through civic works (clean water, the press, financial tools), giving them about a year before Hadrian reaches Rome. The chapter ends with Danny sitting on the press-room floor beside his son Lucanus (now ~seven), who has been gradually reconnecting with him, holding Celer's buckle and asking when someone is coming.

## Chapter 33
During the political terror following Hadrian's accession, Danny watches Rome grow frightened in the wake of the extrajudicial killings of the four consular senators (Palma, Celsus, Quietus, Nigrinus) by the praetorian prefect Attianus before Hadrian even arrives. Crispus revives his impiety argument before a senatorial gathering but fails to carry the room, partly because news of the Atlantic ship's return has lent Danny's map credibility. Danny's two main patrons abandon him: Scaeva quietly drops him, and Macer — past seventy and frightened — tells Danny to make himself "small and dull" and stop coming to his door. A spy posing as an oil importer visits the press-house, but Marcia deflects him with dull answers while Tyche sits against the locked door hiding Danny's private papers; the man leaves empty-handed. Bituitus's second ship has returned from the Americas carrying a man named Aco, who sits in the warehouse unable to return home, while Danny — aware he arranged this — admits he does not know what it means. For a month Danny follows Macer's advice, removing his name from contracts and letting Marcia front everything, and at the chapter's end watches Lucanus quietly practice reading figures beside him.

## Chapter 34
In autumn of Hadrian's early reign, Danny (now 37) spends his days in humiliating waits at minor bureau porticoes, seeking any foothold in the new administration. The business survives only because Marcia runs it under her own name with Tyche as her partner; Danny's name is a liability after the purge, so he stays invisible. Unable to stay away longer, he visits the aging Heras, who brokers an introduction to Eudemus, a Greek treasury freedman in the imperial fiscus; Eudemus, impressed that the new numerals expose fraud at a glance, promises only to say a name in the right room someday — a crack in a door, not the door. Sparked by Tyche's remark about enterprises too big for one head, Danny refines his long-shelved western-voyage idea into a new pitch: sell Hadrian not conquest but knowledge — a mapping voyage using idle legions, prestige without province — which Marcia judges genuinely different from his old plan, though they have no room to say it in yet, so Tyche records it in her private script. The chapter closes with eight-year-old Lucanus, teaching himself the numerals from a printed sheet, observing that the marks are the same everywhere even when the words differ.

## Chapter 35
Danny (37, as Marcus Ulpius Danihel) is summoned to a senatorial house on the Palatine slope for a private audience with Emperor Hadrian, arranged through Eudemus. Marcia coaches him to let Hadrian feel he sees through the deceptions; Danny leaves their young daughter Ulpia playing on a garden swing. The meeting is a long, probing exchange in which Danny demonstrates his numerals using a real province's grain accounts, showing how the marks expose fraudulent strokes Roman numerals hide; Hadrian is fast, impressed, claims the insight as his own, and Danny lets him. It turns dangerous when Danny inadvertently delivers a maxim — that an empire running on individual emperors dies when it runs out of good ones — and then, watching Hadrian sketch a stepped dome, recognizes with a shock that Hadrian is the young Spaniard Apollodorus publicly mocked years ago, meaning the master now works for the pupil unknowing. Though Hadrian invites Danny to name what he wants (the world-map expedition right there), Danny holds it back, calculating Hadrian would seize it or resent the source, and asks only to audit a province's accounts in obscurity; Hadrian grants it through Eudemus, and Danny returns home cold, warning Marcia only that he kept the map in the box.

## Chapter 36
Danny (now past 40) leverages careful audit work on the province of Lugdunensis to earn a second private audience with Hadrian, who has grown suspicious of his cultivated quietness and summons him under pretext of auditing Belgica. Before the meeting, Danny and Tyche retrieve the long-hidden world map; Tyche names aloud what he is doing: drawing Rome a road toward his own home shore. In the audience Danny presents the full map — including the two unnamed western continents — pitching the western voyage as the achievement that would make Hadrian the man who measured the whole world, while knowingly lying that the far shore is uninhabited, even as Illa, an indigenous woman brought back by his secretly funded Atlantic probe, sits counting figures in his compound. Hadrian accepts it in bounded form: a proper coastal survey, a shipwright school on the western coast, and a standing prize for a seagoing clock that can track longitude, ordering Danny to write down everything he knows about why the clock has failed. Danny returns home, where Marcia reads it at a glance — Hadrian took only "a tenth of it," keeping the map but refusing a fleet — and Danny picks up the Belgica accounts and returns to work.

## Chapter 37
Danny learns that Apollodorus, now past seventy, has publicly mocked Hadrian's new Temple of Venus and Roma by saying the seated goddesses were built too large for their ceiling — a fatal criticism of an emperor who designed the temple himself. Danny visits Apollodorus at a work yard to beg him to stop, but the old man is unrepentant, cheerfully owning the remark and accusing Danny of spending his life folding truth small enough to fit through powerful men's doors. Danny tries a quiet back-channel through a fiscus intermediary named Theophanes, who warns him off immediately. Over the following season Apollodorus is exiled and then dies, the cause never officially named. Marcia greets the news without reproach — Danny bent and they still have the wharf — while their ten-year-old Lucanus, told only that a great builder died, says "he did all right" because the bridges still stand. The chapter ends with Danny hanging Apollodorus's plumb-line in the press-room and asking Tyche to record the old man's words in their private cipher as a testament from "a better engineer."

## Chapter 38
Set in Rome during Danny's mid-forties (he is 46), likely the 120s AD. The chapter centers on Danny realizing his eyesight is failing — he can no longer read close text and must hold pages at arm's length — which Heras confirms is presbyopia, untreatable because Danny cannot grind quality corrective lenses. Marcia confronts him with the deeper problem: he has spent twenty years making himself the sole keeper of all his projects (press, contest, survey), so everything will die with him. Danny comes to accept, in conversation with Marcia and then Heras, that his remaining work must shift from chasing steel, the clock, and the ocean crossing to building institutions — a chartered press, an endowed prize fund, and above all the written "ladder" of knowledge that can survive him. The chapter closes with his daughter Ulpia Severa, nine, found late at night copying multiplication tables to spite her brother Lucanus's claim that girls cannot do them; Danny, standing at the far edge of lamplight just to read her figures, resolves that the cipher book must be written soon, while he still can, and that she may be the one to carry it.

## Chapter 39
Danny (middle-aged, presbyopic) focuses entirely on building permanent institutions to outlast him. With Marcia's legal ingenuity he converts the printing press into a formally endowed *collegium*, bringing in a twelve-year-old apprentice named Vitalis to learn block-cutting under the aging Eros. She shelters the invention contest inside a perpetual religious endowment to Minerva, making it a protected rite rather than ownable property. Danny establishes a patronage fund that pays craftsmen to fail systematically at hard problems, putting the elderly Hermes on repeatable steel and recruiting Felix — a forty-year-old former slave who once won the contest and now owns a bronze foundry — to chase the steam-pressure principle. The chapter climaxes with Danny achieving the first wireless radio transmission across open air (twenty paces, no wire, using a Wimshurst machine, copper-wound iron coil, and a galena crystal with cat's-whisker contact), sending SOS and the word FELIX in dot-dash code, with Tyche pressing him to record it as a "rung." Danny quietly accepts that his son Lucanus wants the law, not the workshop, while eleven-year-old Ulpia shows exceptional math ability at Tyche's bench — though Marcia warns him not to raise the girl's hopes against Rome's legal limits on women.

## Chapter 40
Set in Rome, Danny now 48, centering on the years he spends writing a set of foundational scientific and mathematical books in his "homeland script" (English) to survive past his death. The books cover numerals and arithmetic, algebra, geometry, basic calculus (which he admits he cannot prove), Newton's three laws (with explicit uncertainty about the second), planetary astronomy, biology, disease transmission, governance, and trade — each marked with a special "hooked stroke" wherever Danny is guessing rather than certain. Tyche (now 44, the only other living reader of the script) rigorously checks his work — catching a backward seven, pressing him to distinguish what he knows from what he feels — and the preservation plan calls for three dispersed copies plus one sealed in lead, but Tyche confronts him that the script's secret rests in only her single aging head. The chapter closes with the unspoken decision that Ulpia (11–12), who already works tally columns with exceptional skill and writes her sevens correctly, will be taught the homeland script; and Danny leaves one page intentionally blank for chemistry — the atomic structure he once knew but has lost — writing across it an admission of the gap and an exhortation to future readers to fill it themselves.

## Chapter 41
Set at the wharf press over a spring and the following season (Danny now ~50–51, thirty-odd years into his Roman life). Danny sits silently at a bench while Tyche teaches the secret English script and figures to three students: his daughter Ulpia, the fifteen-year-old block-cutter Vitalis, and an eleven- or twelve-year-old Bithynian slave girl named Chloe whom Marcia freed almost immediately. Marcia confronts Danny in the storeroom, arguing that educating Ulpia fully is a cruelty because no path exists for a freedman's daughter to hold a charter or run the business openly, while Danny insists the real locks are fear and ignorance, not iron; they leave it unresolved, and Ulpia herself resolves it by asking Tyche about borrowed names and deciding to pursue the knowledge first. Felix wins that year's contest with an essay arguing that roads carry ideas outward and Rome survives by giving back more than it takes, and Danny awards him the prize. Near season's end the elderly Heras — seventy-nine, walking with two sticks — visits to watch Chloe read and calculate in the homeland script, laughs aloud when she answers his Greek question in the new figures, and on the wharf holds Danny's arm a moment longer than necessary before being carried away in his litter, in a parting that feels final.

## Chapter 42
The death of Heras, Danny's oldest friend and lifelong intellectual sparring partner, eighty years old and dying of heart failure in his room on the Caelian Hill. Over three morning visits Danny sits with him; Heras uses the time to settle the press-society's physic-chest accounts, pass his medical practice to his careful freedman Zoticus, and deliver a final reckoning: he insists that clean water and wound-washing — not the balloon, not gunpowder — was Danny's most life-saving invention, and extracts Danny's admission that he was right. Heras also tells Danny that with his death no one in Rome will be left to call Danny's guesses guesses, and that the "hooked stroke" mark in his books is only a clay copy of a living skeptic. Heras dies on the third morning before Danny arrives, peacefully, his last words a joke about gods and physics made to Zoticus. In the days after, Danny sits alone at the wharf bench, drinks the boiled water they argued over for thirty years, finds the habitual retort forming with no one to say it to, and begins marking his own too-confident sentences with the hooked stroke, asking questions aloud into the empty room — badly, because that is what Heras had done for him.

## Chapter 43
Set in the family's home above Marcia's wharf warehouse, during Danny's fifty-third year (~35–36 years into his Roman life). The chapter centers on a winter dinner at the long warehouse table with Marcia, son Lucanus (22, reading law under advocate Caesius), daughter Ulpia (16), Tyche, and Chloe. Danny and Marcia discuss Lucanus's legal career and its ceiling as a freedman's son, and debate finding Ulpia a husband who will tolerate her exceptional intellect, since as a woman she can never openly use her gifts or hold office. While copying survey notes with Ulpia by lamplight, Danny is caught humming a half-forgotten song from his original world, and in exhaustion-induced honesty tells her it is from "where I'm from"; she asks him to teach it, and they hum it together including the gap where words are lost. The chapter closes with a year-end visit from the aged Macer (now eighty-six), to whom Danny serves coffee — the first cup made from beans returned by the Atlantic expeditions — and who tells Danny "you did all right, boy" before falling asleep; Danny also admits the prize-fund ran hundreds of denarii over budget, which Marcia quietly resolves.

## Chapter 44
Danny, now fifty-six, walks to a newly cut milestone outside Rome where "zero" — the numeral he introduced decades ago — appears officially carved in stone alongside his positional figures. He visits a public library off the Argiletum with Theophanes and finds the shelves lined with identical printed books from his press, including a winning prize submission from a linen-bleacher's widow (the first woman to win the contest), and notices a tax agent has turned the doorman's visitor tally into a covert literacy census using his numerals. He travels south to a shipyard in Gaul where the yard-master Bituitus shows him a third deep-ocean hull being built — two previous ships have already crossed to the western continent — though a longitude-keeping clock (the unsolved "hard half") still prevents precise navigation. On the road home, Danny meets a nine-year-old carter's boy who has memorized Felix's prize tale about a steam-driven ship and acts it out in the dust, fully believing it real; this unsettles Danny because Felix still cannot build the engine and Hermes has not cracked the necessary steel after thirty-three years. Danny returns home to Marcia in wordless exhaustion, then stands alone in the press-room watching Vitalis close up, contemplating how his inventions have all taken on lives independent of him.

## Chapter 45
Danny, now elderly enough to need Ulpia to read small text aloud, is asked by Eudemus to balance provincial accounts from Judaea after the Bar Kokhba war — accounts written in his own numeral system, fair-copied by Ulpia. Over three mornings he and Ulpia work the figures, and Pamphilus relays a discharged auxiliary's report of Roman soldiers starving rebel families out of underground caves over a whole season. Danny confronts the knowledge that his bronze siege bombards, survey grids, accounting system, and camp drainage have all been direct instruments of Rome's destruction of Judaea — walls breached faster, captives enumerated at collapsing prices in a column his own notation made precise. He spends long nights arguing with himself, mourning Heras as the one man who could have made him face the question honestly, and concludes only that he cannot choose a "comfortable stopping point" between knowing and not-knowing his complicity. At the end, Ulpia stops reading at the slave-price column and says nothing; Danny goes home late and sits with Marcia doing ordinary grain calculations, neither naming the other column, until the lamp burns out.

## Chapter 46
Hadrian dies at Baiae of dropsy in July, and Danny hears it from a carter at the wharf, receiving it with quiet relief rather than grief. He reflects on Hadrian as the only restless intellectual equal he had in this world — the only man who could "read him whole" — and mourns not the man but the strange companionship of their cautious, mutually dangerous relationship. The new emperor, Antoninus, is a steady stay-at-home type with no appetite for exploration, so Danny calculates the imperial ocean program will quietly lapse — but the crossings, the Garonne-mouth collegium, and the hull-building are privately endowed on rents and merchant investment (rubber, tobacco, resins) and will survive without crown patronage. He also quietly acknowledges, and finally releases, the private hope he carried for thirty years — that an Atlantic crossing might lead him back to his own time — feeling it grow lighter and then simply gone. That evening his daughter Ulpia Severa, twenty, is already reviewing the western survey charts and handling logistics; they plan to keep Bituitus on retainer, send copies to Lucanus for safekeeping, and keep training Chloe in the script — and Danny sees the work will go on in other hands.

## Chapter 47
Danny suffers a sudden cardiac episode on the wharf while tallying hides — his chest seizes and he nearly blacks out, but it passes — and he reflects on years of bodily decline: failing eyesight, arthritic hands so swollen Marcia's wedding ring will not come off. Unable to write small any longer, he has been dictating his English science books to Tyche (now 57) and Ulpia (now 24), who record them faithfully, Tyche holding him to his earlier doubts and Ulpia pressing him on the epistemics of every claim. In a pivotal act of acceptance he formally closes his lifelong clock-and-escapement project, dictating a frank confession of failure into the books and announcing a standing prize (modeled on the Macer Prize) for whoever solves it; he also gives his quarantine doctrine to the physician Zoticus against the coming eastern plague. He has an audience with the new emperor Antoninus Pius, pitching iron railways on Roman roads, but Antoninus politely declines, and Danny accepts the closed door, trusting the books to find a future reader. Finally the failing Macer — now 94, carried in by servants — has a brief lucid moment, recognizes Danny, and quips that they have outlived all the clever ones; Danny and Marcia sit in silence knowing Macer has little time left.

## Chapter 48
Danny (now 63–65, under Antoninus Pius) experiences a cascade of losses: Pamphilus, the first man he ever freed, dies quietly past seventy; Eros the letterer dies after training his replacement Vitalis; and Macer dies at 96 in his son's house, leaving Danny only his heavy gold ring with a will clause calling him simply "the Thulean." Danny reflects on Macer as cage and protector both, and places the ring in a box with the glass from his home era. That autumn Danny works on two documents at once: a quarantine protocol (fourteen-day isolation, water separation) for Zoticus, now 50 and running the physician line, and a private English-cipher confession that he always knew the Americas were inhabited but withheld it to keep the Atlantic program alive. He makes a failed appeal to Antoninus Pius's secretary for a steam-and-iron-roads program, and notes the press, warehouse, and books now run without him — Vitalis, Chloe, Tyche, and Ulpia have outgrown his guidance. Most devastatingly, Marcia, thirteen years his junior, is diagnosed with inoperable liver cancer; she completes a binding legal deed protecting the enterprise from her family's cousins before dying that same autumn, and the chapter closes with Danny sitting in darkness unable to find a single number in the open day-book, reaching in the night for a sound of breathing no longer there.

## Chapter 49
Set during a winter late in Antoninus Pius's reign, an aging and nearly blind Danny undertakes a methodical "securing" of the knowledge ladder he has built — verifying the three full cipher copies and the partial Latin treatise, and sealing them in redundant arrangements (a mobile working copy kept by Tyche and Chloe, a legally locked collegium copy, and a lead-sealed time-capsule buried under the press-room's great screw). He meets the physician Zoticus to hand over written quarantine protocols, warning of a devastating plague coming from the east fifteen to twenty years after Danny is dead. Lucanus (now 38, a practicing advocate) reviews the deed of trust, closes two legal gaps, and observes that Danny has built something that runs outward from Rome rather than toward it. In a final private scene, Danny and Tyche (both now old) open the hidden box holding his dead phone, Macer's gold ring, and his citizenship tablet, and Danny resolves to bury all three with his body — the phone is not a "rung" anyone can climb and could only endanger whoever held it — while the books remain above ground to speak for him.

## Chapter 50
Titled "Going," set over Danny's final weeks and hours at the wharf storeroom, where he is dying of congestive heart failure (fluid in both lungs) at approximately 70 years old, having not spoken his native tongue aloud in 57 years. The physician Zoticus (Heras's pupil's pupil) confirms nothing can be done. Ulpia is present for most of the days, reading the cipher books back to Danny and keeping the accounts at Marcia's old table; she asks one last time where he truly learned his knowledge, and he gives the old false answer about "the cold north." Lucanus visits on rest-days — now 43 and gray-templed — and brings Danny a clay toy of an unbuilt steam-cart without explanation; father and son sit in silence, their relationship frayed but no longer sharp. Danny dies at night with Tyche, now 70 herself, holding his hands and reciting the quarter's figures aloud to give his fading mind something to follow; his final conscious vision is of a field and Stonehenge at dawn — the morning he arrived as a frightened 17-year-old. (Continuity note: this names the arrival site as a field near Stonehenge in Britain, where ch01–03 render the arrival as walking into the city of Rome and recognizing the intact Colosseum — a discrepancy to check.)

## Chapter 51
Composed of two documents. First, a brief final entry from Danny's private notes (in the "homeland script") recording a quiet conversation with Tyche, in which he reflects that quarantine protocols he designed for western sea voyages have been saving lives in eastern army camps for eleven years — an unintended benefit he will not live to see — after which Tyche reminds him to file a report. The bulk is a letter written at Rome by Gaius Norbanus Rufus to his friend Sextus Caecilius Priscus, dated the day before the Ides of June in the consulship of Clarus and Cethegus, looking back on Danny ("the Thulean") as a figure of the past. Rufus visits the public printed library in the Argiletum portico, confirms Danny's numerals are now in everyday use, and summarizes what "everyone knows": the Thulean came from the far north, introduced the press and numerals, made fire fly in a cloth balloon, sold dubious microscopes, yet also taught water-boiling and quarantine still saving Roman soldiers during the plague of Verus's campaigns. He reports that Danny was buried with a polished black tablet — an amulet of his homeland (his phone) — alongside his bronze citizenship plate, and that a very old freedwoman (clearly Tyche) still keeps the library's books and teaches Danny's "marks" to a slave-girl who teaches two more, spreading the literacy chain.

## Chapter 52
Set roughly a generation after Danny's death, in Rome, following Procula, a freeborn woman of about twenty who grew up in the print-shop founded under Danny's legacy fund. Procula is one of the secret keepers of Danny's English cipher script, trained in a matrilineal chain of literacy stretching back to Tyche, and she takes on building a working clock from Danny's honest but incomplete drawings, guided by his self-annotated failures. Through patient experiment she discovers the escapement principle — that the swinging bar must rule the wheel, not the reverse — and on the thirteenth attempt produces the first ticking, regulated pendulum clock, which she demonstrates at the annual Quinquatrus spring contest to a crowd as astonished as those who first saw the balloon. She records her solution in the fund's book exactly where Danny left the page blank, omitting his "I am guessing" mark for the first time because she is certain, and teaches a boy named Gaius the mechanism. The chapter closes with Procula recognizing the clock still drifts with temperature and cannot yet serve the unsolved longitude problem Danny described — a prize still waiting — and she accepts she will not reach that rung herself but commits to writing down her solution and teaching two others to carry the ladder further.

## Chapter 53
Set centuries after Danny's death, the coda depicts an unnamed pilot and her crew making landfall on an unknown western shore after thirty-one days out of sight of land, guided to their position by a pendulum clock — the box in its gimbal cradle — that keeps home-port time against the ship's observed solar noon, at last solving the longitude problem. The crew silently takes in a coastline dark with forest and a beach where indigenous figures stand at the waterline, fire burning, one arm raised in an ambiguous gesture. The pilot records the soundings, the bearing, the smoke, and the people faithfully in the ship's log but writes nothing of what it means, because she does not know. In the front of that printed log, worn nearly illegible through generations of copying, is Danny's name — not read as a man, just words at the foot of a page, like *finis*. The book closes on the pilot at the rail looking west at the new country while the clock below decks keeps its steady knock, still counting out the hour of the home yard to a shore that has never had an hour told to it before.
