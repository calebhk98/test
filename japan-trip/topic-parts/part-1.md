## Temples and Buddhism

| What | Day | Address | Website | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Senso-ji | 2 | 2-3-1 Asakusa, Taito-ku, Tokyo 111-0032 | https://www.senso-ji.jp/ | Tokyo's oldest Buddhist temple, founded in 645 AD and dedicated to Kannon, goddess of mercy, reached along the covered Nakamise-dori shopping street to the huge red Kaminarimon lantern gate, the main hall and a five-story pagoda. A Buddhist temple is marked by a heavier, roofed gate (sanmon) with guardian statues and incense, unlike a Shinto shrine's simple torii - and Senso-ji shows both at once, since Asakusa Shrine sits inside the same grounds. Free to enter, grounds always open, main hall roughly 6am-5pm. Nakamise-dori is too dense for a stroller by mid-morning, so carry rather than push. |
| Zojoji Temple | 4 | 4-7-35 Shibakoen, Minato-ku, Tokyo (address per site research; postal code unverified) | https://www.zojoji.or.jp/ | The Tokugawa shoguns' family temple, fronted by the towering Sangedatsumon gate and rows of small stone jizo statues in red knit caps. Free, far less crowded than Senso-ji, and stands directly in front of Tokyo Tower for a classic old-gate, old-tower photo. Flat paved grounds throughout, no stairs, fully stroller-friendly. |
| Kotoku-in (Great Buddha) | 7 | 4-2-28 Hase, Kamakura, Kanagawa 248-0016 | https://www.kotoku-in.jp/ | A 13.4m bronze seated Buddha cast in 1252, sitting in the open air since its housing hall was washed away by a tsunami centuries ago - one of Japan's most recognizable single images. ¥300 per adult; the open gravel forecourt is fully stroller-passable, and an optional stairs-only climb into the statue's interior is +¥50. |
| Hase-dera | 7 | Hase, Kamakura, Kanagawa (nearest station Hase, Enoden; address unverified) | https://www.hasedera.jp/ | A Buddhist temple famous for a 9.18m gilded wooden statue of the eleven-headed Kannon, plus a hillside garden with sea views over Kamakura and hundreds of small Jizo statues left by grieving parents. ¥400 per adult; the terraced garden is reached by steep stairs the stroller cannot manage, so carrier only, though the lower grounds are flat. |
| To-ji | 15 | 1 Kujocho, Minami-ku, Kyoto 601-8473 | https://toji.or.jp/ | Head temple of Shingon Buddhism, free to enter, with a five-story pagoda towering over its open gravel grounds. The Kobo-ichi flea-and-food market, tied to founder Kobo Daishi's death anniversary, fills the grounds on the 21st of every month with roughly 1,000 stalls. Main paths are stroller-workable; the market itself is a tight press of stalls better suited to a carrier. |
| Tenryu-ji | 17 | 68 Susukinobaba-cho, Saga Tenryuji, Ukyo-ku, Kyoto 616-8385 | https://www.tenryuji.com/ | A UNESCO World Heritage Zen temple founded in 1339, head of its own Rinzai-sect lineage. Its Sogenchi Garden, one of Japan's oldest surviving garden designs, is built around a pond and uses the wooded Arashiyama hillside behind it as "borrowed scenery." ¥500 per adult; today's ticket covers the garden only, not the hall interior. Flat gravel loop, stroller-workable but often busy. |
| Kiyomizu-dera | 18 | 1-294 Kiyomizu, Higashiyama-ku, Kyoto 605-0862 | https://www.kiyomizudera.or.jp/ | Founded in 778 and named for a waterfall on the grounds; the current main hall dates to a 1633 rebuild. Its wooden stage juts 13m out over the hillside on cypress pillars joined without nails, giving one of Kyoto's best views over the wooded hillside and city. ¥500 per adult, UNESCO World Heritage; the approach lanes and grounds are uneven worn stone, manageable with a stroller on the main paths but expect to fold it on the steepest stretches. |
| Kinkaku-ji | 19 | 1 Kinkakuji-cho, Kita-ku, Kyoto 603-8361 | https://www.shokoku-ji.jp/kinkakuji/ | A three-story Zen pavilion with its top two floors entirely gold-leafed, set over a reflecting pond and among Japan's most recognizable images. Built in 1397 as a shogun's retirement villa; the building standing today is a 1955 reconstruction after a novice monk burned the original to the ground in 1950. ¥500 per adult, exterior and garden walk only, no entry into the pavilion; paved path, stroller-workable. |
| Ryoan-ji | 19 | 13 Ryoanji Goryonoshitacho, Ukyo-ku, Kyoto 616-8001 | https://www.ryoanji.jp/ | Japan's most famous karesansui, or dry Zen rock garden: raked white gravel with fifteen stones arranged so that from any single point on the viewing veranda at least one stone is always hidden from view. ¥600 per adult; viewed from the temple hall's raised wooden veranda, shoes off at the entrance, so it's a carrier stop rather than a stroller one. |
| Byodo-in | 21 | 116 Renge, Uji-shi, Kyoto 611-0021 | https://www.byodoin.or.jp/ | An 11th-century Buddhist temple whose Phoenix Hall, built in 1053, is the building shown on the back of the ¥10 coin - worth holding one up to compare in person. ¥600 covers the pond garden, where the hall's reflection is the classic photo, plus the Hoshokan treasure museum; the Phoenix Hall interior is a separate timed ¥300 ticket down a single narrow walkway, not stroller-accessible. Grounds and museum are otherwise flat, paved and stroller-friendly. |
| Daisho-in Temple | 25 | 210 Miyajima-cho, Hatsukaichi-shi, Hiroshima-ken 739-0588 (nearest station: Miyajima pier, about 15 min walk) | https://daisho-in.com/ | A working Shingon Buddhist mountain temple at the base of Mt. Misen, founded in 806, with an entrance stairway lined with spinning metal sutra wheels each inscribed with a sutra a visitor can "read" just by turning it. The grounds also hold a dim cave lined with 88 icons standing in for the Shikoku pilgrimage and a hillside of hundreds of rakan statues, each with a distinct carved face. Free; the lower grounds are workable but the stepped approach beyond calls for a carrier. |
| Todai-ji Daibutsuden | 20 | 406-1 Zoshi-cho, Nara 630-8211 | https://www.todaiji.or.jp/ | The Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) is one of the largest wooden buildings in the world, even though today's structure is a smaller Edo-period rebuild of a bigger 8th-century original. Inside sits the Daibutsu, a 15m bronze seated Buddha cast in 752 CE, one of the largest bronze Buddha statues on Earth. ¥800 per adult; flat, paved, stroller-friendly paths from the park entrance, though the wild deer along the approach can nip or headbutt at a toddler's eye level. |

## Shinto shrines

| What | Day | Address | Website | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asakusa Shrine | 2 | 2-3-1 Asakusa, Taito-ku, Tokyo 111-0032 (within Senso-ji's grounds) | https://www.asakusajinja.jp/ | A Shinto shrine honoring kami rather than the Buddha, marked by a torii gate and a stone purification basin instead of a temple's heavier, roofed sanmon gate - it sits just inside the grounds of the Buddhist Senso-ji next door, a reminder that after 1,400 years of Shinto-Buddhist blending, most old sites in Japan hold both traditions rather than keeping them apart. Free to enter. |
| Meiji Jingu | 5 | 1-1 Yoyogikamizonocho, Shibuya-ku, Tokyo 151-8557 | https://www.meijijingu.or.jp/ | Tokyo's grandest Shinto shrine, built in 1920 for the deified Emperor Meiji and Empress Shoken, reached along a gravel forest path under a huge wooden torii. The shrine buildings are free; the paid Inner Garden is a separate, older Edo-period strolling garden with an iris pond, ¥500 per adult. Wide gravel paths, gentle grade, fully stroller-passable. |
| Nezu Shrine | 6 | 1-28-9 Nezu, Bunkyo-ku, Tokyo 113-0031 | https://www.nedujinja.or.jp/ | A 1706 Shinto shrine with a row of vermillion torii gates climbing a small hillside, a miniature echo of Kyoto's Fushimi Inari, and ornately carved gates rare for their age to have survived intact. Free, a quiet, uncrowded alternative to the trip's bigger-name shrines. The torii-tunnel path has a short slope but is stroller-manageable at an easy pace; the azalea-garden fee is seasonal (spring) only, so free on this visit. |
| Fushimi Inari Taisha | 15 | 68 Fukakusa Yabunouchicho, Fushimi-ku, Kyoto 612-0882 | https://inari.jp/ | A Shinto shrine to Inari, god of rice and prosperity, famous for the Senbon Torii - thousands of vermilion gates forming a tunnel up the mountainside, each privately paid for by a business or individual as a thank-offering. Free, open 24 hours; the paved loop to the Yotsutsuji lookout is stroller-workable, but the mountain turns to stone steps beyond that, carrier-only to the summit. |
| Heian Jingu | 16 | 97 Okazaki Nishitennocho, Sakyo-ku, Kyoto 606-8341 | https://www.heianjingu.or.jp/ | Younger than it looks: built in 1895 as a roughly 5/8-scale replica of the original 8th-century Imperial Palace's main ceremonial hall, marking the 1100th anniversary of Kyoto's founding as capital. The shrine grounds and huge orange torii are free; the Shin-en strolling pond garden behind it is a separate paid add-on, ¥600 per adult, with stepping-stone crossings a carrier handles more easily than a stroller. |
| Kitano Tenmangu | 19 | Kitano Tenmangu, Bakuro-cho, Kamigyo-ku, Kyoto 602-8386 | https://kitanotenmangu.or.jp/ | Free shrine dedicated to Sugawara no Michizane, the Heian-era scholar-official enshrined as the god of learning. The Tenjin-ichi flea-and-food market, marking his death anniversary, fills the grounds and surrounding streets with up to around 1,000 stalls on the 25th of every month. Open shrine paths take a stroller fine; the packed market stalls are a tighter, carrier-only squeeze. |
| Kasuga Taisha | 20 | 160 Kasuganocho, Nara 630-8212 | https://www.kasugataisha.or.jp/ | Nara's principal Shinto shrine, founded in 768 CE, famous for roughly 3,000 bronze and stone lanterns lining its forest approach, lit twice a year for lantern festivals. The kilometre-long lantern approach is free; the paid inner precinct (Chumon gate and moss garden), ¥500 per adult, has gravel paths slightly uneven enough that a carrier works better than a stroller. |
| Itsukushima Shrine | 25 | 1-1 Miyajima-cho, Hatsukaichi, Hiroshima 739-0588 | https://www.itsukushimajinja.jp/ | A Shinto shrine built out over the tidal flats on wooden pillars, founded in the 6th century and rebuilt in its present form in 1168 - Miyajima itself has long been considered sacred ground, historically closed to births and deaths. The famous vermilion torii stands about 200m offshore and appears to float on the water only at high tide; at low tide the sand flats are exposed and you can walk out to its base, so which one a visitor gets depends entirely on the tide table for the day. ¥300 per adult; the raised walkways are flat and stroller-navigable, and an elevator underpass avoids the one stairway. |
| Namba Yasaka Shrine | 27 | 2-9-19 Motomachi, Naniwa-ku, Osaka 556-0016 | https://nambayasaka.jp/ | A Shinto shrine whose main hall is built in the shape of an enormous lion's head, about 12m tall with its mouth open as if swallowing evil spirits - a striking piece of architecture unlike anything else on this trip. Free; a flat, ground-level courtyard with no stairs and nothing to enter. |

## Castles and fortifications

| What | Day | Address | Website | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Imperial Palace East Gardens | 4 | 1-1 Chiyoda, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo 100-8111 | https://www.kunaicho.go.jp/event/higashigyoen/higashigyoen.html | The public portion of the Imperial Palace grounds - the former Edo Castle's inner citadel, opened as a garden in 1968, with the stone Tenshudai foundation (the burned keep's base) still standing. Free, no admission fee or advance booking needed, same-day entry at the gate; closed Mondays, Fridays, and late December through early January. Flat gravel paths throughout except the Tenshudai's stone stairs, which need a carrier. |
| Nijo Castle | 19 | 541 Nijojo-cho, Nakagyo-ku, Kyoto 604-8301 | https://nijo-jocastle.city.kyoto.lg.jp/ | Built in 1603 as the Kyoto residence of the Tokugawa shogun, Japan's military ruler - a deliberate show of shogunal power inside the emperor's own capital. The Ninomaru Palace's corridors have "nightingale floors," floorboards engineered to chirp underfoot as a low-tech intruder alarm, still audible today. ¥1,300 per adult covers both the grounds/garden and the shoeless, no-photography palace interior; leave the stroller at the entrance for the interior, though the outer grounds are gravel and stroller-workable. |
| Hiroshima Castle | 26 | 21-1 Motomachi, Naka-ku, Hiroshima 730-0011 | https://hiroshimacastle.jp/ | A five-story castle keep, originally built in the 1590s and destroyed instantly in the 1945 bombing; what had stood since 1958 was a concrete reconstruction closely following the original exterior design, with a small history museum inside. The keep closed permanently on March 22, 2026 for safety, due to concrete deterioration and aging infrastructure, ahead of a planned wooden rebuild - only the exterior, grounds and moat path can be seen on this visit, not the interior museum. Grounds and moat path are flat and fully stroller-workable. |
| Osaka Castle keep | 29 | 1-1 Osakajo, Chuo-ku, Osaka 540-0002 | https://www.osakacastle.net/ | The keep looks like a wooden Sengoku-era castle from the outside but is not one: Toyotomi Hideyoshi's original 16th-century keep burned in 1615, a Tokugawa-era rebuild burned again in 1665, and what stands today is a 1931 reinforced-concrete reconstruction sitting on the surviving Toyotomi-era stone base. Inside is a museum on Hideyoshi and the castle town, an 8th-floor observation level, and critically a lift, which is why this castle works with a stroller-and-carrier family when a genuine wooden keep like Himeji's does not. ¥600 per adult, infants free; park grounds are free and open all day, the stroller is parked at the entrance while the party rides up. |
