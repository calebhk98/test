### What Kyoto actually is

Kyoto was Japan's imperial capital for over a thousand years, from 794 until
1868, when the emperor moved to Tokyo after the Meiji Restoration. Almost
everything a visitor associates with "traditional Japan" - the wooden temple
halls, the rock gardens, the geisha and maiko, the seasonal court aesthetics of
cherry blossoms and maple leaves - was built up over that millennium as the
capital's own culture, not a museum reconstruction of it. That distinction
matters because Kyoto's historic core is mostly the *real thing*, standing on
its original site, not a postwar rebuild.

The reason it's still standing is a specific, documented decision. Kyoto was
largely spared the firebombing that flattened most other Japanese cities in
1944-45, and in July 1945 US Secretary of War Henry Stimson personally had
Kyoto struck from the shortlist for the atomic bomb, arguing the city's
cultural and historical weight outweighed its military value as a target
[VERIFIED, blog.nuclearsecrecy.com "The Kyoto misconception," Getty
Foundation "From Kyoto to Baghdad to Tehran"]. The popular story that he did
it because he honeymooned there is a myth historians have since debunked
[VERIFIED, blog.nuclearsecrecy.com]; the real reasons were a mix of cultural
argument and Stimson's postwar political calculations. The practical result
for a traveller in 2026: Kyoto has roughly 2,000 temples and shrines, several
UNESCO World Heritage sites, and entire streetscapes of pre-modern wooden
buildings that Tokyo and Osaka simply do not have, because Tokyo and Osaka
were rebuilt from the ground up after 1945.

### The texture of the city

Kyoto sits in a flat basin ringed on three sides by mountains, and the city
itself is a low-rise grid, laid out on a Chinese-style checkerboard plan in
794 and still legible today in the numbered, named streets (Shijo-dori,
Karasuma-dori, and so on). Three zones matter for a visitor:

- **Higashiyama** (the eastern foothills): where the mountains meet the grid,
  a dense band of temples, sloped stone-paved lanes (Sannenzaka, Ninenzaka),
  and the old geisha quarter of Gion. This is hilly, narrow, and crowded -
  the postcard Kyoto, and the hardest part of the city to push a stroller
  through comfortably.
- **The flat central grid** (Shijo-Kawaramachi, Nishiki Market, the Nishijin
  weaving district, Kyoto Station): shopping streets, department-store
  basements (depachika), covered arcades, and machiya - the narrow wooden
  townhouses with a shopfront facing the street and rooms running back from
  it. Flat, walkable, and stroller-friendly.
- **Arashiyama** (the west): a separate riverside district at the foot of its
  own hills - bamboo grove, temple gardens, a scenic gorge railway - more a
  half-day excursion than part of the central grid.

### How Kyoto differs from Tokyo and Osaka, concretely

Within an hour of arriving, a traveller coming from Tokyo would notice: no
skyscrapers (a long-standing height-limit and viewshed-protection policy
keeps the skyline low so temple roofs and the surrounding hills stay visible),
far fewer neon signs, and a much quieter, more residential streetscape even
in the center. Coming from Osaka, the difference is tone: Osaka is loud,
commercial, food-and-comedy-forward; Kyoto is reserved and formal, with a
real live geisha/maiko economy still operating in Gion and Pontocho rather
than a nostalgia act.

The single most important practical difference for this trip: **Kyoto is a
bus city, not a subway city.** Tokyo and Osaka both have extensive subway
networks that get most visitors most places. Kyoto has only two subway
lines (Karasuma running north-south, Tozai running east-west, crossing once
near Kyoto City Hall) - nowhere near enough to cover the temple districts.
The workhorse is the city bus network, and that has real consequences with a
stroller: buses board through a narrow rear door, fares are paid by IC card
tap or coin on exit at the front, buses can be standing-room-only during peak
temple hours, and folding a stroller one-handed while holding an infant on a
moving bus is a genuinely different task than gliding down a flat subway
platform. This itinerary already leans on walking and taxis at the points
where a packed bus with two infants would be the wrong call (see Day 16's
taxi leg back from Nanzenji).

### Temple fatigue is real

After two or three days of gates, gravel courtyards, and ¥500 admission
temples that start to blur together, most travellers - let alone travellers
managing two nap schedules - hit a wall. Over a 10-night stay, the honest fix
is pacing: mix temple days with market/food days, garden days, and at least
one deliberately low-content day with no headline sight at all (this segment
already does that on Day 23). Don't stack two major temple-heavy days
back-to-back, and treat any day with more than two paid admissions as a long
day requiring a correspondingly light day on either side.

### What Kyoto is world-class at, and what it isn't

Kyoto is one of the best places on Earth for: pre-modern Japanese
architecture and garden design at a density found nowhere else; traditional
craft (textiles, ceramics, lacquerware, sake) still made where it was
invented; and a walkable historic core you can experience on foot and by bus
without a car. It is *not* the place for modern pop culture, nightlife,
shopping-mall-scale retail, or big-city energy - that's Tokyo and Osaka's
job. It is also not a place to "do" quickly; a two-night stopover barely
scratches Higashiyama alone, so the 10-night stay in this itinerary is
appropriately generous, provided the pacing rule above is respected.
