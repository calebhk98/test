# Hiroshima and Miyajima

Hiroshima today is a rebuilt, modern, green city of about 1.2 million people, spread
across a river delta where six branches of the Ota River fan out to the Seto Inland Sea.
It has wide boulevards, a working streetcar network, a baseball team (the Carp) the whole
city follows, department stores, an active downtown shopping arcade, and a skyline that
looks like any other mid-size Japanese city. This surprises a lot of first-time visitors,
who arrive expecting a memorial site and instead find a normal, functioning, fairly
prosperous city going about its business - commuters, students, shoppers - with the Peace
Memorial Park sitting in the middle of it rather than defining the whole place. That
contrast is deliberate and worth understanding before arriving: Hiroshima's identity is
not "the atomic bomb city," it is a city that was destroyed and rebuilt, and the rebuilding
itself, done with wide fire-break boulevards and deliberate green space, is part of the
story.

The history has to be held seriously, and this itinerary is honest that it takes real
planning, not just goodwill. The Peace Memorial Museum is confronting. It documents the
bombing through personal artifacts, photographs, and testimony, and the lower-floor
exhibits in particular are not softened for visitors. There is no age restriction and no
requirement to see everything, but with two children under two in the party, the plan
already built in a sensible response: it doesn't insist the whole family go through the
museum together. One or two adults go through while a third stays outside in the park with
the children, and the party can swap partway through if more than one adult wants to see
it. That is the right way to handle it - not a compromise, but the standard approach
recommended by people who've actually visited with young children. The surrounding Peace
Memorial Park itself is a different register entirely: open green riverside space, the
Atomic Bomb Dome viewed from outside (its structure is too fragile to enter), the
Children's Peace Monument with its cranes, walking paths along the river. It's possible,
and arguably better with infants, to spend real time in the park itself without pushing
everyone through the museum's most difficult rooms.

The everyday texture of the city is worth knowing too. The Hiroden streetcar network is
the main way to get around downtown - flat citywide fares, frequent cars, some of them
genuinely vintage rolling stock - and it is the cheap, easy way this plan already gets the
party between the station, Peace Park, Shukkei-en, and the castle. The city's food
identity centers on Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki, which is layered (noodles, cabbage, egg,
and toppings stacked and griddled rather than mixed into a batter like the Osaka version) -
a genuinely different dish, not a regional variant of the same thing - and on oysters,
which the Inland Sea is well known for. And a day trip away sits Miyajima, which functions
as a completely separate world: a small island across a strait, with its own shrine, its
own mountain (Misen), its own pace, reached by a short ferry ride that is itself part of
the experience (the floating torii gate is one of Japan's most photographed sights).

Compared to Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Hiroshima's other three stops on this trip, Hiroshima
is the smallest and calmest city on the itinerary - noticeably less crowded streets, a
slower pace, and a downtown that's walkable and human-scaled in a way none of the bigger
three cities are. What it's best at: a serious, well-paced history day handled with care,
an easy and rewarding island day trip, good food, and a genuinely relaxed few days after
Kyoto's density. What it is not right for: nightlife or big-city energy (there isn't much
after dark outside Hondori arcade), a wide range of major museums or shopping beyond what's
already in the plan, or a rushed visit - the museum and the island day both deserve the
unhurried treatment this plan already gives them.
