# Tokyo

*Covers Days 1-10 (Oct 7-16 2026), based at Hotel Livemax Higashi-Ueno, 3-5-1 Motoasakusa,
Taito-ku. Written against MASTER-BRIEF.md and days-01-10-tokyo.md.*

---

Tokyo is not a city with a downtown the way most first-time visitors picture one. It is
closer to a cluster of a dozen or so full-sized city centres - Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ueno,
Asakusa, Marunouchi/Ginza, Ikebukuro, Roppongi - each with its own train station, its own
department stores, its own restaurant scene, all stitched together by the densest urban
rail network on earth. Greater Tokyo holds around 37 million people, more than all of
Canada, spread across an area roughly the size of a mid-sized US metro. The practical
consequence for a traveller is that you don't "go downtown" once and see the city; you
pick one centre per outing, spend three or four hours there, and move to a different one
for the afternoon or the next day. That is exactly the shape of the current plan: a
Shibuya day, a Shinjuku half-day, a Marunouchi half-day, an Asakusa/Skytree day, each
self-contained.

Walking around feels different depending on which half of the city you are in, and this
matters directly for this trip because the hotel sits on one side of a real divide. The
west side - Shinjuku, Shibuya, Harajuku - is the Tokyo of the postcards: glass towers,
vertical neon, department stores stacked eight storeys high, crowds that move fast and
in sync. It is thrilling for an hour and tiring for a whole day, especially with a
stroller cutting through rush-hour foot traffic. The east side - Asakusa, Ueno, Yanaka,
the old shitamachi ("low city") - is lower-rise, older in street pattern, and slower.
Temples sit next to century-old wooden shopfronts, alleys are narrower but far less
crowded outside a few honeypot streets like Nakamise-dori, and the whole area closes
earlier and feels more like a neighbourhood than a spectacle. Hotel Livemax
Higashi-Ueno is deep in this east side. Day to day that means: mornings and evenings
around the hotel are quiet and walkable with a stroller; the loud, dense, "wow" Tokyo of
Shibuya and Shinjuku is a 20-30 minute train ride away and is best treated as a
deliberate excursion, not the default backdrop of the trip. It also means cheaper food
and lodging near base, and a gentler re-entry after a big day out west.

Compared with the other four stops on this trip, the contrast is stark within an hour of
arriving anywhere. Kyoto is one-tenth the population, low-rise almost everywhere outside
the station, and organised around wooden temples and preserved streets rather than
skyscrapers - it feels like a large historic town, not a metropolis. Kawaguchiko is a
lake-and-mountain resort area with a few thousand residents; the pace is rural, Mount
Fuji dominates the skyline instead of towers, and there is no metro, no convenience
store on every corner, no crowd noise. Hiroshima is a mid-sized, rebuilt post-war city,
flat, easy, centred on a single tram network and the Peace Park, with none of Tokyo's
scale or verticality. Osaka is the closest in energy - dense, neon, food-obsessed - but
it is one-third the size, more compact, and rawer and more informal than Tokyo's
polish. A traveller landing in Tokyo after any of the others (or vice versa) will notice
the difference in the first train ride: platforms are longer, trains more frequent,
signage more layered, and there are simply more people moving at once.

On practicalities: transport is excellent and this is Tokyo's genuine superpower - trains
run every 3-5 minutes on core lines, are almost always on time, and English signage,
announcements, and ticket machines are thorough on JR and Metro (less so on some private
lines' local details, but never a real barrier). Stroller access is good on platforms
with elevators, patchy at smaller station exits, and the real obstacle is crowd density,
not infrastructure - Nakamise-dori or Shibuya Scramble at peak times are carrier
situations, not stroller ones, purely because of how many people are moving through a
narrow space at once. Tokyo is expensive by Japan's internal standards - the priciest of
this trip's five stops for lodging and dining out - though chain restaurants and
konbini keep a self-catering, budget-conscious trip very workable, as this itinerary's
heavy kitchenette use reflects. Food culture spans from ¥380 gyudon counters to
Michelin-starred sushi within the same block, which is unusual even by Japanese
standards.

What Tokyo is genuinely best in the world at: scale and density done well - the transit
system, the sheer range of museums and only-here experiences (teamLab, Ghibli Museum,
depachika food halls, Akihabara), and a food scene with more depth per square kilometre
than anywhere else on earth. What it is not the right place for: quiet, nature, or a
sense of "old Japan" as the dominant note - for that, this trip is right to lean on
Kyoto, Kawaguchiko, and the shitamachi pockets (Yanaka, Asakusa) rather than expecting
Tokyo itself to deliver it.
