# ROME — Roman Republic — STATE (210 BC)

*Player-facing. What the Republic knows of itself. Pre-divergence: Rome behaves as its real self until ~100 BC.*

## Identity
- The **Roman Republic**, governed by elected consuls and the Senate. No king; a citizen-soldier state with a fierce martial and civic tradition.
- Master of the Italian peninsula, ruling through a web of **alliances with the Italian peoples (socii)** rather than direct annexation — that web has been tested by Cannae and has, largely, held.
- Now also an **overseas power**: holder of provinces beyond Italy for the first time in its history.

## Extent
- Peninsular Italy south of the Alps (including **Cisalpine Gaul**, secured by defeat of the Po valley Gauls and Roman colonization).
- **Sicily** — Rome's first overseas province (from 241 BC), now partially contested again by Carthaginian pressure but largely held; Syracuse recaptured (212 BC).
- **Sardinia and Corsica** — seized opportunistically after the First Punic War; held as a second province.
- **Spain** — Roman armies operating there actively, contesting Carthage's base of power; not a province yet, but a front.
- **Southern Italy** — formally held but war-ravaged; Capua recaptured; Hannibal still roams the south.

## Population
- ~3–4 million in the Roman–Italian confederation (citizens plus allied manpower). Some allied communities lost or wavered (Capua, parts of Bruttium and Samnium), but the core — Latins, most Italian allies — remains loyal and contributing.
- The deep levy has absorbed catastrophic losses and continues to furnish armies; this is the Republic's single greatest strategic asset.

## Treasury & economy
- **Strained to the edge.** Two enormous wars within a generation — the naval attrition of the First Punic War, then the full weight of Hannibal in Italy — have drained coin, consumed productive farmland in the south, and forced emergency measures (emergency levies, contributions from allies, debasement of coinage where necessary).
- Revenue disrupted by Hannibal's ravaging of the south; Sicilian grain now more important than ever.
- The Republic sustains itself on **allied manpower and land wealth more than on coin reserves**. Recovery is possible but not yet begun.

## Military
- Now simultaneously a **naval and land power** — the war-fleet built against Carthage in the 260s BC has become a permanent institution; Rome controls the western Mediterranean sea-lanes.
- The **manipular legions**, raised from citizens and allies, have absorbed losses at the Trebia, Trasimene, and Cannae that would have destroyed a lesser state. Veterans of years of Italian campaigning are hard, experienced, and unbroken in will.
- **Fabian strategy** has been validated: Rome has learned to fight a war of attrition, to refuse decisive engagement when disadvantaged, to contest Hannibal indirectly while pressing other fronts.
- Significant forces now deployed on multiple fronts simultaneously: Italy (shadowing Hannibal), Sicily (consolidating), Spain (pressing Carthage's base).
- Losses have been appalling — Cannae alone stripped the Senate and equestrian order of hundreds — but the manpower reserve remains deep.

## Tech & institutions
- Military engineering, roads (**Via Appia** and growing network), fortified camps, siegecraft (demonstrated at Syracuse under Marcellus against Archimedes' engines).
- **Naval competence** now genuine and institutionalized, not improvised.
- Republican institutions holding under pressure: the Senate has shown strategic consistency across years of crisis; the office of Dictator used and relinquished as designed.
- Pragmatic military adaptation continues — learning from each enemy encountered.

## Unrest drivers
- **War exhaustion**: a generation of near-continuous war, enormous losses, ruinous expense.
- **Allied defections**: Capua (recaptured), parts of Bruttium, Samnium, and Lucania turned to Hannibal after Cannae — a wound to the confederation's unity, not yet healed.
- **Hannibal in Italy**: the enemy army still moves in the south; the psychological and economic strain is ongoing.
- **Sicily contested**: the island that cost the First Punic War is again a theater; Syracuse's fall (to Rome) settles it somewhat, but it remains a drain.
- Underlying patrician–plebeian tensions and questions of war's burden distribution remain in the background, suppressed by crisis.

## Status
- **Pre-divergence.** Plays real Roman history until its divergence (~100 BC). The Hannibalic war will be won; the trajectory toward Mediterranean dominance is intact, though the cost has been severe.
