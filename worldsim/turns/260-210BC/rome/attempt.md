# ROME — attempt (260–210 BC)

## REASONING

The Republic stands at the edge of the sea, uncomfortable, but committed. The Senate did not choose this war for the water — it chose it for Sicily, and the water was the price of entry. We must hold that framing: Sicily is the prize, Carthage is the enemy, and the navy is the instrument, not the end in itself. Italy is secured and the allied web holds, but that web is not passive — it has weight and cost. The socii provide the levy that makes our legions renewed and deep; they must not feel the war as Rome's war alone.

The treasury is strained, but the Republic has never lacked resolve in war. The naval losses — storms claim more than Carthage does — are ruinous in timber, coin, and trained oarsmen. Yet to quit the sea is to cede the initiative and leave Carthage free to land armies in Sicily and threaten the island beyond our reach. We built fleets before and will build them again. The corvus gives us the only naval doctrine we can execute with green sailors: bring their ships alongside and let the legionary settle it.

The deeper danger is not Carthaginian victory at sea — it is a long war that exhausts allied patience and strains the census rolls. If the allies begin to see Roman demands as extraction rather than common cause, the web cracks. Loyalty is maintained through shared victory and through Rome being visibly the one who carries the heaviest burden. We must not draft too deep from any single people.

Meanwhile: Gallic pressure in the north is not yet acute, but it is real. We cannot strip Italy bare for Sicily; garrisons and colonies in the north hold what we secured from Pyrrhus's campaigns.

The war will be won at sea — there is no other way to end it. Carthage cannot be starved out of Sicily from the land alone, not while they hold the ports. We must press the naval campaign, keep building when storms destroy, and refuse to negotiate from weakness.

## DECISION

1. **Press the naval war for Sicily** — Continue building and crewing fleets, accepting the cost. Employ the corvus-boarding doctrine that converts sea engagements into the infantry contests the legions win. The objective is to cut Carthaginian supply lines to their Sicilian strongholds and blockade their ports, forcing either battle on our terms or surrender of the island.

2. **Prosecute siege operations on the island** — Where Carthage holds fortified ports (notably in the west), commit legions and Roman siege engineering to reduce them methodically. Do not allow Carthage to sit behind walls indefinitely; the war must be seen to move.

3. **Hold Syracuse as a firm allied anchor** — The Syracusan alliance is the hinge of our position on Sicily. Treat its people as partners in the war, not as a tributary, so as to demonstrate to all Sicilian Greeks that Roman alliance means security and not subjugation. Their grain and their ports are essential.

4. **Maintain the allied levy without rupturing loyalty** — Draw manpower from across the confederation but distribute the burden. No single allied people is to bear a ruinous draft. Where allies have contributed heavily, rotate them to lighter duty. Victory spoils, when they come, are shared visibly. The Senate should be seen rewarding allied service.

5. **Fortify and colonize the northern frontier** — Plant Latin colonies in the Po borderland and reinforce existing garrisons to deter Gallic incursion while our main effort is in Sicily. Roads already begun should be extended to speed response. We do not invite a northern war while one is in progress in the south.

6. **Manage the treasury by linking expenditure to the campaign's pace** — The Senate must be ruthless about naval construction cycles: build in deliberate bursts when timber and coin allow, repair rather than replace where possible, and sell Sicilian booty forward against future fleet costs. The war will not be won by bankruptcy. If further levies on citizens and allies are required, they must be framed as loans against future victory — the Republic's word is its credit.

7. **Make no peace from weakness** — Carthage will probe for a negotiated settlement if the war grows costly for them too. Rome does not negotiate while the outcome is in doubt except on terms that yield Sicily or the clear path to it. The Senate holds this line.
