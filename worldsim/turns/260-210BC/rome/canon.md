# ROME — History Slice — 260–210 BC

The First Punic War was won by attrition and will. The Republic lost whole fleets to storms — timber and crews swallowed by the sea more than by any Carthaginian sword — and rebuilt them, again and again, because the Senate would not hear of surrender. At the Aegates Islands in 241 BC the last Carthaginian fleet was broken, and Carthage sued for terms. Sicily passed to Rome: the island that had cost so much blood and wood became the first province ever held beyond Italian soil. A few years on, while Carthage's mercenary soldiers turned on their masters in Africa, Rome moved with cold opportunism and took Sardinia and Corsica as well. The Republic had entered the sea and would not give it back.

The north had long been unfinished business. The Gauls of the Po valley — peoples who had sacked the city in living memory's memory — were brought to battle and beaten. Roman colonies were planted, Roman roads driven northward, and Cisalpine Gaul was made to feel the weight of the Roman peace. An Illyrian piracy that had grown bold on the Adriatic was chastised in its turn. By the time the second great storm broke, the Republic was larger and more confident than it had ever been: master of Italy, holder of islands, a land power that had discovered it could also win at sea.

Then came Hannibal.

Carthage had not been idle in Spain. It had rebuilt its power there through two generations of Barcid energy, and in 218 BC the Barcid son crossed the Pyrenees and the Alps with his army — and his elephants — and descended into Italy itself. The Republic moved to meet him and was destroyed at the Trebia, then at Trasimene, then — most terribly — at Cannae in 216 BC. At Cannae the legions were encircled and butchered in such numbers that Rome scraped gold rings from the fingers of the fallen to count the senators and knights among the dead. It was the worst defeat in the Republic's memory. Some allies broke: Capua opened its gates to the Carthaginian, Syracuse in Sicily shifted to the enemy, and the southern Italian peoples wavered.

But the confederation held. This is what Hannibal never understood and what the Senate never forgot: Rome was not its armies. It was the sum of its citizens and its allies, and that sum was very large. Fabius Maximus gave the strategy its name — shadow the enemy, deny him battle, starve him of decision — and even those who cursed the Dictator as a delayer came in time to see its necessity. While Hannibal marched and raided and won no city of consequence, Roman arms moved elsewhere. Capua was besieged and recaptured. Syracuse fell to Marcellus after a long siege. Roman armies pressed into Spain to threaten Carthage's base of supply and reinforcement.

By 210 BC the war is not over — Hannibal is still in Italy, still dangerous, the treasury is wrung nearly dry, the fields of the south lie trampled and untended. Yet the shape of things has changed. The mass defections never came. No Latin colony turned. Rome stands as it stood before Cannae: battered, poorer, leaner, but unbroken and still fighting on every front it chose to open. The Republic does not know how to yield, and it is beginning to show.
