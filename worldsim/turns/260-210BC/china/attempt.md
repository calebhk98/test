# CHINA — attempt (260–210 BC)

## REASONING

Changping has shattered Zhao's field army. For the first time in generations the balance of force tilts so decisively toward Qin that the question is no longer whether unification is possible, but how quickly and at what cost we can prosecute it. The rival states will not sit idle; they will scheme, form emergency coalitions, bribe our ministers, and throw bodies at our flanks. The window is open but not eternal.

The Legalist machine we inherited from Lord Shang is precisely built for this moment. Land rank by merit of the sword; grain flowing to state granaries; irrigation in Guanzhong and the Chengdu basin feeding armies that no rival can match in depth. The engine runs hot — our task is to feed it, aim it, and not let it seize from overextension.

**The problem of sequencing.** We must not lunge everywhere at once. Zhao is shattered but not yet absorbed; its northern cities still hold. Wei and Han, pressed between us and Zhao's wreckage, are the nearest prizes and also the most dangerous if left to rearm. Chu is vast and dangerous in the south but slow to mobilize. Yan and Qi are distant; Qi in particular has largely abstained from the wars and sits fat and unprepared. The correct order: press the wound in Zhao now; then strangle Han (smallest, most surrounded); then Wei (controls the river crossings into the east); then Chu (hardest, needs the most force, buy time by diplomacy while we marshal it); then clean up Yan and Qi.

**The northern steppe frontier.** The mounted raiders along our northern face — the Xiongnu and their kin — will grow bolder as they sense our attention fixed southward. We cannot afford a two-front war once we press deep into the east. The existing walls and fortified lines must be reinforced and garrisoned adequately. This is not a war of conquest on that front — it is a war of containment, buying decades while our southern work is done.

**Digesting conquered lands.** Each rival state we absorb brings alien aristocracies, local customs, and populations accustomed to their own law. The Legalist apparatus — standardized weights and measures, written law posted at crossroads, merit bureaucracy replacing hereditary clan power — must extend into each conquest swiftly. Slow digestion breeds revolt; fast extension of standard law and grain incentives converts populations into productive subjects.

**Crossbow armies and logistics.** Our military advantage is mass and missile. The crossbow lever our soldiers above those who carry only bronze blades; mass production of iron crossbow mechanisms and bolts at state workshops is a standing priority. Equally critical: the logistical chain of granaries, roads, and canal transport that lets us sustain vast armies far from Guanzhong. Without the road and the granary, the crossbow army starves.

**What we must not do.** We must not attempt the steppe in force — it is a waste of the conscript army against an enemy that retreats into grass. We must not fragment our effort by campaigning in too many directions simultaneously. We must not allow the court to be corrupted by rival-state gold and bribes to our ministers (a constant danger — the rival states know they cannot beat us in the field and will try to rot us from inside).

## DECISION

1. **Press Zhao to final submission (260–245 BC)**
   - *Aim:* Reduce remaining Zhao fortresses north of the Yellow River; eliminate Zhao as a military power capable of resisting or allying against us.
   - *Means:* Maintain field armies in the east; conduct sequential siege operations against Zhao's remaining walled cities; deny Zhao time to reconstitute its army by keeping pressure constant. Offer generous surrender terms to cities that open their gates, harsh example to those that resist — divide the Zhao population's will to fight.

2. **Subjugate Han (245–235 BC)**
   - *Aim:* Eliminate the smallest rival state, which occupies the passes and valleys between our eastern frontier and the Central Plains. Han's fall isolates Wei.
   - *Means:* Han is already hemmed on three sides. Lean on it with diplomatic ultimata backed by demonstrated military force; if it resists, commit a sufficient striking force to its capital and key passes. Absorb its iron-working districts and conscript its population under Qin law.

3. **Reduce Wei, seize the river crossings (235–225 BC)**
   - *Aim:* Control the Yellow River crossings into the eastern plain; deny Wei the ability to coordinate with Chu or Yan.
   - *Means:* Attack Wei's capital region with combined arms — crossbow-heavy infantry backed by cavalry for pursuit. Use river transport to supply forces; position pontoon-capable bridging equipment. The fall of Wei opens the road to Qi and cuts Chu off from northern allies.

4. **Campaign against Chu and Yan; isolate Qi (225–215 BC)**
   - *Aim:* Crush the two largest remaining powers; leave Qi surrounded and without allies.
   - *Means:* Chu requires the largest commitment — commit a mass army under our best commander; use the Han and Wei populations now under Qin levy to swell the ranks. Yan is distant but smaller; a northern thrust after Wei's fall, combined with pressure on the steppe frontier to keep Yan from retreating northward, should suffice. Qi, long peaceable and unprepared, will fall to an eastward march once every other rival is gone.

5. **Contain the northern frontier throughout (continuous)**
   - *Aim:* Hold the steppe raiders off our northern face while the southern wars are fought; prevent a two-front catastrophe.
   - *Means:* Reinforce the existing frontier walls and garrison points with adequate troops — not a punitive expedition into the steppe, but a strong defensive posture with patrols and warning fires. Draft and post frontier garrisons from the least-needed corps; accept some raiding in exchange for not diverting main-force armies.

6. **Extend Legalist administration into every conquered territory (continuous)**
   - *Aim:* Convert each absorbed state into a productive Qin province — taxpaying, conscript-levying, loyal to the new order — rather than a smoldering subject territory.
   - *Means:* Immediately on conquest: destroy hereditary noble titles; post standard Qin law codes; install merit-appointed administrators from the Qin bureaucracy; redistribute land to cultivators under the merit-rank system; open granary networks and link to Qin canal and road infrastructure. Harsh suppression of any aristocratic revival; generous treatment of common cultivators who comply.

7. **Sustain and expand the agrarian-logistical base (continuous)**
   - *Aim:* Ensure our armies never outrun their supply; deepen the productive capacity of Guanzhong, Shu, and newly absorbed lands.
   - *Means:* Extend the irrigation canal network into newly conquered lowland territories; compel peasant labor on road-building between campaigns; maintain the state granary system with multi-year reserves against bad harvests; keep the iron-smelting and weapon-workshop capacity running at full output throughout.
