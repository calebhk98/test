# CHINA — HISTORY SLICE: 260–210 BC
*In the court's voice. Appended to the chronicle.*

---

## The Swallowing of All Under Heaven — 260 to 221 BC

The blade that had broken Zhao at Changping did not rest. Year upon year, the armies of Qin descended through the passes and across the rivers, moving always by the counsel of patient strategists and the merciless logic of the Legalist machine. The smaller states fell first, hemmed in and starved of allies; Han was extinguished, then Zhao what remained of it, then Wei, each in its turn. Great Chu, vast and proud, required the full mobilization — hundreds of thousands of men, years of grinding campaign — before it too was brought under. Yan fell isolated in the north. And at the last, Qi, which had stood apart and sued for peace with empty hands, surrendered without a battle. In the twenty-sixth year of the King of Qin's reign — the year we now reckon the first year of the new age — there were no more rival kings. All Under Heaven was one.

## The Name That Had Not Existed Before

The King summoned his ministers and put to them a question that had never before been asked: what title befits the man who has done what no man in history has done? The old royal title "wang" was deemed too small, already worn by a hundred kings who had come to nothing. The ministers reached back to the most ancient and august words — "huang" and "di," the sacred names of the legendary sage-sovereigns — and combined them. He was henceforth the Huangdi, the August Emperor, the First Emperor. He decreed that those who came after him should be numbered: the Second, the Third, and so on through ten thousand generations. There was no false modesty in this; the court believed it.

## One Script, One Law, One Road

The First Emperor's ministers understood that a realm held only by armies is not a realm but an occupation. The work of true unification was administrative. A single script was imposed across all the old states — the clerks of Chu and Yan must henceforth write as the clerks of Qin wrote, so that an imperial decree could be read from the southern rivers to the northern steppe. Weights and measures were made uniform; a cart's axle must fit every road in the empire; the round coin with the square hole was the single currency of all transactions. The old Legalist law of Qin, already refined over generations, was extended to every commandery. The feudal lords who had ruled the old states as hereditary masters were swept away; in their place, appointed officials governed on behalf of the throne, removable and accountable. The realm was carved into thirty-six commanderies — later more — each with a civil administrator, a military commander, and an overseer who checked them both. The empire that had been built to conquer now built to endure.

## Wall, Road, and Canal

Three great works bound the empire's body together. To the north, where the old frontier states had each built their own defensive earthworks against the steppe nomads, the First Emperor's general Meng Tian joined and extended these walls into a single continuous barrier running thousands of li — the Long Wall — garrisoned by hundreds of thousands of men. To the interior, a network of imperial roads was driven outward from the capital at Xianyang, broad enough for the emperor's carriage, straight through hill and valley by the labor of the condemned and the conscripted. And to the deep south, the Lingqu canal was cut through the ridgeline that divided the waters flowing north from those flowing south, so that grain and troops could move by water from the Yangtze basin all the way into the distant Lingnan. No project of this kind had ever been attempted; none of it was accomplished without cost.

## The Frontiers Pushed

While the wall was built, the armies moved. In the north, the general Meng Tian led three hundred thousand soldiers against the Xiongnu confederacy that had been pressing down toward the great bend of the Yellow River. The Xiongnu were driven back beyond the Ordos loop; the river bend was reclaimed and settled; garrisons held it at enormous expense of men and grain. In the south, a separate army of half a million was sent into the Baiyue lands — the rivers and forests and mountains of what would become Lingnan — and after years of difficult campaigning and heavy losses to the climate and the terrain as much as to the enemy, the southern reaches were subdued and brought within the imperial frontier. The empire now stretched from the steppe's edge to the southern sea, from the eastern coast to the western highlands. No state in the memory of history had encompassed so much.

## The Emperor's Death and the Poisoned Succession

The First Emperor did not rest. He traveled ceaselessly — inspection tours along the great roads, pilgrimages to the eastern mountains to perform the feng and shan sacrifices that proclaimed heaven's mandate received, searches for the elixirs of immortality that his court alchemists promised and never delivered. He was already an old man by the standards of his age when, in the thirty-seventh year of his reign and the eleventh year since unification, he fell ill on tour far from the capital. He died before he could return. The legitimate heir, the elder prince Fusu, was with Meng Tian on the northern frontier. The inner circle around the dying emperor — the eunuch Zhao Gao and the minister Li Si — forged the imperial edicts. The true succession order was destroyed; Fusu was commanded to die and obeyed; the younger prince Huhai was installed as Second Emperor. He was weak, and Zhao Gao was not. The machine of empire stood at the peak of its power and its reach — the wall built, the roads laid, the realm unified, the frontiers held — and at its heart the succession had already been poisoned. Those who know the signs of a dynasty's brittleness would have seen them clearly. The men in the court did not allow themselves to see.
