# INDIA — attempt (260–210 BC)

## REASONING

What does the divergence actually give us that Ashoka's pacifist turn would have denied?

First and most immediate: Kalinga is now a *forward base*, not a wound to apologize for. Its coast gives us ports on the Bay of Bengal — the first time the Maurya treasury can tax eastern maritime trade rather than merely watching it pass. That is not a small thing. The Tamil kingdoms and Sri Lanka receive goods from those waters; so do any eastern coasts beyond our present horizon. Kalinga's absorption must be completed ruthlessly but quickly — garrison it, integrate its administrators into the imperial system under a trusted viceroy, settle Maurya colonists in the port towns to dilute resentment, and put the deported population to work on state projects (road-building, canal irrigation) where they are watched but productive. Pacification through exhaustion and economic dependency, not through forgiveness. The Arthashastra is clear on this: a conquered people must be made to feel that prosperity comes from submission and that rebellion means poverty.

Second: the army of 600,000 with 9,000 elephants is not a static inheritance — it is an ongoing *fiscal drain* if it does nothing. A professional standing army in peacetime costs more than it earns. We must use it, or selectively draw it down in sectors where threat is low, banking the surplus. The question is where to use it.

**The Tamil south.** The Chola, Pandya, and Chera are independent, wealthy, sea-trading peoples at our doorstep. They are not a single unified opponent. They have never faced a Maurya army. The approach is not a single overwhelming invasion but a staged sequence: first diplomatic pressure backed by a visible military presence on the border — demand tribute, free passage for Maurya merchants, and a garrison right at the northern edge of Chola territory. If they pay, we take revenue without a campaign. If they resist, we have the army to reduce them one at a time, using local rivalries (Chola against Pandya, Pandya against Chera) to create allies-of-convenience who then find themselves surrounded and dependent. The goal is not racial extermination but economic subjugation: their ports become Maurya ports, their spice and ivory and pearl trade flows through Maurya tax stations. Sri Lanka (Tamraparni) is reachable by short sea crossing from the southern tip; its pearls and its position astride the sea lane are worth having. A small fleet — river-craft adapted, not deep-ocean — can carry an advance force.

**Stabilizing the north-west without surrendering it.** The Seleucid frontier is quiet under the old treaty, but the Seleucid realm is large, far, and (by report) not without internal stresses. We do not need to attack westward; we need to ensure the Gandhara passes remain ours and that no successor or breakaway Greek power tests us. The spy service should be tasked with understanding the succession dynamics of the Seleucid west better — not because we plan to invade Persia, but because foreknowledge of a weakened or distracted neighbor lets us extract better terms (more territory in the passes, more favorable trade concessions) without a battle. Agents placed in the trading towns between Gandhara and the Seleucid interior can gather this.

**The Bay of Bengal — a sea-trade revenue system.** Kalinga's ports are the key. The court should invest in controlled expansion of those ports: warehousing, state-controlled docking fees, a small coastal patrol force (not a blue-water navy — we do not yet have the ships or the knowledge — but armed river-capable and coastal craft sufficient to deter piracy and enforce tax collection). Merchants from the east trade through these waters; some carry goods whose origins we do not know. Our spies should be embedded in port towns to gather what intelligence coast-trade brings in: what peoples lie further east, what goods they carry, what they will pay for.

**Internal compounding — road network and information control.** The empire is vast and the distances kill speed of reaction. The royal road tradition (already present from Chandragupta's time) should be extended aggressively into Kalinga, the Deccan, and southward as military advance follows. Roads serve three functions: army movement, tax collection (state weighing stations on roads are a standard Arthashastra tool), and intelligence (the post-riders who carry edicts also carry the spy service's reports). A governor who is ten days' march from Pataliputra is manageable; one who is forty days' march without a road is a potential rebel. Extend the roads, and you tighten the grip.

**What my divergent advantage lets me do that an ordinary power could not:**

An ordinary empire — one that had renounced conquest — would be consolidating, building stupas, and losing the credibility of its military threat. The army would begin to atrophy. Provincial governors would sense that the center will not march. Over decades, centrifugal drift would accelerate. That is what the pacifist turn would have cost.

Instead: I have a *proven, recently victorious, professionally maintained army* whose officers have just won a brutal war. Their loyalty is to conquest and reward. I can use them within the next decade or two before the post-Kalinga veterans age out — and by using them, I extend the culture of military service and the expectation of reward to a new generation. The south is the obvious target: it is close, it is rich, it is divided, and it does not know what a Maurya army looks like in the field.

The war elephants are a specific advantage worth noting. Tamil kingdoms and Sri Lanka have no comparable elephant-warfare doctrine. The psychological effect of a disciplined elephant corps on an opponent who has never faced one is enormous — it does not merely win battles, it ends them before they are fought, because the news travels ahead of the army. We should use them as the vanguard of the southern campaign specifically to build that reputation in the south before it can be countered.

**What I will NOT do:**

I will not attempt a western campaign toward Persia or beyond. The Seleucid treaty is more valuable as a stable western frontier than any territory I might seize at enormous logistical cost. The passes are already mine; I do not need Persepolis to be rich. The spy service watches the west; that is enough for now.

I will not pursue deep-ocean expansion. Our ships are river and coastal craft; we do not have the maritime knowledge for open-ocean voyaging. The Bay of Bengal coast-trade is achievable. The open ocean is not, yet.

I will not neglect Kalinga. A poorly garrisoned Kalinga that revolts in fifteen years undoes the entire eastern position. The first decade's investment is in making Kalinga loyal enough — or exhausted enough — to stay.

## DECISION

1. **Pacify and integrate Kalinga (Years 1–15, ongoing).** Station a permanent garrison of 40,000–60,000 troops under a Maurya prince-viceroy. Settle loyal Maurya administrators and merchant families in the main port towns (Tosali, Dantapura). Set the deported population to supervised state labor on the southern road extension and port infrastructure. Tax the coast-trade from year one to demonstrate economic benefit to collaborating local elites. Aim: make rebellion impractical within fifteen years by making compliance profitable.

2. **Extend the royal road southward into the Deccan and toward the Tamil frontier (Years 1–20).** The road is the army's speed and the spy service's nervous system. Mandate construction crews — drawn partly from Kalinga deportees — to extend paved and maintained road from the Deccan nodes southward to within striking distance of Chola territory. Aim: reduce march-time to the south by at least one-third; enable rapid redeployment of northern army corps.

3. **Apply diplomatic pressure to the Tamil kingdoms — tribute or conquest (Years 10–25).** Send embassies backed by a visible military build-up on the northern Chola border: demand recognition of Maurya suzerainty, payment of annual tribute in spice, ivory, and pearls, and guaranteed safe passage for Maurya merchants through their ports. Use the spy service to identify which Tamil king is most internally contested and make him an offer of Maurya support in exchange for submission. Divide and subordinate. If diplomacy yields tributary status, consolidate before the next step. If it fails, campaign against the weakest Tamil polity first (likely Chera, furthest west and smallest), then Pandya, then Chola — using the elephant corps to open each engagement.

4. **Seize Sri Lanka (Tamraparni) by coastal crossing (Years 20–35, conditional on southern pacification).** Once the southern Tamil fringe is under tributary control or subdued, adapt existing coastal and river craft into a landing force sufficient to carry 10,000–15,000 troops across the short sea strait. Sri Lanka's pearl fisheries and its position on the eastern sea lane make it a revenue asset. The crossing is short; it does not require deep-ocean capability. Aim: close the southern maritime circuit so that all trade passing from the west to the eastern waters pays Maurya tolls.

5. **Expand and militarize the Kalinga port system (Years 5–30).** Invest treasury funds in expanding the Kalinga coast ports: deeper anchorages, state warehousing, enforced docking fees. Station a coastal patrol force (armed oared vessels, drawn from existing river-fleet expertise) to suppress piracy and ensure Maurya merchants receive preferential access. Embed spy-service agents in port markets to collect intelligence on eastern trade origins and on any peoples or powers beyond present knowledge. Aim: turn Kalinga from a conquered wound into the empire's eastern revenue engine within a generation.

6. **Task the spy service northward and westward for Seleucid succession intelligence (Years 1–50, continuous).** The Seleucid realm is large and by report not without instability. Our treaty holds, but foreknowledge of succession crises, breakaway governors, or internal wars gives us the option to extract better pass-and-trade terms without military action. If a moment of Seleucid weakness appears, negotiate new concessions in the passes, not conquest — the logistics of a western campaign are prohibitive, and the frontier we hold is already excellent. Aim: use intelligence to maximize diplomatic yield without spending an army on it.

7. **Selective army rationalization and reinvestment (Years 1–10).** 600,000 men is an immense payroll. In sectors where threat is demonstrably low — the Ganges heartland, pacified central Deccan — draw down garrison strength and concentrate the savings into the southern campaign force and port investment. Retain the full elephant corps and the professional officer cadre. Do not disband; redeploy and concentrate. Aim: maintain offensive capability where needed, reduce fiscal drain where not.
