# ROME — KNOWLEDGE (what we believe; may be wrong/incomplete)

We do not know how many powers exist in the world.

- Our war with Carthage over Sicily consumes us; we are learning the sea. Carthage is a great naval
  and trading power.
- North: the Gauls, a perennial danger.
- East: the Greek cities of southern Italy, and beyond the sea the wealthy Hellenistic kingdoms
  (heirs of Alexander), including rich Egypt by the Nile — known by reputation and trade.
- Lands further east than the Greeks are little more than legend.

Open questions: none recorded yet.

## ~105 BC — what we now perceive
- We are master of the Mediterranean, but torn within: our Italian allies demand the citizenship and
  may rise if refused; ambitious commanders with armies loyal to them, not the Senate, contend for power.
- The kingdom of Egypt by the Nile is the richest realm left unconquered — and it has now opened its own
  ships' road to the wealth of India, growing richer still. Its grain could feed Rome forever; its
  treasure would make whoever seized it the first man in the Republic. Voices in the Senate and the camps
  argue openly that Egypt should be ours. Others warn it is strong, and far, and threw back the Seleucid
  unaided.

## 90 BC — what we now perceive
- A renegade of ours sailed on Egypt without the Senate's leave. He is destroyed. His army drowned and
  starved in the Delta; the Egyptian fleet mastered the sea crossing. We know only that he is gone and
  the Senate has disowned him. Egypt's strength in its own waters is now proven against Roman arms.
- Egypt has answered by closing its papyrus to us and to those who trade with us. Half the world wrote
  on that reed; we must now write on something else. Our workers have found a way — beaten fiber, rags,
  linen pressed flat. We call it by the name we use for the material (we have no finer word yet). It
  is coarse and not as fine as the Nile's reed, but we improve it and we are not without writing.
- We believe Egypt's court and fleet are strong, that the eastern sea is presently theirs, and that
  they will not forget this insult any sooner than we will.
- At home: the Italians rose, and we gave them the citizenship to end the war. Marius broke the Cimbri
  in the north. But our generals hold armies that answer to the man, not the Senate. Civil war between
  rival commanders is no longer unthinkable — it may be close. The Republic's constitutional fabric
  strains under the weight of professional armies and ambitious men.
- We believe ourselves masters of the Mediterranean basin. We do not yet know what further lies east
  beyond Egypt and the Parthians. Egypt's India-trade shows there is great wealth still beyond our reach.
- Open question: who will be the first man in Rome when the civil contest comes, and what will be left
  of the Republic after it?
