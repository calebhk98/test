# ROME — HISTORY (as we experienced it)

## 260 BC
- The war with Carthage rages over Sicily. We have taken to the sea — a new fleet with boarding
  bridges lets our legionaries fight as if on land. Rome is learning to win at sea.

## 235 BC
- We won the long war. We broke Carthage at sea and took Sicily, then Sardinia and Corsica after.
  The sea itself cost us more than the enemy did — twice great storms swallowed our fleets whole —
  but we build our ships now in like sections, with timber stockpiled, and we rebuild faster than
  grief allows. Carthage pays us tribute; our treasury mends.
- We learned not to promise our veterans land that belongs to Syracuse and our Greek friends — they
  shall have the land we take from Carthage, or our own public fields. The socii stood by us, and we
  by them. The Gauls we watch, and road southward to move our legions fast.

## 205 BC
- Peace did not last. Carthage found a thunderbolt in Hannibal, who crossed the very Alps and loosed
  ruin on Italy — at Cannae we lost an army such as no other people could survive. And yet we did not
  break. We will not give him the open battle he craves; we shadow and starve him, and we carry the war
  to Spain, where young Scipio strips Carthage of its silver and its base. The tide turns. The end of
  Carthage's war is in sight, and it will not be the end Carthage wished.

## 170 BC
- We finished Carthage on its own African soil and dictated the peace. Rome is now first among the
  powers of the west, from Spain to Sicily. When the Macedonian king grew grasping in the Greek seas,
  the Rhodians and others cried to us, and we crossed over, humbled him, and proclaimed the Greeks free
  — meaning, free under Rome's eye. The east is a tangle of Greek kingdoms; we mean to be its arbiter,
  not yet its master. There is a rich, strong kingdom by the Nile we had thought might fall to us in
  time, as such things do — but it has not fallen, and stands as proud as ever. Curious.

## 90 BC
- The years have not been kind to our dignity, though Rome still stands and grows.

- A commander whose name the Senate now refuses to speak aloud took legions and ships that were Rome's
  and turned them toward Egypt without leave of the Senate, without a vote of the people, for no cause
  the Republic could own — only his greed for a throne on the Nile. He sailed. He did not return. The
  Egyptian fleet held the sea-crossing against him; the great Delta, with its channels and its marshes,
  received his army and kept it. We received only silence, and then word of ruin. The Senate met and
  declared him no commander of Rome. It was easy to say, once he was dead.

- Egypt did not let the insult pass quietly. The Nile's reed, the papyrus on which half the world
  keeps its records and carries its letters, has stopped coming. The warehouses on the Tiber docks
  stand empty of it. Scribes and merchants and magistrates howled alike. But Romans, when necessity
  presses, do not simply wait. Our workers began tearing apart old linen rags, flax stalks, the inner
  bark of trees — beating them to pulp, pressing them flat, drying them in the sun. The result is
  rough, but it holds ink, and it will serve. We have begun to make our own writing-material, and we
  will not be hostage to the Nile for it again.

- In the north, Marius gave us the victory we needed. The Cimbri, who had shaken Italy with terror,
  who had broken Roman armies in the passes and made mothers weep at their sons' call-up, were met
  by Marius's new legions and broken utterly. These new soldiers are different: long years under the
  eagle, professional to the bone, bound to their general as much as to the city. Marius saved us.
  The Senate thanks him. But the Senate also fears what he represents — and fears the others who now
  say, with their hands on their sword-hilts: I too have an army.

- The Italian allies rose against us, and we fought them. It was a bitter and costly war among men
  who had bled for Rome in every campaign for two centuries. In the end we gave them what they had
  always deserved: the citizenship. They are Romans now. The rolls are swollen; the assemblies must
  find ways to count them. It is the right outcome and it nearly tore the Republic apart to reach it.

- Egypt stands across the sea from us: rich, unconquered, master of the eastern waters, and now our
  declared enemy. The Senate will say this is the renegade's doing, not ours. Egypt will not hear the
  distinction. We will not forget what happened in the Delta. We do not believe Egypt will forget either.
  There will be a reckoning, in a year or a generation. We write this down — on our own paper now,
  not theirs — so that whoever comes after us will know what we owe.
