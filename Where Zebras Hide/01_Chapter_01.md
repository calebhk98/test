## Chapter One

The fluorescent lights in the office had been flickering since Tuesday, and by Friday afternoon I was ready to throw my stapler at them. Not that it would help. Maintenance had already come by twice, looked at the ceiling with their hands on their hips, and said they'd put in a work order.

"Mira, you still here?" 

I looked up to find Marcus leaning against my cubicle wall, jacket already on, messenger bag slung over his shoulder. 

"Trying to finish this report before the weekend."

"It's 4:30 on a Friday. The report can wait."

"The report is due Monday morning."

"Then Monday morning you can finish it." He grinned. "Come on. Some of us are getting drinks at Murphy's. You should come."

I glanced at my screen. Three more pages to review, citations to check, formatting that kept breaking every time I touched it. If I stayed another hour I could wrap it up. If I left now, I'd be thinking about it all weekend.

"I can't. I've got plans anyway."

"What plans?"

"Coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin widened. "Ah. The mysterious Jason. When are you two finally going to make that official?"

"Make what official?" I knew what he meant. Everyone thought they knew what this was.

"You meet him every single Tuesday for coffee. You talk about him constantly. Last month you cancelled on girls' night because he was in town and you hadn't seen him in a while." He ticked items off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it yet."

My face felt warm. "We're friends."

"Uh huh."

"We are." And I'd learned to be okay with that. Or at least I'd learned to stop hoping for different. Jason had never given me a reason to think otherwise. Two years of careful distance had made that pretty clear.

"Okay." He pushed off from the wall. "Well, have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

He left, and I turned back to my computer. The cursor blinked at me accusingly. I read the same paragraph three times without absorbing a single word.

I stared at the pivot table on page 42\. It had shifted three pixels to the left for no reason I could discern. My eyes felt like they were packed with sand. Somewhere three cubicles down, Dave was loudly eating baby carrots, the *snap-crunch, snap-crunch* echoing in the quiet office. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. And I hated that if I didn't fix this table, the VP would make a passive-aggressive comment about 'attention to detail' on Monday.

My phone buzzed.

**Jason: Still on for coffee? I'm heading over now.**

I looked at my screen. At the three pages that still needed work. At the flickering fluorescent lights that were giving me a headache.

**Me: Yeah. See you in 20\.**

The report could wait.

\---

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday evening. The usual crowd of students camped out with laptops, a few couples on dates, someone's book club meeting in the corner with four different pastries spread across their table. I spotted Jason at our usual table by the window, already halfway through whatever he was working on.

He looked up when I walked in, and for just a second his whole face lit up \- then he caught himself, smoothed it over into something more careful. That familiar almost-smile that never quite became what I wanted it to be.

"Hey," he said as I sat down. "Long day?"

"How can you tell?"

"You've got that specific look. Henderson account?"

I slumped in my chair. "They want to add two more sections. Two. And they still want it Monday."

"That's unreasonable."

"I know it's unreasonable. They know it's unreasonable. But they're paying us, so it's happening anyway." I rubbed my eyes. "I left it at the office. I'll deal with it tomorrow."

"On a Saturday?"

"I don't have a choice."

Jason frowned but didn't push it. He knew how my job worked. He closed his laptop and pushed it to the side. No phone check. No "hold on, one second." Just his attention, full stop. Marcus would call this proof of something. But Marcus didn't see how Jason never let our knees touch under the table. How he always kept a careful twelve inches between us. He gave me his attention, but he wouldn't give me anything else.

Jason didn't even ask. He just looked at me, raised an eyebrow at my slump, and stood up. "Large latte, extra foam, shot of vanilla because you look like you're about to cry?"

"Two shots," I corrected. "And a blueberry scone if they haven't gone stale yet."

"You hate the scones here. You say they taste like sawdust."

"I need the sugar, Jason. Don't judge me."

He grinned, that slow, easy smile that made the knot in my shoulders loosen just a fraction. "Sawdust and sugar, coming right up."

He got up and headed to the counter. I watched him navigate through the crowd with that easy confidence he always had. He could walk into any situation and just belong there without trying.

When he came back with my latte and his black coffee, I took a sip immediately. Perfect. Extra foam, just how I liked it. He remembered everything. What I drank, how I took it, which scones I pretended not to hate. He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

"Thank you."

"Of course." He wrapped his hands around his cup. "So. Weekend plans besides the Henderson account?"

"That's pretty much it. Sarah wants me to come to some gallery opening tomorrow night, but I'll probably be too buried in work." I took another sip. "What about you?"

"I've got some projects I'm working on. Nothing too exciting."

"What kind of projects?"

"Just some contract work. Programming stuff." He gave a small shrug. "Hey, how's that camera you got last month? The Pentax?"

My whole face lit up before I could stop it. "It's amazing. I finally got the light meter working properly, and I've been shooting with this expired film stock I found on eBay. The grain is incredible."

"Did you develop any of it yet?"

"Yeah, I did a batch Wednesday night. There's this one shot of the bridge downtown at sunset that came out exactly how I wanted. The sky has this weird greenish cast from the expired film, but it works somehow."

"You'll have to show me sometime."

"Most people think I'm crazy for spending money on film when my phone takes perfectly good pictures."

"Most people are boring." He smiled. "Besides, there's something different about film, right? The whole process of it. It's more intentional."

"Exactly." I loved that he got that. That he'd actually listened when I'd tried to explain why I liked it, why it mattered.  That he remembered. That he did everything right except the one thing I wanted him to do. I pushed the thought away. "My sister keeps telling me it's a waste of money. Like, why bother when digital is easier and cheaper."

"Because easier and cheaper isn't always better."

"I'm stealing that line next time she gives me grief about it."

He laughed, and I felt that familiar warmth in my chest that I got when we were like this. Easy. Comfortable. Like we'd known each other longer than two years.

"How is your sister?" he asked. "Still trying to convince you to move to Chicago?"

"Always. She sent me like five apartment listings this week." I rolled my eyes. "I keep telling her I'm happy here, but she's convinced I'm wasting my life in Columbus."

"Are you happy here?"

The question caught me a little off guard. "Yeah. I mean, my job is fine. I've got friends. My apartment is decent." I looked at him. "I've got reasons to stay."

Something shifted in his expression. Not quite a smile, but something softer. "That's good."

The students at the table next to us had been having some kind of debate for the past ten minutes, but now one of them raised his voice loud enough that half the coffee shop could hear.

"No, you're not listening. If you could know every single variable in the universe, every particle, every force, you could predict everything that would ever happen. Free will is literally an illusion."

"That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard," his friend shot back, equally loud. "Quantum mechanics exists. Heisenberg uncertainty principle. You can't know everything."

"Quantum randomness doesn't equal free will\!"

"It literally does\!"

I winced. Jason glanced over at them, then back at me with a slight grimace.

"You want to get out of here?" he asked. "Go somewhere quieter?"

"Please."

"There's that Thai place a few blocks away. We could grab dinner."

My heart did a stupid little flip. *Dinner.* Two years of coffee, and now this.

"I've been wanting to try it," he added.

Oh. Right. He was hungry. I was convenient.

"The one Sarah keeps recommending?"

"That's the one."

I checked my phone. It was just past five. I should probably go home and work on the report. But the report had been hanging over my head all week, and the idea of spending more time with Jason instead sounded much better.

"Okay," I said. "But you're buying since you're the one who talked me into procrastinating."

"I didn't talk you into anything. You were already procrastinating when I got here."

"Details."

He grinned and started packing up his laptop. I grabbed the lid for my coffee and snapped it on, even though I'd already drunk half of it.

We headed out into the February evening. It wasn't as cold as it could have been, but there was still that sharp edge to the air that made me pull my jacket tighter. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left, heading in the direction of the restaurant.

"My mom called yesterday. Wanted to know when I'm getting promoted."

"Are you?"

"No idea. But she's decided I should be by now, so." I took a sip through the lid. "She's got this whole timeline. Where I should be. And I'm apparently behind."

"That sounds exhausting."

"My dad's sending me grad school applications. Like I want to pay someone to stress me out again." I shook my head. "They mean well."

"But."

"But I don't know. I just want, " I gestured vaguely. "To not hate my job? Pay rent without panic? That feels like enough, you know?"

He nodded.

"Do your parents do this? The whole,” I said, waving my arms around, “timeline thing?"

Something shifted in his face. "We don't really talk much."

"Oh. I'm sorry."

"It's fine. We didn't really get along." He said it casually, but there was something in his tone that suggested it wasn't fine at all. "Hey, have you thought any more about that darkroom space you were looking at?"

There it was again. The deflection. Answer briefly, then redirect to something about me.The door closing. I told him everything, my annoying boss, my pushy parents, my failed hobbies and embarrassing stories. And he listened to all of it. But the moment I tried to know him back? He'd find an exit. I'd learned to stop trying. Mostly.

"I can't afford it," I said. "The rental alone would eat up my fun money for the month. Plus I'd need to buy equipment, chemicals, all of that. It's just not practical right now."

"That's too bad. It sounded like a good setup."

"Maybe someday. When I get that promotion my mom keeps asking about."

He smiled at that.

We'd reached a quieter stretch of sidewalk. The park on one side, a row of closed businesses on the other. Fewer people around now. Just us and the sound of our footsteps and the distant hum of traffic from the main road.

A gust of wind hit us and I stumbled slightly. Jason's hand came up automatically \- then stopped six inches from my elbow, hovering in the space between helping and not touching. He dropped it back to his side. "You good?" "Yeah." I was. I always was. And he never touched me unless he absolutely had to.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"Sure."

"Do you ever think about getting a normal job? Like, with coworkers and an office and everything?"

He was quiet for a moment, considering. "Not really. I like working alone."

"Don't you get lonely?"

"Sometimes. But I'm not great with people in general. Offices seem like they'd be exhausting." He looked at me. "Besides, this way I can set my own schedule. Work when I want. Take time off when I need to."

"That does sound nice."

"It has its perks."

"But you're good with me," I pointed out. "You can't be that bad with people."

"You're different."

The way he said it made something flutter in my chest. Different. But not different enough, apparently, because he was already looking away. And just like that, the door closed. Like it always did when I got too close to asking what I actually wanted to know.

"Different how?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

The air cracked.

That was the only way I could describe it later. Like the atmosphere itself had split open. There was a flash of blue light, electric and wrong, gone so fast I almost thought I'd imagined it.

Jason's hand clamped around my arm hard enough to hurt.

Then something hit me. Not physically. It didn't touch me. But I felt it anyway, like a wall of pressure slamming into my chest. Like the air had become solid and was trying to crush me.

I couldn't breathe. My lungs seized. There was a high-pitched ringing in my ears and another flash of blue and why was Jason pulling me and what was happening and I couldn't breathe couldn't think.

The world lurched.

Everything tilted sideways. Up became down. My stomach flipped over and kept flipping. The pressure in my chest turned to fire and my skin felt like it was being peeled off and put back on wrong. I tried to scream but I had no air and everything was spinning and breaking apart and I was going to die, I was dying right now.

Ground.

I hit hard. Dirt and leaves and rocks digging into my palms. I gasped and choked and couldn't remember how breathing worked. Everything hurt. My chest, my head, every inch of my skin. Like I'd been electrocuted and thrown out of a moving car.

My vision was blurry. I blinked and it slowly came into focus.

Trees. There were trees everywhere. Bare branches against grey sky. Dead leaves covering the ground.
