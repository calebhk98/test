# Reading journal: Where Zebras Hide (Haiku 5.5 reader)

Ground rules followed: one document at a time, journal entry written before the next file was opened, no earlier entries revised. Where a later document changes my view, the later entry says so.

## After 01_Chapter_01.md

- **Overall opinion so far**: A competent-but-flat contemporary opening that turns into a portal-jump thriller in its last third. The coffee-shop scenes are carried by specific, non-generic details (the expired film and the bridge shot, Dave's baby carrots, the scone "that tastes like sawdust"). The emotional material is stated more than shown, and the fantasy hook (blue flash, pressure, Mira ending up in a forest) is clearly the strongest thing on the page. Grade: **4/10**. Reason: readable and occasionally specific, but the romance beats narrate their own subtext and the copy-editing is rough.

- **Characters**:
  - *Mira* (first-person narrator): a grounded, self-deprecating office worker with a real hobby. The film-camera passage (lines 105 to 117) is the most alive writing in the chapter. Her self-awareness is sometimes overdone: "I know what he meant. Everyone thought they knew what this was." and "I'd learned to be okay with that. Or at least I'd learned to stop hoping for different." She is consistent across the chapter.
  - *Jason*: a warm, attentive love interest in dialogue (he orders her coffee exactly right, line 79) and withholding in narration. The narration keeps telling us he withholds: "He gave me his attention, but he wouldn't give me anything else." The "contract work. Programming stuff" line is the only thing that might be a clue, and it is not given any weight.
  - *Marcus*, *Sarah*, *Dave*, *the VP*: thin but functional. Sarah is consistent as the sister (lines 97, 117, 125, 159).
  - *The debating students*: they exist to say the theme out loud. "If you could know every single variable in the universe... Free will is literally an illusion." That is a thematic plant delivered as dialogue, and it is too on the nose.
  - No robotic or stoic-for-its-own-sake behavior, but Jason's "He grinned," "He smiled," "Something shifted in his expression" and "Something shifted in his face" are repeated beats. Two "Something shifted" lines in one chapter is one too many.

- **Plot**:
  - Good: the hook lands. The "air cracked" sequence (lines 239 to 255) changes register, the sentences shorten and the sensory detail sharpens. It is the first place the prose feels like it is paying attention.
  - Good: the Thai dinner that does not happen, and the "Oh. Right. He was hungry. I was convenient." beat, is efficient characterization.
  - Bad: Jason is in the blast with her, and the chapter never says what he knows. Either he is part of the phenomenon or he is a bystander. The chapter does not earn the question yet, so it reads as a cliffhanger rather than a mystery.
  - Pacing: the first 200 lines are a long lead-in to a single event. The office and coffee-shop scenes could lose a third and still establish everything.
  - Logic: the blue flash, the pressure and the landing in a forest with "Bare branches against grey sky" are a jump with no explanation and no first reaction beyond fear. That may be intentional. It is a first-chapter hook, so it is fine for now.

- **Prose**:
  - Worst offenders, quoted exactly:
    - "There it was again. The deflection. Answer briefly, then redirect to something about me.The door closing." Three problems: the narrator names the device ("The deflection") as if the reader cannot see it; there is a missing space after the period; and "The door closing." is a fragment that is not a fragment of anything, so it reads as a typo. It also breaks the rule that the reader should work out the door-closing image from the scene.
    - "And just like that, the door closed. Like it always did when I got too close to asking what I actually wanted to know." This is the chapter's clearest case of telling the reader what the moment means. The door image is set up (line 199 has it too) and then explained.
    - "A gust of wind hit us and I stumbled slightly. Jason's hand came up automatically \- then stopped six inches from my elbow, hovering in the space between helping and not touching. He dropped it back to his side. "You good?" "Yeah." I was. I always was. And he never touched me unless he absolutely had to." The image of the hand stopping works. The last two sentences then explain it. The stray backslash before the dash is a formatting artifact.
    - "I just want, " I gestured vaguely. "To not hate my job? Pay rent without panic? That feels like enough, you know?" The comma-then-quote mistake is a copy-editing error, and the sentence is good dialogue, so the error stands out.
    - "The whole,” I said, waving my arms around, “timeline thing?" Mismatched curly and straight quotes, same issue.
    - "He was quiet for a moment, considering." The "He was quiet" beat appears once here and is harmless, but it is in the family of filler beats to watch for.
    - "My heart did a stupid little flip." and "The way he said it made something flutter in my chest." These are stock romance phrasings.
  - What works, and why:
    - "He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date." The reversal lands because the list before it is specific (drink, how she takes it, which scones she pretends not to hate) and the final clause is short.
    - "Oh. Right. He was hungry. I was convenient." Three short beats, each one a step down. It works.
    - "I hated Dave. I hated his carrots." The repetition is funny and in character.
    - "*snap-crunch, snap-crunch*" is a good sound detail.
    - "I need the sugar, Jason. Don't judge me." Believable voice.
    - The portal sequence's shorter sentences ("Ground." / "I hit hard.") are the right move.

- **Predictions and questions**:
  - I expect Jason to be implicated in the blue flash, either as a cause or a survivor who knows the rules, and his "You're different" and his distance to be explained by that.
  - Mira is in a forest, probably not Columbus. I want to know where she is and whether anyone else landed with her.
  - Confusing: the "Sarah wants me to come to some gallery opening tomorrow night" line is a plot-free detail. Is it setup or set dressing?
  - Confusing: nothing in the chapter explains why the lights are flickering. If that is a foreshadowing detail, it is too minor to read as one.
  - Open question for later: is the theme of predictability and free will (the students' debate) going to be argued by the story, or only announced?

## After 02_Chapter_02.md

- **Overall opinion so far**: The book has a working premise (two people dropped into a woods that is 300 miles from where they were) and Mira's interior reasoning is the best part of the draft so far. The trouble is that Jason, who was warm, funny and specific in chapter one, has become a monosyllabic plot device for most of this chapter, and the central decision (not calling 911 for twenty minutes) is held up by narration that argues with itself. Grade: **4/10**. Reason: the concept is moving, but the dialogue is repetitive and the stakes are being asserted rather than felt.

- **Characters**:
  - *Mira*: the interiority is credible. Her attempts to rationalize (concussion, hallucination, lucid-dream tests) are a real strength, and "So either I wasn't dreaming, or my brain was being extremely detailed about maintaining the illusion." is the kind of line that sounds like her. She is consistent with chapter one: the same self-deprecating, careful voice, the same habit of explaining her own feelings.
  - *Jason*: this is the main problem. In chapter one he speaks in full, specific sentences and jokes ("Most people are boring."). In chapter two most of his lines are one- or two-word stalls:
    - "I don't know." (line 47)
    - "I know." (line 51)
    - "I don't know." (line 55)
    - "I don't know yet." (line 65), answered by Mira with "You keep saying that."
    - "I know." (line 153)
    - "I can't. Not yet." (line 135)
    He is not stoic in any interesting way. He is the same stall repeated, which is the filler-beat failure the brief warned about, and it reads as the author not knowing what Jason knows. There is also a break from chapter one: a man who could explain his coffee order in detail now cannot explain a sentence. The reader can not tell whether this is a concealment or a writing shortcut, and the text does not give a clue.
  - *Sarah, Mom, roommate*: Sarah's "You never skip gallery stuff" is consistent with chapter one. Mom's "Did you get the milk?" is a nice small domestic thread. The roommate ("Then my roommate: **Staying with a friend tonight, back tomorrow**") is new. Chapter one said "I've got friends. My apartment is decent." and never mentioned a roommate, so this is an unannounced addition, not a contradiction.

- **Plot**:
  - Good: the time discrepancy works. Mira saw "past five" on her phone in chapter one and now sees "4:12 PM." Her concussion theory is a reasonable first reaction, and the GPS showing Tennessee is a strong, concrete hook.
  - Good: the text-message test (lines 185 to 195) is an effective, grounded way to show her trying to prove reality to herself.
  - Bad, logic hole: "I could press it. Jason wouldn't stop me. He was too far away and too distracted." Then "But I didn't press it." She has no road, no people, no sirens, a GPS placing her in another state, and a companion who will not explain. The chapter spells out that "every logical part of my brain was screaming at me to call for help" (line 219), and then gives her a reason to wait that is just a feeling about a man she has known for two years. Her own list of reasons ("I didn't know what had happened or if anyone else was hurt or if there was any actual danger") argues for calling, and she does not call. The reader is asked to accept the decision because the narration says she trusts him.
  - Bad, pacing: the chapter is mostly waiting. Two checks of the phone, a text exchange and a timer. Some of this could be compressed into a paragraph.
  - Hint worth keeping: Jason's "Whether we're alone. Whether anyone followed us." (line 127) implies he expects other people to be involved. That is the most plot-bearing line in the chapter.

- **Prose**:
  - Worst offenders, quoted exactly:
    - "Trees. Trees everywhere. Bare branches against grey sky. Dead leaves covering the ground in thick drifts." This repeats the end of chapter one almost word for word ("Trees. There were trees everywhere. Bare branches against grey sky. Dead leaves covering the ground."). A deliberate echo would need a change. As written it reads as the same paragraph pasted twice.
    - "Even though every logical part of my brain was screaming at me to call for help." The telling sentence is the emotion, stated for the reader.
    - "Because as angry as I was, as scared as I was, I trusted him." Its "as ... as" structure is fine as a rhythm, but it is followed by "Two years of coffee and conversation and careful distance, and I still trusted him more than I trusted my own understanding of what was happening." That is the third use of the "two years" refrain (chapters one and two, lines 35, 123, 153, 227) and it is now doing explanatory work the scene should do.
    - "His hands were at his sides but his fingers kept twitching slightly." Stock tell-the-emotion gesture.
    - "He looked at the screen. His face was carefully neutral. Too neutral." The narrator is interpreting Jason's face for the reader rather than letting the reader see it.
    - "I didn't want to lie to her more than I already was." Accurate, but the sentence after the "Good." is the clearest example in the chapter of the narrator explaining the moment. "She didn't respond to that. Good." is the sort of beat the brief asked me to look for.
  - What works, and why:
    - "So either I wasn't dreaming, or my brain was being extremely detailed about maintaining the illusion." The joke is in the word "extremely" and in the fact that the sentence turns her own reasoning against her. It is the best sentence in the chapter.
    - The text-message test. "I opened a text message and typed: The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." Then the check-navigate-return sequence is concrete, and the reader can run it too.
    - "I dug my fingernails into my palms, using the pain to focus." Physical, specific, and it sets up the palm scrapes from the opening.
    - "Read it. Navigated away. Came back. Read it again." Short imperative beats mirror the action. This works better than the average beat in the draft.

- **Predictions and questions**:
  - I expect Jason's silence to be explained by his knowledge of the place, or of "whether anyone followed us." The "followed us" line makes it likely that other people are involved, possibly the people behind the blue flash.
  - I want to know why the timer exists at all: Jason's twenty minutes is an arbitrary ticking clock, and I cannot tell if it is a plot device or a character trait.
  - Mira's last check is "Just me and Jason and trees..." so the chapter ends with the same two people alone. Chapter three needs to break that isolation.
  - Confusing: why she did not try calling Sarah, her mother, or a 911 dispatcher once she had messaged them. She texts everyone but does not call anyone.
  - Earlier I thought Jason was simply a withholding romantic lead (chapter one). The chapter-two stalls make me now suspect the author has not decided what he knows, and I will test that against the later documents.



## After 03_Chapter_03.md

- **Overall opinion so far**: This is the first chapter where the premise pays off on the page. Jason finally talks in full sentences, the stakes (mortality, memory erasure, a choice with no informed consent) are concrete, and the central question is good: if the man you love can edit your memories, how would you ever know? The chapter is also the most overwritten so far, with the same few beats repeated until they stop meaning anything. Grade: **5/10**. Reason: the best conceptual material in the draft so far, delivered in a voice that keeps explaining its own emotions and running long.

- **Characters**:
  - *Jason*: this chapter contradicts chapter two. In chapter two he was a stall ("I don't know." "I know." "I can't. Not yet."). In chapter three he is fluent, emotional and often specific. The best single line of his in the draft is: "I didn't mean, I mean, I did mean it, but I wasn't going to say it. Not like this." That is a person, not a stall. The reader is left to wonder which Jason is the real one, and I suspect the author wrote chapter two's Jason as a placeholder. He also now has a concrete backstory and a mission ("They want what I know"), which means chapter one's "contract work. Programming stuff." was a lie told to Mira's face. That is a useful thing for the author to keep: it makes his "I wasn't lying. I just wasn't telling you everything" (line 129) a sharper line than it would otherwise be.
  - *Mira*: she is consistent in her panic and her reasoning, and she is good at refusing the options given to her ("That's not fair," "Don't you *ever* suggest that again."). One real contradiction: chapter one says "I'd learned to stop hoping for different." (line 35). Chapter three says "Two years of waiting to hear those words and he was saying them like this" (line 117). She cannot have stopped hoping and also been waiting for two years. The author needs to pick one, or make her lie to herself, which the text does not signal.
  - Stoic or robotic behavior: none in Mira. Jason's "I know." recurs (lines 279, 313), but it is now a reaction to specific things, not a stall.

- **Plot**:
  - Good: the memory-erasure reveal (lines 165 to 185) is the strongest plot idea so far. It makes every conversation in chapters one and two suspect. "I wanted to believe him. But how would I know? How could anyone know if their memories had been edited? The thought made me nauseous." is the chapter's cleanest, most unforced beat.
  - Good: the "I love you" confession (line 115) arrives as a reason to leave, which is the right way to undercut a romance trope. It is a fair escalation from the careful distance of chapter one.
  - Good: the "informed consent" problem is a real moral dilemma, not just a plot obstacle. The ideal version of this book is built on it.
  - Bad, continuity: Mira says, "You said they don't care about me. That I'm useless to them." (line 327). Nothing like that appears in this chapter. Jason says "They want what I know" (line 49) and "They know I was in Columbus" (line 59), not that they find her useless. Either a conversation is missing or Mira is misremembering, and the text does not signal which.
  - Bad, logic hole: the plan to get home is never stated. Jason says "I get us back to Columbus" (line 55), then "I was planning to get you back to Ohio as soon as I could manage it. Tomorrow, probably." (line 309). Nothing explains how two people walk out of the Tennessee woods, at dusk, with no road and no transport, to Ohio by morning. The teleport-or-not question ("Is it teleportation?" "Well. Not really. I mean, kind of. Sort of.") makes this worse, because the reader does not know what Jason can actually do.
  - Bad, the ticking clock: the decision deadline shifts three times (twenty minutes, then "twelve hours," then "until we get back to Ohio"). Each shift is a reasonable character move, but the chapter presents them as if they were settled.
  - Pacing: the chapter is about 3,500 words of one conversation. The middle (lines 199 to 249) repeats the same options and the same refusals three times. A cut of a third would sharpen it.

- **Prose**:
  - Worst offenders, quoted exactly:
    - Broken punctuation and formatting, which keeps happening: "There's always a choice." My voice came out harder than I meant it to. " then a new paragraph, "Not this time." Also "I stopped breathing.The number didn't make sense." and "You said kids end up with this sometimes.Their brains just figure it out?" That is the same missing-space error from chapter one ("me.The door closing."). It is in the manuscript three times now and is a copy-edit problem, not a style choice.
    - Line 131 ends mid-sentence with no punctuation: "You decided you'd just let me twist myself in knots wondering what I was doing wrong, why you wouldn't" The sentence is truncated; it may be a missing paragraph.
    - The "Two years" refrain. It appears roughly nine times in this chapter alone, for example "Two years. Two *fucking* years of sitting exactly the right distance apart" (line 123) and "Two years. Two years of sitting exactly twelve inches apart..." (line 143). The refrain is doing the emotional work the scene should do with action or detail, and by the third use it reads as a tic.
    - "It didn't feel like a gift. It felt like a door slamming in my face." The door image is now used in chapters one (line 199 and line 233) and three. The third use is the most explicit, and it follows a line that already tells the reader what the moment means.
    - "Just yes. Simple as that. Your safety matters more than your opinion." The second sentence explains the first.
    - "He didn't ask why I was considering it then. He had to know. I'd already made it clear I didn't want to lose him. That was answer enough." Three narrator explanations in a row.
    - Line 283 switches tense mid-scene: "he spins his hands around like he's trying to think". The rest of the chapter is past tense.
    - The ending: "It didn't work." / "Nothing worked." after "Hard enough to see stars." These are the profound one-line paragraphs the brief warned about. They land as a summary, not as a moment.
    - "Breathing. Existing. Trying to remember how to do both." A three-beat fragment with a tidy last clause. It sounds like a closing line. It is one of several in the chapter.
  - What works, and why:
    - "I wanted to believe him. But how would I know? How could anyone know if their memories had been edited? The thought made me nauseous." Plain, unforced, and it is the right question.
    - The option list at lines 271 to 273 is a good device for a mind skipping tracks. "Option one: He leaves, I never see him again, I'm safe. Option two: I follow him and die. Option two: I follow him and die. Option two" The stutter is the point. It is a little too neat, but the device is earned.
    - "Imagine uplifting your pet to human level intelligence." The pet analogy is a clear, well-chosen image for the consent problem, and it is in Jason's voice.
    - "I'm not interested in the abilities," I said. Her motive is stated clearly and it is the right motive for her.
    - Sister's graduation in June, and the roll of film she might never develop. These are specific, personal stakes, and they are the kind of detail the chapter needs more of.
    - "I didn't mean, I mean, I did mean it, but I wasn't going to say it. Not like this." Jason's stutter here is characterization. It is the only place in the draft where the broken syntax is doing something.

- **Predictions and questions**:
  - I expect Mira to choose the "tell me everything" path, or for the erasure option to come back later as a betrayal. The memory-trust problem will carry the book if the author lets it.
  - The zebras in the title have not appeared in three chapters. Either they are coming, or the title is a decoy. I am curious and suspicious in equal measure.
  - Confusing: what exactly does "randomized" mean? Jason says it hides their location from "them," but "them" is still undefined. Who are the people in Columbus, and how did they find Jason in the first place?
  - Confusing: the body change is described as "compatible with the skills," then as a change that starts "the process" just from being told. That is a rule the author has to fix before the reader can trust any later consequence.
  - Open question: Jason's "I love you" lands harder than it should, given that the chapter gives us almost no evidence of it beyond the careful distance. If the book leans on this romance, chapter one and two need more of the feeling, not less.


## After 04_Chapter_06.md

- **Overall opinion so far**: This is a short, mostly expository scene in a car, and it is the first place the book's title earns its keep. The zebra metaphor is a good, clear line that the reader can hold onto. But the chapter explains a whole cosmology in about six hundred words, to a character who in chapter three said he would not explain more, and it skips whatever happened between the woods and the highway. I am now reading a book in which the premise is being delivered rather than dramatized. Grade: **4.5/10** overall. Reason: the best concept material yet, delivered as a lecture. Chapter six alone: **4/10**.

- **Characters**:
  - *Jason*: he is fluent, dry and consistent with chapter three's Jason, and that is good. The one-line "He said it without softening it, which I appreciated." is in character. But he has changed his position without the page showing why. In chapter three he refused more detail: "No." The word was flat. Final. "I'm already worried I've said too much. I'm not going into more detail." In chapter six he gives a full explanation of another dimension, a mana economy and predator behaviour. The narration says "He was quiet for a moment, the way he got when he was deciding how much to say and in what order." That sentence tells us he is a careful explainer, which is a character trait, but it does not explain why he changed his mind about how much to say. This is an inconsistency between scenes that the reader will feel.
  - *Mira*: the best character moment in the chapter is her dry response: "Tell me what I need to know to stay alive in the next three months and I'll have an existential crisis about the rest of it later." It is funny, it sounds like her, and it is a real change from the panic of chapter three. But it is a change the reader was not shown. Between chapter three (she has not decided) and chapter six (she is already in the car and already "stepped out of the grass"), there is nothing on the page. Chapters four and five are missing, so this may be a gap in the draft, but as a reader I have to take her choice on trust.

- **Plot**:
  - Good: the zebra model. "So we're zebras," I said. / "Hidden in tall grass." It is the title payoff, it is clear, and it gives the reader a model for the whole premise. I was wrong in my chapter three note that the zebras might be a decoy. The metaphor is in the title and now in the text, and it works.
  - Good: "Were invisible." / "Were," he agreed. A small callback that lands.
  - Bad, a rule broken: in chapter three Jason says that telling Mira "enough to understand even part of it" will make her brain work out the rest and her body start changing. In chapter six he tells her a great deal, and says "You're producing very little right now." If the telling triggers the change, he has already done it. The draft has not explained whether Mira is changing yet, and the reader is left to assume the rule has been quietly dropped.
  - Bad, timing error: "I hadn't known existed this morning." Chapter three ends at dusk on the day of the woods scene, so this car ride is at least the next day. "This morning" is wrong. Either the author meant "yesterday," or the car ride is on the same day, which chapter three's ending rules out.
  - Bad, the gap: chapters four and five are missing, and the opening of chapter six starts with a question ("How long before something notices me?") that implies a conversation we have not seen. This is expected in a draft at this stage, but the reader does not know what to make of the scene's abrupt entry.
  - Bad, a number: "Seven billion of us" is out of date for a 2026 setting, since the world population passed eight billion in 2022. It is a small detail, but it is the sort of error a reader of a contemporary novel notices, and it is in the one line that is supposed to be clever.
  - Logic: "Humans produce zero mana naturally. We're invisible to them." The "invisible" rule makes the predators' indifference a feature of the world, which is a good idea. But the chapter then says "they notice anything that produces," and that Mira's small output will get her noticed "In a few weeks." The rule is stated, but how the predators find people who produce mana is not explained, and the reader will need it later.

- **Prose**:
  - Worst offenders, quoted exactly:
    - "The creatures there are predators in the way anything is a predator, they eat what's available and avoid what costs more energy than it's worth." This is a comma splice inside a sentence that is also grammatically awkward ("in the way anything is a predator"). Read aloud, it is a lecture.
    - "Crossing between dimensions costs energy. A lot of it. And the cost scales with how much mana you have, the more powerful something is, the more expensive the crossing." The second half is a run-on that stacks three clauses with no clear subject for "the more powerful something is."
    - "You're producing very little right now. Think of it like, you've just lit a match in a dark room. The flame is real but the room is large." The match analogy is fine; the problem is "Think of it like," which is how a person talks, and it sits awkwardly in a sentence that is otherwise a textbook. The next line, "The problem is the flame gets bigger as you develop," then explains the analogy the reader already has.
    - "He said it without softening it, which I appreciated. Softening it would have made it worse somehow." The second sentence explains the first. It is a small instance of the narrator explaining a moment that works without the explanation.
    - "That's the only reason any of this works. Not because we're hidden or they don’t know about us or can’t, just because we're not worth the trip." A good line in its last clause, undercut by the stacked "Not because... or they don't know about us or can't" before it. The curly and straight apostrophes are mixed in the same paragraph.
    - "completely unaware it was balanced on something I hadn't known existed this morning." Ornate for a car-window description, and the timing error is in it.
  - What works, and why:
    - "So we're zebras," I said. / "Hidden in tall grass. Seven billion of us and none of them can see us because we don't smell like food." The zebra line is clear, funny in its bluntness, and it puts Mira's voice in the middle of the exposition, which is the right place for it.
    - "Were invisible." / "Were," he agreed. Two words, a callback, and a beat of dry humour from both characters.
    - "Tell me what I need to know to stay alive in the next three months and I'll have an existential crisis about the rest of it later." The best sentence in the chapter. It has a joke, a character, and a real emotional state under it.
    - "That might actually be the healthiest response I've ever seen to this conversation." / "Don't get used to it," I said. The exchange has rhythm and it lets both characters be funny. It is the first place in the draft where Jason's wit and Mira's voice play off each other without a stall in between.
    - The window details (the gas station, the billboard for a personal injury lawyer, the minivan with a dog's head out the back window) are specific and ground the scene. The lawyer billboard is good, and it is better than any explanation in the chapter.

- **Predictions and questions**:
  - I expect the car ride to be where the mana rules get tested, probably with some predator or some sign that the "invisible" rule is failing. The "weeks, maybe less" clock is now the book's real timer.
  - I want to know where chapters four and five went, and what changed Mira's mind between chapter three and this scene.
  - Confusing: "Some do. They don't care. We're not worth the energy cost." The smart predators know about humans but do not bother with them. If that is true, the "notice" mechanism has to be aimed at a different class of predator, and I cannot tell which.
  - Earlier I thought the book's central problem was how the romance and the memory trust would hold together. Chapter six makes me think the central problem is different: the author seems to want the world-building to carry the book, and the characters are being used to deliver it.
