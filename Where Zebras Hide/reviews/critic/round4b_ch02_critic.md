## Chapter 2: summary

- **Grade: 5/10** as prose a published novel could contain.
- The first scene is the strongest thing in the draft. The nausea, the water, the asked-before-touching ribs, the cold moss and the cheekbone-shadow line are specific and unforced. After that the chapter becomes the outline put into past tense. The tell is that nearly every beat is the outline's own sentence with "she" changed to "I": "I tried a concussion", "I tried a glitch", "she sees only that he is standing still", "I read it twice before I answered", "I deleted the numbers one at a time", "I didn't move from the bottom of the rise". Several of these are the exact constructions the brief names as amateur: a clue-pointer ("I noticed that"), the "twice" repetition, and a closing sentence that records a non-action. The four-text parade in Scene 3 is handled with the same rhythm each time (message arrives, one-line reply, next), so it reads as a checklist and not as a frightened woman using her phone. Her fear is not on the page: the 911 beat is a tidy list of practical obstacles, and the decision the chapter is meant to turn on is never felt. Jason is the best-handled character: composed, exact and never fumbling. The ending lands on the outline's allowed end, but with a redundant closing sentence. Fact errors are small except two: the clock arithmetic, and Jason agreeing to "all of it".

## Ruling on the drafter's claimed conflict ("He comes down" vs the Limit)

The Limit is "Describing what Jason is doing up on the rise: at most 2 times in the chapter, each in different terms." Beat 6 is "He comes down." Coming down is leaving the rise, not describing what he does up there, so the Limit does not block the beat. The drafter's stated reason does not hold.

There is a real conflict, but it is elsewhere in the outline. Beat 6 ("He comes down") duplicates the last line of the outline, "Next chapter opens: Jason coming down off the rise." The outline's own End line also offers "her standing her ground at the bottom of the rise" as an acceptable ending. So the drafter's choice to stop before he moves is allowed by the End line. Beat 6 is omitted, which the outline permits.

Ruling: no Limit conflict. The outline double-books the descent. Fix in the outline: cut beat 6 and let Chapter 3 own the descent (see the outline fixes below).

Separately, the draft is itself at or over the Limit. Descriptions of Jason on the rise:
1. "stopped near the top with his back to me. A few leaves came loose from a branch above him"
2. "From where I sat I could see only that he was standing still."
3. "Up on the rise, Jason was standing where he had been."

Items 2 and 3 are the same terms (standing still / standing where he had been). That is either three descriptions or two in the same words. Both break "each in different terms".

## Findings

### 1. Wrong story facts

1.1 **The clock arithmetic.** "The clock at the top of the screen read 4:47 PM. It was an hour early." Ch 1 fixes the attack at 5:47 Eastern, which is 4:47 Central, the landing minute. By the time she reads the phone she has been sick, been checked over, searched her pockets and bag, sat down and opened the map. That is at least ten minutes. The phone should read 4:57 or later. As written, no time passed. Ch 1 is the author's to alter; the fix is in the outline (see list).

1.2 **Jason promises everything.** "Then you tell me what happened, all of it. I'm holding you to it." / "All right." The outline says Jason does not promise everything. His "All right." accepts "all of it". He should not reply to that clause, or should reply only to the number ("Twenty.").

1.3 **Prints the time the outline says to withhold.** Outline S1.7: "It gives neither time." Draft: "read 4:47 PM." The outline contradicts itself here (its Set-up says "The phone reads 4:47"). The draft took the sensible reading, but it still breaks the literal line.

1.4 **"Saw", not "noticed".** "I need to know whether anyone out here saw us come down." The set-up is that someone could have noticed them arrive, which Ch 3 and later chapters treat as sensing mana. "Saw" narrows it to a visual witness and sends Jason up a hill to look. This steers Ch 3's explanation the wrong way. Use "noticed", or "felt".

1.5 **Phone location.** "My phone was in my coat, where I had put it at the office." Ch 1: the phone buzzed "against the table" in the shop and she "checked my phone" on the walk. It was out of the pocket twice after the office. The fact log says the draft never shows it going back. Keep the pocket, drop the claim about where it was put.

1.6 **"Checked the timer once."** "At ten minutes I checked the timer once." She already checked it in Scene 3: "I checked that the timer was still running, which it was" and "I looked at the timer". The outline's "once" does not survive the draft.

1.7 **Slope and rise.** The log is "at the foot of the slope". Jason "walked up a rise where the trees thinned". Later "The wind came down the slope" and "I stood up and walked to the bottom of the rise." If the rise is the slope, she is already at its bottom and the closing walk is meaningless. If not, no distance is given. State the geography once.

### 2. Characters built from traits, not lives

2.1 **Mira as an outline-follower.** "I tried a concussion, because it was the explanation I already had." / "I tried a glitch." Her reasoning is accurate and neat, but it is the same shape twice, each ending on a clean verdict. A frightened woman would skip around, try a wrong idea and abandon it, or get the reason wrong. She tests two theories in turn and moves on.

2.2 **Fear missing from Scene 3.** The outline says "She is frightened in this scene." Nothing on the page shows it. She answers four people in order with composed one-liners. The one place fear could show is the 911 beat, and that is a list of reasons: "A dispatcher would ask where I was." / "She would want an address, and I didn't have one." A real person would not decide this by logic. The beat needs a body or a mistake in it (her thumb hits the wrong digit, she can't think what she'd say first).

2.3 **Jason's narrated competence.** "He moved fast, and his hands went straight to what they needed." The narrator hands the reader a verdict on him. The actions already show it. Cut.

2.4 **Jason's apology.** "That was my hand." He held my eyes a moment. "I'm sorry." The outline wants kindness without comment. A flat apology plus "He held my eyes a moment" (a lingering-look microgesture the STYLE bans) is the draft adding a romance beat, not a reaction he would have.

### 3. Manufactured quirks and props

3.1 **Red knuckles.** "His knuckles were red from the cold." This is a word-for-word repeat of Ch 1 ("His knuckles were red from the cold."). In Ch 1 it was an observation in a warm shop; here it is a callback with no new reason. STYLE bans repeating a line from another chapter. Cut or replace with something she sees now.

3.2 **Timer as a prop.** "I checked that the timer was still running, which it was." / "I looked at the timer, which had started counting down while I wasn't watching." Both are filler gestures. The second is also nonsense: the timer began when she set it.

3.3 **Marcus's photo.** "A woman from accounts I barely knew was half cut off at the edge, laughing at something outside the picture. At the end of the row a man had his hand flat on the bar, and somebody's coat hung off the back of a stool." About 70 words of invented detail about people who do not matter, in a scene where she is supposedly afraid. The outline says "Add no ... invented places" and to pick two textures. The warm-light line earlier in the same paragraph ("the lights had turned every face a shade warmer than it was") is the only part that earns its place.

3.4 **The wind-and-leaves paragraph.** "The leaves on top of the drift had dried at the edges, and the ones underneath were still dark with wet." This repeats the paragraph-one observation ("brown on top and black underneath, where they had been lying in the wet"). The paragraph is length with no job.

### 4. Polished banter, and jokes in the wrong places

No jokes. Good. But the exchanges are clean and symmetrical:
- "Fifteen," / "Twenty." / "Fine," ... / "All right." A tidy bargaining pair that ends on a neat agreement.
- "Does this hurt?" / "No." / "Move your fingers for me."

The outline asks for one interruption and one repeated question ("She interrupts him once and repeats a question."). Neither is in the draft. Where she says "Out here where?" and "Who would be out here, Jason?", she waits for him to finish, then asks two different questions. Have her cut him off mid-sentence and ask "What do you mean, see us?" twice.

### 5. Not trusting the reader

5.1 **Clue-pointing.** "The words came out smooth, smoother than anything I'd written all day. I noticed that, and then I looked at the timer." The brief gives "I noticed that, and put it next to the clock" as the example of this problem. The outline asked for one notice of the ease; the draft used a pointing sentence and then buried it under a second pointer. Show the ease: the reply sent before she has thought about what to say, the thumb already moving.

5.2 **Repetition.** "I read it twice before I answered." The brief's own example. Caused by the outline. Drop "twice" and show her hesitating over a word.

5.3 **Non-action as closing beat.** "I didn't move from the bottom of the rise." The previous sentence says she walked to the bottom. The last line states the staying put, which is the null-space beat STYLE forbids, and it is a thesis-by-gesture. End on "Time's up," I called up, or on his first step.

5.4 **Unexplained test.** "The sentence hadn't changed. I didn't have another test." The text never says what she tests for (dreaming). "Another test" refers to nothing the reader was told. Either name it in four words ("People say text won't hold still in dreams.") or leave it unlabelled and cut "another test".

5.5 **Sun pointer.** "The gray over the trees had gone darker, and there was no sun anywhere to account for it." In February at 4:47 Central the light is going, and a photographer would know that. This reads as hinting at the clock, which the outline says not to hint. It also repeats the darkening already given twice ("darker along the edges of the woods", "the woods seemed darker than they had a minute before").

5.6 **Narrated mental state.** "Nobody in Columbus knew yet that I hadn't come home." Telling the reader the stakes of the text exchange. Cut.

5.7 **Shown reason for the lie.** "Sarah knew how my job went, so I gave her that." This turns the planned lie into a stated tactic, which is opposite to the outline's unplanned smoothness.

### 6. Sentence-level habits

6.1 **Short-sentence cluster.** Metrics: longest run 3. Worst: "Sarah answered within a minute. 'You okay? You never skip gallery night.'" Three sentences under six words in a row, which STYLE forbids.

6.2 **Photograph physics.** "the screen lit my thumb from below, hard-edged, with no falloff into the dark around it." A small source held close to a thumb has the steepest falloff there is, and in overcast daylight the screen would barely register. A photographer would say the opposite: the thumb lit cold and blue from below, everything else flat. The line is dramatic, not accurate, and it is also the outline's own model sentence barely reworded. Later "The screen was the brightest thing for a long way around" has the same problem in daylight.

6.3 **"Rows".** "Behind him they went back in rows, dark and wet, until they blurred into the gray." Natural woods do not stand in rows. This reads as a stock forest description.

6.4 **"Stomach turned over."** One use, allowed by the metrics (x1). Plain, but it is the chapter's single stock phrase, and the scene has seven body reactions around it.

6.5 **"None of it was the sharp kind of pain that means something has broken."** A contrast setup, and an explanation.

6.6 **"The way someone does on a slope"** is a slightly vague comparison. What does he do with his weight, and why is he on a slope? He is on level ground a few yards from the log.

### 7. Scene logic that falls apart when traced

7.1 **Why 911 would be considered.** She is not badly hurt and has a friend beside her. Nothing in the draft says what she would call about. The justification offered, "She would want an address, and I didn't have one", is false: the map has a dot and a town, and a 911 call from a phone is located automatically. She could say "Crossville" in the first sentence.

7.2 **Sarah/Jess logic.** "Sarah might ask Jess about me, and Jess would have to answer." Answer what? She told Sarah work swamped her and told Jess she is at a friend's. These do not conflict. The reader cannot work out the worry.

7.3 **Marcus's text.** "Are you meeting your friend tonight, or did he talk you out of this?" Marcus knew she was meeting Jason at five and is writing after six. A person would ask "Did you make it to your friend?" or "Is he still your friend." The "or did he talk you out of this?" depends on "this" meaning Murphy's, which was a minute-long exchange that morning.

7.4 **Repeated pointless darkness.** The light darkens in three places (see 5.5) and each time is treated as new.

7.5 **Sat, shifted, stayed.** "The wood was soaked through the seat of my coat within seconds, and I shifted forward off it and stayed where I was anyway." She sat, then shifted off, then stayed where she was. Later she is back on the log: "I sat back down on the log" and "The cold came up through the seat of my coat from the wet log." Choose: she sits on the wet log and it is cold, or she perches on the edge.

7.6 **Ribs.** "The second, lower down, hurt, and I told him so. ... 'Nothing feels broken.'" A man who is careful would say "bruised" or "tender", and the draft never gives him a conclusion about the sore spot. Also her palms are visibly scraped and bleeding and he never looks at them.

7.7 **Screenshot of trees.** "Then I turned a slow circle and took one of the nearest trunk". A screenshot cannot capture the trees. This is a photo. A photographer would take more than one frame, and would not turn a full circle to photograph one trunk.

### 8. Side characters as functions, and padding

8.1 Sarah, Marcus, Mom and Jess are each reduced to one message and one plot function (lie, tease, milk, worry). Marcus's photo has the only life, and it is the part that should be cut (3.3).

8.2 Padding: the wind-and-leaves paragraph (about 100 words), the Marcus photo detail (about 70 words), the "I looked past him for a road or a fence" listening paragraph (about 55 words, repeating that no road is on the map).

## Observed or performed?

## Fixes

**Fixes to the outline (chapter level)**
1. Scene 1 beat 7 and the Set-up: change "She looks at the time: 4:47 PM" and "gives neither time" to one rule, for example "the phone reads 4:5x, a few minutes after the landing clock, and the prose names that number once and never says 5:47." Ch 1 fixes the landing at 4:47 Central, so the clock cannot still read 4:47 after ten minutes of checking.
2. Scene 4 beat 6 and "Next chapter opens": remove the duplication. Ch 2 ends at "Time's up," called from the bottom of the rise, and Ch 3 opens with him coming down.
3. Scene 2: replace "He says this in his own words, once" with the specific word: "noticed" or "felt", not "saw". Add "Jason answers 'twenty' and does not reply to 'all of it'."
4. Scene 3: drop "reads it twice" and the "notices this once" instruction in favour of "show the ease: the reply is sent before she has decided what to say." Add what is frightening her (what she can't say aloud), and give the 911 beat a reason she considered calling.
5. Scene 3 and Texture: remove "Every text message ... is shown in quotation marks" from the outline's prose and put it in STYLE once, where it already is. Add a limit: 40 words on any text message photo or description.
6. Scene 1 beat 8: say "photographs the trunk", not "screenshots the trees". Ask for two or three photos of different things so it reads as a photographer's reflex.

**Fixes to STYLE**
1. "No word except a name appears more than three times in a chapter." Restate as "no noun, verb or adjective", and name the exemptions (function words, "said", the person's name). As written it is unmeasurable and was broken by almost any prose.
2. "The screen lighting her thumb from underneath is a good model; do not reuse it word for word." Take this out of the outline and put in a rule: "Do not use a light-on-skin observation that the outline or STYLE gives as an example; the drafter makes their own."
3. Add: "A narrator's observation of light must be physically right for the time and place. Overcast daylight is flat; a phone screen in it is dim, not 'the brightest thing'."
4. Add: "When the outline says she does not do something, write what she does instead. Never write a sentence whose only content is the non-action ('I didn't ask', 'I didn't move')." (It is already under Null-space beats, but the outline's wording keeps triggering it.)
5. Add a rule against sequential message handling: "When several messages arrive, do not answer them in order with equal weight. Let one interrupt another, leave one unanswered, or combine two replies."
6. Add: "If a character is afraid, show it in one body detail or one mistake, not in a plan. Competent reasoning is not a sign of fear."
