## Chapter 1: summary

- **Grade: 4/10** as prose a published novel could contain.
- **One paragraph:** The draft is clean and hits almost every outline beat in outline order, and that is the trouble. It reads as the beat list performed: office, Marcus, text, latte, Pentax, darkroom, Nora, parents, milk, Jason's parents, gift, students, Thai place, walk, stumble, loneliness, "You're different," attack. Nothing in the chapter is observed in a way only this narrator would observe it, so the stated goal ("makes the reader like her in it") is not met. Mira never notices a face, a light, a pair of hands. The dialogue is a chain of Jason asking a question and Mira answering it. Where the draft needs a gap between short lines it fills it with meaningless gestures (a cup moved, a cup kept in front of her). Narration repeatedly explains the beat it just showed ("I let him make the turn," "so I wouldn't have to say anything yet," "He said it evenly"). Several logic problems come straight from the outline (the instant latte, Nora's location, the students' topic), and the chapter is about 40% under length, with the old-life material squeezed into one expository paragraph that the outline said to spread out. The attack is the strongest part, but it leans on a near-verbatim copy of the outline's skin sensation and on a flat "I was going to die" line.

## Findings

### 1. Wrong story facts and outline breaks

1. "\"You good?\""
   - Jason's line after her stumble. CHARACTERS and STYLE say he speaks in measured full sentences; this is slang a different man would use.
   - Instead: "Are you all right?" or an action, such as him asking whether she turned her ankle.

2. "\"Sometimes.\" He was quiet for a moment. \"I'm not great with people in general.\""
   - CHARACTERS: "He isn't socially awkward; he's guarded. He reads people well." The invented self-description is false for him, and it is a set-up so that "You're different" lands. The outline only has him say "sometimes."
   - Instead: let him answer "Sometimes" and add one specific, true, guarded thing (that he works from home and can go days without speaking aloud). Do not give him a rule about himself.

3. "\"It's almost five on a Friday.\""
   - Marcus says this at the office. The outline puts the office at about 4:30, and Jason's text says coffee at five. At almost five she cannot reach the shop and find Jason still at the counter. The clock also invites the reader to ask how she got there.
   - Instead: "It's almost half past four on a Friday." Leave with time in hand.

4. "Three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots"
   - The outline says "at the next desk." Small, but it breaks the stated layout and weakens "loud enough to fill the nearly empty floor."
   - Instead: "At the next desk, Dave was eating baby carrots." Keep the verbatim line.

5. "At the next table, two students had been arguing about free will for ten minutes. Now they were loud enough for the whole room to hear."
   - The outline says they "start arguing," "get louder." The draft turns it into ten minutes of argument, which Mira has not mentioned in the five pages before it, so the reader is told it was happening unseen.
   - Instead: the argument starts while she is still looking at the film. Show it start.

6. "Nora had called that afternoon, convinced she'd be lonely in Chicago, and I kept thinking about it."
   - The pronoun is ambiguous (Nora or Mira). It also contradicts the earlier paragraph, where Nora already lives in Chicago and sends Mira listings. Both come from the documents (see Documents section).
   - Instead: pick one fact, with Nora in Chicago already and Mira unsure what she thinks about that.

7. "\"Sarah keeps recommending that Thai place. We could try it.\""
   - The outline says "a few blocks away." The draft never says how far, so the later "We had walked four blocks, and the restaurant still wasn't in sight" has nothing to contradict.
   - Instead: one clause, "it's about a ten-minute walk," in her suggestion.

8. The attack time.
   - The outline says "about 5:50." The draft never states it. "We left at twenty to six" is the last clock reading, and nothing later tells the reader how long they have walked. Chapter 2 cannot match a time the chapter never gave.
   - Instead: one glance at her phone clock or a church bell would fix it. The phone is in her coat, so a quick look is natural.

9. "Under these lights" (Jason's line, "Your green film would look strange under these lights").
   - The outline puts them "under the orange streetlights." The narration never establishes streetlights, a dusk sky, or the colour of anything, so Jason's line refers to something never set up.
   - Instead: add a half-sentence in the walk, such as the streetlights coming on one at a time along the park edge.

10. Outline "Do not": "Have Jason do experiments, give technical explanations, or react to the students." The draft writes: "I winced. Jason went on as though the argument were happening somewhere else."
    - The narration is commenting on his non-reaction. That is the null-space beat the STYLE bans, and it is also an analogy ("as though"). It also points at Jason's calm in a scene where the outline says nothing about him.
    - Instead: cut the Jason sentence. Mira can say "Can we get out of here?" and he can say yes.

11. Outline Scene 2 beat 5: "She is ridiculously happy and tries not to show it."
    - The draft delivers only the hiding: "I put the roll in my coat pocket and took a sip of latte so I wouldn't have to say anything yet." The happiness never appears on the page.
    - Instead: let her say something slightly too much about the box or the stock, then stop herself. That shows both.

12. Outline Scene 3 beat 4, working up to a question.
    - The draft writes: "I had been working up to asking Jason something since the coffee shop." This is the outline's note pasted into narration, and the coffee shop scene shows no sign of it.

13. Limit check, "Information given to the reader twice: 0": fails (see problem 5). Short-line volleys: fails (see problem 6). Jokes or banter at most 4, only in Scenes 1 and 2: at the edge, with these candidates: "ready to throw my stapler at them" (1), "No promises." (2), "I hated Dave. I hated his carrots." (3), "the VP would make a passive-aggressive comment about attention to detail" (4), "promised a work order" (5, arguable). Count it as 4 to 5, so at or just over.

Treat the cliche cap as used, and note the metrics script may be penalising a required line (see Documents).

### 2. Characters built from traits, not lives

1. Mira is the premise, not a person, in this draft. CHARACTERS says she "tells a bad day as a good story," "plays down her job, her city and her hobby before anyone else can," and notices "light, faces, what people do with their hands."
   - Of those, only the playing-down appears: "It's not practical right now." There is no bad-day story ("Henderson account." / "I'm not going to talk about it" is the opposite), and no observed face, light or hands anywhere in the chapter.
   - Instead: give the coffee-shop scene two or three observations only she would make (what the window light does to his face at 5:15, the way his fingers stay flat on the cup).

2. "I typed \"Yes\" before I could change my mind."
   - She already told Marcus "I have plans" and "Coffee with Jason" before the text arrived. There is nothing left for her to change her mind about. The reaction is there only to dramatise the Friday oddity.
   - Instead: she types "Yes" and puts the phone down.

3. "I looked at my screen. Three more pages to review ... If I stayed another hour, I could get most of it done. If I left now, I would spend the whole weekend thinking about it."
   - The deliberation is fake. She has coffee at five, so staying another hour is not an option she has.
   - Instead: cut the deliberation. She says "I have plans," Marcus leaves, she turns back to the screen.

4. "\"You're good with me,\" I said. \"You can't be that bad with people.\""
   - A woman in love who has asked about loneliness does not reason from one case to a general claim. The line only exists to be answered by "You're different."
   - Instead: "You seem fine to me." Short, a little embarrassed.

5. Jason's gift: "\"A camera shop across town. They had one box left.\" He shrugged. \"I saw it and thought of you.\""
   - The outline wants "I saw it and thought of you." The draft makes the three lines a neat sequence of question, location, line. A man who spent weeks looking for it would say something less tidy, perhaps how many shops he tried. That also tells her how long he searched without announcing it.

6. Marcus: "You meet him every single Tuesday. You talk about him constantly. Last month you skipped girls' night to have dinner with him."
   - Marcus is a function here: three parallel sentences that tell the reader exactly what the outline needs the reader to learn, with a gesture ("counted the items off on his fingers") to sell it. The invitation to Murphy's gives him one flicker of life and then he is gone.
   - Instead: let him say one of the three, not recite them, and let the girls' night detail come out as a complaint ("Sara's still mad at you about that").

### 3. Manufactured quirks and props

1. "I kept my cup in front of me." and "He moved his cup aside."
   - Both are filler gestures that do no work, which is exactly what STYLE warns against. Counting cup, coffee and latte beats in Scene 2: "I kept my cup in front of me," "went back to his coffee," "He moved his cup aside," "between our cups," "took a sip of latte so I wouldn't have to say anything yet." That is five against the two-use limit for repeated gestures.
   - Instead: cut the first two and the third. Keep the gift between the cups and the sip only if the sip is doing something.

2. "a table of contents that reformatted itself every time I touched it"
   - An invented prop, and the same office frustration as the pivot table, so the reader has two fiddly-document problems in a row. The outline asks for the pivot table only.
   - Instead: cut it.

3. "a book club had taken the corner with four pastries spread across one table"
   - Set dressing with a number, added to make the shop seem real. The book club and the pastries are never used.
   - Instead: one detail that Mira, as a photographer, would actually notice about this room.

4. "The benches had been chained up for winter"
   - Park benches are bolted down and are not chained for winter; a skeptical reader stumbles, and the detail does no work.
   - Instead: "The park was empty, the grass flat and brown."

5. "Marcus tipped his head and waited for a better answer." and "He grinned." / "Marcus's grin got wider."
   - A grin twice in one exchange and a head-tip. Stock gesture for a tease.

6. "a little breath of steam came out of it into the cold"
   - Not a prop, but the most "pointed" image in the walk: it sits in the exact beat the outline needs and signals that something is about to happen. It works, but it is one of only two physical observations in the scene (see What works).

Jason does nothing but interview. CHARACTERS says he is warm and has spent two years paying attention to her, and the draft gives him no unprompted observation, no remembered detail except the final gift.

### 6. Sentence-level habits

1. Short-line volleys, quoted. The STYLE says to put a full sentence of action or thought between short lines of dialogue. These fail:

   - "\"Uh huh.\"" / "\"We are.\"" / "\"Okay.\"" in the Marcus scene.
   - The metrics report a run of 6. I could not isolate which run; these three are the worst I found and likely include or sit beside it. Verbatim lines are exempt from the count, but none of these are verbatim.
   - Instead: after "Open it," give two sentences of her opening it, with the paper rustle, the box, her reading the label.

2. Present-tense slips in past-tense narration:

   - "the kind that makes you want to pull your collar up"
   - STYLE: "Stay in past tense for asides too."
   - Instead: "She was sure I'd be happier there. My parents had opinions about what I should do next, and they shared them over Sunday dinner."

3. Stock phrases and cliches:
   - "Up became down."
   - "to read the same paragraph three times without taking in a word of it"
   - "a kitchen the size of a closet"
   - "I looked down at the table."
   - "He was quiet for a moment."
   - "my skin felt like someone was peeling it off and pressing it back on wrong" (the outline's phrasing nearly word for word, with "someone" added, which personifies a physical sensation).

4. Analogy count: Scene 2 contains "a kitchen the size of a closet" and "as though the argument were happening somewhere else." Two, against the limit of one per scene. Scene 3 has the peeling-skin one only.

5. A triplet: Marcus's three parallel sentences ("You meet him every single Tuesday. You talk about him constantly. Last month you skipped girls' night to have dinner with him.") count as the chapter's triplet.

The attack paragraph is the best varied stretch; Scene 2 narration is almost all one-clause statements ("I let him make the turn. I told him I was still thinking about it").

### 7. Scene logic that falls apart when traced

1. The latte.
   - "Jason was at the counter when I came in, ordering with his back to the room. I walked past him to our table by the window. By the time I'd sat down, my latte was on the table and Jason was across from me with his own black coffee."
   - If he is ordering when she walks in, a latte cannot be made, delivered and sat in front of her seat during her walk to the table. The outline caused this (see Documents) but the draft did not notice.

2. "\"Sorry,\" I said. \"I've talked about nothing but the darkroom for twenty minutes.\""
   - The page shows about a minute of talk, and not only about the darkroom (Pentax, film, bridge, then the darkroom). She says twenty minutes, nothing on the page backs it, and "Most people are boring" has nothing to answer.
   - Instead: show her running on (at least three more exchanges, with Jason asking something that pulls the tangent along), then the apology.

3. "Nora had called that afternoon, convinced she'd be lonely in Chicago." / "She's sure I'd be happier there."
   - Nora lives in Chicago (CHARACTERS) and is sending Mira listings there. "She'd be lonely" in the future tense does not fit someone who already lives there. Whoever "she" is, the reader cannot tell. Also Nora "had sent me another apartment listing that morning" means Mira already knows Nora's mood, so the call is a second contact on the same subject.

4. "\"Yeah,\" I said, and kept walking, because the sidewalk was a mess and I didn't want to fall again."
   - She did not fall; she stumbled. "Again" invents a fall. The cause of the stumble is also doubled: wind and cracked sidewalk. The outline says wind.

5. "We had walked four blocks, and the restaurant still wasn't in sight."
   - The outline says a ten-minute walk. Four blocks is about five minutes, so either the pace is slow or time is missing. The sentence is also the author setting up the attack at the right time; it is the only clock-adjacent fact in the scene and it is an awkward one.

6. Convenient timing.
   - "He opened his mouth, and a little breath of steam came out of it into the cold. / The air cracked."
   - The attack arrives exactly as he is about to answer. The outline demands this, so it is a document problem, but the draft makes it more visible with the steam image on the cut.

7. The street.
   - No passer-by, car, bus or sound is in the walk, in a downtown street at about 5:50 pm on a Friday. A blue flash and a hit that Mira feels in her chest has no witnesses and no reaction. A skeptical reader accepts "Nobody is visible" for the attackers, not for the whole street.
   - Instead: one car going by at the intersection, or a couple behind them. This also gives later chapters witnesses to worry about.

8. Where is the paper bag?
   - "He set it on the table between our cups." / "I put the roll in my coat pocket." The bag and the box are never accounted for. Not important now, but any later mention of the film will need to know what she is holding.

9. "Mira has a crossbody bag." The draft says "slung my bag over my shoulder" and "my bag on mine" (shoulder), and "My bag hung off my chair." The bag could be crossbody; the draft never says. A reader will imagine a shoulder bag that could slip in the jump.

10. Light and time of year. The outline sets orange streetlights at about 5:40 to 5:50 pm on February 9 in Columbus, with a grey sky at the end in Tennessee. Dusk is plausible but marginal at that time, and the draft sets neither.

## What works

1. "Both times they'd stood under the fixture with their hands on their hips and promised a work order." Specific, dry, observed, and it sets up the flickering light without leaning on it.
2. "\"Because once I develop it, I'll know what I actually shot.\"" A real reason for putting something off, and the only line where Mira sounds like herself.
3. "There was a flash of blue light right in front of us, close enough that the sidewalk turned blue under it." The sidewalk turning blue is a concrete, visual detail that shows how close it was without a word of explanation.
4. "Jason's hand was on my arm before I understood that anything had happened, and his grip was hard enough to hurt." Delivers "instant and silent" through one action and a consequence she feels.
5. "He opened his mouth, and a little breath of steam came out of it into the cold." The interruption lands without Jason speaking during the attack, and the image is plain and accurate.

## Cross-chapter section

Only Chapter 1 was supplied, so there are no contradictions between chapters to report. Patterns to watch in later chapters, because the Chapter 1 draft shows them and the documents encourage them:

- Narration that explains the beat it just showed (Section 5). The STYLE bans it, but the draft does it in nearly every scene, so expect it again.
- Filler gestures between short lines of dialogue. The STYLE's remedy ("put a full sentence of action or thought now and then") produced meaningless gestures, not thought. Later drafters will do the same unless the instruction changes.
- Beats delivered in outline order, one after another, with no scene-specific observation. The phone full of lies in Chapter 2 and the "accidental I love you" in Chapter 3 will be vulnerable to the same checklist reading.
- Facts that Chapter 2 must now carry are in the next section; in particular, the film is in her coat pocket, so any lie-sending or phone scene must remember that the phone is also in the coat pocket and she has just been through a jump.
