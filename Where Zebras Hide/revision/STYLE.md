# Style sheet for drafting chapters

Every chapter is first person, past tense, from Mira's point of view. Stay in past tense for asides too: if she describes a habit of his, it is "he laughed through his nose first", never present tense. She can only report what she sees, hears, senses or thinks. If someone else is impressed, they have to say it or show it.

## Voice

- The reader is an adult who has read books. Write for that reader: trust them to notice things, and never explain what a scene already shows.
- Mira's voice is warm, observant and specific, with dry humor that's occasional and mostly in what she notices. In danger, confession or grief her humor drops away, and no humor is scheduled there.
- Use plain words. Prefer a concrete detail to an abstract one.
- Each scene gets one specific observation of light, faces or hands that only this narrator would make. It is not a joke and not a metaphor. Photography vocabulary (print, exposure, flash, grain, frame) is used once per chapter, where she'd actually reach for it. Describe light and colour in a photographer's specific terms. No word except a name appears more than three times in a chapter.
- **Sentence length:** mostly full sentences, mixed with some longer ones and a few short ones. Join clauses only when the second follows from the first. Don't chain unrelated observations with "and". Never more than two sentences in a row under six words, counting dialogue and tags together. At most three fragments per chapter. Between short lines of dialogue, put something Mira thinks or sees that she hasn't mentioned before. A repeated detail doesn't count, and neither does a gesture whose only job is to fill the gap.
- **Dialogue tags and speech.** Attach a tag to the same sentence as the speech; a standalone "he said." counts as a short sentence. Dialogue and text messages stay in quotation marks, every one. A reply uses the tense of the question ("Tell me if anything is sharp." "I don't feel anything sharp."). A speech over about 80 words is broken by the listener or the setting.
- The Ch 30 countdown is exempt from the fragment limit.
- Act IV must sound like the same woman as Act I.
- Real people interrupt, misunderstand, repeat themselves a little, and answer the question they wish they'd been asked. Not every reply is a comeback.
- Jason is a composed, capable adult who speaks in measured full sentences. His hands do work or are still. Delilah is nosy and warm. Kael is delighted by things that scare everyone else. CHARACTERS.md has the rest.
- People use different words for mana, the other side and each other (RULES.md). Let that show in dialogue.

## Prose habits that make it read as amateur

**The principle behind all of these:** write what happens to real people, not a text assembled to hit targets. Every action, line and detail needs a reason that comes from the character's own situation, not only a reason the plot or the outline needs it. If something is in the scene only to foreshadow, to seem quirky, to fill length or to make the reader feel a certain way, cut it.

Each of these is a pattern, not one banned sentence.

- **Quips in serious scenes.** In danger, confession, grief or a revelation, no jokes, except at most one nervous one that isn't polished. Elsewhere, keep banter to what two real adults would actually say. A witty line must also mean something; a line that only sounds clever is cut.
- **Personifying things.** Objects, animals, plants and weather don't have attitudes. Nothing is rude, cheerful, betraying or offended.
- **Symbolic stand-ins.** Don't use an outside event or object to mirror a feeling, and never have the narration or a character point at the symbol.
- **Props and repeated gestures.** No object or gesture appears more than twice in a chapter unless the outline asks for it. Props the outline names are exempt, and nothing else is. Cups, phones and bags count as props once they are used as a gesture. Place a bag or backpack once and don't move it again unless the scene needs it. Don't track an object through a scene just to use it later. No habits invented to make a character seem real.
- **Body-language clichés.** A warming face, a fluttering chest, a flipping heart, a dropping stomach, a lighting-up face: at most one of these in the whole chapter. No vague microgestures, such as a mouth doing something complicated, a smile arriving sideways, or a look held a beat too long. Don't follow a feeling with a sentence that dismisses it ("It was only dinner").
- **Pointing at clues.** Don't have Mira note that something is suspicious or file it away. Report it, and let the reader notice.
- **Saying things twice.** Don't repeat information within a scene, restate a feeling the dialogue already showed, or restate a time, distance or detail already given. Don't follow a planted detail with a sentence saying she didn't think about it.
- **Technical details that don't hold up.** Any science, mechanics or procedure must survive a skeptical reader. If you aren't sure it's right, leave it out.
- **Logic of space and time.** Track where everyone is, how far things are, what time it is, and what people are holding. A walk doesn't stretch to fit a conversation. Anything a character handles or puts away must have been placed in the scene first. Before ending a scene, check what everyone carries against the page.
- **Supporting characters as functions.** A side character shouldn't only exist to deliver a plot point or a tease.
- **Padding.** A chapter more than 15% under its length has probably skipped texture; add observation. Never add jokes, quirks, side beats or extra exchanges to hit a count. If the beats are written and it is still short, stop.

## Punctuation

- No em dashes. Use a full stop, a comma, a colon or parentheses, or rewrite the sentence.
- Don't splice two sentences together with a comma where a dash used to be.
- Use straight quotes, and put dialogue punctuation inside the quotes.
- No markdown escapes or formatting marks in prose.

## How to read the outline

- **Lines marked verbatim are copied exactly.** They are exempt from the short-sentence count and count toward every other limit and pattern.
- **A chapter's own Limits override the default counts.** A limit on a repeated phrase or image counts verbatim lines too.
- **When the outline says what a character doesn't do**, that's an instruction to you. In the prose, write what they do instead: a task, a question, a gesture, the wrong words.
- **At most one simile or analogy per scene**, counting "as if" and "looked like". If the outline lists one, it is the only one in that scene.
- Reserve "weather" for the creatures.
- Every chapter has a romance beat: one concrete moment between Mira and Jason, small or large. Write it. Old-life beats are included where they fit.

## Never do these

Each one is a pattern to avoid, not one banned sentence.

**Null-space beats.** A character receives something important and the text records an absence: he doesn't answer, she doesn't respond, he notes it without comment, neither of them says it. Narration also may not describe a character's non-reaction, with or without a comparison. Instead, give the character something to do or say: a task, a deflecting question, a physical act, the wrong words.

**Explaining a moment after showing it.** A line of narration that tells the reader what a look, a gesture or an exchange meant. Cut any paragraph-ending sentence that names what Mira trusts, fears, concludes or notices; if the paragraph still works, it goes. Instead, end the beat on the action and trust it.

**Reversals and contrast setups.** Sentences built as "not this, but that", or a feeling set against its opposite. Instead, say the precise thing directly.

**Stock filler.**
- A short stand-in reply where a real answer belongs. "I know" as a reply: at most once per chapter, only when agreeing with a fact. A bare "Yeah" is not an answer to an emotional question.
- A character going quiet "the way he got when" something happened.
- Stock descriptions of an expression shifting, a jaw working, a face being too neutral.
- A jacket pulled tighter, blood going cold, something close enough to touch and too far to reach, "maybe both, probably both", and "not dramatically".

**Overused motifs.** Each gets at most one use per chapter, and only where it earns it.
- Mira counting how long she and Jason have known each other. The hovering hand.
- A door as a metaphor for emotional access.
- Coffee as an image or metaphor.
- Mira filing details away as a figure of speech. (Allowed in Ch 30, where it shows the colony's detachment.)
- Hands shaking, stomach dropping, can't breathe. Pick one body reaction per scene.

**Thesis lines.** No chapter ends on a sentence that states what the chapter meant. End on an image or an action.

**Triplets and countdown rhythms.** Three short parallel words or phrases in a row, or a list of three abstractions. No more than one triplet per chapter.

**"The way you ___" similes.** At most two per chapter.

**Repeating a line from another chapter.** Someone has to notice.

**Labels for Jason's genius.** Never call him brilliant, a genius, a prodigy or Newton-level in narration. Show him working something out or failing at it.

**Ominous one-liners.** Jason doesn't deliver portents, lines that announce what is coming. A line that states a rule is fine if the outline marks it verbatim.

## Scenes, not summaries

- Write every beat in the outline as a scene with a place, a moment and dialogue. "Weeks pass" is allowed for one paragraph at most, and the paragraph has to be built from specific days.
- Fights have a shape, a cost and one idea that belongs to Mira. No two fights in the book end in the same words.

## The rules

Every scene has to follow RULES.md. If an outline beat seems to break a rule, follow the rule and flag it.

## Copying from the outline

- The outline describes intentions, not sentences. Only lines marked **verbatim** are copied exactly. Don't copy its explanations into the prose. Outline notes that read as a verdict ("Whatever this is...", "She trusts...", "which frightens her more than...") are director's notes, not sentences.
