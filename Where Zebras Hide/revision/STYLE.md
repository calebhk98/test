# Style sheet for drafting chapters

Every chapter is first person, past tense, from Mira's point of view. Stay in past tense for asides too: "he laughed through his nose first", never "he laughs through his nose first". She can only report what she sees, hears, senses or thinks. If someone else is impressed, they have to say it or show it.

## Voice

- The reader is an adult who has read books. Write for that reader: trust them to notice things, and never explain what a scene already shows.
- Mira's voice is warm, observant and specific, with dry humor that's occasional and mostly in what she notices. In danger or grief her humor drops away.
- Use plain words. Prefer a concrete detail to an abstract one.
- **Sentence length:** mostly full sentences of 12 to 25 words, mixed with some longer ones and a few short ones. Never more than two sentences in a row under six words. At most three fragments per chapter. Short, choppy sentences read like a children's book.
- Act IV must sound like the same woman as Act I.
- Real people aren't purely logical, and they aren't constantly witty either. They interrupt, misunderstand, repeat themselves a little, and answer the question they wish they'd been asked. Not every reply is a comeback.
- Jason is a composed, capable adult who speaks in measured full sentences. Delilah is nosy and warm. Kael is delighted by things that scare everyone else. CHARACTERS.md has the rest.
- People use different words for mana, the other side and each other (RULES.md). Let that show in dialogue.

## Prose habits that make it read as amateur

**The principle behind all of these:** write what happens to real people, not a text assembled to hit targets. Every action, line and detail needs a reason that comes from the character's own situation, not only a reason the plot or the outline needs it. If something is in the scene only to foreshadow, to seem quirky, to fill length or to make the reader feel a certain way, cut it.

These came out of the Ch 1-3 test drafts. Each is a pattern, not just one banned sentence.

- **Quips in serious scenes.** In danger, confession, grief or a revelation, no jokes, except at most one nervous one that isn't polished. Elsewhere, keep banter to what two real adults would actually say. A witty line must also mean something. ("Sawdust is the point. You look like you need it." sounds clever and means nothing.)
- **Personifying things.** Objects, animals, plants and weather don't have attitudes. No rude trees, cheerful phones, betraying shoelaces, offended squirrels, or rot dust that wants a say.
- **Symbolic stand-ins.** Don't use an outside event or object to mirror a feeling (a bird taking off at the confession, broken sticks standing for his mistakes), and never have the narration or a character point at the symbol.
- **Props and repeated gestures.** No object or gesture appears more than twice in a chapter unless the outline asks for it. Don't track an object through a scene just to use it later. No habits invented to make a character seem real.
- **Body-language clichés.** Face warming, chest fluttering, heart flipping, stomach dropping, face lighting up: at most one of these in the whole chapter. No vague microgestures ("his mouth did something complicated", "a smile came out sideways", "his face went tight and careful", "looked at the door a beat too long").
- **Pointing at clues.** Don't have Mira note that something is suspicious or file it away ("I noticed that, and put it next to the clock"). Report it, and let the reader notice.
- **Saying things twice.** Don't repeat information ("listened to it twice", "folded it flat, twice") or restate a feeling the dialogue already showed.
- **Technical details that don't hold up.** Any science, mechanics or procedure must survive a skeptical reader. If you aren't sure it's right, leave it out. Characters don't give technical explanations nobody asked for.
- **Logic of space and time.** Track where everyone is, how far things are, what time it is, and what people are holding. A walk doesn't stretch to fit a conversation.
- **Convenient timing.** Things don't happen at the perfect dramatic second (someone arriving with nine seconds left on a timer).
- **Supporting characters as functions.** A side character shouldn't only exist to deliver a plot point or a tease.
- **Padding.** Word counts are approximate. A shorter chapter is better than a padded one. Never add jokes, quirks, side beats or extra exchanges to hit a word count.

## Punctuation

- No em dashes. Use a full stop, a comma, a colon or parentheses, or rewrite the sentence.
- Don't splice two sentences together with a comma where a dash used to be ("The light came back, wrong.").
- Use straight quotes, and put dialogue punctuation inside the quotes.
- No markdown escapes or formatting marks in prose.

## How to read the outline

- **Lines marked verbatim override everything in "Never do these".** Copy them exactly, even if they use a pattern banned below.
- **A chapter's own Limits override the default counts below.** For example, Ch 3 may use "Two years" three times.
- **When the outline says what a character doesn't do** ("he doesn't argue", "she doesn't ask"), that's an instruction to you. In the prose, write what they do instead: a task, a question, a gesture, the wrong words.
- **Analogies and similes listed in the outline are the only ones in that scene.** Don't add your own on top of them.
- Coffee as an image or metaphor is limited. Ordinary cups of coffee in a scene are fine.

## Never do these

These were found across the draft by seven reviewers. Each one is a pattern to avoid, not one banned sentence.

**Null-space beats.** A character receives something important and the text records an absence.
- Examples: "He doesn't answer, which is answer enough." "She doesn't respond." "He notes this without comment." "He doesn't praise her. Just nods." "Jason watches but doesn't intervene." "Neither of them saying it out loud." "He doesn't say anything. Neither does she."
- Instead, give the character something to do or say: a task, a deflecting question, a physical act, the wrong words.

**Explaining a moment after showing it.**
- Examples: "He gave me his attention, but he wouldn't give me anything else." "Something settles between them, not romance exactly, not yet, but honesty." "which she finds both touching and slightly suspicious".
- Instead, end the beat on the action and trust it.

**Reversals and contrast setups.**
- Examples: "Not X. Y." "It didn't feel like a gift. It felt like a door slamming in my face." "It's not sight. It's not echolocation. It's something without a human name." "Not contained. Not driven back. Gone."
- Instead, say the precise thing directly.

**Stock filler.**
- "I know." as a reply. "Yeah." as the answer to an emotional question.
- "He was quiet for a moment, the way he got when..."
- "Something shifted in his expression." "His jaw works like he's going to say something, then doesn't." "Too neutral."
- "pulled my jacket tighter"
- "her blood goes cold", "close enough to touch, too far to reach", "Maybe both. Probably both."
- "not dramatically"

**Overused motifs.** Each gets at most one use per chapter, and only where it earns it.
- "Two years". "Twelve inches". The hovering hand.
- The door metaphor for emotional access.
- Coffee imagery.
- "filed that away". (Allowed in Ch 30, where it shows the colony's detachment.)
- Hands shaking, stomach dropping, can't breathe. Pick one body reaction per scene.

**Thesis lines.** No chapter ends on a sentence that states what the chapter meant ("how I was supposed to choose between dying and losing him when both of those things felt exactly the same"). End on an image or an action.

**Triplets and countdown rhythms.**
- "Breathing. Existing. Trying to remember how to do both." "Methodical. Clear. Brilliant." "present, registered, filed".
- No more than one triplet per chapter.

**"The way you ___" similes.** At most two per chapter.

**Labels for Jason's genius.** Never call him brilliant, a genius, a prodigy or Newton-level in narration. Show him working something out, testing it, or failing at it.

**Ominous one-liners.** Jason doesn't deliver portents ("This is the opening act." "The day they start caring is the day everything ends."). A chapter can keep one line like this if the outline marks it verbatim.

**Repeating a line from another chapter.** If a character repeats something said earlier, someone has to notice.

**Analogy pileups.** At most one new analogy per scene. Mira makes some of them herself.

## Scenes, not summaries

- Write every beat in the outline as a scene with a place, a moment and dialogue. "Weeks pass" is allowed for one paragraph at most, and the paragraph has to be built from specific days ("the Tuesday she burned the rice").
- Fights have a shape, a cost and one idea that belongs to Mira. No two fights in the book end in the same words.
- Every chapter includes one beat from her old life and one romance beat (see the outline). Both can be a single line.

## The rules

Every scene has to follow RULES.md. If an outline beat seems to break a rule, follow the rule and flag it.

## Copying from the outline

- The outline describes intentions, not sentences. Lines marked **verbatim** are the only ones to copy exactly.
- Don't copy the outline's explanations ("This is the chapter's emotional center") into the prose.
