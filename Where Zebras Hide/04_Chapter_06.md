## Chapter Six

"How long before something notices me?"

Jason glanced over from the driver's seat. Outside, the highway unspooled through grey afternoon light, bare trees on either side. "Weeks. Maybe less."

"Less."

"You're producing very little right now. Think of it like, you've just lit a match in a dark room. The flame is real but the room is large." He paused. "The problem is the flame gets bigger as you develop. The more skill you build, the more mana you produce, and the more you produce, the more noticeable you become."

"So learning to protect myself also makes me more of a target."

"Yeah."

I filed that away. "What's doing the noticing?"

He was quiet for a moment, the way he got when he was deciding how much to say and in what order. "There's another dimension. It overlaps ours completely, same space, different layer. Everything there runs on mana the way everything here runs on sunlight. The plants. The animals. The ground, essentially." He checked the mirror. "The creatures there are predators in the way anything is a predator, they eat what's available and avoid what costs more energy than it's worth."

"And we're available."

"We weren't. That's the thing." He shifted lanes. "Crossing between dimensions costs energy. A lot of it. And the cost scales with how much mana you have, the more powerful something is, the more expensive the crossing. So for most of what lives there, Earth isn't worth the trip. There's nothing here to eat. Humans produce zero mana naturally. We're invisible to them."

"Were invisible."

"Were," he agreed.

I looked out the window. A gas station passed. A billboard for a personal injury lawyer. The ordinary world doing its ordinary thing, completely unaware it was balanced on something I hadn't known existed this morning.

"So we're zebras," I said.

He looked at me.

"Hidden in tall grass. Seven billion of us and none of them can see us because we don't smell like food." I kept my eyes on the road. "That's the model, right?"

"Yeah. That's the model."

"And I just stepped out of the grass."

"Small step. You're barely producing. But yes." He said it without softening it, which I appreciated. Softening it would have made it worse somehow. "The things that do cross over are the low-mana ones. The ones where the energy cost is cheap enough to bother. They're not powerful. They're not smart. But they're here, occasionally, and they notice anything that produces."

"Like me."

"Like you. In a few weeks."

I turned that over. Outside my window, a minivan full of children passed us going the other direction. A dog had its head out the back window, ears flattened in the wind.

"The smart ones," I said. "The powerful ones. They know about us?"

"Some do. They don't care. We're not worth the energy cost." He paused. "That's the only reason any of this works. Not because we're hidden or they don’t know about us or can’t, just because we're not worth the trip."

"Okay," I said.

He glanced at me. "Okay?"

"I'm not going to process all of that right now." I pulled my jacket tighter. My head was starting to throb again, low and persistent behind my eyes. "Tell me what I need to know to stay alive in the next three months and I'll have an existential crisis about the rest of it later."

He was quiet for a moment. Then, almost to himself: "That might actually be the healthiest response I've ever seen to this conversation."

"Don't get used to it," I said. "What's the next thing I need to know?"
