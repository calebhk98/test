# **Chapter Thirty**

The thing arrived the way the first one had, wrong.

Not wrong like a stray crossing over, the ambient shimmer of something small and hungry slipping through a thin patch of dimensional membrane. Wrong like weight. Like pressure dropping before a storm. I felt it before I saw it, the way you feel a truck on the highway before you hear it, a displacement of something fundamental in the mana around us.

Jason felt it at the same moment. I saw it in his shoulders.

We'd had three days since the first crossing in Rajasthan. Three days of tutorials uploaded from our living room, of practitioners surfacing from hiding to do what they could, of hospitals reporting symptoms that sounded exactly like what I'd felt in a cabin eight months ago. Warmth in the chest. Headaches. A fever that wasn't a fever.

Three days of knowing the math wasn't going to work out.

This one hadn't come through where the others were converging, the clusters of new mana signatures that made certain cities look like bonfires from the other side. It had come through here, in what should have been a quiet patch of rural nothing, drawn by something specific. Drawn, maybe, by the two of us, a Newton-level signature and whatever I'd become, sitting together in one place for too long.

Jason said something. I was already looking toward it.

It was at the tree line, maybe two hundred meters out. I couldn't see it clearly, it didn't want to be seen clearly, I understood that instinctively, the same way you understand a face is watching you from a dark window without being able to make out the features. It was large. It was patient. It was doing the thing intelligent creatures do when they're deciding whether something is worth the effort.

It wasn't going to decide we weren't worth the effort.

The slave scout Jason had boxed, back before everything broke open, had taken everything he had to contain. He'd been down for days. And the scout had been the equivalent of someone's disposable asset, sent out for entertainment and easy kills. This was not that.

This was one of the early movers. Something that had looked at the new math, seven billion humans producing mana, helpless, lit up like a signal flare, and decided the crossing cost was finally justified. Not one of the massive intelligences I'd sensed on the other side, the ones that moved like weather systems. But not the bottom of the hierarchy either. Something in between, and something that had decided that we, specifically, looked like a significant prize.

I did the math the same way I'd learned to do everything since January: practically, without sentiment.

Jason could contain it, maybe. It would cost him. And while he was down, there were more coming. I could feel them, distant pressure on the edge of my range. If he spent himself here, there would be nothing left for what came after.

I started with two hundred.

Not because two hundred was enough. Two hundred was a distraction, a signal, the first move in something larger. I felt them spin up around me, two hundred versions of me standing in the field and the tree line and the road behind us, and I sent one of them forward.

It died in four seconds. I barely registered the loss, she'd known she was going to, she'd walked forward anyway, and in those four seconds the creature had to look at her instead of at me. Four seconds was enough to finish the second round.

I heard Jason shouting. His voice was one input among many and I noted it the way you note a car alarm: present, registered, filed. He was running toward me, I knew that too, a single moving shape in my peripheral awareness. He'd seen two hundred and thought that was the ceiling. He didn't know about the second round yet.

When forty thousand of me appeared across the landscape, I heard him differently. Not just shouting. Something sharper in his voice, something that in another context I would have turned toward immediately. Five of me looked at him, watched him running with that expression I'd only seen a few times, the one he reserved for things that genuinely scared him.

The other thirty-nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-five watched the creature.

It had stopped being patient.

The third round took longer than the others. Not because it was harder, I'd done this before, the recursive branching, each of the forty thousand spinning up their own two hundred, but because the cognitive weight of it was something I'd learned to manage slowly, the way you learn to walk with a heavy pack. I was not one person anymore. I was not forty thousand people. I was a colony of eight million, and the colony's attention was almost entirely on the threat, because the threat was what mattered, and that was not a decision I was making consciously, it was simply true the way gravity is true.

I was aware of Jason. The five of me who could see him were aware of him, they noted what he looked like, they registered what his expression meant, they felt the pull of it somewhere that was still Mira and still his and still ours. But five voices in a chorus of eight million didn't carry far. Whatever he was saying was reaching me the way a conversation carries through a wall: present, human, unable to compete with the thing he was trying to stop me from doing.

The creature attacked.

I don't know what it expected. Something to hit, maybe. Something that would scatter and leave the source exposed. Instead eight million of me were already moving and the attack broke against layered shields like a wave against a breakwater, and then the colony responded, and the response was not elegant, it was not Jason fighting, precise and economical, it was eight million iterations of everything I knew, overwhelming in the way that fire is overwhelming, not because any one part is impossible to stop but because there is so much of it.

It took time. Time felt different as the colony. I was aware of duration without experiencing it the way a single person does.

And then it was over.

The creature was gone. Not contained. Not driven back. Gone.

I sat with that for a moment that was not really a moment because eight million of me were sitting with it simultaneously, and the silence where the threat had been was strange the way any sudden silence is strange. The colony didn't celebrate. It just noted the absence and reoriented.

I didn't like how this felt.

I hadn't liked it the first time, with a thousand clones in a field on a Tuesday afternoon that felt like a lifetime ago. This was that, times eight million, times something I didn't have a word for, the specific texture of not quite being a person anymore, of being something larger than a person and therefore in some important way less. The colony was functional and effective and utterly efficient and it was not a thing I wanted to be for longer than I had to.

The threat was gone.

I started pulling them back.

The headache hit at about four million, which was sooner than I'd expected, a spike of white pressure behind my eyes that I recognized and noted and kept moving through. Three million. Two million. The mana in the environment was crashing back into me, too fast, all those brains full of everything they'd learned and everything I was, collapsing back into one skull that hadn't been built to hold it. I kept pulling. One million. Five hundred thousand. The headache was not a headache anymore, it was a sound, or what sound would be if it existed inside your skull without traveling through air, and I kept pulling because the alternative was leaving them and I was not going to leave them out there.

One hundred thousand.

Ten thousand.

The last five of me were the ones who could see Jason. He was right there. He was holding something, or maybe holding me, I couldn't quite, 

The headache became everything.

I pulled the last of them back.

And then I couldn't see.
