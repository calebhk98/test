# Chapter 1: Normal

The fluorescent lights on our floor had been flickering since Tuesday, and by Friday afternoon I was ready to throw my stapler at them. Maintenance had come by twice already. Both times they'd stood under the fixture with their hands on their hips and promised a work order.

"Mira, you still here?"

Marcus was leaning against my cubicle wall with his jacket already on and his messenger bag over one shoulder.

"Trying to finish this report before the weekend."

"It's almost five on a Friday. The report can wait."

"The report is due Monday morning, and I still have two sections to write."

"Then write them Monday morning." He grinned. "Come on. A few of us are going to Murphy's. You should come."

I looked at my screen. Three more pages to review, and a table of contents that reformatted itself every time I touched it. If I stayed another hour, I could get most of it done. If I left now, I would spend the whole weekend thinking about it.

"I can't. I have plans."

"What plans?"

"Coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin got wider. "The mysterious Jason. When are you two going to make it official?"

I knew exactly what he meant. Everyone on the fourth floor had decided what Jason and I were a long time ago.

"Make what official?"

"You meet him every single Tuesday. You talk about him constantly. Last month you skipped girls' night to have dinner with him." He counted the items off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it yet."

"We're friends," I said, keeping my eyes on the screen.

"Uh huh." Marcus tipped his head and waited for a better answer.

"We are." Two years of Tuesdays had taught me to be fine with that, or at least to stop hoping for something else.

"Okay." He pushed himself off the wall. "Have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

He walked off toward the elevators, and I turned back to my screen to read the same paragraph three times without taking in a word of it.

The pivot table on page forty-two had moved three pixels to the left, and I couldn't work out why. Three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots, snap-crunch, snap-crunch, loud enough to fill the nearly empty floor. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. And if I didn't fix that table, the VP would make a passive-aggressive comment about attention to detail on Monday.

My phone buzzed against the desk. Jason had texted, "Still on for coffee at five?"

Our coffees happened on Tuesdays. He had never once asked for a Friday, and I had spent the afternoon trying not to think about why.

I looked at the three pages and then at the lights. I typed "Yes" before I could change my mind.

The report could wait.

I left the two blank sections exactly as they were. I put my phone in my coat pocket, slung my bag over my shoulder, and walked out past the flickering lights.

---

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday night. Students had laptops open at nearly every table, and a book club had taken the corner with four pastries spread across one table. Jason was at the counter when I came in, ordering with his back to the room. I walked past him to our table by the window.

By the time I'd sat down, my latte was on the table and Jason was across from me with his own black coffee. My bag hung off my chair. His backpack sat on the chair beside him.

"Long day?" he asked.

"Henderson account." I kept my cup in front of me. "I'm not going to talk about it."

"Then we won't," he said. "How's the Pentax?"

"It's great." I was already leaning over the table. "I've been shooting this expired film I found online, and it comes out with a green cast over everything. It's strange, and I love it."

"Did you develop any of it?"

"A batch on Wednesday night. There's a shot of the bridge downtown at sunset that came out exactly how I wanted, green sky and all."

"You'll have to show me."

"There's still a whole roll in the camera at home, and I keep putting it off."

"Why?" he asked.

"Because once I develop it, I'll know what I actually shot." I shrugged. "There's a darkroom space on Fourth Street I keep walking past. The rental alone would eat my fun money for a month, and then I'd need equipment and chemicals on top of that. It's not practical right now."

"What would you print first?"

"The bridge, probably. Bigger." I stopped, then went on anyway. "I want to see what the green does to the water."

"Sorry," I said. "I've talked about nothing but the darkroom for twenty minutes."

"Most people are boring." He said it mildly and went back to his coffee.

Nora had sent me another apartment listing that morning, a one-bedroom in Chicago with a claw-foot tub and a kitchen the size of a closet. She's sure I'd be happier there. My parents have opinions about what I should be doing next, and they share them over Sunday dinner. I'd promised Mom I'd bring milk, and Jess had finished the carton this morning.

"What about your parents?" I asked. "Do you get to see them much?"

"We didn't really get along," he said. He said it evenly.

"Oh." I looked down at the table. "I'm sorry."

"It's fine." He moved his cup aside. "Have you thought any more about that darkroom space?"

I let him make the turn. I told him I was still thinking about it, which was mostly true.

He reached into his backpack and took out a small paper bag, folded shut at the top. He set it on the table between our cups.

"For you," he said.

"What is it?"

"Open it."

Inside was a roll of film in its box, the discontinued stock I'd complained about once, months ago, in passing. I had been sure I'd never see another box.

"Where did you find this?"

"A camera shop across town. They had one box left." He shrugged. "I saw it and thought of you."

He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

I put the roll in my coat pocket and took a sip of latte so I wouldn't have to say anything yet.

At the next table, two students had been arguing about free will for ten minutes. Now they were loud enough for the whole room to hear.

"If you knew every particle in the universe, you could predict everything that's ever going to happen," one of them said. "Free will is an illusion."

"That's not what quantum means," his friend said, louder.

I winced. Jason went on as though the argument were happening somewhere else.

"Can we get out of here?" I asked. "Sarah keeps recommending that Thai place. We could try it."

"Yes," he said, and reached for his backpack.

We left at twenty to six, with Jason's backpack on one shoulder and my bag on mine.

---

Outside, the air had a sharp edge to it, the kind that makes you want to pull your collar up. I pulled mine up. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left toward the restaurant.

We walked past the park. The benches had been chained up for winter, and the grass beyond them lay flat and brown.

"Your green film would look strange under these lights," Jason said. "Would you shoot it anyway?"

"Probably," I said. "That's the only way I'd find out what it does."

We had walked four blocks, and the restaurant still wasn't in sight.

A gust came off the park, and I stumbled on a cracked piece of sidewalk. Jason's hand came up. It stopped a few inches from my elbow, and then it dropped back to his side.

"You good?"

"Yeah," I said, and kept walking, because the sidewalk was a mess and I didn't want to fall again.

Nora had called that afternoon, convinced she'd be lonely in Chicago, and I kept thinking about it. I had been working up to asking Jason something since the coffee shop.

"Do you ever get lonely?" I asked.

"Sometimes." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm not great with people in general."

"You're good with me," I said. "You can't be that bad with people."

"You're different."

"Different how?"

He opened his mouth, and a little breath of steam came out of it into the cold.

The air cracked.

There was a flash of blue light right in front of us, close enough that the sidewalk turned blue under it. Something invisible hit the light hard, and the force came through the air and struck me in the chest. Jason's hand was on my arm before I understood that anything had happened, and his grip was hard enough to hurt.

My ears rang, high and thin. There was another flash, and the world tipped sideways. Up became down. The pressure in my chest turned into a burn, and my skin felt like someone was peeling it off and pressing it back on wrong.

I was going to die. I was sure of it, and there was nothing I could do.

Then I was on the ground.

Dirt and dead leaves pressed into my palms, and everything hurt. My chest hurt, and so did my head.

The trees came into focus slowly. Bare branches crossed a grey sky above me, and the leaves under my hands were cold and wet.
