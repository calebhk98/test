# Chapter 3: The Ultimatum

The phone buzzed against my knee, three short pulses, and I jumped like it had bitten me. The timer. Twenty minutes, which had felt like two minutes and two hours at the same time.

Jason still had two fingers hooked in my sleeve. He seemed to notice at the same moment I did, and let go.

"We're clear," he said. "Nobody followed us."

"How do you know?"

"I checked. Three times, the long way around." He looked back over his shoulder at the trees, as if they'd been rude to him. In all our Tuesdays I had never once seen him check a street. Now he did it like breathing. "We're safe here. For now."

He had a speech. I could tell because his phone was out, screen up, and he kept glancing down at it between sentences. Not checking messages. Reading.

"Are you reading off your phone?"

"I'm looking at my notes." He turned the screen toward me, then seemed to regret it. The list said *1. Stay calm. 2. Start with the history. 3. Don't say "powers." 4. Do not mention feelings.*

"Number four," I said.

"Number four is the important one."

"Is it the one you're going to break in the first minute?"

He locked the phone and shoved it in his pocket so fast he nearly dropped it. "Okay. So. There are people who can do things. Not many. The history is kind of fascinating and I'm going to skip most of it, but the short version is that there's a community, and I'm better than most at a couple of things, and some of the people who do this don't like that." He stopped, and started again. "Some of them don't like me."

"Is that what happened back there? The blue light?"

"That was one of the things. The emergency version." He ran a hand through his hair, and a dead leaf came loose and drifted down past his ear. I had known about that leaf for an hour. I did not mention it. "It's not something I can do whenever I want."

Then he said something under his breath, and for a second his face changed. He looked like a man who had forgotten the sidewalk, the attackers and me. "The thing is, it's a clean problem. You pick a landing from every open point on the map, and then you have to make sure the choice is actually random, which is harder than it sounds, because nothing in nature wants to be random on purpose." He caught himself halfway through. "Sorry. I'm not supposed to be excited about it. The notes say calm."

"Is it teleporting?"

"Well. Not really. Kind of. Sort of." He winced. "Close enough."

"Close enough," I repeated. My voice came out thin. "Jason, twenty minutes ago we were walking to dinner. Then there was a blue light, and something hit me in the chest, and now we're in Tennessee."

"There were people. Back in Columbus. They were attacking us."

Attacking. I tried the word the way you test a dark step with your foot. Not following. Not watching. Attacking.

"Attacking us."

"Attacking me." He pressed his lips together. "You just happened to be there."

"I just happened to be there."

"You were right next to me, so I could grab you. That's the only reason you came along."

The casual way he said it made my stomach turn. Being attacked was apparently something that happened to people sometimes, and vanishing across the state was the sensible response.

"How many of them were there?"

"Three. Four, if you count the one in the van."

"Did they know you?"

"Some of them." He looked at the trees. "They want what I know. The things I've learned to do. They want me to teach them, or they want to take it, and I honestly couldn't tell you which one is worse."

"Teach them what?"

"Sure. Let's start with the easy question." He tried to smile. It came out sideways.

"Did you do this?"

"I did the part that moved us." He said it fast, like pulling off a bandage, then looked at me as if I might hit him for it. "I didn't pick Tennessee, if that's what you're asking. That's what randomized means. It could have been here, or Oregon, or a parking garage in Ohio. Nobody knows where we landed, so nobody can follow. Theoretically."

"Theoretically."

"It's the first real test of it, so the theory is still..." He made a flat, wobbling motion with one hand. "Ninety percent."

"That's a very specific number for something you've tested once."

"It's a very specific number for a very bad feeling." He almost smiled, then didn't.

"So how do we get home?"

"Bus, probably. Or a rental car tomorrow, under a name that isn't mine. I can't aim the thing, so I can't just send us back. Columbus is three hundred and seventy miles from here." He glanced at his phone, though the screen was dark. "Tomorrow, probably."

"And after that?"

"After that I leave." He said it to the leaves. "They know I was in Columbus. They'll keep looking, and I can't stay there anymore. I came tonight to say goodbye, Mira. That's why it was a Friday."

Friday had never been our day. I'd typed "Yes" to his lunchtime text and spent far too long wondering why I cared. Now I knew why, and I wished I didn't.

My fist was still closed around the coffee lid. I'd been holding it since the coffee shop. I shoved it into my coat pocket, where the roll of film was knocking against my hip, and looked at the trees instead, which were no help at all.

"When would I see you again?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "You wouldn't," he said.

"Just like that."

"I don't have a choice, Mira."

"Can I come with you?" The words were out before I'd decided to say them.

"No."

It came too fast, with no pause in front of it.

"Why not?"

"Because it's dangerous. What happened today could happen again. Probably will. Next time I might not react fast enough, or you might not be close enough to grab, or they might have something I can't get around. If you're with me, you're in it."

"Then teach me. However it works. Teach me so I can handle it."

"That just means more danger. Most people who try this end up..." He stopped. "Most people die, Mira."

"Then I'll be one of the ones who doesn't."

"I was too slow, you know. I saw that hand come up, and I was too slow, and you were standing right there." He looked at his own hand like it belonged to somebody else. "I keep seeing it."

"You got us out."

"Barely."

"Is this a cult?"

"No."

"Would a cult say no?"

"Yes. Probably. Mira, please."

"That's not how it works." He threw both hands up, then let them drop. "Okay. Fine. I'm deciding. Somebody has to."

"Nobody asked you to be the one who decides."

"I can carry things," I said. "I can read a map. I'm extremely good at spreadsheets, Jason. I could be useful."

"That's not what this is about."

"Then what is it about?"

"Because I'm the one who knows what happens!" His voice went up, louder than I had ever heard it. I'd never heard him raise his voice at anyone, not even the barista who put oat milk in my latte, and that should have been funny. It wasn't. "It wouldn't be fair of me to risk your life just because I love you!"

The words hit the trees and stayed there. A bird took off from the branch above us with a clatter of wings, and I watched it all the way to the next line of trees.

For one breath I didn't think about anything at all. He loved me. He'd loved me the whole time. My heart went up with that bird, stupid and fast, and I let it, because nobody was there to see me be happy in the middle of a forest. One full breath of it.

I'd imagined this moment a thousand times, in the break room, walking to my car, maybe late at night over wine. I'd never imagined it like this: screamed at me in a forest as an excuse to leave me behind.

Then the anger came in behind the happiness, and it had brought the last two years with it.

"You love me so you've been lying to me for two years?"

"I wasn't lying. I just wasn't telling you everything."

"That's the same thing!" The anger was easier to hold than the rest of it. "You decided I couldn't know. You decided I could spend every Tuesday wondering what I'd done wrong, and you just let me."

"Do you have any idea how many speeches I've rehearsed to you on the walk to my car? Forty. Forty of them, Jason, and every single one was better than this."

"Mine were better," he said, and then looked horrified at himself. "Mine had bullet points."

"Mira, I didn't..."

My hand found the roll of film in my coat pocket, the cardboard edge pressing into my fingers. *Saw it. Thought of you.* He'd said that across the table like it was a sweet thing. Now it sounded like a receipt.

"You bought me film," I said. "You were saying goodbye."

He picked up a stick from the leaves, looked at it as if the answer might be written on the side, and set it back down. Then he picked up another one and started on it.

"There's another option," he said, after a while. "I could take today out of you."

My mouth had gone dry. I had to lick my lips before I could answer.

"Take it out."

"Tonight. The attack, the woods, the last few hours. You'd remember the coffee, and me saying I was moving away. A normal goodbye. You'd be sad for a while, and then you'd be fine."

"You can do that."

"I do it sometimes." He said it like he was mentioning that he could parallel park. "When I have to."

"How many?"

"Enough to know how it goes." He glanced at his phone again, which was still dark.

"Does it hurt?"

"Not for you. Probably." He tried a grin. "I'm not great with fine detail, so you'd probably wake up with a typo somewhere in your life. You'd hate Thursdays for no reason."

"That isn't funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's a little funny," I admitted, which was the worst part.

"You could do it to me right now."

"Yeah. You haven't started yet, so it would work."

"Started what?"

"Not right now." He looked at the ground. "Mira. Not right now."

"How would I even know if you'd already done it?"

I looked down at my own hands, the scraped palms and the grit still under the nails, and tried to think of a memory I could check. Every one I found had come from him, or from a Tuesday with him, and I could not tell which ones had been handed to me already sorted.

He opened his mouth and closed it again. A squirrel crossed the leaves in front of us, stopped halfway, and stared at me as if I'd asked it a personal question. I stared back until it lost interest and ran up the nearest trunk.

"I haven't," he said. "I swear. Every Tuesday happened. Every coffee. I've never touched your memories. Not once."

I wanted to believe him, and the trouble was that I did. The part of me that had spent every Tuesday with him believed him completely. That was no use at all, since a memory somebody had edited would feel exactly like one they hadn't.

"Would I still be me, after?"

"Mostly." He winced at his own answer. "You'd be you with a hole in it. Maybe a small hole."

"Don't you ever offer that again. My mind is mine. You don't get to edit me for my own good."

"Okay," he said quietly.

He could reach into my head and take out every Tuesday, one at a time. The coffee order he remembered. The afternoon he helped me move, when we ate pizza on the floor for four hours and talked about nothing. The Tuesday in October when it rained so hard he came in with his hair flat to his head, announced that the parking lot was a lake, and laughed through his nose before he'd finished the joke. The scone he'd bought me even though he hated them. The bridge at sunset, and what he'd said about deciding the prints for himself. All of it, gone, and me sitting there with the coffee lid in my pocket, wondering why I felt so tired.

I started pacing, because standing still was making it worse. My wrong shoes slid on the wet leaves.

"Okay," he said, and held up his fingers one at a time. "Here's everything. One: I take you home and leave, and you never see me again, and you remember all of it. Two: I erase today, and you go home thinking I moved for a job. Three: you come with me without knowing what you're walking into, and you're probably found and killed. Four: I tell you everything, and it changes you, and then you have to learn to survive it."

"Four's the one where I die."

"Four's the one where you might."

"Did your notes say anything about odds?"

He pulled the phone back out and read it, and winced. "Number five says be honest, even if it sucks."

"Then tell me enough to pick."

"That's not how it works." He lifted both hands and started talking faster. "If I told you there's gold buried in a field somewhere, that's useless. But if I said it's twenty-five feet down near yellow oak trees? Now every time you see an oak tree, your brain checks." He stopped with his hands still in the air. "You won't mean to. You won't even want to. It just keeps digging until it hits something." He lowered his hands. "Sorry. That's a good one, though. Isn't it a good one?"

"It's a terrible one," I said. "Now I'm going to look at every oak tree in Tennessee."

"Okay. But that's the problem. If I tell you enough to choose, it starts whether you want it or not. If I keep you in the dark, you choose blind." He held my eyes. "There is no informed consent here. I know that's a terrible thing to hear."

"That's what the clinic said before my wisdom teeth. Right before they put the mask on me."

"Did you sign something?"

"Yes. I signed everything. I didn't read any of it." I took a breath. "What are my real chances? Real numbers."

"I don't have real numbers. Most of them figure it out alone. Kids stumbling into it. Loners teaching themselves."

"And the ones who figure it out? How long do they last?"

"A few months, usually. Sometimes less."

My legs quit on me then, and I sat down on the log harder than I meant to. Jason sat on the other end. The log dipped under both of us, and a little rot dust puffed up between us like it wanted a say too.

Nobody said anything for a while. A bird started up somewhere in the trees, then thought better of it. I thought about the summer I'd been half planning, a rented darkroom I couldn't afford, with an enlarger I didn't own yet. I thought about Nora in June, squinting into the sun at her graduation in a gown that wouldn't fit right, yelling my name across the lawn because she'd finally finished. I thought about the roll still sitting in my camera at home, which I'd been saving for the perfect light, and the perfect light had never come.

"It's like making your pet smart enough to understand a contract," Jason said. "Can the pet agree to that before it's smart enough to read the contract?"

"Don't compare me to a pet."

"I'm not, I'm..." He stopped. "Okay. I'm a little comparing you to a pet."

"Why do they want it? The people who came for you."

"Because they want it, and I won't give it to them."

"That's all?"

"That's all I'm giving you right now."

"Are they coming here?"

"Not tonight. I don't think." He looked at the trees, then at the path behind us, then at his phone. "I don't think."

"How long do I have to decide?" I asked.

"Until we get you home. Tomorrow, probably."

"That's not a decision. That's a bus schedule."

"It's what I've got."

"Then I'm not deciding it tonight." My voice cracked on *tonight*, and I hated that it did. "I need real time. Not a few hours in the woods with a countdown."

"How much real time?"

"A week."

"A day."

"Two weeks, and you bring me a bagel every Tuesday until I'm done."

"You're not getting two weeks," he said, but the corner of his mouth moved. "Maybe a bagel."

He studied my face like it was another experiment that wasn't going the way he'd planned. "Okay," he said at last. "You can have until we get back to Ohio. However long that takes. But once you're home, once you're safe, you need to choose. Because I can't stay there, and I can't keep you safe if those people find me again."

"That's not a lot of time."

"No."

"It's more than I had an hour ago."

"Can I tell anyone?" I asked. "My sister. Somebody."

"No."

"Not even Nora?"

"Especially not Nora. If you tell people, you put them in it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry about all of it."

"You've got a whole list. I think you can manage 'sorry' without it."

He looked down at the broken sticks scattered in the leaves around his boots, the ones he'd snapped before he walked off, and started gathering them up.

"Leave them," I said.

"They're a mess."

"Leave them, Jason. You made the mess. You can look at it for a minute."

He left them. We both looked at them for a while.

I checked my phone once more. The clock still said a time that didn't match the sky, and I stopped trusting it again before I'd finished reading it. Somewhere past the trees a truck downshifted on a road I couldn't see, and we both turned toward the sound at the same time, the way two people do when they've each decided not to be the first to say they heard it.

The light kept draining out from between the trunks. He took out his phone and turned on the flashlight, looked at the ground around us, and turned it off again, as if the light had become a problem. The cold had come up out of the ground and into my jeans, which were still damp from the log. Somewhere in Columbus the Henderson report was going to be late on Monday, and for once the fluorescent lights in the office were not to blame.

"We should start walking toward a road," Jason said. "Before it gets too dark."

I stood up. I brushed the dead leaves off my jeans, one hand and then the other, and waited for him to tell me which way the road was.
