# Chapter 1: Normal

The fluorescent lights in the office had been flickering since Tuesday, and by half past four on Friday I was ready to throw my stapler at them. Maintenance had come by twice already. Both times they had stood under the tubes with their hands on their hips and promised a work order. The light flattened everything it touched. It turned the shadows under people's jaws a faint, unhealthy green.

"Mira, you still here?"

I looked up. Marcus was leaning against the wall of my cubicle with his jacket already on and his messenger bag over one shoulder. The light made him look sallow, and he kept grinning through it anyway.

"Trying to finish this report before the weekend."

"It's half four on a Friday. The report can wait."

I didn't look up from the screen. The report was due Monday morning, and every hour I spent on it tonight was an hour I wouldn't have to spend then.

"Then finish it Monday morning." He pushed off the wall. "Come on. Some of us are getting drinks at Murphy's. You should come."

I glanced at my screen. Two sections were left, plus a set of tables that kept breaking their formatting every time I touched them. If I stayed another hour, I could finish. If I left now, I would spend the whole weekend thinking about it. Past the windows at the end of the floor, the sky had gone the flat grey it always went by four in February, and the parking structure across the street had started showing its lights one row at a time.

"I can't. I've got plans."

He waited with his eyebrows up, and I gave in.

"Coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin widened. "Ah. The mysterious Jason. When are you two going to make it official?"

"Make what official?" I knew exactly what he meant. Everyone in the office thought they knew what this was.

"You meet him every Tuesday for coffee. You talk about him constantly. You skipped girls' night last month to have dinner with him." He counted the items off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it yet."

My cheeks went warm. "We're friends."

"Uh huh."

He leaned on the cubicle wall again, obviously enjoying himself.

"We are." I had learned to be fine with that, or at least to stop hoping for something else. Two years of careful distance had made the situation clear enough.

"Okay." Marcus shifted his bag higher on his shoulder. "Have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

He left, and I turned back to my screen. I read the same paragraph three times and couldn't have said what was in it. We had coffee on Tuesdays. On Monday Jason had asked whether I was free tonight instead, and I had been turning that over ever since. He had never once asked me to meet on a Friday.

Three cubicles down, Dave had started on his baby carrots. Each one snapped, and the sound carried across the quiet floor. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots.

The chart on page forty-two had shifted three pixels to the left again. I had pasted it back in twice, and each time it moved on its own. If I didn't fix it, the VP would make a remark about attention to detail on Monday.

My phone buzzed against the desk.

**Jason: Still on for five? Same place.**

I looked at the three pages that still needed work. Then I looked at the lights, which were giving me a headache, and then back at the message.

**Me: Yes. See you there.**

I saved the file and closed the laptop halfway. I put my phone in my coat pocket and slung my crossbody bag across my body, the strap settling where it always rubbed my left shoulder. The report could wait.

---

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday. Students had claimed the tables with laptops and textbooks, a couple sat close together on the sofa by the door, and a book club had crowded into the back corner with four pastries between them. Jason was at the counter when I came in, waiting on two cups. I took our table by the window and hung my bag over the back of my chair.

His backpack sat on the chair beside his, the strap looped over the back the way he always left it.

He came back before I had my scarf off and set a latte down on my side of the table. I was already reaching for it.

"Thanks," I said.

He sat down across from me. The window light came in from his left and dropped half his face into shadow. If I had been shooting him, I would have moved him two feet toward the glass. He wrapped both hands around his black coffee, chapped across the knuckles from the cold outside, and held it the way people do when the cup is mostly for their hands.

"So," he said. "How's the Pentax?"

I was off before I could stop myself. The light meter had finally started behaving, after a week of me holding the camera up to the kitchen window and guessing. The Pentax was heavier than it looked, and the shutter was loud in a way I had come to like. I'd been shooting a roll of expired film I found online, and it came out leaning green, the sky especially. The grain ran heavier than I'd expected, and I loved it anyway. I developed a batch Wednesday night in the bathroom with the fan running, and the strips hung from the shower rod with a clothespin on each end while they dried. The best one was the bridge downtown at sunset. The water under it had gone the color of old copper, and the sky had that sick green cast that made the whole thing look older than it was. I scanned it on my kitchen table, and the green was still there. There was another roll still in the camera at home, because I kept meaning to finish it and then not. There was also a darkroom space over on Ninth Street with a real enlarger, a sink, and a timer bolted to the wall. The owner rented benches by the hour. It cost more each month than I spent on groceries, and I had walked past the listing three times pretending I wasn't doing the math. Nora kept sending me apartment listings from Chicago, and I kept not opening them.

"Did you try to correct the green," he asked, "or leave it?"

"Leave it. Fixing it would mean three filters and a week of guessing. The green is the part I like."

"So you keep the mistake and build the picture around it."

Nobody had ever put it that way to me. I picked up my cup and found it was nearly empty.

"Sorry." I put my cup down. "I've been talking your ear off about film for ten minutes."

"Most people are boring."

He said it lightly and sat back in his chair.

"What's the occasion?" I asked. "We've never done a Friday."

"No occasion."

I had promised my mom I'd bring milk to Sunday dinner, and I had already forgotten it once this week. I thought about that and didn't say so.

"Do your parents ever ask what you're doing with your life?" I asked him.

"We don't really talk."

The espresso machine hissed behind the counter. I wished I had asked something easier.

"Oh. I'm sorry."

"It's fine. We didn't really get along." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "Have you looked at that darkroom again?"

"I can't afford it," I said. "My dad thinks I should be saving for grad school instead, which is a whole other conversation."

"That's too bad. It sounded like a good setup."

He reached over to the chair beside him, unzipped the top of his backpack, and came up with a small paper bag folded over twice at the top. He set it on the table between our cups.

"You said you wanted to try that stock once," he said. "Back when you were only thinking about buying a camera. I found a shop across town that still had a few rolls. I saw it and thought of you."

I pulled the bag open. Inside was a box with one dented corner, the film I had mentioned once, months ago, in passing.

"Jason, you didn't have to do that."

"Thank you." I said it again, and then I asked him how old it was and whether the shop had kept it in a fridge, which he couldn't possibly know. He didn't pretend he did. I heard myself and stopped. I folded the bag back over and put it in my coat pocket.

He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

I'd show Jess when I got home.

At the next table, two students had started arguing about a sublet. Somebody owed somebody for a deposit, and nobody could agree on which month it was for. Their voices kept climbing, and the man at the book club table looked up and then looked back down at his pastries. Jason turned his cup another quarter turn and kept his attention on me.

"Do you want to get out of here?" I asked. "Sarah keeps recommending that Thai place on Fourth. It's about a ten-minute walk."

"Sure."

The clock above the pastry case said twenty to six. I stood, buttoned my coat, and watched him lift the backpack onto one shoulder.

---

Outside, the air had the sharp edge February gets once the sun is down. I pulled my coat closed. The paper bag shifted in my pocket against my hip. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left, toward the restaurant, with the park running along our right and its bare trees black against the street. The wind came off the grass smelling of wet dirt and somebody's woodstove. Jason had one hand in his coat pocket and the backpack hanging from the other shoulder, and he walked at exactly my pace without seeming to think about it.

Nora was finishing her master's in June, and she kept saying I should come to Chicago and join her. I still didn't know what I wanted. I had been thinking about that all afternoon, in pieces, and I didn't know how to bring it up.

The streetlights had come on while we were inside. Under their orange light the blue had drained out of Jason's coat, and the grass along the park looked brown and flat. The wet pavement took the lamps and gave them back in long smears, the kind of thing I would have stopped to shoot a month ago without thinking twice about it.

A car came through the intersection ahead of us. A couple crossed in front of it, laughing, their breath showing white. We waited on the curb until they reached the other side. I took my phone out of my pocket and checked the time. It was quarter to six, and the restaurant still wasn't in sight. Then we went on.

"Your green film would look strange under these lights," Jason said. "Would you still shoot it?"

"I'd shoot it anyway." I looked up at the nearest lamp, its glare sitting on the wet sidewalk. "I'd want to see what it does to the orange."

A gust came off the park and shoved us sideways. My heel caught on a heave in the pavement, and I stumbled. Jason's hand came up toward my elbow and stopped a few inches short of it. Then it dropped back to his side.

"You good?"

"I'm fine."

We walked on. I had a question in my throat, and I let it out before I could decide against it.

"Do you ever get lonely?"

"Sometimes."

I walked a few steps before I spoke again. "But you're good with me," I said. "You can't be that bad with people."

"You're different."

I stopped, half a step ahead of him, and turned around.

"Different how?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

The air cracked.

A flash of blue light opened right in front of us, electric and wrong, bright enough to throw color across the side of Jason's face. Something slammed into it. The force came through the light and landed square in my chest, and it kept pushing.

His hand closed on my arm before I understood that anything had happened.

The street tilted. My ears filled with a high ringing, and the streetlights smeared sideways across my vision. Pressure sat on my chest, and I couldn't get any air past it. Every inch of my skin registered something at once, a pull and a squeeze with no edge to it. I tried to shout and got nothing out.

Then the ground came up under me. Leaves and grit pressed into my palms, damp and cold, and my knees had hit something hard. Above me, bare branches stood out against a grey sky.
