# Chapter 2: Displaced

My ears were ringing, high and constant, as if someone had struck a tuning fork inside my skull. I tried to push myself up and my arms wobbled under me. My palms were scraped raw, grit ground into the skin, and both knees throbbed where they'd hit the ground. Dead leaves were stuck to my coat, and the sleeve had split at the elbow. That coat came from a thrift store and cost eleven dollars, and I'd been smug about it for weeks. The coffee lid was still crushed in my fist. I had no memory of closing my hand around it, which seemed like the least important thing that had happened to me all day.

My stomach rolled. I swallowed hard and stayed on my hands and knees until it passed.

Then I checked myself, the way you'd check a room after a loud noise. I started with my hands and worked up, which hurt at the elbow. I touched my scalp. Leaves, dirt, a sore spot above my left ear, and when I pulled my fingers back there was no wet warmth on them. My ribs were tender, but they moved when I breathed. Nothing sharp. Nothing that felt broken. One of my boots had come untied, and I spent a good five seconds glaring at the lace as if it had personally betrayed me.

Okay. Banged up but working. That would do for now.

I got one foot under me, then the other, and stood slowly, waiting for the ground to agree.

Jason was about ten feet away. He was on his feet already, turning in a slow circle with his head tilted, watching the trees like he expected one of them to step out and make an announcement. He looked fine. No blood, no limp, a leaf caught in his hair. He looked steadier than I felt, which I resented on principle.

"Jason?"

He stopped turning. "Mostly. You?"

"Nothing broken, I don't think." My voice came out thin. "What happened? Did you see what it was?"

"Give me a minute," he said over his shoulder.

I'd already been sitting in the cold for what felt like a minute. I still didn't know where I was.

I ran through the options. A bomb, first, because it was the most obvious: a flash, a pressure wave, being thrown backward. We'd been walking near the park, so maybe a gas line had blown near the curb and caught us at the edge. When that didn't fit, I tried a concussion, which at least explained why the ground kept sliding a few degrees to the left. Then I looked up, and none of my theories had a park in them anymore. I hate being wrong with nobody watching. It's worse somehow. There was no one to be embarrassed in front of, just the trees, and the trees were not going to pretend they hadn't heard me.

Trees, everywhere. Bare branches over a flat grey sky, dead leaves piled in drifts up to my shins. No path. No people. No buildings. No street.

The quiet was the worst part. Columbus was never quiet. There was always a bus somewhere, a truck backing up, a siren two blocks off. Here there was wind in the branches and one bird complaining about something, and that was all. Where was the hum of the main road? Where was the row of closed businesses across from the park, the bus shelter, the little playground on the far side? Ten seconds ago I'd been walking past all of it.

"Where's the road?" I asked.

"I don't know."

"What do you mean you don't know? We were on a sidewalk."

He crouched, picked up a stone, and turned it over in his fingers without looking at it.

I dug my phone out of my coat pocket. The screen was unbroken. It woke when I pressed the button, bright and ordinary, and that small normal thing nearly made me cry. The map took its time loading. Then a blue dot settled on a patch of green, and a label appeared under it.

Crossville, Tennessee.

I read it twice. The words didn't change.

"Tennessee," I said out loud, to hear how it sounded. It sounded wrong.

The clock on the lock screen said 4:47 PM.

I looked at it, then at the trees, then back at the clock. We had left the coffee shop at twenty to six. I remembered because I'd glanced at my phone in the doorway and thought about the Henderson report, due Monday, which I had not started and was not going to start. Then we'd walked out into the cold. A few minutes later a man had raised one hand in the street, and then there were trees.

Jason had held the door for me. He'd said it was cold, and I'd said it was February, and we had both laughed at how obvious that was. That was the last ordinary sentence I could find.

Four forty-seven. An hour, give or take, had gone missing. Hours were supposed to disappear forward, into meetings. This one had gone backward, which was new.

I ran the sequence again, slowly. Doorway. Sidewalk. The man. The blue flash and the pressure in my chest. Then the trees. Five minutes, maybe ten, from the coffee shop door to the woods. The phone disagreed with that too.

The date underneath said Friday, February 9. That matched. Everything else was open for discussion.

I decided the phone was wrong. Phones were wrong constantly. Then I decided that I might be wrong, which I liked even less, because a phone could be reset and I couldn't. Last October I had insisted for a month that my oven was lying about its temperature. It had not been lying. I'd been too stubborn to say so.

So I did what I always do when I don't trust my own head. I screenshotted the clock. I screenshotted the GPS again, in case it had changed its mind. Then I took pictures of the trees, the drifts of leaves, the grey sky threaded through the branches. If this was a bad dream, at least I'd have bad pictures. The third shot was actually good, the light coming down between the trunks in a way I would have paid forty dollars for on a roll of film. I was annoyed to catch myself framing it. Facts are easier to keep when they're in a picture.

Behind me a branch cracked, and I jumped, and the phone went on saying 4:47 PM the whole time.

I put it away. The numbers didn't add up, and I had no other numbers to put in.

When I walked back to him, I didn't bother being gentle.

"Okay," I said. "Now. What happened?"

He turned to face me. His hands wouldn't stay still. He shoved them into his jacket pockets, pulled them out again, picked a dead stick off the ground and snapped it in half, then snapped one of the halves again. He picked up the longer piece and turned it over, looking for something inside it, and then he put it down like it might bite him. The pieces dropped into the leaves. He still had the leaf in his hair. I didn't tell him. Some petty part of me wanted him to walk around with it for a while.

"Okay. So the thing is..." He stopped. "The thing about the GPS is, it's probably... no. Not the GPS. Hang on." He let out a breath through his nose that wasn't quite a laugh. "Sorry. I'm going too fast."

He was talking fast, without much punctuation, and this time the ideas didn't seem to have him so much as chase him.

"You haven't finished one sentence."

"I'm getting there."

"Get there faster. Tell me the part you can say."

"Not here."

"Where, then? We're in the middle of nowhere. Who's going to overhear us, the squirrels?"

"You're scared," I said. "Don't tell me you're not. You keep snapping sticks."

He looked down at the broken stick in his hand. "I'm scared too. I need you to trust me for a bit."

"Then tell me why. Those men on the sidewalk. Did they know you?"

He opened his mouth and shut it again. "Maybe," he said, and went back to the pieces at his feet, as if they might tell him something.

"Did you do this?"

He looked at me, and then he looked away again. "Can you do something for me first?"

"That depends on what it is."

"Stay right here. Don't walk off, and if you hear something, don't go looking for it."

"You want me to sit in the woods like a dog while you go check on things." I heard my voice climbing and pulled it back down. "I'm not a dog, Jason."

"I never said you were a dog."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking you should stay put." He said it fast.

"Fine. What are you checking, then?"

"Whether anyone followed us."

I opened my mouth and closed it again. Followed us. We'd been walking to dinner. The only thing following us had been my bad attitude about the Henderson report, and the only thing waiting at the end of the block was a plate of pasta I would have ruined at home. But he'd thought about being followed before he'd thought about where we were. I noticed that, and I put it next to the clock in my head.

"How long do you need?"

"Thirty minutes. Maybe less."

"No. Fifteen."

"I need at least twenty."

"Fine. Twenty minutes. Then you tell me everything, figured out or not."

"Okay. Twenty."

I set the timer on my phone and pressed start. The numbers began to fall. "I'm holding you to that."

"Yes," he said, and walked off toward a rise where the trees thinned out.

Twenty minutes in a clearing with a broken stick and a man who wouldn't finish his sentences. I had sat through worse meetings. Not many, but some.

My legs made the decision for me. A fallen log lay at the edge of the clearing, soft and grey with rot, and I sat down on it. Damp soaked through my jeans right away. The rot smelled sweet and sour and old. I sat there anyway.

My heart was pounding hard enough that I could hear it over the ringing. I dug my thumbnails into my palms and let the sting give me something to think about besides my pulse.

At the top of the rise Jason put one hand flat against a tree trunk and stayed there, checking something I couldn't see. I wanted badly to know what it was, and I was too proud to ask him twice.

My phone had been buzzing in my pocket since before the woods. I answered the people who would notice first.

I typed to Sarah: Something came up, can't make it tomorrow. Will explain later.

I sent it before I could second-guess myself. Her answer came back almost at once.

Sarah: You ok? You never skip gallery stuff

Me: Fine, just got caught up with something. Promise I'm okay.

Sarah: Call me later?

Me: Will do.

I put the phone away before she could ask anything I couldn't answer. Then I took it out again, because Jess would notice if I didn't come home.

I texted Jess: Staying with a friend tonight, back tomorrow. Jess was my roommate, which meant she'd eaten half the cheese in our fridge that week and would blame the cheese situation on me. She'd assume I'd finally met someone with a car. She would be wrong, but in a way that would make her happy for a week.

Mom had texted earlier. If she'd known where I was, she would have turned it into a speech about Sunday dinner. Instead she got the milk.

Mom: Did you get the milk for Sunday?

Me: Not yet, will grab it tomorrow.

Mom: Everything ok?

Me: Totally fine, just busy.

I lied to my mother in about two seconds. My thumbs didn't even slow down. She would have believed me, too, which was the part I kept circling back to while I waited for my pulse to settle.

I put the phone face down on the log beside me and tried to breathe slowly. In through my nose, out through my mouth, the breathing my old therapist had taught me for bad nights.

This wasn't a panic attack. This was a legitimate reason to panic. But the breathing still helped.

Then I opened a blank message and typed: The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.

I read it, switched to my photos, and came back. The words were exactly the same. Same capital T, same period, nothing scrambled.

In dreams, text never held still. You looked away, looked back, and the sign said something else. I'd read somewhere that lucid dreamers test themselves this way, probably on some forum at two in the morning, and the idea had stayed in my head for no good reason. Now it had a use.

So either I wasn't dreaming, or my brain had gotten extremely good at detail.

Or I was hallucinating. A head injury could do that. I could be sitting on a curb in Columbus right now, staring at nothing, making up a forest.

But the log under me was rough and wet, and the cold had soaked through my jeans in a way that didn't feel invented. Nothing about this was vague. My brain had never been that thorough about anything, including my taxes.

The light had started to go thin and flat under the branches.

Then I opened the dial pad and typed 911. The keys sat there with their neat little numbers, looking cheerful about the whole thing. My thumb rested on the call button.

I could hear the call before I made it. Hi, I was in Columbus this afternoon and now I'm in Tennessee. No, I'm not hurt. Yes, I'm sober. No, I don't know how I got here. Could someone come look at a clump of trees? The dispatcher would have that bored, patient voice, the one people use on drunk uncles.

Through the trees I could see Jason on the rise. He was watching the woods for something, shoulders up around his ears, and he looked like a man who'd bolt if anything stepped out of them.

If a deputy drove out here, the first thing he'd see was Jason's face, and Jason looked like someone who'd either lie badly or tell the truth in a way that got us both put in a van. Whatever this was, the police couldn't do anything about it. Jason knew something I didn't, and a stranger with a notepad would only stand between us and whatever he was trying to say. Partly, I trusted him. Two years of Tuesdays had taught me how he looked when he was lying, and he didn't look like that now. He looked like someone trying not to fall apart at the top of a hill. The other part was colder. Calling would make everything worse, and faster.

I deleted the numbers one at a time and put the phone in my pocket. A minute later I took it back out and checked the timer. It said 6:40.

Jason had come down off the rise and was walking back through the leaves toward me, not fast. The timer said nine seconds when he got close enough that I could see the dirt on his knees. He reached for my sleeve. His hand was shaking. I turned the phone face down on my knee and waited for him to start.
