# Chapter 2: Displaced

The ringing came first. It was high and steady and seemed to be inside my skull rather than around me. I stayed on my hands and knees with my head lowered, because the ground was the one thing that wasn't moving. Dead leaves lay under my palms. They were brown on top and black underneath, where they had been lying in the wet, and when I lifted one hand I saw grit ground into the creases and a bead of blood where the skin had split. My knees throbbed where they had hit. Everything hurt in a dull, general way, and none of it was the sharp kind of pain that means something has broken.

"Jason?" I said, and then I couldn't find the rest of the sentence.

I breathed in for four counts and out for four, and it helped a little.

Then my stomach turned over. I crawled the few feet to the nearest tree and was sick against its roots. The bark was cold and wet under my hand, and I kept my eyes on the leaves until it was over. Somewhere above me the wind moved through the branches, and that was the only sound. The pale patches on the trunk were lichen, a dull green-white, and they were the closest thing to color I could see.

Jason was beside me before I'd finished. He crouched down and took hold of the strap of my bag, which had swung forward across my chest, and held it back over my shoulder so it stayed clear of my face. Then he shrugged his backpack off one shoulder, unzipped it and took out a water bottle. He moved fast, and his hands went straight to what they needed.

"Small sips," he said. "Don't gulp it."

The water was cold and tasted of plastic. I held the first mouthful for a moment before I trusted myself to swallow it.

He put the backpack back on, then took hold of my wrist.

"Does this hurt?"

"No."

He turned my hand over, slowly, and moved the wrist one way and then the other. His fingers were cold, and they didn't grip. He watched my face the whole time, not my hand.

"Move your fingers for me."

They moved, a little stiffly, with grit under the nails.

"Does your chest hurt?"

"It hurts to breathe all the way in."

"I'm going to press along your ribs. Tell me if anything is sharp." He asked before each press and waited for me to nod. The first, under my left arm, was fine. The second, lower down, hurt, and I told him so. He lifted his hand away and started again a few inches to one side.

"Nothing feels broken," he said, and sat back on his heels.

"My arm hurts." I touched my upper arm through the sleeve, where his hand had clamped on the sidewalk.

"That was my hand." He held my eyes a moment. "I'm sorry."

His knuckles were red from the cold. He flexed the hand once, then stood and turned slowly, looking at the trees.

I tried a concussion, because it was the explanation I already had. A hard knock to the head can make the ground tilt, ring the ears and turn the stomach, and every part of that fit. It didn't fit the blue light, or the force that had come through it and hit my chest. It didn't explain the forest either. I tried to make those pieces go into it anyway, and they wouldn't go. I didn't ask Jason about the light.

I checked my pockets. My phone was in my coat, where I had put it at the office, and the film box was in the same pocket, pressing against my hip through the lining. The bag was still across my body. I unzipped it and felt for my wallet and keys. Both were there, and I zipped it shut again.

My scarf wasn't on me. I'd taken it off in the coffee shop and hadn't put it back on, and the cold was getting in at the back of my neck.

I sat down on a fallen log at the foot of the slope. The log had a skin of moss along its top, and it came away on my fingers in a soft green smear. The wood was soaked through the seat of my coat within seconds, and I shifted forward off it and stayed where I was anyway.

I got the phone out. It had two bars of signal. I opened the map, and the screen lit my thumb from below, hard-edged, with no falloff into the dark around it. The blue dot was in a green patch. The nearest road was a thin gray line at the bottom edge of the screen, nowhere near the dot, and the label underneath said Crossville, Tennessee. I pinched the screen to zoom out until I found Columbus, a gray smudge far to the north. The dot didn't move from where it was.

The clock at the top of the screen read 4:47 PM. It was an hour early, and nothing I knew explained that.

I tried a glitch. Maybe the jolt had knocked the clock off, or the phone had reset itself somewhere between there and here. But a glitch that big would have broken the map too, and the map was working. The dot was steady in the middle of the woods. I couldn't get any further than that.

I took a screenshot of the time. Then one of the map, with the label showing. Then I turned a slow circle and took one of the nearest trunk, which filled the picture with bark and nothing else.

Jason was a few yards off among the trunks, looking out at the woods and not at me. He had put his weight on his back foot, the way someone does on a slope, and he kept it there.

"What happened?" I said. "I was on a sidewalk in Columbus. Something hit us, and now I'm sitting in the woods. Look at me, Jason."

He did, after a moment. When he turned, his hands stayed at his sides. The overcast gave his face no shadow under the cheekbones, only a thin line where his jaw met his neck.

"I'll explain what I can," he said. "Before I do, I need to know whether anyone out here saw us come down."

"Out here where?" I stood up off the log. "Who would be out here, Jason?"

"Give me twenty minutes."

I looked past him for a road or a fence. There were only trunks. Behind him they went back in rows, dark and wet, until they blurred into the gray. Nothing moved between them except the wind. I listened for a car, and heard nothing at all.

"Fifteen," I said.

"Twenty."

The overcast hadn't thinned since we'd come down. If anything it was darker along the edges of the woods now, and the air had a bite to it that I hadn't noticed before.

"Fine," I said. "Twenty minutes. Then you tell me what happened, all of it. I'm holding you to it."

"All right."

I set a timer on my phone for twenty minutes and switched it to vibrate, so the alarm wouldn't carry. Jason walked up a rise where the trees thinned, and stopped near the top with his back to me. A few leaves came loose from a branch above him and fell straight down through the still air. I thought about going after him. Then I thought about what I would say when I got there, and I sat back down on the log. From where I sat I could see only that he was standing still. I checked that the timer was still running, which it was.

Sarah had texted while I was looking at the map. I opened the message.

"Still coming to gallery night tomorrow?"

I typed back that I couldn't make it. The words came out smooth, smoother than anything I'd written all day. I noticed that, and then I looked at the timer, which had started counting down while I wasn't watching.

Sarah answered within a minute. "You okay? You never skip gallery night."

Sarah knew how my job went, so I gave her that. "Work has swamped me. Sorry."

Then I opened a new note to myself and typed "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." The cursor blinked at the end of it.

I looked up at the branches and the gray between them, then back at the screen. The screen was the brightest thing for a long way around, and when I looked up again the woods seemed darker than they had a minute before. The sentence hadn't changed. I didn't have another test.

Marcus had sent a photo from Murphy's. A row of people from work leaned together under the yellow lights, glasses raised, and the lights had turned every face a shade warmer than it was. I spread two fingers on the screen to make it bigger. A woman from accounts I barely knew was half cut off at the edge, laughing at something outside the picture. At the end of the row a man had his hand flat on the bar, and somebody's coat hung off the back of a stool. Under the photo Marcus had written, "Are you meeting your friend tonight, or did he talk you out of this?"

I read it twice before I answered. "I'll see you all Monday." I didn't mention the friend.

A few minutes later my mom wrote, "Still bringing the milk Sunday? And have you heard anything about the promotion?"

I hadn't bought the milk. I typed, "Yes, bringing the milk. Nothing new on the promotion."

Nobody in Columbus knew yet that I hadn't come home. I thought about the apartment, and the dishes soaking in the sink, and Jess coming home to them. I typed, "Staying with a friend tonight." My thumb stopped over send. Sarah might ask Jess about me, and Jess would have to answer. I sent it anyway.

Then I typed 911 and held my thumb over the call button. A dispatcher would ask where I was. I would have to tell her that I had been in Columbus, and that I was in Tennessee now. I couldn't find the words for the second part. She would want an address, and I didn't have one.

Up on the rise, Jason was standing where he had been. I didn't ask him whether I should call. I deleted the numbers one at a time, until the screen was empty again.

The cold came up through the seat of my coat from the wet log. I pulled my hands up into my sleeves and held the phone in them.

The Henderson file was open on my office computer, two sections blank and the table still broken. Monday morning was going to arrive without it, and I had no way to reach it from here.

The roll I hadn't finished was in the Pentax, on my desk at home. The film in my pocket had no camera to go into.

The wind came down the slope and moved the dead leaves around my feet. They made a dry sound, and then they settled. The leaves on top of the drift had dried at the edges, and the ones underneath were still dark with wet. The gray over the trees had gone darker, and there was no sun anywhere to account for it. The wind stopped once, and the woods went so quiet that I could still hear the ringing, faint, under everything. Then the wind came back and covered it.

At ten minutes I checked the timer once. Half of it was gone. I didn't look at it again.

When it reached zero, the phone buzzed once in my hands. I touched the screen, and the buzzing stopped. Nothing else happened.

I stood up and walked to the bottom of the rise.

"Time's up," I called up.

I didn't move from the bottom of the rise.
