# Chapter 1: Normal

The fluorescent lights over the Henderson team had been flickering since Tuesday. Maintenance had come up twice, looked at the ceiling with their hands on their hips, and promised a work order. Under that light everyone's skin came out the same flat gray-green. In a print I would have corrected it in a minute. Up there I could do nothing about it, so I looked at my spreadsheet instead.

"Mira, you still here?"

Marcus was leaning against the wall of my cubicle with his jacket already on and his messenger bag over one shoulder. I looked up.

"Trying to finish this before the weekend."

"It's half past four on a Friday. The report can wait."

"The report is due Monday morning."

"Then you can finish it Monday morning." He grinned. "Come on. A few of us are going to Murphy's."

I looked back at the screen. Two sections of the draft were still blank, a regional breakdown the client had asked for twice and an appendix I'd been avoiding. The three pages before them needed checking, and one table kept breaking its formatting every time I touched it. One more hour and I could send the whole thing. If I left now, I would spend the weekend thinking about it.

"I can't. I'm meeting Jason."

"Ah. The famous Jason." Marcus ticked items off on his fingers. "You skipped girls' night last month to have dinner with him. You get coffee with him every Tuesday, and then you come back in a good mood and pretend you're not."

"It was one dinner. We're friends."

"Uh-huh."

"We are." That was the truth, or the version of it I'd settled on. Two years of Tuesdays and careful distance had made it clear enough, and I had learned to stop hoping for anything else.

Marcus checked the clock above the door, which said 4:31. "You're meeting him at five, right? Don't let me keep you." He pushed off the wall. "Have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

He left, and the office settled back into its ordinary noise, keyboards and the buzz of the lights. Past the high windows the light had gone the flat blue it turns in February, and one of the blinds on the far wall hung crooked.

My phone buzzed against the desk. "Still on for five?" Jason had written.

Coffee was on Tuesdays. We had settled into Tuesdays without either of us ever deciding on them. Jason had never asked for a different day, and then on Wednesday he had asked for Friday, as if it were nothing. I had said yes before I thought about it, and I'd been turning it over ever since without deciding what it meant.

"Yes, see you there," I typed, and sent it before I could talk myself out of going.

On the screen, the pivot table on page forty-two had shifted three pixels to the left for no reason I could find. My eyes felt packed with sand. Three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots, each snap loud in the quiet office. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. And I hated that if I didn't fix that table, the VP would make a comment about attention to detail on Monday.

I had picked this job because it paid on the fifteenth and didn't ask for more than my hours. For most of the past year that had been enough, and I was not sure it was enough anymore.

I saved the file and put my phone in my coat pocket. I slung my bag across my body, the strap crossing my chest, and walked out before I could decide to stay.

---

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday. Students had laptops open along one wall, a couple sat close together near the door, and a book club in the corner had four kinds of pastry spread across their table. Someone had hung framed prints of barns along the brick, and the ceiling lights were too bright for them. The steam wand behind the counter hissed every few seconds. Jason stood at the counter with two cups in his hands while the barista finished something with foam. He had his back to the room. He had gotten his hair cut since I last saw him, shorter at the back, and I noticed it before I noticed anything else.

Our usual table was free, the one at the front with the wobbly leg. I took off my scarf and put my bag on the chair beside me. The latte was in front of me before I had my coat open. Jason sat across from me with his black coffee, and his backpack on the chair beside him. His knuckles were red from the cold.

The lamp above our table lit his face from straight above, the way an on-camera flash does, and it took out the lines that usually showed when he was tired. I wanted to move the lamp.

"Long day?" he asked.

"It's the Henderson thing. Still not finished." I pulled my coat off my shoulders and folded it over my knees.

He nodded and waited for the rest.

I had meant to ask him about Friday. I'd rehearsed it on the walk over, three different ways, and each one sounded more like a complaint than a question. Then he asked about my camera, and the question got put off.

"How's the Pentax?" he said.

"It's good. It's honestly really good." The meter was dead when I bought it, and the man at the shop was so pleased with himself that I didn't check it until I got home. I put in a new battery and it's been fine since. Then I found a box of expired color stock online, the kind they stopped making years ago, and I bought three rolls because they were cheap. It's gone green. Not much, just enough that every shadow picks up a tint and people look a little ill. My first roll was all test shots of my kitchen, which was a mistake, because nobody wants to look at a kitchen. The second roll I took to the bridge downtown at sunset. I shot it from the walkway, low, with the railing across the bottom of the frame so the water would catch the light. The sky came out a color I've never seen on a real sky. I still can't decide if I'm happy about it. I've been looking at the contact sheet for days.

"Does the green come through in the prints, or only the negatives?" Jason asked.

"Both, and the prints are worse. I'd need a darkroom to fix it properly, which is its own problem. There's a roll still in the camera at home that I haven't finished, because I keep thinking the next frame will be the good one. And there's a co-op on the east side, above a print shop, with a real enlarger and a sink. I walk past the building every week and look up at the second-floor windows, as if the rent might have changed since last time."

"I'm sorry," I said, and laughed at myself. "You asked how it was going, and I've talked for five minutes straight. You didn't need the whole history of my camera."

"Most people are boring," Jason said.

"You can't check a roll until it's developed, though. I like that part. You don't find out what you got until it's done."

The latte had gone lukewarm, and I drank it anyway.

My phone buzzed against the table. It was Nora, in Chicago, with another apartment listing: a one-bedroom in Lincoln Park with a photo of an empty kitchen. The sun came in low across the counter in that picture, the way it does in listing photos taken before anyone has moved in. I turned the phone over. I had no plans to move.

"Are you going to look at it?" Jason asked.

"I probably won't," I said. "My parents have opinions about what comes next, and they're happy to share them."

"I have to get home early Sunday," I said a minute later. "I promised my mom I'd bring milk to dinner."

"Do yours do the timeline thing too?" I asked. "Your parents?"

"We don't really talk much."

"I'm sorry."

"It's fine. We didn't really get along." He said it evenly. "Have you thought any more about that darkroom space?"

"I can't afford it," I said. "Not this year. There's the rent, and then the equipment, and the chemicals, and it just isn't practical right now."

"That's too bad. It sounded like a good setup."

He reached down to the backpack by his chair, unzipped the front pocket, and took out a small paper bag folded over twice at the top. He put it on the table between our cups and pushed it toward me with two fingers.

"Open it," he said.

Inside was a roll of film, the discontinued stock I had mentioned once, months ago, back when I was only thinking about buying a camera and didn't know yet what I'd want to shoot. He had kept hold of the name, which I had only said once.

"I found it at a camera shop across town," he said. "I saw it and thought of you."

I took the box out and read the whole label aloud, including the expiration date, which was useless now. Then I thanked him. Then I thanked him again. "All right," I said, and set the box down, and picked it up again. "Thank you. Really."

He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

I put the roll in my coat pocket and didn't open it.

At the next table, two students who had been arguing for a while got louder. One said someone owed someone for a sublet, the other said the number was wrong, and they kept raising their voices until a couple of tables turned around. It sounded like Jess and me arguing about the dishwasher.

Jason slid his laptop into his backpack.

"Can we go somewhere quieter?" I asked. "That Thai place Sarah keeps going on about? It's about ten minutes if we walk."

"Yes," he said. "Let's go."

My heart did a stupid little flip. It was only dinner.

The clock over the counter said twenty to six when we stood up.

---

We stepped out into the February evening. The air had a sharp edge, and I turned my collar up. Someone had left a crust of salt in the cracks of the sidewalk, and my shoes were not made for it. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left toward the restaurant, and my bag bumped my hip with every stride.

Along the park, the streetlights were coming on one at a time. We passed a bank with its lights off and a couple walking a dog on a long leash. In a second-floor window a man stood at his sink, and the light from his kitchen fell across the sidewalk in a yellow square that I had to step over. At the corner a car went through with its radio on, and then the street was quiet again.

Past the trees on the left, the park ran down a slope toward a river. I could see the water through the railing, a dark line with a few lights lying on it. I looked for a moment and kept walking.

I checked my phone. It was 5:45.

Under the orange lamps the brick had gone brown and my navy coat looked nearly black. Only the red sign of a dry cleaner still had any real color.

"Your green stock is going to look strange under these," Jason said, nodding up at the lamps. "Would you still shoot it?"

"Of course I would. It's the only thing I shoot that doesn't look like everything else."

I was thinking about Nora. She would finish her part-time master's in June, and she kept saying there was room for me in Chicago when she did. I still didn't know what I wanted. I was also working up to asking him about Friday, and I kept not starting.

Jason's breath showed a little when he spoke, and then it was gone.

A gust came up off the river. I stepped onto an uneven square of pavement and stumbled. Jason's hand came up toward my elbow. It stopped a few inches short of me, and then it dropped back to his side.

"I'm fine," I said. I was.

"Do you ever get lonely?" I asked. I hadn't planned to ask it that way.

"Sometimes."

His profile was dark under the lamp. He took his time with the answer, and we walked as far as the next corner.

"You're different," he said.

"Different how?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

The air cracked.

There was a flash of blue light right in front of us, electric and wrong. Something invisible slammed into it, and the force came through the light and hit my chest. It was 5:47. I had checked my phone two minutes before.

Jason didn't say anything. His hand was already closing on my arm, hard enough to hurt, before I understood that anything had happened.

Pressure filled my chest, and I couldn't breathe. A high ringing filled my ears, there was another flash of blue, and the world tipped sideways. For about a heartbeat the lurch curved, like a car swinging under me around a bend too fast, and then it straightened out. I felt it. Everything else was happening too fast for me to think about it.

Prickling spread over my whole skin, sharp and everywhere at once.

Then the ground. I came down on my hands and knees, and dirt and dead leaves pressed into my palms. The air smelled of wet wood. Above me, bare branches stood against a gray sky.
