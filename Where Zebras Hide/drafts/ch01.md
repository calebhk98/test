# Chapter 1: Normal

The fluorescent lights in the office had been flickering since Tuesday, and by Friday afternoon I was ready to throw my stapler at them. Not that it would help. Maintenance had come by twice and promised a work order, and the lights had kept on doing it anyway.

Three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots. Snap-crunch, snap-crunch, loud enough to carry over the hum. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. And the pivot table on page 42 had shifted three pixels to the left for no reason I could figure out, which meant the VP would have something passive-aggressive to say about attention to detail on Monday.

"Mira, you still here?"

I looked up. Marcus was leaning on my cubicle wall with his jacket already on and his messenger bag over one shoulder.

"Trying to finish this report before the weekend."

"It's four thirty on a Friday. The report can wait."

"The report is due Monday morning."

"Then Monday morning you can finish it." He grinned. "Some of us are getting drinks at Murphy's. Come on."

I looked at my screen. I'd been promising myself since October that I'd get ahead of the Henderson account, and every Monday somebody added a tab. Three pages still needed review, and the table of contents kept renumbering itself every time I touched it. If I stayed another hour, I could get it done. If I left, I'd spend all weekend thinking about it.

"I can't. I've got plans."

"What plans?"

"Coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin got wider. "Ah. The mysterious Jason. When are you two finally going to make it official?"

"Make what official?" I knew exactly what he meant. Everyone in the office seemed to think they knew what this was.

"You meet him every Tuesday for coffee. You talk about him constantly. Last month you cancelled on girls' night because he was in town." He ticked the items off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it yet."

Marcus had worked there four months and already believed he was the office gossip columnist.

My face went warm. "We're friends."

"Uh huh."

"We are." I had gotten used to it, or at least I'd gotten good at acting like it. Two years of Tuesdays had made it pretty clear. Jason had never given me a reason to think otherwise.

"Okay." He pushed off the wall. "Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

Nora had left a voice memo at lunch, eight minutes long, about a Chicago apartment with a radiator that clanked all night. I'd listened to all of it twice, because she does the voices.

My phone buzzed against the desk, and I looked at it faster than I meant to. Friday was not our day. Tuesdays were our day. Jason had texted at lunch to ask if we could meet tonight, and I'd typed "Yes" and then spent far too long wondering why I cared.

The new message was from him.

Jason: Still on for coffee? I'm heading over now.

I typed back "Yeah. See you in 20" and looked up. Marcus was still there, eyebrows raised, pretending he wasn't watching me.

"Nothing," I said. "A work thing."

"Sure. A work thing." He wiggled his fingers at me. "Have fun with it."

He left. I looked at the Henderson file, at the three pages that still needed work, at the lights buzzing over my head like they had something to say. The report could wait.

* * *

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday night. Students were camped out behind their laptops, and a book club had taken over the corner with a tower of pastries and a fierce argument about a dragon. Jason was at the counter when I walked in. He saw me, and for a second his whole face lit up. Then he caught himself and smoothed it into the almost-smile that never quite turned into what I wanted it to be.

By the time I'd unwound my scarf and reached our table by the window, he was on his way back with a latte in one hand and a black coffee in the other. He set the latte in front of my chair. Extra foam. Two shots. Beside it sat a blueberry scone on a paper napkin.

He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

"You hate those," I said, nodding at the scone. "You told me they taste like sawdust."

"Sawdust is the point. You look like you need it."

"Hey," he said, sitting down across from me. "Long day?"

"How can you tell?"

"You've got that specific look. Henderson account?"

I slumped. "They want two more sections. Two. And they still want it Monday."

"That's unreasonable."

"Everyone knows it's unreasonable. They're paying us, so it's happening anyway." I rubbed my eyes. "I left it at the office. I'll deal with it tomorrow."

"On a Saturday?"

"I don't have a choice."

"Bill them for the weekend," he said. "Hazard pay."

"I'm not invoicing Henderson for my soul."

He frowned, then closed his laptop and slid it to the far end of the table. No phone check, no "hold on, one second." Marcus would have called that proof of something. Jason just picked up his cup and started tapping the side of it with one fingernail.

"What are you doing?"

"Timing how fast it cools." He tapped his phone. "It's losing heat slower than it should. Either the sleeve is doing something weird or the cup is, and I want to know which, and I'm going to figure it out whether or not you care."

"I care a little."

"A little is fine." He set the cup down, pleased with himself. "Sarah wants you at some gallery opening tomorrow, right? Are you going?"

"Probably not. I'll be buried in this." I took a sip. "What about you?"

"Some projects. Nothing exciting."

"What kind of projects?"

"Contract work. Programming stuff." He shrugged. "Hey, how's that camera? The Pentax?"

My whole face lit up before I could stop it. "It's amazing. I finally got the light meter working, and I've been shooting this expired film I found online. The grain is incredible. Everything comes out a little green."

I'd bought the Pentax from a man in Dublin, Ohio, who kept it in a shoebox with a note taped to the strap: Don't drop it, it's older than you. I haven't dropped it. I have come close, several times, and each time I apologized to it out loud.

"Did you develop any of it?"

"A batch on Wednesday night. There's a shot of the bridge downtown at sunset that came out exactly how I pictured it. The sky has this greenish cast from the expired stock, and somehow it works." I pointed my scone at him. "I've still got one roll in the camera, and I'm scared to finish it. The light that day was so good I don't think it'll happen again."

"You'll have to show me sometime."

"The prints will be terrible," I said. "The green is a whole personality."

"I'll decide that myself," he said, and didn't look away while he said it. I liked that he assumed there would be something to see.

"Most people think I'm crazy for spending money on film when my phone takes perfectly good pictures."

"Most people are boring." He smiled. "Besides, there's something different about film. The whole process. It's more intentional."

"Exactly." I loved that he got it. He'd actually listened when I tried to explain why it mattered. "My sister keeps saying it's a waste of money. Why bother when digital is easier and cheaper?"

"Because easier and cheaper isn't always better."

"I'm stealing that line the next time she gives me grief."

He laughed, and I felt the familiar warmth in my chest that came with nights like this. He laughs through his nose first. I've never told him, because he would do it on purpose for the rest of my life. Easy and comfortable.

"How's your sister?" he asked. "Still trying to get you to move to Chicago?"

"Always. She sent me five apartment listings this week." I rolled my eyes. "I keep telling her I'm happy here."

"Are you?"

The question caught me off guard. "Mostly. I have friends, and my apartment is decent." I looked at him. "I've got reasons to stay."

His mouth did something complicated, then settled into a smile. "That's good."

"My mom called yesterday, by the way. She wants to know when I'm getting promoted."

"Are you?"

"No idea. But she's decided I should be by now, and she has a whole timeline I'm apparently behind on." I took a sip. "My dad is mailing me grad school applications. Like I want to pay someone to stress me out again. They mean well."

I'd also promised Mom I'd bring milk to Sunday dinner. Jess had finished ours, so that was a problem for future me.

"Do your parents do the timeline thing too?"

He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "We don't really talk much."

"Oh. I'm sorry."

"It's fine. We didn't really get along." He said it lightly. "Hey, have you thought any more about that darkroom space?"

"I can't afford it," I said. "The rental would eat my fun money for a year, and then I'd need an enlarger on top of that." I shrugged. "Maybe someday, when I get that promotion Mom keeps asking about."

He smiled at that.

"Does your coworker still think I'm your boyfriend?"

"I never said that."

"You told me he said it. You told me the whole story, and you did his voice."

"I did not do a voice." I had done a voice. It was a very good voice. "He's going to meet you one day and be disappointed that you're so normal."

"I'm extremely normal."

"You are not."

He laughed before I'd finished the sentence, which he does with his own jokes too.

At the table beside us, two students had been arguing for ten minutes. Now one of them was loud enough for half the shop to hear.

"If you knew every variable in the universe, you could predict everything that will ever happen. Free will is an illusion."

"That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard. Quantum mechanics exists. You can't know everything."

I winced. Jason glanced over at them with a small, private smile, like he already knew how it ended. Then he looked at the door, a beat too long, and back at me.

He had a crease between his eyebrows that showed up when he was about to say something important and was hunting for the least important way to start. I figured that was what this was.

Then he reached down beside his chair and came up with a small paper bag, the crinkly-top kind, folded over twice. He slid it across the table.

"What's this?"

"Open it."

Inside was a roll of the film stock I'd moaned about a few months ago, the one the company had quietly stopped making. I'd checked every camera shop I could find and come up empty. I'd honestly forgotten I ever complained about it.

"Jason. Where did you even find this?"

"Saw it. Thought of you."

I was ridiculously happy. I tried to keep my face neutral and failed completely. "You didn't have to," I said, in the voice of someone who was going to think about this all week. I slid it into my coat pocket, where it knocked against my hip for the rest of the night.

He glanced at the window, and back. His face went tight and careful. He opened his mouth, then picked up the crumpled top of the paper bag and folded it flat, twice.

"You want to get out of here?" he asked. "Go somewhere quieter?"

"Please."

"There's that Thai place a few blocks away. Sarah keeps recommending it. Apparently the peanut sauce is life-changing."

"Sarah says that about everything."

"Sarah's right about this one, I think."

My heart did a stupid little flip. Two years of coffee, and now dinner.

"I've been wanting to try it," he added.

Oh. Right. He was hungry. I was convenient.

It was just past five. I should have gone home and worked on the report. Instead I said, "Okay, but you're buying, since you're the one who talked me into procrastinating."

"I didn't talk you into anything. You were procrastinating when I got here."

"Details."

I was already planning my order. Medium spice, which I would then pretend I could handle. He grinned and started packing up his laptop. I pulled the lid off my latte to let it cool and kept it in my hand.

* * *

We went out into the February evening. It wasn't as cold as it could have been, but the wind had an edge, and it kept finding the gap at my collar. My shoes were the right shoes for a desk and the wrong shoes for a sidewalk, which I had known when I put them on and had decided not to care about. I was still holding the lid, turning it over in my fingers. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left toward the restaurant. The Thai place's sign was lit up half a block ahead, its letters buzzing on and off one at a time.

We reached a quieter stretch of sidewalk. The park ran along one side and a row of closed storefronts ran along the other, and hardly anyone was out. Every half block, Jason checked the street behind us. I noticed it twice and decided not to ask.

A gust came up the sidewalk, and I stumbled on a cracked square of concrete. Jason's hand came up on its own and stopped six inches from my elbow. It stayed there, hanging in the cold, until he let it drop.

"You good?"

"I'm good."

I was thinking about the bridge photo instead, about whether the green would look better in a print, which is the kind of thing I think about when I'm nervous and pretending not to be. I was also thinking about the milk. I'd be at my mother's table on Sunday with no milk, and she would say nothing about it, which would be worse.

We passed under a streetlight, and Jason looked up at it. "Those orange ones make everything the same color. You can't tell a red car from a green leaf. It's a fascinating problem if you care about accurate color, because you'd have to know what the light was doing before you even got to the thing itself. Actually, you'd have to know that for the sodium lamps alone. Sorry. Ignore me."

"I'm not ignoring you."

"You're definitely ignoring me." He was almost smiling. He jammed his hands deeper into his coat pockets and walked a little faster.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Do you ever get lonely? Working by yourself, never going into an office?"

He thought about it, eyes on the sidewalk. "Sometimes. I'm not great with people. Offices would wear me out."

I thought about my own apartment, with Jess's boots in the hall and the dishwasher that only ran on Thursdays, and about how many nights I'd come home to somebody's voice on the other side of a wall.

"But you're good with me," I said. "So you can't be that bad with people."

"You're different."

Two words, and my chest did a small, traitorous turn. I was a grown woman who got a flutter out of two words, and I was going to be embarrassed about it for the rest of the walk.

"Different how?"

I wanted him to finish the sentence more than I'd wanted anything all week.

He opened his mouth to answer.

A man stepped out of a doorway across the street with one hand raised, palm out. He wore a canvas jacket and a knit hat, and he didn't look nervous at all.

Jason's head snapped around. Then the air cracked.

It was the only way I could describe it later. A curved sheet of blue light snapped into being in front of us, bright and electric, and something invisible hit it. I watched the blue ripple the way a windshield ripples when a rock comes at it from the wrong side. A second figure moved out from behind a parked van. The hum from the main road stopped all at once.

Jason's hand clamped around my arm, hard enough to hurt. "Hold on to me," he said, and his voice was tight in a way I'd never heard.

Pressure slammed into my chest. My ears rang. The world lurched sideways, and my skin felt like someone had peeled it off and put it back on wrong. None of it made sense to me.

Somewhere in the spinning, his hand let go of my arm.

Ground.

Dirt and dead leaves, a rock digging into my palm. The air smelled wet and green, which made no sense in February. Bare branches, grey sky, and the coffee lid still in my hand.
