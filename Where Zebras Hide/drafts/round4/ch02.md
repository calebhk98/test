# Chapter 2: Displaced

The ringing hadn't stopped. It was high and constant, like a tuning fork someone had pressed against the side of my head, and under it I could hear my own breathing. I was still on my hands and knees. My palms stung where grit had ground into the skin, and when I shifted my weight my arms gave a little. Dead leaves clung to my coat, and my collar was still turned up, but the cold had got in around my neck anyway. The ground kept tipping slightly to the left, and my stomach climbed toward my throat. The air smelled of wet wood. I swallowed and stayed where I was.

I breathed in for four counts and out for four, and it helped a little.

Under a sky that flat, nothing cast a shadow. The dead leaf on my sleeve looked pasted there. The leaves on the ground were brown and curled at the edges, most of them flattened into a crust, and a few on top still kept their shape. I didn't know enough about trees to name the ones around me. They were tall and bare and gray-barked, and that was as far as I could get.

The ringing dropped to a thin whine under everything else. Then there was a bird somewhere, and branches knocking together behind me.

"Jason?"

My voice came out rough. He was already beside me, down on one knee in the leaves with one hand flat between my shoulder blades. I hadn't heard him move.

"Look at me," he said. "Are you hurt anywhere?"

"I'm fine," I said, and looked down at my hands. "Just my hands. And my knees."

He looked at my face while he worked, and his hands did the rest. He took my right wrist and turned it over, pressing along the bones with his thumb. His fingers were cold and very steady. "Tell me if anything is sharp."

"I couldn't feel anything sharp."

He moved up to my forearm, then my elbow, and when he reached my shoulder he stopped and asked before he pressed. "Does this hurt?"

"Yes, it's just sore." My upper arm ached where his hand had closed on it on the sidewalk, and I didn't mention it.

He tipped my chin up with two fingers and looked into one eye, then the other. He held a finger in front of my face. "Follow that."

I tracked it left, then right. His knuckles were still red from the cold.

"Good," he said, and sat back on his heels. "Nothing's broken, as far as I can tell."

I looked down at my palms. Grit was pressed into the heel of my right hand, and I picked at it with a fingernail until two thin lines of blood came up. It wasn't much. The trunks nearest me were split along the bark, and the leaves under them had been pressed flat by a winter of rain.

Jason stood and turned in a slow circle, watching the trees in every direction before he looked back at me.

I tried an explanation. I'd hit my head, and this was what a concussion looked like from the inside. It was the only explanation I had, and it did nothing about the trees.

I patted down my coat. My phone was in the right pocket, and the film Jason had given me was in there too, its box pressing against my hip through the lining. My crossbody bag was still across my chest with the zip shut. When I felt inside, my wallet and keys were where they should be. Jason's backpack hung from one shoulder, the strap pulled tight across his chest.

The phone was harder to hold than it should have been, because my fingers were stiff with cold. The screen came up at the first press, and the glass had no cracks. The map took a long time to load. A blue dot appeared near a town labeled Crossville, Tennessee, and the nearest road was a thin gray line miles away.

I looked at the clock at the top of the screen. 4:47 PM.

I tried a glitch first, the kind of thing that freezes a screen. Then a clock that had reset itself. Neither one fit the map, which had me hundreds of miles from the last place I remembered being. I stopped trying to make them fit.

I took a screenshot of the clock. Then one of the map with the blue dot. Then I lifted the phone and tried to frame the trees, the split trunks and the gray sky through the branches, and took that too.

---

"Tell me what happened," I said. "Now."

He had stood up and was facing me, the backpack on one shoulder and one thumb hooked under the strap. The gray light lay across his face and had taken the color out of his cheeks, and he looked thinner than he had across the table at the coffee shop. He was calm, which frightened me more than panic would have.

"I'll explain it," he said. "All of it. But I need to know whether anyone followed us before I say anything, and I can't find that out by talking."

"Nobody was on that street," I said. "We were walking to dinner."

I went back over it in my head. The bank with its lights off, the man at his sink, the car with the radio. Nothing had been near us when the air cracked.

"I know."

"Then why are we in Tennessee?"

"That's the part I'm going to explain."

"Should I call 911?" I asked.

"Not yet."

Behind him the trees went on in every direction, all the same gray, and I could see no road through them at all.

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one I can give you right now."

Part of me wanted to walk over and make him look at me while he said it. I stayed on the log.

"I need twenty minutes," he said.

I looked at the damp wood under my hands. "Fifteen," I said.

"Twenty," he said. "Less than that would be a guess."

"Fine," I said. "Twenty, and when it runs out you tell me everything, whether you've worked it out or not."

"I'll tell you everything," he said.

I set a timer on my phone for twenty minutes and pressed start. The numbers began to fall. The first minute went slowly, and I kept my eyes on the screen and off him.

"I'm holding you to that," I said.

"I know."

He turned and walked up the slope to a rise where the trees thinned out. The ground there was rock with a skin of leaves over it, and the trees on it were thinner than the ones below. At the top he stopped and stood with his hands loose at his sides. I sat down on a fallen log at the foot of the slope. The bark had come away from it in patches, and the wood beneath was soft and pale and damp through the seat of my coat. I put my bag in my lap and held the phone in both hands.

---

Sarah had texted while I was watching the trees. Still coming to gallery night tomorrow? I typed that I couldn't make it and sent it before I could add anything. Gallery nights were in a converted warehouse on the north side, with cheap wine and a long table of cheese, and I had never missed one.

She wrote back almost at once. "You ok? You never skip gallery night."

"Work's swamped me," I typed. "Sorry, next time."

The lie came out smooth. I hadn't planned those words, and they came out without my having to look for them. That startled me.

With the timer still running, I opened a blank message and typed: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." Then I looked away at the trees, and back at the screen. The letters were the same size and shape, sitting in the same box on the screen, with the same small gap after the period. The words hadn't changed. It was the only test I had.

Later the phone buzzed again. My mom had written: "Still bringing the milk Sunday?"

"Yes! Still bringing it Sunday," I typed, and sent it.

A few minutes after that I thought about our apartment, and about what Jess would assume when I didn't come home. The dishes would still be in the sink, and she would assume I'd stayed out with Sarah. I texted her that I was staying with a friend tonight.

I opened the dial pad and typed 911. I held my thumb over the call button. The screen was the brightest thing in the woods, and it lit my thumb from underneath. The first thing anyone would ask was where I was, and the phone said Tennessee, and I had no way to explain that. I would have to tell a dispatcher I'd been in Ohio, and that I was in Tennessee now, and then explain how.

Up on the rise, Jason stood at the top with his arms held a little away from his sides and his palms turned forward, as if he were feeling for a wall in the dark. Whatever this was, the police couldn't help with it, and he knew what it was.

I deleted the numbers one at a time.

Two years of Tuesdays had taught me how he talked, and I still trusted him more than I trusted my own sense of what was happening.

---

The timer had a few minutes left. I watched the trees and the screen until the numbers ran down. The light hadn't changed while I waited. It came from no particular direction, and the trunks up the rise were gray on every side. Bare rock showed through the leaves at the top of the slope, and a root had been pushed up out of it, bent like an elbow. A bird called once somewhere to the north and didn't call again. My bag had slid around to my back, and I pulled it forward by the strap.

The screen read zero. I stood with the phone still in one hand and walked across the leaves to the foot of the rise.

"Time's up," I called up the slope. "Come down and tell me."

He started down toward me, picking his way between the trunks. I stayed at the bottom of the rise, my bag strap in my other hand, and waited for him to reach me.
