# Chapter 1: Normal

The fluorescent lights in the office had been flickering since Tuesday, and by Friday afternoon I wanted to throw my stapler at them. Maintenance had come by twice already, stared at the ceiling with their hands on their hips, and promised a work order.

"Mira, you still here?"

Marcus was leaning against my cubicle wall with his jacket on and his messenger bag over one shoulder.

"Trying to finish this report before the weekend."

"It's four thirty on a Friday. The report can wait."

"The report is due Monday morning."

"Come on," he said. "Some of us are getting drinks at Murphy's. You should come."

I looked at my screen. Three more pages to review, citations to check, and a formatting problem that broke every time I touched it. If I stayed another hour, I could finish. If I left now, I would think about it all weekend.

"I can't. I've got plans anyway."

"What plans?"

"Coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin got wider. "Ah. The mysterious Jason. When are you two finally going to make that official?"

"Make what official?" I knew exactly what he meant. Apparently everyone at work did.

"You meet him every Tuesday for coffee. You talk about him constantly. Last month you cancelled girls' night because he was in town." He ticked the items off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it."

My face felt warm. "We're friends."

"Uh huh."

"We are." I had learned to be fine with that, or at least to stop hoping for something different. Two years of Tuesdays had made it clear enough.

"Okay." He pushed off the wall. "Have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

He left. I turned back to the screen and read the same paragraph three times without taking in a word.

The pivot table on page 42 had shifted three pixels to the left for no reason I could see. Three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots, each snap and crunch carrying across the quiet floor. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. And if I didn't fix that table, the VP would make a passive-aggressive comment about attention to detail on Monday.

My phone buzzed. The text said, "Still on for coffee? I'm heading over now."

It wasn't Tuesday. We had never once met on a Friday.

I looked at the three pages still waiting, and at the lights buzzing overhead. Then I typed, "Yeah. See you in 20," and sent it. The report could wait. I left it open on the screen, half checked, and picked up my bag.

The coffee shop was busy for a Friday evening. Students had claimed the outlets, and a book club had four kinds of pastry spread across a table in the back. Jason was at our usual table, halfway through something on his laptop.

He looked up when I came in.

A latte was already waiting across from my chair when I sat down. Extra foam, the way I liked it.

"Hey," he said. "Long day?"

"How can you tell?"

"You've got that specific look. Henderson account?"

"They want two more sections. Two. And they still want it Monday."

"That's unreasonable."

"I know it is. They know it is. But they're paying us, so it's happening anyway." I rubbed my eyes. "I left it at the office. I'll deal with it tomorrow."

"On a Saturday?"

"I don't have a choice."

Jason frowned, but he let it go. He knew how my job worked. He closed his laptop, slid it to the side, and turned his full attention to me. He kept his chair angled a little away from mine, as he always did.

I took a sip of the latte. It was exactly right. He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

"Thanks," I said.

"How's the camera?" he asked. "The Pentax you got last month?"

"It's great." I was talking before I'd decided to. "I finally got the light meter working, and I've been shooting an expired film stock I found on eBay. The grain is incredible."

"Did you develop any of it?"

"A batch Wednesday night. There's one of the bridge downtown at sunset that came out the way I wanted. The sky went green at the top from the expired stock, and I'd never have gotten that on purpose." I shrugged. "There's still a roll in the camera from last weekend. I keep meaning to develop it."

"You'll have to show me sometime."

"Most people think I'm crazy for paying for film when my phone takes perfectly good pictures."

"Most people are boring." He smiled. "Besides, with film you have to commit before you know what you got. That's the appeal, right?"

"Exactly." I loved that he'd actually listened the first time I tried to explain it. "My sister thinks it's a waste of money. Digital is easier and cheaper, she says."

"Easier and cheaper isn't always better."

"I'm stealing that line for the next time she gives me grief."

He laughed, and for a minute the table felt easy.

"How's Nora?" he asked. "Still trying to get you to Chicago?"

"Always. She sent me five apartment listings this week." I took a breath. "Mom called yesterday to ask about my promotion. She has a timeline for me, and apparently I'm behind. I promised her I'd bring milk to Sunday dinner. Dad mailed me another grad school application, the same as last month."

"Are you happy here?"

The question caught me off guard. "I am, mostly. My job is fine. I've got friends. I've got reasons to stay."

"That's good," he said.

"Do your parents do the timeline thing?" I asked.

"We don't really talk much."

"Oh. I'm sorry."

"It's fine. We didn't really get along." He said it lightly. "Have you thought any more about that darkroom space you were looking at?"

"I can't afford it," I said. "The rental alone would eat my fun money for a month, and then there's equipment and chemicals. It's just not practical right now."

"That's too bad. It sounded like a good setup."

"Maybe someday. When I finally get the promotion my mom keeps asking about."

He smiled at that. Then he slid a small paper bag across the table.

"What's this?"

"Open it."

Inside was a roll of film, the discontinued stock I'd complained about once, in passing, back in the fall. I had forgotten I'd said anything about it.

"I saw it at a camera shop across town," he said. "I thought of you."

I was ridiculously happy, and I tried not to let it show. I put the roll back in its bag and slid it into my coat pocket.

At the next table, two students had been arguing about free will for ten minutes. Now one of them raised his voice loud enough for half the room.

"If you could know every variable in the universe, you could predict everything. Free will is an illusion."

"That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard," his friend shot back. "Quantum mechanics exists. You can't know everything."

"Randomness doesn't equal free will!"

"It literally does!"

I winced. Jason glanced over at them, then back at me.

"You want to get out of here?" he asked. "Somewhere quieter?"

"Please." I thought about it. "We could try that Thai place Sarah keeps recommending. It's a few blocks away."

"Yes," he said. "Let's do that."

It was just past five. Jess had a late shift, so the apartment would be empty anyway, and the report would still be waiting for me on Monday. "Okay," I said. "But you're buying."

"Fine," he said, and started packing up his laptop.

We walked out into the February evening. It wasn't as cold as it could have been, but the air had a sharp edge that made me zip my coat. Jason fell into step beside me as we turned left, toward the restaurant.

We passed the park. The streetlights were orange, and they made the wet sidewalk look warmer than it was.

"Under these lights, that green film would probably look..." He stopped.

"Look what?"

"It would look strange," he said. "Would you still shoot it?"

"I'd shoot it anyway," I said. "I like seeing what it does."

A gust came up the sidewalk, and I stumbled on a crack. Jason's hand came up and stopped six inches from my elbow, hovering in the cold air. Then it dropped back to his side.

"I'm fine," I said.

The sidewalk was quieter now, the park on one side and a row of closed businesses on the other. The traffic from the main road was a distant hum.

"Do you ever get lonely?" I asked.

"Sometimes."

"Not with me, I'd guess."

"You're different."

"Different how?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

The air cracked.

That's the closest I can get to describing it. There was a flash of blue light right in front of us, electric and wrong, gone before I was sure I'd seen it. Something unseen hit it hard, and I felt the force of it in my chest. I never saw who did it.

Jason's hand was already clamped around my arm before I understood anything had happened.

A high ringing filled my ears. Another flash of blue. The world lurched sideways and kept lurching, and my skin felt peeled off and put back on wrong.

Then ground.

Dirt and dead leaves under my palms, rocks biting into my hands. Everything hurt.

Above me, bare branches stood black against a gray sky.
