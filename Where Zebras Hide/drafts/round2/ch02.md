# Chapter 2

My ears were ringing. A high, steady whine filled both of them, as if someone had struck a tuning fork inside my skull and left it there. I pushed up onto my hands, and the ground tilted under me like the deck of a boat. Nausea climbed into my throat. I stayed where I was and swallowed until it passed.

My palms stung. When I turned them over, the heels were scraped raw, with grit ground into the skin in a dozen small places. My knees throbbed where they had hit. Dead leaves clung to the front of my coat, and through the lining of my pocket I could feel the paper bag that held the roll of film.

The last clear thing I remembered was Jason's hand closing around my arm. After that there was only the blue flash and a lurch that went on too long. I tried to put the order of it together the way you do after a car accident, and every time I got stuck at the flash.

Jason was beside me before I'd finished noticing the ground. He knelt close, one hand on my shoulder, and his face came level with mine.

"Mira, tell me your name."

"My name is Mira."

"Good. What day is it?"

"It's Friday." I swallowed. "It's Friday, Jason."

"Good. Follow my finger for me."

He held one finger about a foot from my face and moved it left, then right, then back. He lifted one of my eyelids with his thumb, then the other, and studied my pupils for a long second before he let go. His fingers moved through my hair, slowly, feeling my scalp for blood. He did all of it without hurry, the way a person does a thing he has done many times before.

"No blood," he said. "You're going to feel sick for a while. That's normal."

"I already feel sick."

"Then sit still a minute longer."

He stood and turned in a slow circle, watching the trees. There was a leaf caught in his hair and dirt down one side of his coat, but he didn't sway once, and he didn't favor either leg.

I sat back on my heels and tried out explanations, since that was something I could do. A bomb, I thought. Something had gone off on the sidewalk, and the blast had thrown us both into the dirt. Or a gas line had blown near the park. Or I had hit my head harder than it felt, and my brain was making up a bigger story to explain the ringing. I had read that concussions made people lose time and see things that weren't there.

My phone was in my coat pocket. I pulled it out and pressed the side button, and the screen came on with no cracks in it. The map opened on its own. A small blue dot sat in the middle of country I had never looked at, and the label under it read Crossville, Tennessee.

I checked the signal next. One bar. Battery at sixty-one percent.

I listened for traffic and heard nothing at all, not even a car on a distant road. Wind moved through the bare branches. Somewhere uphill, a bird called twice and stopped.

The clock in the top corner of the screen said 4:47 PM. I had checked my phone as we left the coffee shop, and it had said just past five. Now it said 4:47. The clock had gone backward, and I stared at the number until it stopped looking like a number.

I took a screenshot of the clock. Then one of the map, with the blue dot and the word Tennessee. Then I recorded a short video, turning slowly in place, and said the date and the time out loud for it. Then I took still pictures of the trees, the leaves on the ground, and the gray sky through the branches. I needed something I could show someone later, even if I couldn't explain it to them.

I zoomed in on the bark of the nearest trunk. Every ridge was sharp and every patch of lichen was distinct, and the picture looked like any picture I would have taken on an ordinary walk.

Jason had come back toward me while I worked. He stopped a few yards away and waited until I looked up.

"What happened to us?" I asked.

"I'll tell you." He said it evenly, the way he said everything, and that scared me more than shouting would have. "But I need to be sure we're alone first."

"Alone from what?"

"Give me twenty minutes. I need to know whether anyone followed us."

I wanted to shout at him. Instead I looked past him at the trees, then back at his face.

"You're asking me to sit here while you decide how much I'm allowed to know," I said.

"Thirty minutes would be safer."

"Make it fifteen."

"Twenty is the least I need."

"Fine. Twenty minutes. Then you tell me everything, whether you've figured it out or not."

"Agreed."

I set the timer on my phone for twenty minutes and pressed start. The numbers began to count down, and I made myself look away from them. I sat down on a fallen log, because standing was getting harder than I wanted to admit.

Jason walked up the slope to where the trees thinned and a low shelf of gray rock showed through the leaves. At the top he stopped. From the log I could see his back and his shoulders, and I couldn't tell what he was doing.

I opened my messages. Sarah had texted while we walked, and so had my mom, and both messages had been waiting when I looked. I answered Sarah first. "Something came up, can't make it tomorrow. Will explain later." I sent it before I could second-guess myself.

Her reply came within a minute. "You ok? You never skip gallery stuff"

I wrote back, "Yeah, fine. Just got caught up with something. Promise I'm ok." Sarah wrote, "Call me later?" and I wrote, "Will do."

Jess had a late shift, so she wouldn't see anything for hours. I texted her anyway: "Staying with a friend tonight, back tomorrow."

My mom wanted to know whether I was still bringing milk to Sunday dinner. I wrote, "Yes, of course. See you Sunday." It came out smoother than anything I had written in weeks, and that bothered me more than I wanted it to.

Then I opened a blank note and typed the sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." I read it through once. Then I looked away at the gray trunks, and back. The words hadn't changed. I had read somewhere that lucid dreamers use text as a test for exactly that reason, because dream text shifts every time you look away.

I put the phone face down on my knee and breathed. In through my nose, out through my mouth, the way my therapist had taught me for panic attacks. This wasn't a panic attack. This was a legitimate reason to panic. But the breathing still helped.

I picked the phone up again and typed 911. My hands were shaking, so I held it with both of them and let my thumb hover over the call button. I imagined the dispatcher asking where I was, and what I would say back. I was in Ohio, and now I'm in Tennessee. How would that sound to a stranger on a line?

Up on the rise, Jason stood without moving, doing whatever he was doing. Whatever had happened to us, no patrol car could drive us out of it, and he seemed to know that already.

I didn't press call. Two years of coffee and careful distance had taught me that he wouldn't hurt me, and I wasn't ready to stop believing it. I deleted the numbers one at a time.

The light through the branches had gone flat and gray. It would be evening soon.

When I checked the phone again, the timer read zero. It had run out while I was looking at the trees, and I hadn't noticed when.

Jason was still on the rise. Nobody had come out of the trees. Nothing moved except the wind in the bare branches and the bird, which had started calling again somewhere uphill.

I waited a minute, then another, and counted the trees between me and the rise until I lost my place. Then I stood up. My knees complained, and I crossed the leaves to the bottom of the rise and looked up at him.

"Time's up," I called.

He turned his head toward me. Then he started down the slope, placing each foot carefully on the loose rock.
