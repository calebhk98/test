# Chapter 2

My ears were ringing when I first became aware of anything else. A high, steady whine filled both of them, as if someone had struck a tuning fork inside my skull and left it there. I pushed up onto my hands, and the ground tilted under me. Nausea climbed into my throat, and I stayed exactly where I was until it passed.

My palms stung when I turned them over. The heels were scraped raw, with grit ground into the skin in a dozen small places, and my knees throbbed where they had hit. Dead leaves clung to the front of my coat in wet clumps. I had zipped it all the way up at the coffee shop, and it was still zipped. Through the lining of my pocket I could feel the paper bag that held the roll of film, and I pressed my hand over it without quite deciding to.

The air smelled of wet earth and rot, the kind of smell that gets into a coat and stays there for days. The last clear thing I remembered was Jason's hand closing around my arm. After that there was only the blue flash and a lurch that went on far too long. I tried to put the order of it together, piece by piece, and every time I reached the flash I got stuck there.

Jason was beside me before I had finished noticing the ground. He knelt close, one hand resting on my shoulder, and his face came level with mine. His voice was low and even, and his hands were careful, moving only where they needed to.

"Mira, tell me your name."

"My name is Mira." My tongue felt thick, and the words came out slower than I meant them to.

"That's right," he said. "Now tell me what day it is."

"It's Friday, the ninth of February." I swallowed hard, because my voice had come out like a question.

"Now follow my finger for me."

He held one finger about a foot from my face and moved it left, then right, then back toward the middle. I followed it with my eyes until my head swam, and he watched my face the whole time. He lifted one of my eyelids with his thumb, then the other, and studied my pupils for a long second before he let go. His fingers moved through my hair next, slowly, working across my scalp and feeling for blood. He found a tender bump above my left ear, pressed around it with two fingers, and nodded to himself as though that was the answer he wanted. He did all of it without any hurry, the way a person does a thing he has done many times before.

"No blood," he said. "You're going to feel sick for a while, and that's normal."

"I already feel sick."

"Then sit still a minute longer, and tell me if it gets worse."

When I tried to stand, he offered his hand without making a fuss about it. His grip was warm and steady. He took my weight for a moment longer than he needed to, watching my knees as I found my balance, and only then did he let go.

He stood and turned in a slow circle, watching the trees. There was a leaf caught in his hair and dirt down one side of his coat, but he stood steady, and he shifted his weight evenly onto both legs.

I sat back on my heels and tried out explanations, since that was something I could still do with my head. A bomb seemed most likely. Something had gone off on the sidewalk, and the blast had thrown us both into the dirt before either of us understood what had happened. Or a gas line had blown near the park. Or I had hit my head harder than it felt, and my brain was making up a bigger story to explain the ringing. I had read that concussions made people lose time and see things that weren't there, and I couldn't rule out either one.

My phone was in my coat pocket, where it had been all afternoon. I pulled it out and pressed the side button, and the screen came on with no cracks in it at all. The map opened on its own. A small blue dot sat in the middle of country I had never looked at, and the label under it read Crossville, Tennessee. I checked the signal next. There was one bar, and the battery was at sixty-one percent.

I listened for traffic and heard nothing at all, not even a car on some distant road. Wind moved through the bare branches above me, a dry rustle that never quite stopped. Somewhere uphill, a bird called twice and then went quiet, and the quiet afterward seemed to go on for a long time.

Past the nearest trunks the ground dropped into a hollow, and beyond that a long ridge ran off into the haze. There were no roofs and no power lines anywhere I looked.

The clock in the top corner of the screen said 4:47 PM. I had looked at my phone as we left the coffee shop, and it had said just past five. Now it said 4:47, and I could not make that fit with anything. I stared at the digits until they stopped looking like anything at all.

I opened my calendar next. Friday, February ninth, and the only thing on it was the Henderson deadline, due Monday morning. I closed the app and held the phone up toward the gap in the branches, as if more light might change the number. It didn't. The sky above the trees was the same flat gray it had been since I opened my eyes.

So I did the only useful thing I could think of, which was to document everything. I took a screenshot of the clock. Then I took one of the map, with the blue dot and the word Tennessee in the corner of the frame. Then I recorded a short video, turning slowly in place, and said the date and the time out loud for the recording. After that I took still pictures of the trees and of the gray sky showing through the branches. I needed something I could show someone later, even if I couldn't explain it to them.

I zoomed in on the bark of the nearest trunk. Every ridge was sharp, every patch of lichen had its own clean edge, and the picture looked exactly like the pictures I would have taken on any ordinary walk.

Jason had come back toward me while I worked. He stopped a few yards away and waited until I looked up from the screen.

"What happened to us?" I asked.

"I'll tell you." He said it evenly, the way he said everything, and that scared me more than shouting would have. "But I need to be sure we're alone before I start."

"Alone from what?"

"Give me twenty minutes. I need to know whether anyone followed us."

"Are you hurt?"

"I'm not hurt. Neither are you, from what I can tell."

I flexed my fingers, then my wrists and ankles, one at a time. Nothing answered with pain worse than a bad bruise.

I wanted to shout at him. Instead I looked past him at the trees and the thin gray light coming down through them, and then back at his face. It was the same face I had watched across a coffee table every Tuesday, and I had no idea what was going on behind it.

"You're asking me to sit here while you decide how much I'm allowed to know," I said.

"Thirty minutes would be safer."

"Make it fifteen."

"Twenty is the least I need, and I won't pretend it's less."

"Twenty minutes it is, then. After that you tell me everything, whether you've figured it out or not."

"That's fair," he said.

I set the timer on my phone for twenty minutes and pressed start. The numbers began to count down, and I made myself look away from them.

I sat down on a fallen log, because standing was getting harder than I wanted to admit. The bark was wet under my hands, and it left dark marks on my palms where the scrapes were. The trunks around me were tall and straight, spaced far enough apart that I could see a long way between them.

Jason walked up the slope to a place where the trees thinned out and a low shelf of gray rock showed through the leaves. At the top he stopped. From the log I could see his back and his shoulders, and I couldn't tell what he was doing up there.

I opened my messages. Sarah had texted while we walked, and so had my mom, and both threads were waiting for me as if nothing had happened. I answered Sarah first. "Something came up. I can't make it tomorrow, and I'll explain later." I sent it before I could second-guess myself, because gallery night was the one thing I had promised her I wouldn't skip this month.

Sarah had been asking me about gallery night all week, and she would notice a skipped one before anyone. Her reply came back within a minute. "You ok? You never skip gallery stuff"

I wrote, "Yeah, fine. I just got caught up with something. I promise I'm ok." Sarah wrote, "Call me later?" and I told her I would. Each message took me a few seconds to write, and I wrote every one of them without stopping to think.

Jess had a late shift, so she wouldn't look at her phone for hours. I texted her anyway, so she wouldn't worry when she got home to an empty apartment: "I'm staying with a friend tonight, and I'll be back tomorrow."

My mom wanted to know whether I was still bringing milk to Sunday dinner. I typed, "Honestly, I don't know where I am," looked at it for a second, and deleted it. Then I wrote, "Yes, of course. I'll see you Sunday." It came out smoother than anything I had written in weeks, and that bothered me more than I wanted it to.

The Henderson report was still open on my office computer, three pages from done. It seemed like something that had happened to a different woman. We were supposed to be eating Thai food right now, a few blocks from the park, and I would have been complaining about the wait.

Then I opened a blank note and typed the sentence I always used to check a keyboard: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." I read it through once. Then I looked away at the gray trunks, counted two slow breaths, and looked back. The words hadn't changed. I had read somewhere that lucid dreamers use text as a test for exactly this reason, because dream text shifts every time you look away from it.

The battery had dropped to fifty-eight percent, and I turned the screen down as low as it would go. I put the phone face down on my knee and breathed. In through my nose, out through my mouth, the way my therapist had taught me for panic attacks. This wasn't a panic attack. This was a legitimate reason to panic. But the breathing still helped.

I picked the phone up again and typed 911. My hands were shaking, so I held it with both of them and let my thumb hover over the call button. I imagined the dispatcher asking where I was. I imagined trying to answer. I was in Ohio, and then I was in Tennessee. How would that sound to a stranger on a line?

Up on the rise, Jason stood without moving, doing whatever he was doing. Whatever had happened to us, no patrol car could drive us out of it, and he seemed to know that already.

I didn't press call. Two years of coffee and careful distance had taught me that he wouldn't hurt me, and I wasn't ready to stop believing it. I deleted the numbers one at a time, and the dial pad went back to being a grid of keys.
When I checked the phone again, the timer read zero. It had run out while I was looking at the trees, and I hadn't noticed when it happened.

Jason was still on the rise. Nobody had come out of the trees toward us, and nothing had moved except the wind in the bare branches and the bird, which had started calling again somewhere uphill. I waited for the first minute to pass, then the second, and counted the trees between me and the rise until I lost my place. I tried to picture what he was doing up there, and I couldn't. Twenty minutes had sounded reasonable when he asked for them, and now they sounded like a very long time. The sun had dropped behind the ridge, and the light under the trees had gone the color of weak tea. The cold was seeping up out of the ground, and it was beginning to reach through the front of my coat.

Then I stood up. My knees ached when I stood, and I crossed the leaves to the bottom of the rise and looked up at him.

"Time's up," I called.

He turned his head toward me. Then he started down the slope, placing each foot carefully on the loose rock.
