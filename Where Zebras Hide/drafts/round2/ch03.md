# Chapter 3

Jason came down the last stretch of the slope one step at a time, testing each stone before he trusted it with his weight. The leaf was still caught in his hair, and I didn't tell him. When he reached the bottom he stopped a few feet away, close enough to talk without raising his voice, and looked at me instead of the trees.

"Nobody followed us," he said. "I checked from up there, and nothing is coming toward us."

"How can you be sure?"

"I'm sure enough to talk to you about it, which is the most I can give you right now."

I wanted more than that, but it was the only answer I was going to get. Above the hollow the top of the ridge had gone dark, and the light under the trees was thinning out fast. We had maybe an hour of it left.

"Then tell me everything that happened, from the start."

He glanced at the fallen log, then stayed where he was, standing with his hands at his sides. He talked as he always did, in full sentences with the pauses in the right places. I had heard him explain a lease and a tax form in exactly that voice. I had never heard him use it for something like this.

"There were people on the sidewalk tonight, and they were attacking me. You just happened to be there."

"I didn't see anyone. I was looking right at the street."

"You weren't meant to see them. They didn't want to be seen."

"Who are they?"

"They're people like me," he said quietly. "I can do things most people believe are impossible. I'm not the only one who can. There are others, and some of them want what I know."

I thought about the blue light, how it had hung in the air for less than a second before the sidewalk tipped under my feet. "Was that the light, or something you did?"

"Part of it was mine, and the rest was theirs."

"Did you know this was coming?"

"I thought it might come someday. I didn't think it would come on a Friday, with you walking beside me."

"So you've known for a while."

"I've known they were looking. I didn't know they'd find me tonight."

"Was that teleporting?" I asked. The word sounded ridiculous in my mouth, like something from a movie I'd never liked.

"It's the closest word for it. It's random, though. I didn't pick Tennessee specifically, and that's what randomized means. I've done it many times."

"So you've been doing this for a while." I heard how calm I sounded, and I didn't like it.

"I've been doing it long enough to know how it goes."

"How does it work?"

"I won't explain how it works, not tonight and not out here."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving you tonight."

I looked at the gray trunks around us, counting them without meaning to, and tried to picture a bus stop out here. I couldn't. Everything I could see was branches and the dark hollow below.

"How do we get home, then?"

"I can't aim it, so I can't pick where we land. Tomorrow we'll either rent a car or find a bus out of Crossville. It's about three hundred and seventy miles to Columbus, and we don't have a car."

"And after we get home, what happens?"

"After that I leave Columbus." He said it flatly, the way a man crosses out a plan he had hoped he'd never need. "I'd meant to stay there for years, but they know where I live now. I can't go back to my place, and I can't wait around for them to come looking."

I sat down on the log, because the cold had come up through the ground and into the backs of my legs, and I didn't want to stand while he told me the rest. After a moment he sat at the far end of it, with the middle of the log left empty.

"Can I come with you?" The question came out before I had decided to ask it.

He said no before I'd finished asking.

"Why won't you let me?"

"Because what happened today could happen again, and next time I might not be fast enough to get you out." He spoke carefully. He had clearly worked the sentence out before he ever came down the hill.

"Then I'll stay right beside you."

"Being close to me is how people get hurt."

"You don't get to decide that for me."

"I'm not deciding for you. I'm telling you what I'm going to do."

"That's the same thing, and you know it." I turned on the log to face him. "Then teach me. Whatever this is, teach me how to handle it."

"Teaching you would only make you a target for the people who want me." His voice stayed level. "They'd come after anyone standing next to me."

"So I'm supposed to go home and wait to see whether they come for me there?"

"You're supposed to go home and stay away from me."

"I'm an adult, Jason. I'm the one who'd be in danger."

"I'm trying to keep you alive!" His voice came up hard, and he didn't pull it back. "It wouldn't be fair of me to risk your life just because I love you!"

For a few seconds neither of us spoke. The woods were so quiet that every small sound I made seemed loud.

For one full breath I was happy, and I was ashamed of it. Then the anger came up over it, and I held on to that instead.

I'd imagined this moment a thousand times, in the break room, walking to my car, maybe late at night over wine. I had never once imagined it like this, shouted at me in the woods as the reason to leave me behind.

"You love me so you've been lying to me for two years?"

"I didn't lie to you," he said. He kept his voice low this time. "I just didn't tell you everything."

"Two years of Tuesdays, and you decided I didn't need to know."

I pushed my hand into my coat pocket and found the paper bag, with the roll of film still inside.

"You found me discontinued film," I said, "and you never once asked me to dinner."

"I didn't," he said quietly.

I stood up, because I couldn't sit beside him any longer. I walked three steps down the slope and stopped with my back to him, and I counted the gray trunks again.

"There's something else you need to know," he said from the log. "I can make you forget."

I turned around.

"There are two ways I could do it. The first takes only tonight. You'd remember me telling you I was moving away, and nothing from the last few hours. The second takes all of me, the whole two years."

"What would I keep from the coffee shop?"

"You'd keep everything up to the sidewalk, and then you'd remember me saying I was moving."

"Would I keep the blue light, and the woods?"

"The blue light and the woods would be gone. You'd feel confused for a moment, and then it would be an ordinary week."

"You wouldn't notice a gap. There would be no side effects."

"I do it sometimes," he said. "I do it when I have to."

My stomach turned over.

"Could you do it to me?"

"I could," he said. "You haven't started yet."

I didn't ask him what that meant. Below me the hollow had gone blue with shadow, and the air had turned colder against my knees.

I tried to picture waking up on Saturday in my own bed, sure I had spent Friday night at the Thai place. I couldn't tell whether the picture was a memory or a guess.

"How would I know?"

I went back over every Tuesday I could remember, looking for a gap, and I found nothing I could trust. The latte with extra foam. The film he had remembered from something I'd said in the fall.

"I haven't done it," he said. "I've never done it to you, not once."

I believed him, and that was the worst part, because believing him proved nothing.

"Don't ever offer that to me again. My mind is mine. You don't get to edit me for my own good."

"Then we'll talk about the rest of it."

I came back to the log and sat down, because the other choice was standing in the cold until my legs gave out.

"Tell me what my choices are, and say each one once."

"There are four. I'll say each one once. The first is that I take you home and leave. You keep everything, and you never see me again."

"The second is that I erase tonight, or all of me, and leave anyway."

"The third is that you follow me without knowing what you're following. You'd probably be found and killed."

"The fourth is that I tell you everything, and it changes you."

"I'm not doing the third one."

"Then explain the fourth."

"I can't explain it without changing you. It's like this. If I told you there's gold buried in a field somewhere, that's useless. But if I said it's twenty-five feet down near yellow oak trees? Now every time you see an oak tree, your brain checks."

"So you can't tell me the first part without the rest getting in."

"That's exactly the problem."

"So I'm supposed to choose without knowing what I'm choosing."

"There is no informed consent here. Either I tell you enough and it changes you, or I keep you in the dark and you choose blind. Both are terrible."

"So all I have to go on is trust."

"That's most of it. You'd have to trust that I'm telling you the truth about the danger, and that I wouldn't offer this if there were another way."

"What are my chances, if I choose it?"

"I don't have real numbers. A lot of people teach themselves, and the ones who die usually aren't counted anywhere."

"Have you come close to dying?"

"I've almost died more than once myself."

"How long do the people you've known last?"

"A few months, usually. Sometimes less."

"How do people learn it, then?"

"Most of them figure it out alone. Kids stumbling into it. Loners teaching themselves."

I thought about the summer I had been planning, and about Nora's graduation in June, which I had meant to attend. The roll in my camera back home was still undeveloped. The Henderson report would sit half checked on my office computer until somebody else opened it on Monday.

"Why do they want what you have?"

"Because they want it, and I won't give it to them. I won't say more about them tonight."

"I'm not deciding this tonight."

"I didn't expect you to."

"Then give me real time, not a few hours of panic."

"You'll have until we're back in Ohio. Once you're home, you choose, and I'll accept what you choose."

It wasn't enough time. But it was more than I'd had an hour before.

"We should start walking toward a road before the light goes."

I stood and brushed the dead leaves off the knees of my jeans, and waited for him to tell me which way the road was.
