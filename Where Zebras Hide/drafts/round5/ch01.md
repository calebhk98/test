# Chapter 1: Normal

The tubes over our row had been flickering since Tuesday. Each time one caught, the carpet and the spreadsheet on my screen went a shade greener, then the fixture settled back into the flat white the office called normal. Maintenance had come by twice. Both times they stood under it with their hands on their hips and said they'd put in a work order.

"Mira, you still here?"

Marcus was leaning on my cubicle wall with his jacket already on.

"Trying to finish this before the weekend."

"It's half past four on a Friday. The report can wait."

"It's due Monday morning, and I've still got two sections to write."

"Then finish it Monday morning," he said, grinning. "Come on, a few of us are going to Murphy's. You should come."

I looked back at the screen. Three pages still needed review, two citations were missing, and the pivot table on page forty-two had slid a little off center for no reason I could find. The Henderson numbers also had a rounding error in the third column that I'd been meaning to fix since Tuesday. Page forty-two had a legend that said "Q3 (est.)" in a font nobody had chosen on purpose. If I stayed an hour, most of it would be done. If I walked out, I'd spend the whole weekend thinking about it.

"I can't. I've got coffee with Jason."

Marcus's grin spread across his whole face. "The mysterious Jason. When are you two going to make it official?"

"Make what official?" I knew exactly what he meant. Everyone on our floor seemed to have settled on what this was.

"You meet him every Tuesday. You talk about him constantly. You skipped girls' night last month to have dinner with him." He ticked each one off on his fingers. "You're basically dating. You just haven't admitted it."

"We're friends," I said.

Down the row a printer started up with a long, dragging whine.

"Uh huh." He looked me over. "You've worn that same gray cardigan all week, and you've eaten lunch at this desk every day. I'm just saying what I see."

"We are friends." I'd gotten used to that. Two years of careful distance had made it hard to believe anything else.

"Okay," he said, and pushed off the wall. "Have fun with your friend. Try not to work all weekend."

"No promises."

Marcus stayed in the gap of my cubicle a second longer, then went. I read the same paragraph three times without keeping any of it. Half the desks on the floor were empty already. Somewhere three cubicles down, Dave was eating baby carrots, the snap and crunch of each one carrying across the quiet room. I hated Dave. I hated his carrots. I fixed the first subtotal anyway. Then the second, which made the third one look wrong. My mug had gone cold beside the keyboard, with a brown ring inside it.

My phone buzzed on the desk. "Still on for coffee at five?" It was from Jason.

"Yes, I'll see you there," I typed.

I was still looking at the reply when my mom's text came in. "Can you bring milk Sunday?"

"Yes, I'll bring some," I typed.

Then I saved the file with page forty-two still wrong. I pulled on my coat, wound my scarf twice around my neck, tucked it into a pocket, and got the crossbody over my head. The hallway smelled of somebody's reheated soup. The elevator took its time, and in its brushed metal doors my reflection had one side of its hair falling out of the clip, and I didn't fix it. Outside the glass doors the sky had already gone dim, and the poles in the parking lot glowed orange. In the lobby the security guard nodded at me without raising his eyes from his crossword. The report was still open on the screen behind me.

The shop was busy for that time of evening, and I came in with the cold still in my cheeks. Students had claimed the outlets with their laptops, and a book club had taken the far end of the room, with four kinds of pastry covering its tabletop. A woman near the stairs was reading a paperback with her tea going cold beside it. The only hard light came from the bulbs above the counter. They put a sharp edge on every lid and spoon, while the rest of the room was soft and yellow. The espresso machine hissed every few minutes, and nobody looked up. Someone's name was called, and a woman at the back went up for her order. Jason was standing under the bulbs with our two drinks.

Our table was the small round one at the back, and Jason had folded a napkin under its short leg. Jason's backpack sat on the chair beside his. I got my coat off and hung it over the back of my chair, and my scarf went on top of it. My bag went on the seat, and the metal under me was cold through my jeans. Jason sat facing the counter, so I had the room over his shoulder. By the time I sat down my latte was in front of me, its foam poured into a leaf, a little lopsided, and Jason had brought his own cup back. I set it face up beside the latte, and it buzzed almost at once. I drank some first, while the foam was still soft at the edge. Past the laptops I could see the street, already going blue.

Nora's text came first. "Fifth one this week, and this kitchen is your size."

I turned it over. "Nora," I said. "She wants me to look at another apartment in Chicago. I'm not going."

"How's the Pentax?" Jason asked.

"The best thing I've bought in years. I finally got the light meter working, which means I can stop guessing and be wrong in a more educated way."

"I've been shooting this expired stock from eBay. The whole batch has a green cast to it."

"Did any of it come out?"

"Some of them did. There's one of the bridge downtown at sunset, and the sky went strange, but somehow it works."

"You'll have to show me sometime."

"I'll show you." The grain on that one is incredible. I have a roll sitting in the camera at my apartment, and I haven't even got it out.

"Most people think I'm crazy for paying for film when a free app takes decent pictures."

"I'm sorry. I've been talking about film since I sat down."

"Most people are boring." He smiled. "Besides, film makes you slow down. You can't take two hundred shots and keep the one you like without thinking about it."

"That's exactly it," I said. "I like the week between shooting it and getting it back. By then I've forgotten half of what I was trying to do."

"Is the co-op on the east side still the one with the big enlarger?"

"You remember that?" I asked.

"You told me weeks ago," he said.

"It's there, as far as I know. It has a red safelight and a sink that drains, which most basements don't have. The rent's more than I can afford, though, and that's before the equipment."

"Anything planned for tomorrow?" Jason asked.

"Sunday dinner at my parents'." Mom wants to know why I'm not further along, and Dad keeps mailing me applications. They have the same list. They've just split it between them.

"And you?" I asked.

"The same contract work as always."

"Do your parents do that too?" I asked.

"We didn't get along all that well," he said evenly. Then he asked whether I'd thought any more about the darkroom space.

"I hadn't since last week," I said, and let him move us along.

He reached into the backpack beside him and brought out a small paper bag, folded over at the top. "I saw this at a camera shop across town," he said. "I thought of you."

Inside was a roll of the black-and-white stock I'd wanted to try once, months ago, back when I was only looking at cameras. They'd discontinued it years ago. I read the label twice. The box was yellow, and the name had worn off one end.

"I'm going to be insufferable about this," I said. "Thank you, Jason. This is so nice of you. Thank you for thinking of me."

I heard myself start another thank you and stopped. He remembered everything except to ever ask me on an actual date.

I put the roll in my coat pocket without opening it, and set the paper down beside the latte.

The students at the next booth had been arguing for a while, but now one of them raised his voice enough that half the shop could hear. The taller one had a stack of index cards and kept tapping them against his knee. A girl beside them packed up her laptop slowly, so she could hear the end of it.

"If you could know every variable, you could predict everything that will ever happen. Free will is literally an illusion."

"That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard," his friend said, just as loud. "Quantum mechanics exists."

"Quantum randomness doesn't equal free will!"

Someone behind the counter laughed.

"It literally does!"

It sounded exactly like Jess and me fighting about the dishwasher, and I winced.

"Do you want to go somewhere quieter?" I asked. "The Thai place Sarah keeps recommending is about a ten-minute walk."

"We can do that," he said.

I got back into it, tucked the phone away, and got the strap over my head. Jason zipped the backpack and got it onto his shoulders. My scarf stayed on my chair. The drinks and the paper bag sat on the table, and we went out past the book club.

Outside, the air had an edge to it. I pulled the zip up to my chin and was glad of it. My breath showed under the streetlamps and vanished past them. The streetlamps along the park had come on, the old yellow kind, so each one made a warm pool on the sidewalk, and the grass beyond it had gone the blue of open shade. Jason fell into step beside me as we reached the corner. For a block our footsteps didn't match, and then they did. Under each lamp our shadows were sharp and dark, and between the lamps they stretched out long and faded. The thing in my pocket knocked against my hip with every step.

The park path ran downhill on our right. Traffic hummed somewhere beyond the trees. Through the trees I could see the river, dark and moving, with broken yellow lines lying across it where the lamps caught the water. The benches along the path were empty, their slats gone dark with damp, and one had a brass plaque too worn to read from the path. Leaves blew across the path in front of us and piled against the curb. A jogger was on the far side of the road, and then the corner took him. A car slowed at the crosswalk with its headlights on, then went on. Across the road the houses had their porch lights on, and somebody's fireplace was going. The smell of it came down the hill to meet us. A bike was chained to a railing outside a dry cleaner, and each gust knocked its chain against the metal. The Thai place was still somewhere ahead, past the next block.

I took out my phone. It said 5:42. Nora's listing was unopened, and I wasn't sure I was going to open it.

"Would you shoot that expired stock at night?" Jason asked. "Under those streetlights?"

"I would. Those streetlights would push the cast even further, and I'd love that."

"That sounds like you," he said. I laughed, and it came out smaller than I meant.

The sidewalk had heaved where a root pushed up under the pavement, and my heel caught the edge. I lurched sideways. Jason's hand came up toward my elbow and stopped a few inches short of it. He let it drop back to his side.

"I'm fine," I said, and kept walking.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Go ahead," he said. I had been working up to this since the shop.

"Do you ever get lonely?"

"Sometimes," he said.

A bus went past on the main road behind the park, bright inside and empty. Farther down, a man was unloading groceries from the back of a car.

"You're different," he said.

"Different how?" I said.

He opened his mouth to answer.

Something in the air tore. I felt the pressure in my ears before I heard anything, and then a burst of blue light opened several feet in front of us. Something invisible hit it. The impact came through the air and landed in my chest, as if someone had shoved me with the whole street. Jason had hold of my arm, hard, before I understood that anything had happened.

My ears rang. Pressure came in from every side at once, and the ground tipped under my feet. Something went over the whole surface of my skin, and I had nothing to compare it to. For about a heartbeat the motion turned sideways, then went on as it had been going.

Then there was ground. Leaves were cold through my jeans, and a rock dug into my palm. I was sitting, then on my back, and my lungs wouldn't take air.

Jason was on his knees in the leaves, a short way off.

Above me there were only bare branches, and past them a gray sky.
